## Definition

A rare autoimmune endotheliopathy classically taught as a triad — sensorineural hearing loss, central retinal artery occlusion (branch retinal artery occlusions), and white matter lesions (a "snowball"/corpus callosum pattern on MRI). Only a minority of real cases present with the full triad; headache — not even part of the classic triad — is actually more common at presentation than any single triad element, which is a recurring lesson about textbook triads generally.

## How it operates

- **The full triad is the exception, not the rule.** Absence of any one triad component should not be used to exclude the diagnosis, the same lesson that applies to Wernicke encephalopathy's confusion-ataxia-ophthalmoplegia triad (present in under 20% of real cases).
- **Headache is the most common presenting feature**, despite sitting outside the classic triad — a reminder that the mnemonic and the epidemiology can diverge.
- **It is a natural "knee-jerk" association** for hearing loss plus a white matter lesion in neurology — worth naming explicitly as an anchor rather than letting it operate silently, especially when the lesion's location and character are not classic for it.
- **Confirmatory workup** includes fluorescein angiography looking for subclinical retinal vasculopathy alongside anti-endothelial cell antibody testing.

## Examples from RLR episodes

- **A knee-jerk association, named and then set aside.** With hearing loss, a nonspecific white matter lesion, and mild CSF inflammation but a lesion location and character that are not classic for Susac syndrome, the discussant explicitly names anchoring on it while acknowledging the diagnosis doesn't fit cleanly — proposing a fluorescein angiogram to test it rather than accepting it by default; a new headache and progressive weakness ultimately redirect the case to primary CNS vasculitis instead. ([[RLR 078 - Hearing Loss, an Isolated White Matter Lesion, and Primary CNS Vasculitis|RLR 078]])
- **Used as a teaching parallel for incomplete triads.** Susac syndrome's incomplete-triad epidemiology is invoked directly when a normal potassium, calcium, and phosphate almost derail recognition of subacute tumor lysis physiology — a reminder that syndromes taught as complete tetrads or triads often present with only a subset of their classic features. ([[RLR 079 - Paroxysmal Flank Pain, Tumor Lysis Physiology, and an Occult Myeloproliferative Neoplasm|RLR 079]])
