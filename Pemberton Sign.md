## Definition

A bedside maneuver for suspected [[SVC Syndrome]]: sustained bilateral arm elevation overhead, watching for facial congestion and flushing. A positive sign supports obstruction of venous drainage above the thoracic inlet. Classically taught for external compression (e.g., a retrosternal goiter), but it also applies to internal (endovascular) causes such as lead- or catheter-induced stenosis — whether the underlying mechanism differs between internal and external causes is a genuine open question, not settled trivia.

## How it operates

- **Elevate both arms and hold.** Raising the arms further compresses or restricts flow through an already-narrowed thoracic-inlet outflow tract, provoking visible facial congestion within seconds to a minute.
- **Use it alongside other supportive findings**, not alone — venous dilation above the nipple line and subtle upper-extremity edema round out the bedside picture.
- **A change in the sign tracks treatment response.** Resolution of a previously positive Pemberton sign after intervention (e.g., angioplasty) is a useful bedside marker that the obstruction has been relieved.

## Examples from RLR episodes

- **Confirming and then tracking resolution.** A positive Pemberton sign — facial swelling and flushing with both arms raised — helps confirm SVC syndrome from a pacemaker lead; immediately after balloon angioplasty, the sign is no longer positive. The discussant is explicit that he is uncertain whether the sign's mechanism differs for internal (endovascular) versus external (e.g., goiter) causes of compression, naming the gap rather than guessing.
