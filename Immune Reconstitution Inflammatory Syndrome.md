IRIS as the unmasking of a previously subclinical infection or malignancy once antiretroviral therapy restores CD4 function.

## How it operates

- **Mechanism.** A previously immune-suppressed, subclinical infection or malignancy was present but silent while the immune system was too weak to mount an inflammatory response; restoring CD4 function enables that response, and the disease becomes newly symptomatic — paradoxically, in the setting of treatment working.
- **Timing is the key diagnostic clue.** New symptoms arising in the weeks to months after starting or restarting antiretroviral therapy should raise IRIS specifically, prompting a very low threshold for further workup (e.g., lumbar puncture) even when the presentation might otherwise seem benign.
- **ART is usually continued through IRIS**, with specific exceptions — intracranial infections such as CMV retinitis or cryptococcal meningitis, where reconstitution-driven inflammation within the closed cranial vault carries particular risk. Neurosyphilis is not conventionally among these exceptions.

## Examples from RLR episodes

- **Unmasking latent syphilis.** One month after restarting antiretroviral therapy, a woman with a CD4 of 25 develops headache and photophobia; the timing raises IRIS specifically, and the ultimate diagnosis — secondary and neurosyphilis — is framed as a likely paradoxical unmasking of latent infection by immune reconstitution, with ART continued throughout since neurosyphilis is not an ART-interruption exception.
