*Updated 2026-09-28: the Council has since been dropped. The structure is two committees established by the CEO's authorization; the Council's case-level work sits in the Peer Review Committee, and the seat counts below for the PRC and CQC stand. The annual audit plan now goes through the Chair to the CMO and COO at the quarterly meeting.*

Focus: keeping the committee’s as lean as possible.

### Clinical Governance Council

- Clinical Governance Council went from 17 seats to 9
	- Originally included every regional leader by office. Intent was to ensure input from operations, but it makes more sense to replace this large group with 2 enterprise seats: CMO and COO. 
	- The Council is now nine voting members in two classes:
		- The clinical class is seven: the Chair of the Primary Care Committee, the Chair of the Psychiatry Committee, and five at-large practitioners. 
		- The enterprise class is two: the Chief Medical Officer and the Chief Operating Officer, neither of whom sat before.
		- CMO and COO are not present for and not party to any determination about an identified clinician, avoiding concern of making PRC an employment action rather than clinical. They do sit for everything else.
	- At-large seats went from six to five. Five is the floor at which the chairmanship, the vice-chairmanship and a vacancy all still have a pool to draw on, and at which an adverse action vote survives a recusal plus an absence. 
	- My seat (Chair of Clinical Excellence) attends meetings but is non-voting:
		- I am already chair of Quality Committee and the Peer Review Committee. The Council's recusal rule already kept any committee chair from voting on matters coming up from the committee they chair. Applied to me, that reached essentially everything: every recommendation out of peer review, every standard, every audit plan, every policy. 
		- So the seat is now formally ex officio and non-voting. I present every committee matter, answer questions, and step out before deliberation on anything concerning a named clinician.
		- One thing this does not change: the Council's own chair is elected from the at-large practitioners and votes normally. The chair of the Council is a practicing clinician. That is a different seat from mine.


### Peer Review Committee

- The original design had a separate Reviewer Pool that the Council would maintain, with three-member panels drawn from the membership and the Pool together. This created a separate group of clinicians we’d need to recruit.
- I went and asked a hospital peer review committee about this. The committee members review the cases themselves, two members per case, and then the committee decides. No separate reviewer corps.
- That is now what our Charter says. Panels come from the standing membership. The Pool is still available, but it exists for discipline matching when the membership cannot supply it, it is explicitly permitted to be empty, and nobody has to be recruited into it before we launch.
- Reviewers-per-case went from three members to two, with a third at the chair's discretion when a case warrants it. This cuts recruiting needs by a third.
- The one place two-member panels cost something: a physician's case requires two physician reviewers, so with a floor of only two physicians on the committee, a single recusal emptied the panel. The physician floor moved from two to three to close that. Matching rules for nurse practitioners and psychiatric cases are unchanged.

### Clinical Quality Committee

- Traded “seats” for “functions”. 
- The CQC committee has no fixed seat count. What it fixes is the ratio, two-thirds in active practice, which means every non-clinician member costs us two practicing clinicians. This would include any representatives from learning-and-development, outcomes-centered health, or operations. So they will come to the table as functions rather than as members:
	- Full participation in standards, education and systems discussion, no vote, and no presence for anything concerning a named clinician. 
	- What that loses against membership is a vote on the education agenda and on the standards, which I can weigh without formal vote. 
	- They will have a voice in the annual plan: every enterprise priority brought to the committee gets a stated answer in the annual audit plan. 
- Where we do want the enterprise view seated as a member, the Charter caps members holding hiring and firing authority at one, so we pay that two-clinician cost once rather than repeatedly.
- The psychotherapist seat became consultative. A licensed psychotherapist is now consulted before we adopt any standard touching psychotherapy, and the seat comes back if we add psychotherapy to peer review.
- The committee now formally adopts an annual audit plan: the measures for the year, the standard each one measures against, and the response to each enterprise priority. the annual audit plan goes up to the Council, and the Council records whether it addresses enterprise priorities. The plan says what will be measured. The standards themselves stay with the Quality Committee. The Council can say we are not looking at the right things. It does not say what good care looks like.
- Any decision that turns an observation into a finding about a named clinician now requires at least three eligible members present.
- Current makeup of Quality Committee is about seven members (clinicians), with analytics, education, enterprise priorities and the coordinator at the table as functions.
