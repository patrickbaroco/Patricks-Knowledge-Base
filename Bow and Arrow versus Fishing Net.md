## Definition

Two modes of working a diagnostic problem. The **bow and arrow** is routine diagnosis: a single confident hypothesis, aimed and fired, appropriate when the presentation matches a well-known illness script closely enough that one target is worth committing to. The **fishing net** is what a hard case demands instead: broad, parallel testing across several competing hypotheses without early commitment to any one of them, because no single arrow is trustworthy yet. The framework is less about which tool to pick and more about recognizing the moment a case has stopped being a bow-and-arrow problem.

## How it operates

- **Match the tool to the confidence, not the effort.** A bow and arrow fired without real confidence is a guess dressed as a diagnosis; a fishing net thrown out of habit on an easy case wastes time and tests.
- **Escalate deliberately.** Reasonable diagnosticians start with the bow and arrow by default and switch to the net only when the first hypothesis fails to hold up against new data.
- **The deepest learning happens when the net comes up empty.** A missed diagnosis after broad, parallel testing forces a recalibration of the whole approach, not just correction of a single fact — that is a more valuable failure than a wrong single-shot guess.

## Examples from RLR episodes

- **A net that still needed a biopsy to close.** Facing hearing loss, an isolated nonspecific white matter lesion, and an inflammatory but nonspecific CSF, Aaron Berkowitz runs a broad parallel workup (infectious, autoimmune, imaging, CSF) rather than committing to one hypothesis — and even after the net is cast, the answer (primary CNS vasculitis) requires brain biopsy to land, illustrating that a fishing net widens the catch without guaranteeing it.
