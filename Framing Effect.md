Evergreen concept note. Definition plus worked examples, drawn from the RLR / Clinical Problem Solvers series and elsewhere. Source episodes are cited per example.

## Definition

The framing effect is the tendency for a judgment or decision to shift based on *how* information is presented rather than on the information itself. The classic demonstration is Tversky and Kahneman's Asian disease problem: identical outcomes described in terms of lives *saved* versus lives *lost* flip people's risk preferences. In medicine the same statistic — "90% survival" versus "10% mortality" — measurably changes the choices patients and clinicians make.

In clinical reasoning the "frame" is usually the packaging a case arrives in: the chief complaint, the ordering of the problem list, the acuity or chronicity emphasized in a handoff, or the reference range a value is read against. Because a chief complaint is often a diagnostic hypothesis in disguise, inheriting a frame means inheriting someone else's leading diagnosis before you've reasoned independently.

## Two faces: inherited framing vs deliberate reframing

- **Inherited framing is the trap.** The frame you're handed silently steers the workup — toward the metabolic derangement and away from the anatomic complaint, toward the acute finding and away from a reflex mechanism. The distortion is invisible precisely because it feels like "the case."
- **Reframing is the counter-move, and the hosts use it as an active tool.** Deliberately re-describing a finding — as consequence rather than cause, or against the baseline the patient *should* have — is often what breaks a case open. In this vault "[[Framing Effect|framing]]" is tagged both for the bias and for the corrective reframe; they are two directions of the same sensitivity to how information is packaged.

## Examples from RLR episodes

- **A frame built from one salient history detail.** "Splenectomy + headache + photophobia" assembled a meningitis-shaped frame so strong that reassuring exam and labs prompted *more testing within the frame* (CT, then LP) instead of a revision of it. The resident who reframed it benignly made the opposite error — moving to a harmless explanation without ever registering the asplenia — a reminder that a frame can mislead in either direction. ([[RLR 001 - New Onset Headache|RLR 001]])
- **Framing by the handoff label.** The case was presented as "nausea, vomiting, and hyponatremia" rather than "abdominal pain," and the group notes this framing nudged the workup toward electrolyte correction and away from anatomic imaging. The practical guard: re-list the patient's active problems in neutral terms at each handoff rather than inheriting the last presenter's framing. ([[RLR 002 - Nausea, Vomiting, and Hyponatremia|RLR 002]])
- **Reframing the eye-catching vital sign.** Rabih refuses to let a dramatic systolic anchor the case, dissecting hypertension into acute / chronic-primary / chronic-secondary and arguing that primary hypertension can't *acutely* injure — a reframe that converts the blood pressure from suspected cause into downstream consequence. ([[RLR 003 - Hypertension and Severe Hyponatremia|RLR 003]])
- **Framing by chronicity.** A vagal mechanism is cognitively available for a *transient* event (vasovagal syncope) but feels implausible for a *continuous* heart rate of 27; the sustained framing of the bradycardia is exactly what kept increased vagal tone off the differential until a bladder scan forced it on. ([[RLR 006 - Bradycardia and Urinary Retention|RLR 006]])
- **Framing by host physiology.** The same calcium of 11.5 means something different in an ESRD patient, who should be hypocalcemic. Reading the value against the *expected* baseline rather than the population range is what converts a mild abnormality into a meaningful delta — a reframe that rescues the finding from being dismissed. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])

## Examples elsewhere

- **The Asian disease problem.** Identical programs framed as "200 saved" vs "400 will die" reverse the majority's risk preference — the canonical proof that framing, not content, drives the choice.
- **Survival vs mortality in consent.** "90% survival" and "10% mortality" are the same number; patients (and clinicians recommending) choose differently depending on which is spoken.
- **The chief complaint as a hypothesis.** "Rule out PE" or "GI bleed" at the top of a note frames the encounter before any independent reasoning, much like the symptom-lead handoff above.

## Counter-moves

- **Re-list active problems in neutral terms at every handoff** rather than inheriting the previous framing ([[RLR 002 - Nausea, Vomiting, and Hyponatremia|RLR 002]]).
- **Actively reframe the salient finding.** Ask whether it is cause or consequence ([[RLR 003 - Hypertension and Severe Hyponatremia|RLR 003]]) and read it against the baseline the patient *should* have, not the population range ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]]).
- **Notice when acuity or chronicity is doing the framing, and test the opposite** — could a "continuous" finding still have a reflex/transient mechanism? ([[RLR 006 - Bradycardia and Urinary Retention|RLR 006]]).
- **When data don't fit the frame, revise the frame — don't just add tests inside it** ([[RLR 001 - New Onset Headache|RLR 001]]).
- **State a decision both ways** (survival and mortality, benefit and harm) before acting, to neutralize valence framing.
