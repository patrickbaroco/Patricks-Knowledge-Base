# Ben Wanamaker Read-In — Clinical Governance, 2026-09-21

1–1:40pm. Ben Wanamaker (CEO). Scheduled by Shanda Huneycutt after Ben asked on 9/15 to be taught what I am preparing. Source: meeting transcript, ~40 minutes. The conversation Cary said he would seek, five weeks after the structure was first drafted and twelve days after she previewed it at their 1:1.

**Bottom line: he is in, without reservation on the structure.** *"I'm fully supportive of that."* *"I love everything I've heard."* No objection to any of the three bodies, including the one that does not exist today. What he took on instead were three formality checks he owns, one caution about meeting load, and one suggestion he was careful to label a suggestion.

## The case, as delivered

Opened where I have been opening it: there is no infrastructure under quality and education. We set an initiative and have no *what's next*. Education runs alongside the quality priorities rather than to them, so we might be working antipsychotics while the didactics are about something else. And at the individual level the menu is ask a person to do better, educate them, ask more firmly, then at some undefined point it becomes an HR problem. That middle is what a hospital peer review program supplies and what we do not have.

**The three bodies, in the order I explained them:**

- **Quality Committee.** Sets the standards as rules and rates, not aspirations. Builds the education to those standards, then audits to them, including targeted audits of outliers. An open-book test: here is what we expect, here is the flood of education on it, here is the measurement afterward. Chaired by me as part of the job, with the primary care and psychiatry chairs and a number of physicians and NPs to develop the standards and do the auditing.
- **Peer Review Committee.** Monthly. The place an ADCO can take a facility's concern about an NP, and the destination for quality-committee outliers that meet referral criteria. Two reviewers from the same field, findings and a recommendation. Most outcomes are an educational agreement, closed, look back in 90 days, or concurrent review of a set number of charts. The teeth are practice restriction, which in our setting means something like no initial visits without direct supervision, or mandated proctoring. For a physician, a restriction in place more than 30 days is reportable to the National Practitioner Data Bank. Optional for APCs, though most places hold one standard across the board rather than pick and choose.
- **Clinical Governance Council.** The one we have no equivalent of. Restricting a person's practice, and exposing them to reportability, requires executive-level approval, and in a hospital the board does not want privileged material in a board packet, so a medical executive committee holds it. The Council is that: created by the board, delegated the authority to approve or return peer review recommendations and to approve the standards the Quality Committee selects. It is also where clinical and operations meet to confirm the initiatives are aligned.

Stated plainly for him: two of the three we sort of have. The CGSC can be rebuilt into the Quality Committee and the peer review committee can be made real. There is no equivalent of a medical executive committee, and without one the peer review program is not fully compliant.

**On privilege.** We currently have close to the worst of both worlds. We do a little of this, unstructured, which means none of the federal or state protections attach. Designed to the statutes, it becomes privileged and non-discoverable, and nothing said in it supports a defamation claim. All five of our states have peer review protection; Virginia makes it easy, Kentucky and Indiana are more particular about the rules you have to follow. Some of the committee design, the percentage of clinicians on each body in particular, exists to satisfy those statutes. Counsel confirms the specifics.

## Ben's frame, which is better than mine

He offered a parenting analogy and flagged it as one: **most good parents have very few rules, and they are deadly serious about the ones they have.** *"I agree with you that I think we're kind of the flip of that."* Dig through the inventory of wikis and you find evidence of a lot of things we said we cared about, and no seriousness behind any of them. The missing thing is exactly the infrastructure.

He made the same point about himself later, unprompted. The company has historically taken on too many things at once and got too few done well. *"I fell victim to that a little bit in Q2, in kind of buying into that and being part of the problem."* Q3 he has tried to back off to fewer things. Applied here: **"If all we talked about was one thing for six months, and we got that really good, that would be a feature, not a bug, in my view."**

Worth recording that this is the second executive to arrive at the program's premise on their own. Cary got there through the CGSC data. Ben got there through what he has been trying to fix about how the company works.

He also placed this work in a larger pattern: *"This new physician leadership work structure is the first time, honestly, in my career that I've copied something from a health system."* Spiritually aligned, take the good, do not replicate the extreme.

## What Ben owns

He is not opposed to establishing a medical executive body of the board or to the delegation of authority. Before it is created he wants three things checked, and he took them:

1. **The corporate documents.** What is provided for, what is not, and whether creating the Council requires amending them. Work with counsel.
2. **The directors and officers liability policy.** Whether it needs adjustment or expansion to cover this body.
3. **The malpractice scheme.** Whether it contemplates this and is helped or harmed. His instinct is that it should help, possibly earn credit or better rates.

His reasoning is the line to keep: *"You could actually squeeze the risk water balloon in a way that it managed it better in one place and increased it somewhere else."* He expects all three to be silent or adjustable. He does not want to create the body without having looked.

My answers where I had them. Malpractice is priced on the work you do and where you do it, the medical equivalent of how old is the driver and what are they driving, so I would not expect the Council to move it, though an internal patient safety program sometimes earns a discount. Indemnification of the medical executive body for good-faith decisions is largely federal and already exists, which is part of why the design should meet the statute rather than approximate it.

**His question I could only half answer: is there a legal entity to stand up, or does building the block elements create the environment?** Mostly the latter. More protection is available on the quality side by forming or partnering with a patient safety organization, and I went down that road and it did not buy much, especially once the sensitive part of quality routes into peer review, which has HCQIA of its own. He has done this before, standing up a PSO when he built the clinics at Walmart, and allowed that the legalities may have moved since. One for counsel.

## His one caution

**"I want to be really careful not to establish a thicket of meetings that from the outset makes people feel like, oh, all I've added is bureaucracy and overhead in meetings."**

The arithmetic I gave him:

- **Peer Review: one new monthly meeting.** Genuinely additive.
- **Quality: a trade, not an addition.** The CGSC is currently 30 minutes every other week and is not useful. *"It's just not set up well."* One well-agendaed hour a month replaces it. Fewer minutes, better spent. The department committees run their own projects and converge in one place less often.
- **Council: quarterly at best.** A medical executive committee with relatively little to do. A hospital's version carries everything Joint Commission mandates; ours is peer review plus confirming clinical and operational initiatives are aligned.

He closed the topic with a joke: *"The best way to make sure there aren't too many meetings is to make sure I have to attend them, and then let me control the cadence, and it'll be as spare as possible."* Not an offer, and it should not be read as one.

**What it leaves open is where the CEO actually sits.** In a hospital the CEO typically sits on the medical executive committee while the voting members come from the medical staff. Two arrangements are available here and they are different bodies. The CEO attends the Council. Or the board delegates its authority to the CEO, and the Council reports to him. The second keeps the Council clinical and puts executive approval a step above it rather than inside it. Nothing was settled today. The Charter has to pick one, and the answer bears on the delegation language counsel will be asked about.

## Education, and the number that landed

This is where the conversation turned from structure to what the structure is for.

I told him the wiki is the problem and that I have talked to Will about it. Education has to be reachable on a phone. A PDF buried in an internal website does not get read.

**The pathway evidence.** I wrote the UTI pathway. I have never seen it on the wiki. I polled the Virginia NPs and got 19 responses: **three had ever looked at any pathway, one had looked more than once** — and those are the people motivated enough to answer an email. *"We're giving ourselves credit for having written it, and that's not an outcome."* Tree falls in the forest, does it make a sound: who cares. If nobody reads it and no practice changes and no outcome moves, the existence of the document is not the point. I felt good writing it and learned something doing the research. Otherwise a functional waste of time.

What is missing is the second and third steps. If the focus is heart failure, **it should be hard for an NP to get through a week without hearing about heart failure** in several forms: didactics, newsletters, infographics, possibly social media. Then audit. Did it work. If not, is it a systems problem, an education problem, or individuals we need to address. We do not do that last part at all.

**Ben's suggestion, offered explicitly as a suggestion and not a direction.** Instead of ten arrows, one battering ram, and extend the done criteria past the provider: **each provider teaches the focus topic to the staff in their building.** *"Watch one, do one, teach one"* applied to us. His argument for it is commercial as much as clinical. An embedded quality culture and in-service training is something a facility loses if it terms us, which is a reason not to.

I agree and the reason is the same one I have been making about credit. If we are improving outcomes in a facility, the medical directors need the messaging for QAPI, the nursing staff needs the training and the ask, and we should be naming the measure we expect to move and then watching it move. **We advertise what we do inadequately.** We are the product.

Ben, on losing buildings: *"We've lost buildings to lower quality providers who advertised higher quality that they won't deliver, and that irritates me. If I'm going to lose a building to somebody who's doing it better than I am, that'll make me sad, but at least I'll be saying, well, those residents are going to get good care."* Losing one and knowing it is a downgrade for the patients is the part that stings.
