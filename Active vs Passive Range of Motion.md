Comparing pain on active versus passive movement localizes a problem to the joint itself versus the tendon that moves it — with one crucial caveat for inflamed tendons.

## The Mechanics
- In **active** motion the muscle-tendon unit contracts and is *loaded* — it does the work of moving the joint.
- In **passive** motion the examiner moves the joint while the tendon is a bystander, merely along for the ride ([[RLR 062 - Oligoarthritis and Polyarticular Gout Flare|RLR 062]]).

Whether pain reproduces when the tendon is loaded (active only) versus whenever the joint moves (both) tells you which structure hurts.

## The Three Patterns
- **Intra-articular process → pain with BOTH active and passive** ([[RLR 062 - Oligoarthritis and Polyarticular Gout Flare|RLR 062]], [[RLR 061 - Knee Swelling and Calcific Tendinitis|RLR 061]]). The inflamed joint surface is disturbed either way it is moved — a key septic-joint sign.
- **Mechanical tendinopathy (e.g., partial tear) → pain ONLY with active** ([[RLR 062 - Oligoarthritis and Polyarticular Gout Flare|RLR 062]]). Loading the damaged tendon hurts; passive motion, which does not load it, is spared.
- **Inflammatory tendonitis → pain with BOTH** ([[RLR 062 - Oligoarthritis and Polyarticular Gout Flare|RLR 062]]). An inflamed tendon hurts whenever it moves, active or passive — so it mimics a joint. This is the crucial caveat.

## The Practical Rule
- **"Pain with both" = intra-articular OR an -itis** (inflammatory tendonitis); it does not by itself confirm the joint ([[RLR 062 - Oligoarthritis and Polyarticular Gout Flare|RLR 062]]).
- **"Pain with active only" = mechanical tendinopathy** — the tendon is loaded but the joint is fine.
- This distinction sits under the broader [[Anatomical Approach]]: establish *where* the disease is before deciding *what* it is. In [[RLR 061 - Knee Swelling and Calcific Tendinitis|RLR 061]], a monoarticular *tendonitis* (calcific quadriceps tendinitis) presented like arthritis precisely because an inflamed tendon hurts on both.

## Bedside Application
- Isolate the movement: ask the patient to move the joint themselves (active), then relax fully while you move it (passive).
- Combine with palpation over the tendon versus the joint line, and with load/resistance testing, to sharpen localization before imaging.
