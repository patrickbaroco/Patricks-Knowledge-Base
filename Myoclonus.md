Myoclonus is a sudden, brief, shock-like jerk that moves a muscle across a joint — too fast to imitate voluntarily, arrhythmic, and not sustained ([[CPS 276 - Lethargy, Myoclonus, and Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome|CPS 276]]). Most myoclonus is *positive* — a sudden, brief muscle contraction — but the same lightning-fast interruption of motor output can also run in reverse as *negative myoclonus*, a sudden, brief loss of muscle tone (the mechanism behind asterixis, the metabolic "liver flap"). Both share the same abrupt, irregular timing that separates myoclonus from [[Tremor|tremor]]'s smooth, rhythmic, roughly sinusoidal oscillation — the two are defined against each other at the bedside, since there is no confirmatory test for either. As with any [[Movement Disorder Approach|movement disorder]], the diagnostic task is to characterize the movement first — what it looks and feels like — before running a cause schema ([[CPS 247 - Tremors, Myoclonus, and Steroid-Responsive Autoimmune Encephalopathy|CPS 247]]).

## Where myoclonus sits among the movement disorders

Involuntary movements first split into seizure versus movement disorder, and movement disorders then split into hypokinetic (parkinsonism) and hyperkinetic — tremor, myoclonus, tics, chorea–athetosis–ballism, and [[Dystonia|dystonia]] ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]). Unlike weakness, where the question is which anatomic level is involved, nearly all hyperkinetic movements trace back to shared basal ganglia (or, for myoclonus specifically, cortical/subcortical/brainstem/spinal) circuitry — so *naming the movement correctly* is usually more diagnostically productive than localizing it. The disciplined way to name it is to describe the movement in plain terms first (amplitude, rhythm, whether it is sustained or momentary, purposeful or not) and only then map that description onto a formal label — chorea's flowing, dance-like, non-rhythmic drift is distinguished from myoclonus's single, brief, shock-like jerk by exactly this kind of description-before-naming exercise ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]). [[Movement Disorder Approach|Movement-disorder reasoning]] then runs each named movement through its own cause schema rather than a localization tree.

## Classifying myoclonus: anatomic origin and cause

Structurally, myoclonus can be generated at the **cortical**, **subcortical**, **brainstem**, or **spinal** level, and — outside the hospital's toxic-metabolic majority — the anatomic generator often maps onto a specific disease category. Layered onto that anatomy is the four-column cause schema Berkowitz applies to every movement disorder: primary neurologic, structural/lesional, systemic, and drug/toxin ([[CPS 247 - Tremors, Myoclonus, and Steroid-Responsive Autoimmune Encephalopathy|CPS 247]]; [[CPS 276 - Lethargy, Myoclonus, and Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome|CPS 276]]).

| Category | Representative causes | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary neurologic / epileptic** | Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy and other myoclonic epilepsies, Lafora body disease, dystonia-myoclonus, [[Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease\|CJD]] (startle myoclonus) and other neurodegenerative dementias | Cortical generators predominate; myoclonic epilepsy overlaps genuine [[Myoclonic Seizure\|myoclonic seizures]] |
| **Structural / lesional** | Cortical, subcortical, brainstem, or spinal lesions producing a localized generator | Rare compared to toxic-metabolic causes in the inpatient setting |
| **Systemic / toxic-metabolic** | Renal failure, hepatic failure, hypercarbia, post-hypoxic/post-cardiac-arrest state | **The most common cause in the hospital** — roughly nine of ten inpatient cases ([[CPS 247 - Tremors, Myoclonus, and Steroid-Responsive Autoimmune Encephalopathy\|CPS 247]]) |
| **Drug-induced** | Opioids (paradoxically), amantadine, SSRIs | Worth a medication reconciliation before an extensive work-up |
| **Physiologic** | Hypnic jerks at sleep onset | The everyday, universal, benign version of myoclonus |

Two named syndromes are worth anchoring to this schema: **[[Opsoclonus-Myoclonus Syndrome|opsoclonus-myoclonus]]**, a paraneoplastic pattern (neuroblastoma in children; anti-Ri with breast or thymic cancer in adults) that was appropriately considered and excluded in a colorectal-cancer patient because it doesn't fit that tumor type ([[CPS 276 - Lethargy, Myoclonus, and Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome|CPS 276]]); and **post-hypoxic (Lance-Adams) myoclonus**, the classic systemic-category example following cardiac arrest.

## Myoclonus vs. its mimics

Myoclonus sits at the center of a cluster of paroxysmal or twitchy movements that are easy to conflate, and getting the label right changes the differential entirely:

- **[[Clonus|Clonus]]** is rhythmic, not irregular — a sign of hyperreflexia elicited by sustained stretch (e.g., ankle dorsiflexion), not a spontaneous jerk ([[CPS 247 - Tremors, Myoclonus, and Steroid-Responsive Autoimmune Encephalopathy|CPS 247]]; [[CPS 276 - Lethargy, Myoclonus, and Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome|CPS 276]]).
- **[[Fasciculation|Fasciculation]]** is a single muscle fascicle twitching under the skin *without moving the joint* — the LMN/ALS sign, distinct from myoclonus's joint-crossing jerk ([[CPS 247 - Tremors, Myoclonus, and Steroid-Responsive Autoimmune Encephalopathy|CPS 247]]; [[CPS 276 - Lethargy, Myoclonus, and Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome|CPS 276]]).
- **Myokymia**, a visible muscle "ripple," and a painful **cramp/spasm** are two further mimics worth interrogating explicitly at the bedside — asking a patient exactly what they see and feel (or reviewing video) can separate a true cramp (painful) from myokymia (a ripple, no joint movement) from myoclonus (joint movement) when the story is ambiguous ([[RLR 148 - Nocturnal Leg Cramps That Weren't Painful- A Meningioma Presenting as Focal Seizures with Todd's Paralysis|RLR 148]]).
- **[[Tremor|Tremor]]** is rhythmic and oscillatory where myoclonus is a single arrhythmic jerk — but high-amplitude, rapid myoclonus (**polyminimyoclonus**) can masquerade as tremor, so a movement that looks tremulous at a glance still deserves a second look for irregularity ([[CPS 276 - Lethargy, Myoclonus, and Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome|CPS 276]]).
- **Negative myoclonus (asterixis)** is the tone-loss mirror image of ordinary (positive) myoclonus — a brief lapse rather than a brief contraction — and belongs in the same systemic/toxic-metabolic bucket as classic myoclonus (uremia, hepatic failure, hypercarbia).
- **[[Myoclonic Seizure|Myoclonic seizure]]** is the highest-stakes mimic: bedside "myoclonus" can *be* seizure, and only EEG resolves the ambiguity. In one case, bilateral upper-extremity jerking called myoclonus at presentation turned out on EEG to be rhythmic hemispheric delta activity — myoclonic/subtle seizures, not a movement disorder at all — the exact ambiguity flagged at the start of that same encounter ([[CPS 276 - Lethargy, Myoclonus, and Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome|CPS 276]]). The same principle — that a "jerk" or "spasm" complaint can conceal a focal seizure — is why a purely motor focal seizure can present as stiffening rather than classic convulsive jerking, followed by a [[Todd's Paralysis|Todd's paralysis]] mistaken for ongoing weakness ([[RLR 148 - Nocturnal Leg Cramps That Weren't Painful- A Meningioma Presenting as Focal Seizures with Todd's Paralysis|RLR 148]]).
- **[[Faciobrachial Dystonic Seizures|Faciobrachial dystonic seizures]]**, pathognomonic for [[LGI1 Encephalitis|LGI1 antibody]] disease, are brief unilateral face-and-arm jerks frequently mistaken for myoclonus or tics; unlike the facial-brachial myoclonus of an autoimmune encephalopathy, they are stereotyped, lateralized, and far more frequent — a distinction worth drawing explicitly when a unilateral face-arm jerk is on the table ([[CPS 247 - Tremors, Myoclonus, and Steroid-Responsive Autoimmune Encephalopathy|CPS 247]]).
