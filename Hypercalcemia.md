## Framework and approach

- **Hypercalcemia comes from five buckets: PTH/endocrine, malignancy, ingestion, granulomatous disease, and immobilization.** PTH is the first fork of the workup — a suppressed PTH moves you to the non-PTH arm and excludes both [[Primary Hyperparathyroidism|primary]] and [[Tertiary Hyperparathyroidism|tertiary hyperparathyroidism]]. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])
- **For silent, mild, chronic hypercalcemia in an asymptomatic patient, prioritize the two I's — ingestion and immobilization.** Malignancy and granulomatous disease usually announce themselves with symptoms or a changed baseline; the patient "minding his own business" is more likely to have [[Milk-Alkali Syndrome|milk-alkali]] (check the bicarbonate and the TUMS list) or [[Hypercalcemia of Immobilization|immobilization hypercalcemia]]. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])
- **Newly discovered [[Primary Hyperparathyroidism|primary hyperparathyroidism]] is demographically unlikely in an older man** — the classic patient is a middle-aged woman — so base rates alone should down-weight it before any test comes back. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])
- **Confirm true hypercalcemia with an [[Ionized Calcium|ionized calcium]], especially when the patient cannot report symptoms.** A non-communicative patient can't tell you they feel unwell, which raises the stakes on establishing the abnormality is real rather than an artifact of protein binding. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])

## Reading the calcium against the host

- **Interpret the calcium against the baseline the patient *should* have, not the population range.** An [[End-Stage Renal Disease|ESRD]] patient is expected to be *hypocalcemic*, so a high-normal or high calcium is a large delta that should raise the alarm earlier than the same number in a normal host. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])
- **ESRD patients are hypocalcemic for three reasons:** phosphate retention binding calcium; impaired renal [[1-Alpha-Hydroxylation|1-α-hydroxylation]] of vitamin D reducing gut absorption; and often [[Cinacalcet|cinacalcet]] suppressing PTH. Hypercalcemia despite these mechanisms is notable. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])
- **A high calcium in ESRD adds [[Tertiary Hyperparathyroidism|tertiary hyperparathyroidism]] to the differential** — autonomous parathyroid secretion after long-standing stimulation — though a suppressed PTH excludes it. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])

## Bone markers and immobilization

- **Distinguish bony from hepatic alkaline phosphatase before using it.** With normal liver enzymes an elevated alk phos is likely bony, confirmable with a [[Bone-Specific Alkaline Phosphatase|bone-specific alk phos]]; a bony elevation supports a genuine chronic bone-involving disorder but is *non-specific* — hyperparathyroidism, malignancy, granulomatous disease, and immobilization all raise it (it does argue against isolated milk-alkali). ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])
- **Immobilization alone causes hypercalcemia readily in children and adolescents with healthy, active bone; in adults it is uncommon as the sole cause, so before attributing hypercalcemia entirely to immobility you should investigate for a second contributing cause** (e.g., [[Paget Disease of Bone|Paget disease]] plus immobilization). An elevated bone-specific alk phos can be the clue to such a second cause. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])
- **Immobilization hypercalcemia is under-weighted in chronically bedbound patients** — its pretest probability is high in a contracted, demented, long-immobilized patient with a chronic, indolent course. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])

## Malignancy workup

- **[[Multiple Myeloma|Myeloma]] is purely lytic, so pick imaging that sees lysis.** A [[Bone Scan|bone scan]] measures osteoblastic activity and can miss myeloma entirely — unless a lytic lesion has fractured and is repairing; a [[Skeletal Survey|skeletal survey]] is better but still misses ~10%, and CT is first-tier when concern is high. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])
- **Interpret free light chains by the ratio, and correct the ratio for renal function.** Both chains elevated = inflammation; a markedly *asymmetric* elevation = monoclonal process. ESRD clears kappa more poorly than lambda, so the ratio normally skews toward kappa (up to ~3:1) — meaning even a mild *lambda*-predominant skew in a dialysis patient is worrisome despite being under the usual 4:1 cutoff. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])
- **An elevated [[PTHrP]] points to a solid tumor** ([[Squamous Cell Carcinoma|squamous cell]] anywhere, renal cell, breast) and is a high-morbidity lead worth chasing even without reportable symptoms — but it can be *falsely elevated in renal failure*, so weigh its [[Positive Predictive Value|positive predictive value]] against contradicting data (a negative [[PET-CT]], stable weight, a chronic course). ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])
- **[[Hypercalcemia of Malignancy|Hypercalcemia of malignancy]] is usually an end-stage manifestation** — median survival about six months — so a PET-negative, weight-stable, years-long hypercalcemia fits the malignancy illness script poorly. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])

## Management

- **Calcium is dialyzable — lowering the dialysate calcium bath corrects hypercalcemia regardless of the underlying cause,** which lets you separate the management problem from the still-open diagnostic one. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])
- **Diagnostic closure and the decision to act are separable.** When the etiology stays uncertain but the number is controllable and the prognosis is already poor, the real next step is a goals-of-care conversation with the surrogate rather than more testing. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])
