Why hearing loss is usually a peripheral, not neurologic, complaint — and the short list of central causes when it is.

## How it operates

- **The core anatomical fact.** The auditory pathway becomes bilaterally represented almost immediately after the eighth nerve synapses on the cochlear nuclei at the pontomedullary junction, enabling interaural time-difference sound localization. Practically, this means a unilateral central lesion essentially cannot cause bilateral hearing loss.
- **Most hearing loss is peripheral** (inner ear or eighth nerve), which is why these patients are usually managed by ENT rather than neurology, and why hearing loss is a chief concern neurology rarely sees.
- **Rare central/neurologic causes**, offered as a short list rather than a working differential in most cases: sudden sensorineural hearing loss (thought inflammatory, treated with steroids), HINTS-plus (cochlear infarction via the anterior inferior cerebellar artery), Susac syndrome, vestibular schwannoma (unilateral) versus neurofibromatosis type 2 (bilateral), superficial siderosis, mitochondrial disease, anti-Kelch-like protein 11 paraneoplastic syndrome, and sequential multiple cranial neuropathies (leptomeningeal disease, sarcoidosis, skull base disease).
- **True bilateral central hearing loss from bihemispheric lesions is exceedingly rare** — described as encountered only once in over a decade of practice by the discussant.

## Examples from RLR episodes

- **Anatomy narrows the field before any test is ordered.** A 47-year-old woman with progressive, predominantly unilateral sensorineural hearing loss is worked from this anatomical fact first; because her hearing loss doesn't fit a clean central pattern, the case pivots on other findings (a white matter lesion, later a headache) toward primary CNS vasculitis rather than a central auditory-pathway explanation for the hearing loss itself.
