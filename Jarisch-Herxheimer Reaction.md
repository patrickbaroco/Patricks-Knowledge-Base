## Definition

A transient, anaphylactoid-like reaction (fever, hypotension, tachycardia) that follows antibiotic-induced lysis of spirochete cell walls (syphilis, leptospirosis, Lyme, and others), releasing inflammatory content into circulation. It resolves with supportive care alone, typically within hours, and without epinephrine — the absence of epinephrine-requiring severity, plus rapid spontaneous resolution, is what distinguishes it from a true drug allergy.

## How it operates

- **Suspect it after an antibiotic effective against spirochetes**, especially when the reaction follows shortly after dosing and resolves spontaneously within an hour or so.
- **Distinguish from anaphylaxis by course, not just symptoms.** A true anaphylactic reaction should not resolve this quickly without epinephrine and typically includes rash or wheezing; a bradykinin-mediated reaction would not usually produce this hemodynamic pattern.
- **Pattern recognition for it is built through exposure**, including case discussion and not only direct patient care — an uncommon phenomenon can go from baffling to immediately recognizable once seen once, even secondhand.

## Examples from RLR episodes

- **Recognized in real time because of prior exposure.** Shortly after empiric ceftriaxone for incidental pyuria, a patient develops sudden fever, hypotension, tachycardia, and agitation that resolves spontaneously within about an hour without epinephrine — recognized as a likely Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction (rather than a drug allergy) specifically because the discussant had encountered a similar case on a separate teaching episode, and this reaction becomes the clue that redirects the workup toward secondary and neurosyphilis.
