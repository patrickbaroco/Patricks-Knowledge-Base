Facial swelling as a chief concern, branching by laterality and pace into vascular obstruction, systemic edema, and allergic/autoimmune causes.

## How it operates

- **Laterality is the first branch point.** Bilateral, positional, disproportionate facial swelling favors a vascular process (see [[SVC Syndrome]], [[Anasarca]]); unilateral or localized swelling favors a local cause (dental infection, cellulitis, salivary gland disease) or localized angioedema.
- **Duration argues for or against ordinary angioedema.** Angioedema typically resolves within about three days; swelling persisting well beyond that should prompt reconsideration of the working diagnosis (see [[Angioedema]]).
- **Disproportionality is a signal, not noise.** Facial swelling out of proportion to edema elsewhere in the body should soften a reflexive anasarca framing and raise concern for a localized process — vascular obstruction in one case, autoimmune/serositis-type swelling in another.
- **The chief concern can be the distractor.** In both worked cases below, the visible facial swelling consumed the early workup while the true driving process (device-related SVC stenosis; systemic lupus erythematosus) hid behind it — see [[Diagnostic Humility]].

## Examples from RLR episodes

- **Bilateral, positional, vascular.** Facial swelling worst on waking, sparing the arms, in a patient with a pacemaker lead crossing the SVC — localizes to lead-induced SVC stenosis rather than systemic edema. ([[RLR 074 - Bilateral Facial Swelling and Pacemaker Lead-Induced SVC Syndrome|RLR 074]])
- **Unilateral, persistent, autoimmune.** Facial and lip swelling starting unilaterally after a dental procedure, persisting well past the typical angioedema window — ultimately systemic lupus erythematosus with acquired angioedema-type features, not primary allergic disease. ([[RLR 076 - Lip Swelling, Rash, and Systemic Lupus Erythematosus|RLR 076]])
