Parkinsonism is a clinical **syndrome**, not a diagnosis: bradykinesia (slowness and decrement of repetitive movement) plus at least one of rest tremor, rigidity, or postural instability. Idiopathic **[[Parkinson Disease|Parkinson disease]]** is by far its most common cause, but the two terms are not synonyms — the same syndrome is produced by drug-induced parkinsonism and by the neurodegenerative **[[Parkinson-Plus Syndromes|Parkinson-plus (atypical parkinsonism)]]** family, so the diagnostic task is always to name the syndrome first and only then work out which disease is driving it. In the **[[Movement Disorder Approach|movement-disorder method]]**, parkinsonism sits opposite tremor, **[[Chorea|chorea]]**, and **[[Dystonia|dystonia]]** as the *hypokinetic* branch of the hyperkinetic/hypokinetic split — bradykinesia and rigidity rather than an excess movement — within the broader seizure-versus-movement-disorder framing ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]).

## Cardinal features (TRAP)

- **Tremor** — classically a **4–6 Hz resting tremor**, asymmetric at onset, the "pill-rolling" thumb-finger movement that damps with action and re-emerges with sustained posture (see the [[Tremor]] hub's frequency/amplitude/activation table).
- **Rigidity** — increased tone that is uniform through the range of passive movement ("**[[Rigidity vs Spasticity|cogwheel]]**" when a superimposed tremor ratchets against it), distinct from the velocity-dependent catch of spasticity.
- **Akinesia/bradykinesia** — slowness and progressive decrement of repetitive movement (finger tapping, gait initiation); this is the feature that is *required* for the syndrome, with hypophonia and a shuffling gait as downstream expressions.
- **Postural instability** — impaired righting reflexes and falls; typically a *late* feature of idiopathic Parkinson disease, which makes its *early* appearance a red flag for an atypical cause (below).

## Idiopathic Parkinson disease vs. atypical parkinsonism (Parkinson-plus)

Once the syndrome is named, the differential sorts into idiopathic disease, drug-induced parkinsonism, and the **[[Parkinson-Plus Syndromes|Parkinson-plus]]** syndromes — each with a discriminating clinical signature ([[CPS 211 - Episodic Transient Loss of Consciousness and Multiple System Atrophy|CPS 211]]):

| Cause | Discriminating features | Levodopa response | Protein class |
|---|---|---|---|
| **[[Parkinson Disease\|Idiopathic Parkinson disease]]** | Asymmetric onset; rest tremor prominent; postural instability and falls a *late* feature | Robust and sustained | **[[Synucleinopathy\|α-synuclein]]** |
| **[[Multiple System Atrophy\|MSA]]** | Parkinsonism + cerebellar signs + prominent early **[[Dysautonomia\|dysautonomia]]** (**[[Neurogenic Orthostatic Hypotension\|neurogenic orthostatic hypotension]]**, urinary/bowel dysfunction, stridor); MSA-P (parkinsonian) and MSA-C (cerebellar) subtypes; pontine **[[Hot Cross Bun Sign\|hot cross bun sign]]** on MRI | Poor or only partial/transient | α-synuclein |
| **[[Progressive Supranuclear Palsy\|PSP]]** | Early **backward** falls, vertical gaze palsy, "surprised" facies | Poor | Tau |
| **[[Corticobasal Syndrome\|Corticobasal syndrome (CBD)]]** | Very asymmetric; alien-limb phenomenon; myoclonus | Poor | Tau |
| **[[Dementia with Lewy Bodies\|DLB]]** | Dementia, visual hallucinations, cognitive fluctuations (often *before* or alongside the motor syndrome) | Variable, often modest | α-synuclein |
| **Drug-induced** | Temporally linked to a dopamine-blocking agent (antipsychotics, metoclopramide); often symmetric | Improves with drug withdrawal | — |

Both idiopathic Parkinson disease and MSA are **[[Synucleinopathy|synucleinopathies]]** (α-synuclein), while PSP and corticobasal syndrome are **tauopathies** — a classification Berkowitz uses to organize the whole neurodegenerative family rather than memorizing each syndrome in isolation ([[CPS 211 - Episodic Transient Loss of Consciousness and Multiple System Atrophy|CPS 211]]).

## Red flags against idiopathic Parkinson disease

Any of the following should pull the differential away from idiopathic disease and toward an atypical or secondary cause ([[CPS 211 - Episodic Transient Loss of Consciousness and Multiple System Atrophy|CPS 211]]):

- **Early falls or postural instability** — especially early *backward* falls (PSP), rather than the late falls of idiopathic disease.
- **Poor or only transient levodopa response** — the single best bedside discriminator; a robust, sustained response supports idiopathic Parkinson disease, while a poor or fleeting response supports Parkinson-plus.
- **Early, prominent autonomic failure** — orthostatic hypotension without compensatory tachycardia, urinary/bowel dysfunction, or stridor, as in MSA.
- **Symmetric onset** — idiopathic Parkinson disease is classically asymmetric; a symmetric presentation raises drug-induced parkinsonism or an atypical syndrome.
- **Additional-system signs** — cerebellar ataxia (MSA), a supranuclear vertical gaze palsy (PSP), alien limb or myoclonus (corticobasal syndrome), or early dementia/hallucinations (DLB) — findings that don't belong to a "pure" parkinsonian picture.
- **A young patient, or coexisting liver disease/psychiatric change** — a prompt to consider **[[Wilson Disease|Wilson disease]]** rather than any neurodegenerative parkinsonism (below).

## Secondary and reversible causes

- **Drug-induced parkinsonism** is the most important reversible cause: dopamine-receptor blockers (typical and some atypical antipsychotics) and antiemetics like metoclopramide can produce a full parkinsonian syndrome, often symmetric, that improves after the offending agent is stopped ([[CPS 211 - Episodic Transient Loss of Consciousness and Multiple System Atrophy|CPS 211]]).
- **[[Wilson Disease|Wilson disease]]** belongs on the parkinsonism differential in a young patient (onset typically teens–20s), even though its classic descriptor is a proximal, high-amplitude "wing-beating" tremor — Berkowitz is explicit that the movement disorder of Wilson disease "can be almost anything," including dystonia, parkinsonism, or a tremor of any activation state, so a parkinsonian phenotype does not exclude it. The clue is the **[[Unifying Diagnosis|unifying]]** triad of a new movement disorder, new liver disease, and psychiatric change, confirmed by **[[Kayser-Fleischer Rings|Kayser-Fleischer rings]]**, low **[[Ceruloplasmin|ceruloplasmin]]**, and an elevated 24-hour urine copper ([[CPS 447 - Psychiatric Disturbance, Tremor, and Wilson Disease|CPS 447]]).

## Confirmatory testing

Because there is no bedside test that names the underlying disease directly, two probes carry most of the diagnostic weight ([[CPS 211 - Episodic Transient Loss of Consciousness and Multiple System Atrophy|CPS 211]]):

- **[[Levodopa Response|Levodopa response]]** — a robust, sustained improvement is itself evidence for idiopathic Parkinson disease; a poor or only partial/transient response favors a Parkinson-plus syndrome.
- **[[DaT Scan|DaT scan]]** — demonstrates a nigrostriatal dopaminergic deficit, which separates *degenerative* parkinsonism (idiopathic Parkinson disease or any Parkinson-plus syndrome, both abnormal) from *drug-induced* parkinsonism (normal). It **cannot** distinguish idiopathic Parkinson disease from a Parkinson-plus syndrome — that distinction still rests on the clinical red flags above, plus imaging clues like the hot cross bun sign.

## Examples from episodes

- **The syndrome that reframes a case.** A year of falls and urinary incontinence sits unexplained until a discussant names "Parkinsonism," which pulls the differential away from syncope/seizure and toward a neurodegenerative process; the exam then adds cerebellar signs and severe dysautonomia to the resting tremor and cogwheel rigidity, landing on multiple system atrophy rather than idiopathic disease. ([[CPS 211 - Episodic Transient Loss of Consciousness and Multiple System Atrophy|CPS 211]])
- **Parkinsonism as the hypokinetic half of the movement-disorder taxonomy.** Contrasted directly against hemichorea (hyperkinetic, basal-ganglia, classically unilateral in hyperglycemia), parkinsonism is introduced as the bradykinesia-and-rigidity half of the hypokinetic/hyperkinetic split that organizes any involuntary-movement complaint. ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]])
- **A parkinsonian-quality tremor in a young patient with liver disease.** A unilateral rest-plus-posture-plus-action tremor is worked through the same activation framework used for parkinsonism, and the search for a unifying cause — movement disorder plus new liver disease plus psychiatric change in a 20-year-old — lands on Wilson disease rather than a neurodegenerative parkinsonism, illustrating why age and systemic clues matter as much as the tremor's activation pattern. ([[CPS 447 - Psychiatric Disturbance, Tremor, and Wilson Disease|CPS 447]])
- **The parkinsonian tremor entry in the broader tremor table.** The 4–6 Hz, asymmetric, "pill-rolling" resting tremor of parkinsonism is one row in the full tremor-classification table, cited there as the primary-neurologic-disease example alongside essential and cerebellar tremor. ([[CPS 247 - Tremors, Myoclonus, and Steroid-Responsive Autoimmune Encephalopathy|CPS 247]])
