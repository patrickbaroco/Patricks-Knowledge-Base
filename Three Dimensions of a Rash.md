## Definition

A "tip of the iceberg" approach to an unfamiliar rash: judge it along three dimensions — how wide, how deep, and how quickly it is evolving — to decide whether it is likely the visible surface of a visceral (systemic) process or an isolated primary dermatologic condition. A rash that is narrow, shallow, and slow-moving leans toward primary skin disease; one that is wide, deep (necrosis, purpura, ulceration, pain), or rapidly evolving leans toward a systemic process surfacing through the skin.

## How it operates

- **Width** — how much body surface area is involved; a localized patch reads differently than a generalized eruption.
- **Depth** — whether the lesion is superficial (macular, papular) or destructive (necrotic, purpuric, ulcerated, painful); depth is the strongest single signal of a dangerous underlying process.
- **Rate of evolution** — a rash stable over days reads differently than one changing by the hour.
- **Reassuring on all three axes does not close the case.** Even a narrow, shallow, slow rash can still justify systemic labs if the rest of the clinical picture (fatigue, other new findings) raises concern — the framework triages urgency, it doesn't excuse further workup.

## Examples from RLR episodes

- **A reassuring rash that still hid a systemic disease.** A rash judged not wide, not deep (no necrosis, purpura, ulceration, or pain), and not rapidly evolving is read as favoring a primary dermatologic process — yet facial papules plus fatigue in a young woman are enough to justify pursuing labs anyway, and the rash turns out to be a cutaneous manifestation of systemic lupus erythematosus.
