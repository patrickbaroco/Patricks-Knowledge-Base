**The governance structure moved a long way in three emails to Rucci that evening.** Sent her a one-page explainer of how the two committees would work day to day — Clinical Quality Committee for group-wide standards, Peer Review Committee for individual clinicians, the Chair presiding over PRC without a vote — and raised a question that could eliminate the third body entirely: HCQIA requires that any restriction on a clinician's practice be the "healthcare entity's own action," and that authority is normally delegated to a med-exec committee, but there's no evident reason it can't go straight to peer review instead. Indiana is the one state where privilege explicitly requires a board-organized committee, so the board would still need to act there — pending a lawyer's confirmation — but everywhere else the governance/Council layer may not be needed at all. The review-hearing function would move inside peer review (three uninvolved clinicians), and Patrick's own non-voting role would shift from the governance committee to peer review, still avoiding the conflict with his seat on Quality.

Rucci liked the explainer — "much less complex and easily actionable" — with one edit: drop references to "the board," which isn't actually involved and alarms people, in favor of "the executive team" or "Ben," to be confirmed when the full plan goes to him. Patrick's follow-up worked through who can actually authorize a committee absent the board, using his hospital experience as the point of comparison (there, the clinical quality committee and med exec were board committees; peer review reported to med exec but wasn't one itself) while flagging that Eventus, not being a hospital, may not be bound the same way. He closed the thread by admitting to Rucci that he has real working knowledge of hospital governance but essentially none of private-equity governance — he doesn't even know who sits on Eventus's board — and asked her to explain it to him sometime.
