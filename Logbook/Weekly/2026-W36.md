**What Sandra brought that I didn't have.** Hospice is the first concrete initiative: same two high certifiers every month, disproportionate census, patients on hospice for years — visible in the data, undiagnosable from it. I supplied the mechanism (the hospice lists its own medical director as attending, that director signs the initial cert, and **we never see another piece of hospice paperwork**), and the framing that the fix isn't less hospice, it's less bad hospice, with **length of stay as the metric, not volume.** Division of labor agreed: I take structure and education, she takes data. Two harder things also came out of it. I'm not glad it exists; I'm glad I heard about it. It goes past Jeff Wells before it goes anywhere near the PRC.

**Friday was two hours of being told my own shelved project already exists.** FP&A had built [[Acuity-Based SNF Scheduling]] — the current-state map I never made, made better from the inside. Scheduling runs on rules of thumb that ignore short-stay mix and happen to average out to the clinical model, which is why the gap was invisible: **103% on the old dashboard, 75% on the new one.** My two corrections were both accepted and both go to Dan — the model counts APC days only, and a physician day is not a fungible APC day. The finding worth carrying past the model is that **short stay is where we're failing**, and worst in the under-scheduled buildings; that's the hospital-medicine skill set nobody's comfortable with, which is why I say heart failure over and over. Medical necessity exposure is in assisted living, not SNFs, and the answer to both of Cary's worries is individual-level risk stratification. By Friday night the 9/7 Framework decision had three candidates instead of one.
