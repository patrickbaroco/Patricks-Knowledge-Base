## Definition

Classic tumor lysis syndrome is an acute biochemical tetrad — hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, hyperuricemia — reflecting a high-volume cell-lysis event overwhelming the kidney's excretory capacity, typically triggered by chemotherapy. The same underlying physiology (a cell's toxic intracellular contents released faster than the kidney can clear them) generalizes: uric acid in tumor lysis, CK in rhabdomyolysis, hemoglobin in hemolysis — and each has a chronic or subacute analogue, not just an acute one.

## How it operates

- **The acute tetrad is only the fast version of the physiology.** A chronic, high-turnover process (an accelerating myeloproliferative neoplasm) can produce the same mechanism stretched over months rather than days, giving urate crystals time to enlarge and precipitate as macroscopic stones rather than causing acute tubular obstruction.
- **An incomplete biochemical picture does not exclude the diagnosis.** Normal potassium, calcium, and near-normal phosphate despite a very high uric acid should be read as an incomplete, subacute version of tumor lysis physiology — not as evidence against it.
- **Look for a proxy when direct lysis markers are ambiguous.** Urate-type kidney stones serve as a proxy for elevated urinary uric acid concentration, supporting active renal excretion (and precipitation) of uric acid rather than simple accumulation from reduced clearance.
- **Beware in vitro artifact when cell turnover is extreme.** A very high white count can lyse cells within the collection tube itself, producing spurious (not true) hyperkalemia — confirm with a plasma or point-of-care potassium before treating a surprising result as real.

## Examples from RLR episodes

- **A chronic, incomplete tumor lysis picture.** Marked leukocytosis, splenomegaly, hyperuricemia (14.7), and multiple urate-type stones in a patient ultimately diagnosed with a JAK2-positive myeloproliferative neoplasm are read as slow-motion tumor lysis physiology, despite normal potassium and calcium and only mildly elevated phosphate — an incomplete tetrad explained by the subacute, rather than acute, tempo of cell turnover. A subsequent surprising hyperkalemia (6.2) with an undetectable uric acid turns out to be spurious, confirmed by a normal plasma potassium reflecting in vitro cell lysis from the very high white count.
