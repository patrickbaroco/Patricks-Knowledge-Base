A DAT scan (dopamine transporter SPECT imaging, brand name DaTscan) images the density of presynaptic dopamine transporters in the striatum. It answers one question well: is there nigrostriatal dopaminergic degeneration, or not. That makes it good for separating a degenerative [[Parkinsonism|Parkinsonian]] tremor from a non-degenerative one like [[Essential Tremor|essential tremor]], but it can't tell you which degenerative syndrome you're looking at. See [[Tremor]] for where it fits in the broader workup.

## What it images

Ioflupane I-123 is a radiolabeled cocaine analogue. It binds reversibly to the dopamine transporter on presynaptic nigrostriatal terminals in the striatum, so the scan is a proxy for how many of those terminals are still there, not a measure of dopamine receptors or dopamine itself. A degenerating nigrostriatal pathway, whether it's Parkinson disease or an atypical parkinsonism, loses those terminals and shows reduced striatal uptake. A condition that spares the pathway, like essential tremor, most drug-induced parkinsonism, psychogenic parkinsonism, or dystonic tremor, leaves the scan normal.

## Procedure

Adult dose is 111–185 MBq (3–5 mCi) IV, given over at least 20 seconds. Thyroid blockade matters: potassium iodide, or a strong iodine solution like Lugol's, about 100 mg iodide, at least an hour before injection, keeps the thyroid from taking up free iodine-123. SPECT imaging happens 3 to 6 hours after injection, once the tracer has cleared background tissue and settled into the striatum (FDA prescribing information).

## What it can and can't tell you

**Can:** distinguish a degenerative Parkinsonian process from a non-degenerative mimic. That covers the two most common differentials worked at the bedside: essential tremor (normal scan) versus a Parkinsonian syndrome (abnormal scan), and typical drug-induced parkinsonism (usually a normal scan, since the mechanism is postsynaptic dopamine-receptor blockade rather than loss of presynaptic terminals) versus a degenerative process the drug may have unmasked (abnormal scan).

**Can't:** distinguish between the degenerative Parkinsonian syndromes themselves. Parkinson disease, multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy, and corticobasal degeneration all show the same pattern of reduced striatal uptake, because they all involve loss of the same presynaptic terminals. A DAT scan narrows "degenerative or not." It doesn't answer "which one."

## The evidence

The FDA approval trial enrolled 158 patients with parkinsonism, 27 with essential tremor, and 35 healthy volunteers across 6 centers. Trained image assessors hit 97% sensitivity and 100% specificity for correctly separating parkinsonism from essential tremor; an independent expert-panel read landed at 95% sensitivity and 93% specificity. For comparison, movement disorder specialists working from clinical exam alone in the same population hit 92% sensitivity but only 30% specificity, meaning exam alone was calling a lot of essential tremor "parkinsonism." That gap, more than the sensitivity number, is the real case for ordering the scan when the exam is equivocal (AJMC review).

A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis pooling 20 studies and 3,185 patients found DAT-SPECT changed the working diagnosis in 31% of patients (95% CI 22–42%, 13 studies) and changed management in 54% (95% CI 47–61%, 13 studies). Heterogeneity across the pooled studies was high, so treat those two numbers as directional rather than precise (npj Parkinson's Disease meta-analysis).

## SWEDD: scans without evidence of dopaminergic deficit

About 10% of patients clinically diagnosed with early Parkinson disease in trials turn out to have a normal DAT scan (reported range 5.7–14.7% across studies), a pattern labeled SWEDD. In the ELLDOPA cohort, these patients didn't respond to levodopa, and repeat scans stayed normal out to four years, arguing against evolving or atypical PD and for a different diagnosis entirely: dystonic tremor, essential tremor, psychogenic (functional) parkinsonism, and vascular parkinsonism are the usual alternatives. SWEDD is a good reminder that even a textbook clinical picture for PD has a real false-positive rate, and that catching this group is a big part of the scan's value.

## Confounders: drugs that muddy the read

A specific, clinically relevant trap: several drugs that cause tremor by acting near the dopamine transporter can also throw off the scan meant to work up that tremor. Per the FDA label, amphetamine, methylphenidate, phentermine, mazindol, benztropine, cocaine, selegiline, modafinil/armodafinil, and **bupropion** all bind the dopamine transporter directly and can falsely lower or otherwise alter tracer binding. SSRIs, including sertraline, citalopram, and paroxetine, can increase or decrease binding too. Case reports describe bupropion causing outright diagnostic confusion on DAT imaging, and methylphenidate and phentermine producing false-positive scan features (Mayo Clinic Proceedings).

This closes a loop with the [[Curbsiders 068 - Tremor|Curbsiders 068]] pearls: bupropion, sertraline, and fluoxetine are named there as drug-induced tremor culprits. Those same drugs can make the diagnostic test unreliable, so a real medication history matters twice: once for the differential, and again before ordering or interpreting the scan. Hold interfering medications first when it's safe to do so. When it isn't, read an abnormal scan on one of these drugs with real caution rather than at face value.

## When to order it

An expert panel convened around the FDA approval recommended DAT-SPECT for three situations: the parkinsonism diagnosis is genuinely uncertain, a tremor's etiology is unclear, or nonmotor symptoms are present without a classic motor picture. It isn't built for monitoring disease progression, and it doesn't add anything once the clinical diagnosis is already clear. Same logic as [[Test Interpretation|test interpretation]] generally: order it to move your probability estimate, not to confirm what the exam already told you.
