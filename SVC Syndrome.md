## Definition

Obstruction of venous drainage through the superior vena cava, producing facial, neck, and upper-extremity venous congestion. Classic teaching centers the etiology on malignancy — lung cancer and lymphoma — but modern practice has shifted: indwelling intravascular devices (pacemaker and ICD leads, central venous catheters) are now among the most common causes. The danger is not the visible facial swelling itself but occult airway or cerebral edema, since everything that drains above the SVC — face, airway, and brain — is at risk.

## How it operates

- **Ask about devices explicitly.** A pacemaker or ICD lead traversing the SVC, or a central catheter, substantially raises pretest probability and is easy to overlook if the etiology search defaults straight to malignancy.
- **Supportive exam findings.** Venous dilation above the nipple line, subtle upper-extremity edema, and a positive [[Pemberton Sign]] (facial congestion/flushing with sustained bilateral arm elevation).
- **Evaluate even when the presentation is subacute.** SVC syndrome warrants urgent evaluation regardless of tempo, because the occult risks (airway, cerebral edema) may not yet be clinically apparent.
- **CT versus venography.** CT is highly sensitive for line-associated pathology, whether acute (thrombus) or subacute (stenosis), but conventional venography remains more sensitive for a subtle thrombus that CT can miss and is typically needed before intervention is planned.
- **Management is multidisciplinary.** Cardiology assesses pacemaker/lead function; interventional radiology performs balloon angioplasty of a stenotic segment. Durability of angioplasty for lead-related venous stenosis is not guaranteed — recurrence warrants repeat imaging and possibly stenting.

## Examples from RLR episodes

- **Device-induced stenosis, not thrombus.** A pacemaker lead crossing the SVC causes structural stenosis at its junction with the right atrium; the patient — with the era's most common risk factor already in hand — improves after balloon angioplasty, with the Pemberton sign resolving immediately post-procedure.
