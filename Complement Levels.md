C3 and C4 physiology and their clinical interpretation.

## How it operates

- **Synthesis and pathways.** C3 and C4 are synthesized in the liver as inactive precursors. The classical pathway consumes both C3 and C4 en route to the membrane-attack complex; the alternative pathway does not consume C4 — a C3/C4 discordance can identify which pathway is driving consumption.
- **Three settings where complement is most clinically useful:** glomerulonephritis, urticarial vasculitis, and angioedema.
- **An isolated low C4 with a positive rheumatoid factor** is a recognized association with cryoglobulinemic vasculitis.
- **Roughly half of lupus patients have normal complement levels**, and half have low complement; C4 tends to be consumed disproportionately relative to C3 when the classical pathway predominates.
- **A normal C1 inhibitor level does not exclude C1-esterase dysfunction** — quantitative assays can miss a qualitative or functional defect in the pathway.

## Examples from RLR episodes

- **Discordant complements as the clue, not the noise.** Low C3 and C4 with a normal quantitative C1 inhibitor level, alongside leukopenia and a long-standing anemia, redirect a presumed-angioedema workup toward an acquired, lupus-driven C1-esterase abnormality rather than a genetic one.
