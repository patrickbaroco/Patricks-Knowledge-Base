## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A hostless-guest episode in which Matthew Watto lays out a five-question framework for evaluating any supplement a patient brings in, then applies it one at a time to the contents of a real-world supplement list: greens powders (AG1), multivitamins, fish oil and cod liver oil, magnesium (including glycinate and L-threonate), vitamin D with K2, creatine monohydrate, and curcumin with black pepper extract. Heavy on named trials, exact doses, product-quality verification, and negative pearls — including where a widely repeated claim traces back to a twelve-patient study or a rat experiment.

## Pearls

- Ask about supplements as a routine part of the medication history, in the same breath as prescriptions, to normalize the question. Asking only "do you take any supplements?" under-captures; ask specifically about vitamins, minerals, herbals, and — in anyone who lifts — creatine, protein, and pre-workout, which users frequently do not classify as supplements.
- Patients who take pride in being on no prescription medications are frequently the ones taking the most supplements.
- Supplement use is an investment the patient is making in their own health. Blanket dismissiveness is both unkind and clinically counterproductive; it forecloses the conversation in which the actual risks get surfaced.
- Skepticism of the pharmaceutical industry does not license blind trust in the supplement industry. Both are commercial enterprises; only one of them is subject to meaningful pre-market efficacy review. The supplement industry does not deserve good faith it has not earned.
- "I need to look this up and get back to you" is an acceptable and often correct answer at the point of care, provided the framework for how you will look it up gets shared with the patient.
- The FDA does not evaluate supplements for efficacy before they reach the market. An ingredient that is generally recognized as safe can simply be included, the label can claim any quantity, and there is no routine batch spot-checking. Melatonin products containing no measurable melatonin are the standard illustration.
- Third-party certification is the only practical quality check available: USP (United States Pharmacopeia), NSF Certified, NSF Certified for Sport, and ConsumerLab certification. These verify identity, quantity, and absence of significant impurities. **None of them verify that the product works.**
- Proprietary blends are not worth the effort of vetting. The combination as sold has essentially never been studied, even where individual ingredients have been. Scan the label for anything overtly dangerous and tell the patient plainly that the product is a leap of faith.
- Published analyses of men's health supplements have found label contents not matching actual contents the majority of the time, including undisclosed sildenafil — a concrete illustration of why an unverified label is not evidence of anything.
- Doubling up is the most common avoidable harm on a supplement list: a standalone vitamin A plus a multivitamin, cod liver oil plus vitamin D, a greens powder plus a multivitamin. Watch vitamin A, calcium, vitamin D, and the B vitamins in particular.
- Megadose vitamin B6 can cause a peripheral neuropathy.
- A food-first, lifestyle-first approach covers more bases than any supplement stack, and brings along fiber, water, and nutrients that are not individually itemized. Supplementation in isolation repeatedly fails to reproduce the benefit seen with the whole dietary pattern.
- Do not fall in love with mechanisms. A biologically plausible mechanism plus a supportive observational signal is the setup for a negative randomized trial, not a substitute for one. The combination of an ACE inhibitor with an ARB is the cautionary example — mechanistically a slam dunk, harmful in practice.
- Cost is a real harm, not a soft one. A hundred dollars a month for a product with no demonstrated clinical benefit is a health-relevant expenditure.
- Greens powders such as AG1 are, functionally, an expensive multivitamin-and-probiotic combination at roughly $100/month. The published human data amount to two very small crossover trials.
- Multivitamins do not have evidence for preventing cardiovascular disease or cancer and have not been shown to extend life. They are cheap and low-risk, which makes not fighting a patient who wants one a reasonable position — but not one to recommend proactively.
- Over-the-counter fish oil has not been shown to prevent cardiovascular events in primary prevention. The cardiovascular outcome benefit in the literature belongs to prescription icosapent ethyl in secondary prevention, not to the fish oil aisle.
- Cod liver oil is not fish oil. It carries vitamin A and vitamin D in addition to omega-3s, and is the wrong vehicle for anyone who does not specifically need vitamin A.
- Omega-3 doses above roughly 2 g/day increase the incidence of atrial fibrillation. The absolute increase is small and appears dose-dependent, but it has been reproduced across multiple randomized trials and is not a chance finding.
- The claim that half the US population is magnesium deficient is a misstatement of the actual estimate, which is that roughly half do not meet the estimated average requirement through diet. Not meeting an intake target is not the same as being deficient.
- Serum magnesium cannot assess total body magnesium stores — only about 1% of body magnesium is in the blood. There is no practical way to confirm total-body magnesium depletion in the office.
- Magnesium's best-supported indications are documented hypomagnesemia, chronic idiopathic constipation, and migraine prophylaxis. It does not work for leg cramps, and the evidence for sleep and anxiety does not support the enthusiasm.
- The reputation of magnesium glycinate for superior absorption and tolerability traces back, as best it can be sourced, to a 1994 crossover study of twelve patients in which four did better. The magnesium L-threonate cognition story traces to a 2010 rat study.
- Vitamin D levels in populations living outdoors run naturally in the 40–50 ng/mL range. Guideline thresholds are lower: above 30 ng/mL is adequate for most, below 20 ng/mL is low, and 20–30 ng/mL is a gray zone.
- Do not check a vitamin D level without an indication. The indications that hold up are osteoporosis and disorders of calcium (hypocalcemia or hypercalcemia).
- Vitamin D has been difficult to show preventing fractures in otherwise healthy people, despite decades of trying.
- Empiric vitamin D supplementation without checking a level is now recommended for four groups: adults 75 and older, people with prediabetes, pregnant patients, and children.
- Mega-bolus vitamin D dosing (monthly or annual) has been associated with **increased fall risk** in randomized trials, and a portion of a large bolus is shunted to an inactive metabolite. Daily dosing is preferred.
- Refractory vitamin D deficiency that will not correct with adequate repletion is a reason to check for celiac disease.
- Creatine monohydrate improves performance in short-burst strength and power work — sprinting, heavy lifting, CrossFit-style efforts. It does not help endurance exercise, which runs on a different energy system.
- Creatine is not a quasi-anabolic steroid. Lean mass gain is on the order of 1–3 kg, much of it water. The visible results in people who take it come from the training and the protein intake it accompanies.
- Creatine raises serum creatinine artifactually without impairing kidney function. Check a cystatin C–based eGFR instead of stopping the creatine or launching a renal workup.
- Curcumin has multiple meta-analyses supporting efficacy comparable to NSAIDs for knee osteoarthritis pain, drawn from smaller trials at moderate risk of bias.
- Highly bioavailable curcumin formulations — those combined with piperine (black pepper extract) or labeled liposomal — have caused acute liver injury and frank liver failure. The complication is rare but severe, and is the reason to hesitate before recommending curcumin.
- Turmeric is the plant; curcuminoids are the compound class it contains; curcumin is the specific curcuminoid that has been studied. The terms are used interchangeably in marketing and are not interchangeable.
- Protein supplementation — a shake or bar bridging a skipped meal — is close to the only thing worth actively suggesting, and only in someone building muscle or losing weight who is otherwise missing a meal.

## Clinical Framework

### The five questions to ask about any supplement

Applied in order, with the patient, at the visit:

1. **Why are you taking it?** Establish the target — a symptom, a laboratory value, or a medical condition. A supplement taken because "it's generally good for you" has no target, and without a target there is no way to judge whether it is worth continuing. Many patients cannot answer this question, and surfacing that is itself the intervention.
2. **Is there evidence it works for that specific reason?** Convert the stated target into an answerable question ("does vitamin D prevent fractures?") rather than evaluating the supplement in the abstract.
3. **Is there a plausible biological mechanism?** Understand how it would work, while holding the mechanism loosely (see "don't fall in love with mechanisms," below).
4. **Is there something you can track to tell whether it is working?** A biomarker, a symptom score, a clinical outcome. Without a tracking handle there is no exit criterion and the supplement becomes permanent by default.
5. **What are the risks to this patient?** Drug interactions, interactions with their other conditions, direct toxicity, and cost.

The same framework is given to patients, not just used on them — the goal is that they can run it on the next product they encounter.

### The evidence-appraisal caution underneath the framework

Biological plausibility plus observational support is the classic setup for a negative randomized trial. The body is redundant and complicated enough that a mechanism that ought to work often does not, and observational signals are frequently confounded. The worked example given is the combination of an ACE inhibitor with an ARB: mechanistically it should have been additive renal protection, and in practice it produced harm (hyperkalemia among other problems). The instruction is to require the human trial before acting on the mechanism.

A second, related caution specific to this literature: the supplement evidence base is large enough and heterogeneous enough that a motivated reader can find support for essentially any predetermined conclusion. This is stated explicitly about fish oil and vitamin D.

### How to raise the topic

- Fold the question into the routine medication history so it is normalized rather than singled out.
- Ask about non-prescribed medications explicitly, and then ask again with concrete categories: vitamins, minerals, herbals.
- In anyone who lifts weights, ask about creatine, protein, and pre-workout by name. Patients routinely answer "I don't take anything" and then confirm all three when named.
- Take the list seriously and without judgment. The patient in front of you is trying to be healthy.

### Product-quality tiers

| Tier | What it means |
|---|---|
| Third-party certified (USP, NSF, NSF Certified for Sport, ConsumerLab) | Independently tested: the product contains what the label claims, in the stated amount, without significant impurities. Says nothing about efficacy. |
| Single-ingredient, uncertified | Label may or may not be accurate; no routine FDA batch verification exists. |
| Proprietary blend | The combination has essentially never been studied. Not worth vetting. Scan for overtly dangerous ingredients and describe it to the patient as a leap of faith. |

Regulatory reality underneath the table: efficacy is not established before marketing; an ingredient generally recognized as safe can be included without safety demonstration; and there is no routine post-market spot-checking of batches.

### Reference sources for looking things up

- **NIH Office of Dietary Supplements** — free.
- **Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center** herbs and supplements database — free, and the more clinically oriented of the two for interactions and adverse effects.
- **ConsumerLab.com** — paid, roughly $70/year. Reviews are exhaustively cited (the vitamin D review carries 345 citations) and, uniquely, test and compare actual marketed formulations with cost-per-milligram breakdowns. The site is also the practical way to answer "which specific product should I buy?"
- **Examine.com** — paid, more expensive; evidence-based supplement reviews.

Patients can be pointed at the NIH and Memorial Sloan Kettering sites directly, and at the USP/NSF seals as a purchasing rule.

## Drug and Dosing Detail

### Greens powders (AG1)

- **What it is:** a multivitamin plus probiotic powder sold by subscription at approximately **$100/month**. Placed side by side with a multivitamin, the ingredient lists are very similar.
- **Human evidence:** two trials only — one from 2024 (10 men, 10 women) and one from 2026 (15 men, 15 women), largely crossover designs with a washout period.
- **What they showed:** the product is safe; favorable gut bacteria increased; participants met the estimated average requirement for more of 16 vitamins than on placebo; digestive quality-of-life scores were **not worse** (superiority was not claimed); stool consistency did not change.
- **Bottom line:** the clinical data do not support the price. If a patient is taking a greens powder *and* a multivitamin, that is a straightforward double-up — keep the multivitamin, which is cheaper.
- **Provenance note, non-clinical but relevant to how the product is marketed:** the founder, Chris Ashenden, left New Zealand for the United States following a failed rent-to-buy real estate scheme and outstanding warrants, and stepped down as public-facing CEO in 2024 after New Zealand press coverage, remaining on the board. The product's visibility rests substantially on celebrity and podcast endorsement rather than on trial data.

### Multivitamins

- **Cardiovascular disease and cancer prevention:** insufficient evidence (USPSTF, 2022).
- **Mortality:** no conclusive benefit demonstrated.
- **Cognition — COSMOS-Mind (Baker, 2023):** randomized 2×2 factorial trial of cocoa extract and multivitamin against respective placebos, mean age ~73, with baseline and annual cognitive testing over three years. All groups improved by year two; only the multivitamin arm showed better cognition at year three. Authors' own framing: "by this albeit imprecise yardstick, three years of multivitamin supplementation appeared to have slowed aging by 1.8 years, or 60%," with a call for further work.
- **Conflicting prior data:** a larger, earlier trial in male physicians over 65 found no effect of multivitamins on a global cognition composite — though it tested only at baseline and study end rather than annually.
- **Dose/product:** an inexpensive multivitamin is sufficient; there is no basis for buying an expensive one.
- **Cautions:** overlap with standalone supplements — vitamin A, calcium, vitamin D, B vitamins. Megadose vitamin B6 causes peripheral neuropathy.
- **Practice position:** not recommended proactively; not fought in a patient who is on one and wants to stay on one, particularly an older adult on medications that predispose to deficiency (chronic PPI, metformin).

### Cod liver oil

- Contains **vitamin A and vitamin D in addition to omega-3s**. The vitamin A load is the problem, especially alongside a multivitamin.
- **Recommendation:** stop it. If omega-3s are wanted, take a plain fish oil and dose vitamin D (or vitamin A, if there is an actual indication) separately and deliberately.

### Fish oil / omega-3 fatty acids

**Prescription omega-3 for hypertriglyceridemia**
- **4 g/day** (2 g twice daily) lowers triglycerides and modestly raises HDL. This is guideline-supported for triglyceride lowering.
- That triglyceride-lowering effect has **not** been linked to improved cardiovascular outcomes.

**Icosapent ethyl — REDUCE-IT**
- Secondary prevention; **2 g twice daily (4 g/day)**; reduced major adverse cardiac events. This is the single strongest cardiovascular signal in the omega-3 literature.
- Standing caveat: the mineral-oil comparator may have been pro-inflammatory, which would exaggerate the apparent benefit.
- The AF signal below applies to this dose as well.

**Negative primary-prevention trials**
- **VITAL:** fish oil 1,000 mg/day (with a vitamin D arm), CVD and cancer endpoints, 5.3 years — negative for both supplements.
- **ASCEND:** over 15,000 patients, 1,000 mg/day EPA + DHA, nearly 7.5 years — no reduction in major adverse cardiac events.

**Negative secondary-prevention trial with a harm signal**
- **OMEMI:** 1.8 g/day — no reduction in major adverse cardiac events, and an **increased incidence of atrial fibrillation**.

**The atrial fibrillation signal (negative pearl)**
- Appears **dose-dependent**, at doses above roughly **2 g/day**.
- Absolute magnitude is small: on the order of **4% in placebo versus 5% in the supplemented group**.
- Reproduced across multiple randomized trials, not a single-trial anomaly.
- Proposed mechanism is soft — possibly an effect on cardiac electrical conduction.
- Practical consequence: this is the ceiling that defines the recommended dose, and it matters more as patients age.

**Dietary equivalence**
- Two meals of fatty fish per week yields roughly **250 mg/day** of EPA + DHA.

**Practice recommendation**
- Food first: fatty fish two to three times per week.
- If supplementing: **1,000 mg/day of EPA + DHA**. Above that, weigh the AF risk.
- **Product caveat:** many over-the-counter fish oil capsules contain only 200–300 mg of EPA + DHA, far below studied doses. The label figure to read is EPA + DHA content, not capsule weight. This is the specific case where checking a product database before recommending a brand is worth the trouble.

**Brain health (weaker, non-actionable)**
- Observational data associate omega-3 intake with better cognitive function and lower dementia risk.
- Two meta-analyses (2024 and 2025) found improvement in certain markers of cognitive function.
- A 2023 longitudinal cohort analysis within the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (Wei; rendered "Way" in the transcript) followed just over 1,100 patients for six years with blood biomarkers and omega-3 intake and found reduced Alzheimer's disease risk. A pooled analysis with other longitudinal cohorts totaling over 100,000 patients estimated roughly a **20% reduction in cognitive decline and all-cause dementia**, with a larger apparent effect in ApoE4 carriers.
- All of this is longitudinal-cohort data and vulnerable to healthy-user bias. Filed as interesting and plausible; not a reason to start fish oil.

**Omega-3 index (tracking biomarker, optional)**
- A branded test, first published in 2004 (Harris), measuring EPA + DHA as a percentage of red blood cell membrane fatty acids. Because it reflects red cell turnover, it functions as roughly a **three-month** index.
- **≥8%** is associated with better cardiovascular survival; **<4%** with worse. Usually reported alongside omega-6 content.
- Validated by observational association only. No randomized trial has titrated patients above 8% and demonstrated improved outcomes.
- **LabCorp / Quest "OmegaCheck" is not the same test** — it measures whole blood rather than red cell membranes, so it does not provide the three-month average.
- Where it fits the framework: it is the one available tracking handle for omega-3 status, and can establish that a patient eating fatty fish is already above 8% and does not need a supplement. Heavily used in functional medicine. There is a cost, and most patients decline.

### Magnesium

**What actually has support**
1. **Documented hypomagnesemia** — repletion when the serum level is low. The classic inpatient application is refractory hypokalemia that will not correct until magnesium is repleted.
2. **Chronic idiopathic constipation** — AGA guideline-supported. **Magnesium oxide 500 mg, one to three times daily.** Other forms (magnesium hydroxide / milk of magnesia) are also usable.
3. **Migraine prophylaxis** — listed as probably effective in American Academy of Neurology guidance. **300 mg twice daily** is a common dose.

**Where it does not work (negative pearls)**
- **Leg cramps** — a 2020 Cochrane review found it does not work.
- **Chronic insomnia** — the 2025 VA/DoD guideline states there is insufficient evidence to recommend magnesium.
- **Anxiety** — the mechanism (GABAergic effects, dampening excitatory signaling in the brain) is reasonable, but clinical trial support is not there.

**Who is plausibly depleted**
- Patients on diuretics.
- Patients with alcohol use disorder.
- Beyond that, the office cannot tell: only about 1% of body magnesium is intravascular, so a normal serum magnesium does not exclude total-body depletion and cannot confirm it either.

**Formulation claims, sourced**
- **Magnesium glycinate:** the traceable origin of the better-absorption/better-tolerability claim is a 1994 crossover study of 12 patients (Schuette; "Shoot" in the transcript) in which 4 of 12 showed better absorption and fewer side effects with magnesium diglycinate. Nothing more substantial could be found. The remaining glycinate literature examines bone turnover, glycemic control, and blood pressure. The Sleep Foundation's recommendation of glycinate on tolerability grounds is not supported by a traceable citation.
- **Magnesium L-threonate:** the story begins with a 2010 rat study (Slutsky) showing higher CSF magnesium levels than other formulations and improved memory and cognitive performance in the rats. The human data are thin — a 2026 trial (Lopresti) found a **2.6-point improvement on a 130-point cognitive scale**, an effect size reminiscent of the dementia drugs where a statistically significant result sits on a very large scale.
- **Position:** save the money on both L-threonate and glycinate. If a patient wants to buy into it knowingly, the safety profile permits it.

**Safety**
- Generally safe. Megadoses are given therapeutically in pregnancy and asthma. The dose-limiting effect at supplement doses is diarrhea and GI upset.

### Vitamin D

**Levels and thresholds**
- Populations living outdoors — morning and late-day sun, avoiding peak midday — run naturally at **40–50 ng/mL**. Treated as the physiologic reference point.
- Guidelines: **>30 ng/mL** adequate for most; **<20 ng/mL** low; **20–30 ng/mL** gray zone.
- Unit conversion for non-US readers: **20 ng/mL = 50 nmol/L**.

**When to check a level**
- Do not check without an indication. The indications that hold: **osteoporosis**, and **disorders of calcium** (hypocalcemia or hypercalcemia).
- In osteoporosis, calcium and vitamin D are adjuncts to prescribed osteoporosis therapy, not a substitute for it.

**Empiric supplementation without testing (Endocrine Society, 2024)**
- **Adults 75 and older.** Doses across the supporting studies varied widely and averaged roughly **900 IU/day** — round to **1,000 IU/day**.
- **Prediabetes**, defined for this purpose as at least two of three abnormalities: elevated fasting glucose, elevated A1c, abnormal two-hour glucose tolerance test. Based on a systematic review of 24 studies on glycemia. Dose **1,000–2,000 IU/day**.
- **Pregnancy.**
- **Children.**
- Explicitly **not** recommended empirically for adults younger than 75 outside these groups.

**Repletion and maintenance (AACE, 2020)**
- Repletion: **5,000 IU daily for 8–12 weeks.**
- Maintenance: **1,000–2,000 IU/day.**
- 5,000 IU daily for a limited period is the largest routine dose worth using.

**Against mega-bolus dosing (negative pearl)**
- The older 50,000 IU weekly regimen and monthly/annual bolus regimens are being abandoned.
- Two reasons: a portion of a large bolus is converted to an **inactive metabolite**, and several trials of monthly or annual megadoses found an **increased fall risk**.
- Exception where higher/alternative dosing may still be needed: post-bariatric-surgery patients who do not absorb.

**Refractory deficiency**
- Vitamin D that will not correct despite adequate repletion is a prompt to **check for celiac disease**.

**Fracture prevention**
- Difficult to demonstrate in healthy people. Decades of attempts have not produced a compelling result.

**Vitamin K2 combined with D3**
- Rationale is theoretical: vitamin D has a role in hepatic production of vitamin K–dependent factors, and the pairing is proposed to support bone and vascular health better than D alone.
- There is some evidence of greater bone density gain with the combination than with vitamin D alone.
- It is cheap and safe. Not worth arguing about in either direction. (See the anticoagulation caution under Conflicts, below — the episode does not raise it.)

### Creatine monohydrate

- **Mechanism:** supports cellular energy production via ATP regeneration.
- **Where the evidence is strongest:** short-burst strength and power output — sprinting, heavy lifting, CrossFit-style workouts involving heavy loads moved quickly. Users perform more total work and perform better. This finding has been consistent across a long study history.
- **Where it does not work:** endurance exercise, which draws on a different energy system. Not worth recommending to distance runners or recreational joggers.
- **Body composition, honestly stated:** lean mass gain of **1–3 kg**, much of it water weight. It is not an anabolic agent, despite marketing and reputation. Visible muscle in people who take creatine comes from the training and the protein intake that accompany it. The "puffy" observation attached to creatine going back decades is the water weight.
- **Dosing:** **3–5 g/day** as a standard chronic dose. Doses of **10 g/day or more** are what brain-health advocates propose; that dose is not established.
- **Kidney safety (important negative pearl):** creatine is metabolized to creatinine and therefore **raises serum creatinine artifactually** without impairing renal function. The widespread warning against creatine in kidney disease derives from this artifact, not from demonstrated nephrotoxicity. **Practical move: check a cystatin C–based eGFR**, which is unaffected.

**Brain and cognition (promising, not actionable)**
- **Alzheimer's disease pilot (Smith, 2025):** 20 patients, **20 g/day for 8 weeks**. Primarily a feasibility and tolerability study. Brain creatine rose **11%**, with statistical improvements in cognitive and global/fluid composite scores.
- **General cognition:** five or six studies, most small (largest around 120 patients), with conflicting results across cognitive domains. Meta-analysis suggests **older adults benefit more than younger adults**. Also studied in sleep-deprived states.
- **Mechanism:** adequate supplementation raises brain creatine, giving the brain more available energy for cognitive work or under sleep deprivation.
- **Position:** interesting, plausible, probably something there — but the effective dose and the population most likely to benefit are both unknown. Safety is well established and harm appears low, so this is an area to expect updates.

### Curcumin / turmeric

- **Terminology:** turmeric is the plant; it contains curcuminoids; **curcumin** is the curcuminoid that has been studied for health effects. Marketing uses the words interchangeably.
- **Knee osteoarthritis:** multiple meta-analyses show efficacy **comparable to NSAIDs** for pain. Underlying trials are smaller and at moderate risk of bias; much of the research base comes out of Iran. The initial evidence suggests there is something real here.
- **Dosing in trials:** **500–2,000 mg of curcuminoids daily.**
- **Hepatotoxicity (the reason for caution):** cases of acute liver injury and frank liver failure have been reported, concentrated in **highly bioavailable formulations** — specifically those combined with **piperine (black pepper extract)** and those labeled **liposomal** or "highly bioavailable." At least ten cases are documented, and the signal is carried in the Memorial Sloan Kettering database, the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements site, and ConsumerLab. Risk appears with **longer-term** use rather than short courses. A rare but catastrophic complication is a poor trade for a pain benefit that has alternatives.
- **Counterpoint worth knowing:** some studies in fatty liver disease show curcumin **lowering AST and ALT**, without hard outcomes. The liver signal runs in both directions depending on formulation and context.
- **Practical recommendation:** avoid the highly bioavailable and piperine-combined formulations. Limit a trial to **one month**, then reassess whether the patient believes it is helping; stop it if not.

### Protein

- The only supplement actively suggested with any regularity, and only situationally: a protein bar or shake to bridge a genuinely skipped meal in someone trying to build muscle or manage weight, paired with eating something alongside it rather than replacing food.

## Diagnostic and Exam Detail

- **Cystatin C–based eGFR** — the test to order when a patient on creatine has a rising serum creatinine, before attributing it to kidney injury or stopping the supplement.
- **Serum magnesium** — interpretable only in the direction of a low result. A normal value does not exclude total-body depletion, because roughly 1% of body magnesium is intravascular. Do not order it expecting to confirm or refute "magnesium deficiency" as patients use the term.
- **25-hydroxyvitamin D** — order for osteoporosis or a calcium disorder. Do not order to justify or monitor routine supplementation, and do not order in the four groups now recommended for empiric supplementation, where the level does not change the action.
- **Celiac serology** — indicated in vitamin D deficiency that fails to correct with adequate repletion.
- **Omega-3 index** — red cell membrane EPA + DHA as a percentage of fatty acids; approximately a three-month index; ≥8% associated with better cardiovascular survival, <4% with worse. Observational validation only. Distinguish from LabCorp/Quest OmegaCheck, which measures whole blood and does not give the three-month average.
- **Supplement label reading** — for fish oil, the number that matters is **EPA + DHA content per capsule**, not total capsule weight; for any product, the presence of a USP or NSF seal; for curcumin, whether the formulation includes piperine or is described as liposomal or highly bioavailable.
- **Liver enzymes** — worth attention in a patient on a bioavailable curcumin formulation, particularly on longer-term use.

## Errors and Misconceptions

- **Treating supplement use as trivial or silly.** Dismissiveness ends the conversation before the interaction risks come out, and misreads a patient who is actively trying to be healthy.
- **Asking about supplements once, generically.** "Any supplements?" reliably under-captures. Weightlifters in particular do not classify creatine, protein, or pre-workout as supplements.
- **Distrusting pharmaceutical companies while extending blind good faith to supplement companies.** Both are commercial; only one faces meaningful pre-market efficacy review.
- **Assuming the label reflects the bottle.** No routine batch verification exists. Men's health products have been found not to match their labels the majority of the time, sometimes containing undisclosed sildenafil.
- **Assuming a third-party seal implies efficacy.** USP and NSF certify identity, quantity, and purity — nothing about whether the product works.
- **Trying to vet a proprietary blend.** The combination has not been studied; the exercise cannot produce an answer.
- **Taking a greens powder and a multivitamin together.** A pure double-up; the greens powder is the expensive redundancy.
- **Treating cod liver oil as interchangeable with fish oil.** It carries vitamin A and vitamin D, creating an overlap problem with any multivitamin or vitamin D supplement.
- **Extending REDUCE-IT's result to the fish oil aisle.** The cardiovascular benefit belongs to prescription icosapent ethyl in secondary prevention, at 4 g/day. Over-the-counter fish oil in primary prevention is negative across VITAL and ASCEND.
- **Assuming more fish oil is safer than less.** Doses above ~2 g/day raise atrial fibrillation incidence, reproducibly.
- **Repeating that half of Americans are magnesium deficient.** The estimate is about dietary intake falling below the estimated average requirement, not about deficiency, and the leap between the two drives a great deal of unnecessary supplementation.
- **Believing a normal serum magnesium rules out depletion — or that a magnesium level can rule it in.** Only ~1% is in the blood.
- **Recommending magnesium for leg cramps or insomnia.** Cochrane 2020 is negative for cramps; the 2025 VA/DoD guideline finds insufficient evidence for chronic insomnia.
- **Paying a premium for magnesium glycinate or L-threonate.** The claims trace to a 12-patient study and a rat study respectively.
- **Checking a vitamin D level without an indication**, and **rechecking it to monitor routine supplementation.**
- **Using mega-bolus vitamin D (monthly, annual, 50,000 IU weekly by default).** Some is diverted to an inactive metabolite, and several trials show increased falls.
- **Stopping creatine, or diagnosing kidney disease, because the creatinine rose.** The rise is an assay artifact of creatine metabolism. Order a cystatin C.
- **Expecting creatine to build substantial muscle on its own, or to help endurance performance.** Neither is true.
- **Assuming that turmeric with black pepper is the superior, "better-absorbed" choice.** That is precisely the formulation class implicated in the liver failure cases.
- **Falling in love with a mechanism.** Plausible biology plus an observational signal is where negative randomized trials come from.
- **Reaching a conclusion by literature-shopping.** In the fish oil and vitamin D literatures specifically, a reader with a prior can find support for it.

## Case Examples

**The composite case — a 52-year-old man's supplement list, worked through the framework.**

*Presentation:* 52-year-old man at an annual visit. History of hypertension, dyslipidemia, mild knee osteoarthritis, intermittent constipation, and difficulty sleeping. Recently started lifting weights three times per week. Medications: losartan, rosuvastatin. Triglycerides 190 mg/dL. Kidney and liver function normal. Asked about supplements, he produces a phone list.

| Supplement | His stated reason | Disposition |
|---|---|---|
| Once-daily multivitamin | General health | Continue if he wants it. Not recommended proactively; cheap and low-risk. Watch for overlap with the vitamin D and any vitamin A source. |
| Over-the-counter fish oil | Heart health | No primary-prevention benefit. If continued, target 1,000 mg/day of EPA + DHA and read the label for actual EPA + DHA content. Food first: fatty fish two to three times weekly. |
| Cod liver oil (intermittent) | Heart health / general | **Stop.** Vitamin A and vitamin D overlap with the multivitamin and his D3. |
| Magnesium glycinate | Sleep and constipation | Constipation is a legitimate target — but the guideline-supported agent is magnesium oxide 500 mg one to three times daily. Sleep is not supported; the glycinate premium is not supported. |
| Vitamin D3 with K2 | Bones, immunity, mood, testosterone, cardiovascular health | Age 52 with no osteoporosis and no calcium disorder places him outside every empiric-supplementation group and outside the indications for checking a level. K2 is cheap, safe, theoretical. Confirm the dose is not a mega-bolus. |
| Creatine monohydrate | Strength training | Reasonable and evidence-supported for the training he is actually doing. 3–5 g/day. Anticipate and pre-empt a rising creatinine — check cystatin C rather than reacting. |
| Curcumin with black pepper extract | Knee osteoarthritis pain | The one item with a real safety concern. The piperine combination is the formulation class implicated in liver injury. Switch off the bioavailable formulation, limit to a one-month trial, and reassess. |

*The teaching point of the list as a whole:* seven products, one clear stop (cod liver oil), one safety switch (curcumin formulation), two dose or formulation corrections (magnesium form for the actual indication, fish oil EPA + DHA content), one item that is well-matched to the patient (creatine), and two that are permissible but unsupported (multivitamin, vitamin D). No item on the list required a confrontation to address.

## Conflicts with Established Positions

**No direct conflicts identified.** Nothing in this episode contradicts the committed positions carried elsewhere (anticoagulation, NSAIDs, sulfonylureas, clonidine/hydralazine, hospice threshold, thickened liquids, fall rate, EOL symptom management).

**Reinforcements worth carrying forward:**

- **Fall rate.** The finding that monthly and annual mega-bolus vitamin D dosing *increases* falls is a direct, trial-based argument against a dosing convenience that is attractive in institutional settings precisely because it reduces administration burden. This strengthens rather than complicates any position on fall reduction, and it is a rare instance where the convenient option is also the harmful one.
- **NSAIDs.** Curcumin performing comparably to NSAIDs for knee osteoarthritis is a genuine NSAID-sparing option in a population where NSAIDs are hard to justify. The hepatotoxicity signal in bioavailable formulations means it is not a free swap — but a plain, non-piperine curcumin on a time-limited trial is a defensible step before an NSAID.
- **Deprescribing.** The five-question framework is structurally the same instrument used for prescription deprescribing (what is the target, does it work for that target, can you track it, what does it cost the patient), applied to a category usually left off the medication list entirely.

**Gaps the episode leaves that matter for the LTC population** — flagged here because they are foreseeable consequences of the episode's own recommendations, not because the episode gets anything wrong:

- **Vitamin K2 and warfarin.** Vitamin D3 is now commonly co-formulated with K2, and the episode's position is that K2 is cheap, safe, and not worth arguing about. In a warfarin patient, supplemental vitamin K is an interaction that changes INR control. Any adoption of the "don't get worked up about K2" position should carry an explicit warfarin exception.
- **Magnesium oxide and renal function.** Magnesium oxide 500 mg one to three times daily is a genuinely useful and guideline-supported constipation option, and constipation is near-universal in LTC. The episode discusses magnesium safety in terms of diarrhea and GI upset, in a population with normal kidneys. Magnesium accumulates in renal impairment, and this dose in an LTC resident with reduced eGFR needs a renal caution the episode does not supply.
- **High-dose omega-3 and anticoagulation.** The episode's ceiling on omega-3 dosing is set by atrial fibrillation risk. In an anticoagulated patient the more immediate consideration is bleeding, which is not addressed.
- **The creatinine artifact in an institutional setting.** The cystatin C pearl is framed for a healthy lifter. Operationally, the higher-stakes version is an LTC resident on creatine whose routine chemistry shows a rising creatinine, triggering a hold on renally-cleared medications, a hydration protocol, or a transfer — for an assay artifact.

- **Handbook — new chapter candidate:** *Supplements and Over-the-Counter Products.* The handbook currently has polypharmacy/deprescribing drafted and an open question about what comes next; this is a natural adjacent chapter and shares its analytic machinery.   - *"The supplement that makes your creatinine look worse"* — creatine and cystatin C. Short, counterintuitive, immediately actionable, and structurally identical to the existing issues that correct a reflexive clinical response.
