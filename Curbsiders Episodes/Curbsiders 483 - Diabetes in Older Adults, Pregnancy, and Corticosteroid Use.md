## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Endocrinologist Jeff Colburn works three cases — a 78-year-old veteran with mild cognitive impairment and an A1c of 6.3% after a hypoglycemic fall, a 32-year-old at 26 weeks with gestational diabetes, and a 53-year-old with lupus and CKD started on prednisone 20 mg — and builds each into a general approach. The highest-value material is dense and numeric: relaxed glucose and A1c targets for older adults with the reasoning behind them, the ADA's 4Ms framework, regimen simplification and over-basalization, a rehabilitation of meglitinides and pioglitazone as lower-hypoglycemia-risk alternatives to sulfonylureas, the complete reversal of metformin and sulfonylureas in pregnancy in favor of insulin, and the Clore NPH dosing table for corticosteroid-induced hyperglycemia with the pharmacokinetic rationale for why NPH specifically matches a prednisone profile.

## Pearls

- **Every diabetes decision starts with the type.** Type 1 and type 2 are the common forms, and the agent choice follows from the type before it follows from anything else.
- Type 2 diabetes is usually taught as insulin resistance, but **beta cell function is lost across time** — far enough into the disease course, insulin production fails and insulin becomes necessary. The longer a person has had diabetes, the more likely they need it.
- After type, the next question is goals: **the ABCs — A1c, blood pressure, cholesterol.**
- **All guideline-writing agencies now favor relaxing the A1c in older adults**, for two stated reasons: reducing the burden of polypharmacy, and reducing hypoglycemia risk.
- **It is not the A1c achieved, it is how it was achieved.** An A1c under 7% on metformin with or without an SGLT2 inhibitor is fine — both are low hypoglycemia risk. The same A1c achieved with a sulfonylurea, a meglitinide, or insulin is a different and more worrying finding.
- Older adults detect hypoglycemia poorly: cognitive dysfunction blunts awareness of body signals, they may be less engaged with glucose testing, and their ability to *act* on a low is reduced. Then the fall and fracture risk compounds it.
- **Patients and families have been told for years that high blood sugar damages the body. Almost none have been told that low blood sugar does.** The two harms to name in that conversation are (1) recurrent hypoglycemia meaningfully worsens cognition, and (2) falls. Cognition is the one families are already afraid of losing — once it goes, independence in self-care disintegrates — which makes it the effective argument for deintensifying.
- Ask two specific questions of every older adult on a hypoglycemia-capable regimen: **where are your test supplies, and what floor of the house do you live on?** A patient who answers that they walk downstairs to the fridge when low in the middle of the night has a plan that is itself a fall.
- **Glucose tablets rather than candy, because nobody snacks on glucose tablets** — pure glucose does not taste good. Sweet-tasting foods are sucrose and the other sugars.
- **It takes four white sugar packets to get the 15 g of glucose needed to raise a blood sugar.** Surprising to most people. Only the white packets — the other colors are non-nutritive sweeteners and will do nothing.
- Rule of 15: **15 g every 15 minutes, rechecking every 15 minutes until it rises.** If it does not rise, the patient goes to the emergency department.
- **Empagliflozin should be taken toward the morning** so the increased urination does not happen overnight — a small change that matters disproportionately in an older adult with nocturia and fall risk.
- **Older patients are often deferential to the physician** and will not volunteer, admit, or necessarily remember that they are missing insulin doses. A large insulin dose on a thin older person should prompt the question of whether the whole dose is actually being injected, not a further increase.
- **Do not discount an older person's technology ability.** The guest has patients aged 90 and 95 working their phones fine. CGM setup does not require complex computer skills, clinic staff can do the download and pairing, and the family usually helps from there.
- Medicare and Medicaid now **cover CGM for anyone on insulin** — it no longer has to be a multiple-daily-injection regimen, and patients no longer have to prove they are doing finger sticks.
- The CGM-to-phone route has two advantages over a receiver: data uploads to the clinic, and follow-on apps let family "spy" on the patient and receive alerts.
- CGM makes it easy to see **which time of day actually needs mealtime insulin**, which is what makes single-mealtime-dose regimens workable.
- **A1c reads artifactually low in pregnancy** — increased red cell turnover and higher intravascular volume. That is why the pregnancy target is under 6%: "just because you got pregnant, you're not allowed to cheat."
- **Metformin and sulfonylureas were once the sweetheart agents in pregnancy, thought safer than insulin. That position has completely reversed.** Both cross the placenta, and while pregnancy-level complications are not the issue, exposed children show higher rates of metabolic dysfunction in later childhood and adult life.
- **After gestational diabetes there is a 50% chance of type 2 diabetes within 5 years.** Gestation does not cause the metabolic dysfunction; placental, fetal, and maternal growth hormones and growth factors create insulin resistance that *unmasks* dysfunction already present.
- **Corticosteroids raise glucose all day, but the dramatic effect is postprandial** — via glucose disposal and the steroid effect on insulin-sensitive GLUT receptors in muscle, liver, and fat.
- **Insulin is renally cleared.** In declining renal function it lasts longer than expected and stacks, producing hypoglycemia — the counterweight to piling insulin onto a hyperglycemic patient with an AKI.
- **NPH still has a job.** Its pharmacokinetics happen to match short- and intermediate-acting steroids, and most clinicians trained in the last two decades have never used it.
- SGLT2 inhibitors get more effective the further glucose is **above the renal threshold of 180 mg/dL**.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists do not start working for several weeks** because of the required slow titration — which is why they are not the answer to an acute steroid-driven glucose surge, however well-suited they are to the postprandial physiology.
- "You gotta have the data to treat." CGM is the guest's recurring answer across all three populations.

## Clinical Framework

### The ADA 4Ms framework for older adults

New to the ADA Standards of Care section on older adults, per the guest, and a direct descendant of the Geriatric 5Ms.

- **Mentation** — self-care capacity. Can they detect hypoglycemia? Can they respond to it?
- **Medications** — polypharmacy burden, whether the agents in use are actually effective, and the end-organ disease and complications that constrain choice (renal failure especially).
- **Mobility** — foot complications, fall risk, frailty and sarcopenia. This is where the GLP-1 caution lives: an older, frail, thin patient cannot afford to lose weight, and some GLP-1-associated weight loss is muscle.
- **What Matters Most** — shared decision making, and with cognitive impairment, the family's expectations of lifespan and symptoms. The guest's example: if he can have a little taste of ice cream, let him.

Watto's addition from the Penn geriatrics series: the fifth M, **Multicomplexity** — the other comorbidities in play. Otherwise the two frameworks are the same, and the guest's view is that it applies well beyond geriatrics.

### Glucose and A1c targets by population

| Population | Fasting / preprandial | 2-hour postprandial | A1c |
|---|---|---|---|
| Younger adults, not pregnant | 80–130 mg/dL | <160 mg/dL | <7% |
| Older adults (~70+) | 100–150 mg/dL | up to 180 mg/dL | 7–8% |
| Very limited prognosis / hospice | ~150–250 mg/dL, keep <250 | — | target drops away |
| Gestational diabetes | 70–95 mg/dL | 100–120 mg/dL | <6% |

Conversion anchors the guest uses at the bedside: **an average glucose of 150 is roughly an A1c of 7; an average glucose of 180 is roughly an A1c of 8.**

Deintensification triggers that follow from the table: in an older adult, **any fasting value under 100, or a postprandial under 150, means the regimen is tighter than it needs to be** and something should come down.

*(Numbers as stated. When comparing to pregnancy targets later in the episode the guest restated the non-pregnant young adult fasting range as 90–130 rather than the 80–130 he gave earlier; the discrepancy is his, not a transcription artifact.)*

**Age threshold:** the guest declines to name a magic number for "old," but notes the guidelines start asking for thoughtfulness about the regimen at **70 years or older**.

**ADA tables referenced:** Table 13.2 (older adults — targets stratified by health status, with the A1c target eventually dropping away entirely in favor of "avoid hypoglycemia and symptomatic hyperglycemia") and Table 15.2 (pregnancy).

**Hospice and very limited prognosis:** the guest has telehealth-managed a hospice patient off insulin entirely. But with a couple of months of lifespan, letting glucose run very high produces significant glycosuria — wetting, sitting in a wet diaper, a changing burden on family. The goal is not life extension; it is preventing glycosuria as a symptom.

### CGM time-in-range does not transfer to pregnancy

Standard time-in-range is 70–180 mg/dL. The pregnancy sensor goal range is **63–140 mg/dL**. The guest's explicit instruction: **use the actual glucose range, not the time-in-range percentage**, because the upper bound of standard TIR is far too high for pregnancy — "you don't want them over 140 at all."

### Three diabetes categories in pregnancy

A person who becomes pregnant may have (1) pre-existing type 1 diabetes, (2) pre-existing type 2 diabetes, or (3) no prior diabetes with dysglycemia developing during pregnancy — gestational diabetes. All three occur, and the management differs.

### Matching insulin pharmacokinetics to steroid pharmacokinetics

The organizing idea behind the NPH strategy: pick the insulin whose time-action profile matches the steroid's.

- **Short- and intermediate-acting steroids** — hydrocortisone, prednisone, prednisolone, methylprednisolone — produce a glucose rise whose duration closely resembles the NPH profile. NPH is the match.
- **Dexamethasone** has a very long-acting, flat profile. Here **glargine, or dialing up existing glargine, is the better match** than NPH.

**Steroid potency, all in prednisone equivalents** (as stated by the guest, who corrected himself once mid-sentence): hydrocortisone **20 to 5**; methylprednisolone approximately **equal** to prednisone; dexamethasone approximately **1 to 5** — much stronger.

## Drug and Dosing Detail

### Sulfonylureas and meglitinides — the mechanism that makes them dangerous

The beta cell senses glucose by metabolizing it to ATP; ATP causes the ATP-sensitive potassium channel to eject potassium; the resulting charge change admits calcium; insulin is released. **Sulfonylureas bypass the glucose-sensing step entirely and simply open the potassium channel** — so the beta cell releases all the insulin it can regardless of how low the glucose already is. This is the whole reason they are the wrong drug in someone who cannot detect or treat a low.

- **Glimepiride** — the index drug in the first case, and the one to come off.
- **No sulfonylurea stands out as better for an older person.** Asked directly for a favorite, the guest has none and redirects to meglitinides instead.
- **Meglitinides — nateglinide, repaglinide.** The "weaker cousins" of sulfonylureas: same ATP-sensitive potassium channel, same override of glucose sensing, so hypoglycemia remains possible — but **shorter duration of action means less hypoglycemia risk and less weight gain.** The guest's framing: this is the sulfonylurea for an older person. He does not use them often, but **the older patient is specifically who he looks for them in.** Potentially more expensive; in his experience gettable, especially if the prior authorization explains that a sulfonylurea is a poor choice in this older patient. Neither host was taught to use them in internal medicine training; the guest first encountered them in fellowship.

### DPP-4 inhibitors — "GLP-1 light"

- **Sitagliptin** as the example. **A1c drop is small, 0.4–0.5%**, with a very minimal side effect profile.
- Mechanism: DPP-4 is the enzyme that degrades the body's own GLP-1, secreted by intestinal cells after eating. Blocking it raises endogenous GLP-1 — so the labeled side effect profile resembles a GLP-1 agonist's, but much milder, since levels never reach injected-GLP-1 concentrations.
- **Renally dosed**, but the guest is emphatic that this is not a toxicity concern: most experts say be careful with *initiation*, then the dose can be brought up as tolerated. **Above the GFR cut point, the patient is completely safe.** A patient sitting right at the cut point is not a reason for alarm.
- Practical use: expensive, so a narrow use case. Worth taking when a metformin combination lands on tier 1 of a formulary — "kind of a freebie, low risk, and it might help a little bit enough to stop using a higher risk agent." Williams's framing: almost a psychological medication, for the patient fixated on getting the A1c slightly better when nobody has a real stake in the number.

### Pioglitazone — worth reconsidering in this exact patient

A class "dragged through the mud." Pioglitazone is the only TZD left on the market; rosiglitazone and troglitazone are gone. The historical concern — increased risk of heart disease and cardiac events, more than 10–15 years ago — is **the reason the FDA now requires cardiovascular outcomes trials of every new diabetes agent.** Pioglitazone itself was **absolved of causing heart disease, and if anything may reduce it.**

- **Dosing: 15, 30, or 45 mg once daily.** A1c lowering **up to 1%**.
- **Low hypoglycemia risk** — the reason it enters this conversation at all.
- **Contraindicated in heart failure**: it causes fluid retention, so patients with heart disease and CHF should not receive it.
- Increased abdominal circumference — which the guest notes may not be a problem in an older frail patient.
- **Bone loss and osteoporosis** concern, which matters in this population. Less so in males: with a normal bone mineral density in a man, he is not especially worried.
- **IRIS trial: reduced risk of stroke.**
- Some good evidence it **may reduce or delay the onset of cognitive loss.**
- Mobilizes visceral fat out of the liver into the subcutaneous depot — **potential liver benefit in MASH.**
- Bottom line as used by the guest: he uses it **quite a bit in patients who are not right for intensive insulin and not safe for sulfonylureas.**

### GLP-1 receptor agonists

- Postprandial glucose is where they work, which is exactly where corticosteroids do their damage — a good mechanistic fit for a long steroid course.
- **Not for the acute steroid surge**: they take several weeks to work because of the required slow titration.
- **Caution in the frail, thin, sarcopenic older adult** — some of the weight lost is muscle.
- **Stopped in pregnancy** — see below.
- **Cost** is the recurring practical barrier; the guest calls them the best and safest option for postpartum weight loss and immediately notes they are often ungettable.

### SGLT2 inhibitors

- **Empagliflozin dosed toward the morning** so urination does not occur overnight.
- Good adjunct in steroid-induced hyperglycemia with CKD: helps prevent fluid onloading (relevant when steroids are causing fluid weight gain and the patient is already on furosemide) and manages glucose.
- **Do not start around the timing of an acute kidney injury** — let things heal and settle. Chronic kidney disease is a different matter and a good indication.
- Efficacy scales with how far glucose sits above the **renal threshold of 180 mg/dL.**
- **Stopped immediately at discovery of pregnancy.**

### Insulin — simplification in the older adult

The second version of the first case: glargine 50 units plus aspart 15 units before meals, taken once or twice a day depending on what he remembers, always 15 units because that is the number that stuck.

- **Scrutinize large doses in an older, thinner person before escalating.** Both of those are solid doses; the more likely explanation for an unexpected pattern is that not all of it is being injected.
- **Recalculate from weight rather than from the stated dose**: go back to **0.2–0.3 units/kg** and rebuild.
- **Simplify:** focus on the once-daily insulin, achieve a reasonable glucose on that, and hold the mealtime insulin. If mealtime coverage is still needed, **reduce it to the single biggest meal of the day** — often a big dinner or a big breakfast. Watto reports this works very well in practice and that CGM makes identifying the right meal easy.
- **Over-basalization:** glargine 50 units with an A1c of 6.3% is too much basal for the target. Reduce it.
- **Correction scales are frequently beyond the patient's capacity.** Before prescribing one, do a **teach-back**: "your glucose is 300 going into dinner — based on what we wrote out, what are you going to do?" If the arithmetic that comes back is not appropriate and reasonable, do not prescribe the scale. Getting it wrong hurts the patient.

### Insulin in pregnancy

- **Insulin is the preferred agent** across all three pregnancy categories.
- **All insulins are now considered reasonable.** Which insulin to use was debated for years; there are no controlled trials establishing superiority or safety of one over another. The guest's reasoning: glargine and aspart are both insulin and must both fit the same receptor — the difference is the substances added to slow or speed absorption, and those are not generally things that cross the placenta or affect the fetus.
- **Initiation itself is no different from any other patient. The follow-up is.** What changes is how fast the titration happens — a nurse or NP doing roughly **every-three-day phone check-ins** is the ideal structure; absent that resource, this is one of the situations worth spending a couple of your own minutes on a call for. "Where are we at with the sugar today? Okay, great, let's do this change."
- **The one time to start rapid-acting insulin before basal.** Pregnancy — type 2 and especially gestational — tends to produce big postprandial spikes out of proportion to the fasting elevation, unlike the typical adult with obesity and diabetes. So look at the profile first: a professional CGM, a personal CGM, or at minimum **three days of fasting plus pre- and post-breakfast, pre- and post-lunch, pre- and post-dinner** values. If the meals are what is blowing up, tackle those first.
- Basal starting dose when basal is the answer: **0.2 units/kg**, which usually lands around **15–20 units of glargine once daily.**
- **Type 1 in pregnancy carries increased DKA risk**: pregnancy itself adds insulin resistance, and first-trimester nausea and vomiting cause dehydration, which can prompt DKA.

### What stops in pregnancy, and what starts

- **GLP-1 receptor agonists: do not use in the pre-pregnancy period; discontinue at the discovery of pregnancy.** Not well studied in pregnancy, and they induce substantial weight loss. Retinopathy concerns attach as well, alongside the emerging signal of optic neuropathy reported with GLP-1 use (rendered in the transcript as "non-ischemic optic neuropathy"; the entity under FDA and advisory-body review is non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy). The pathophysiology is not understood; the guest's position is that the signal requires further literature investigation, and that it is a reason for extra watchfulness rather than a settled finding.
- **SGLT2 inhibitors:** same — discontinued immediately at the onset of pregnancy discovery.
- **Metformin and sulfonylureas: no longer first line.** Both cross the placenta. The harm signal is not in-pregnancy complications but **later metabolic dysfunction in the exposed child**, in later childhood and adult life. Watto adds the small-for-gestational-age signal. These are acceptable *before* pregnancy and discontinued during it.
- **Topiramate is absolutely teratogenic** — a "big, big, big no-go" as a weight-loss agent in any patient who could become pregnant.
- **Folic acid 400–800 mcg per day** to prevent neural tube defects — the guest's closing addition to the pregnancy list.
- **Gestational diabetes is first treated with lifestyle:** nutrition counseling and regular moderate exercise. Many cases are controlled this way. If that is not sufficient, insulin.

### Preconception and interconception care by the internist

- Dysglycemia **in the lead-up to a pregnancy** affects the pregnancy, and the patient may not know she is pregnant yet.
- So: **young women with fertility potential need consistent counseling** to either use effective contraception or have their diabetes well controlled.
- The internist is frequently the one initiating therapy and deciding what to stop and start **before the patient can get in with OB or MFM** — a wait that can be long. Management will likely transition to a specialist, but the start belongs to the generalist.
- **Dilated eye exams: yearly** in any young woman with diabetes and fertility potential, **in the first trimester** when pregnant, and **every trimester** thereafter if there is any concern. Pregnancy is a period when retinopathy can take off and macular edema can develop, causing blindness — acute, high-risk vision change.

### Postpartum

- **Continue insulin until breastfeeding concludes and avoid other agents** during breastfeeding — most medications are a concern.
- Once breastfeeding ends, the priority is **weight loss, by medication and/or lifestyle**, against that 50% five-year type 2 risk.
- **GLP-1 agonists are the best and safest option for that weight loss** — limited only by cost.
- **Topiramate is excluded** for the teratogenicity reason above.

### Corticosteroid-induced hyperglycemia — the insulin strategies

**First move: adjust the insulin the patient already has.** Give the patient insulin they are familiar with if you can.

- **Titration increments: 10% or 20%**, chosen by how much hyperglycemia there is — 20% when there is a lot. For the case patient on aspart 15 before meals, a 20% increase is **3 to 5 units more per meal up front**; because the starting A1c was 9.2%, the guest would go higher still, **5 or 6 units on top of each mealtime dose** to begin with, then assess and go up further.
- **These patients may need far more than that.** Titrate in quick succession, day by day, or they will simply stay hyperglycemic.
- **The renal counterweight:** insulin is renally cleared, and with an AKI it lasts longer than expected and stacks.

**Converting basal to NPH.** Very high basal insulin does not help much against meal-driven excursions, which is where steroids hit.

- **NPH profile: onset 2–4 hours, peak 4–10 hours, duration up to 18 hours.** Unlike flat glargine, the peak "goes up and grabs and pulls down" the spiking sugars.
- **Conversion: stop the glargine, convert to NPH, give two-thirds in the morning with the steroid and one-third at dinner** — the evening third covers dinner and serves as the rest of the overnight basal. Worked example from the episode: 90 units of glargine becomes **60 units in the morning, 30 units at dinner.** Unit-for-unit conversion, or add a few units.
- **Keep the mealtime aspart** and use it to cover meals.
- **NPH must be dosed twice daily — and the patient must eat three times a day**, because the midday peak can cause a low if lunch is skipped. This is the compliance cost of the strategy and needs saying out loud to the patient.
- NPH is **cheaper and more accessible** than modern analogs, which matters for patients whose settings cannot get the newer insulins.

**The Clore table — NPH dosing for the insulin-naive patient starting steroids.** From a paper by **John Clore, MD** (VCU); the ratio is NPH **units/kg matched to prednisone-equivalent dose**, given at the time of the steroid:

| Prednisone (or equivalent) | NPH dose |
|---|---|
| 10 mg | 0.1 units/kg |
| 20 mg | 0.2 units/kg |
| 30 mg | 0.3 units/kg |
| 40 mg or higher | 0.4 units/kg (cap) |

**Adding NPH on top rather than converting.** For a patient already on a steady glargine-plus-mealtime regimen who starts a morning steroid, simply **add NPH at the same time as the steroid dose** and leave the home regimen alone — Watto's analogy of continuing home pain medication and adding more on top for breakthrough pain. The guest does this, but **mostly in the hospital**: sending someone home on three different insulins gets tricky.

**Steroid-induced diabetes in a patient without baseline diabetes** — e.g. polymyalgia rheumatica, where prednisone can start as high as 80 mg and taper over roughly six months. These patients **probably need to be friends with insulin**: glucose can rise very quickly, GLP-1s will not work in time, so **start glargine** and add the other insulins as needed the way you would in any other patient.

## Diagnostic and Exam Detail

- **Structured meal testing.** A patient not on insulin does not need to test around the clock. A fasting morning value is usually enough day to day, plus **at least once a week a paired pre-meal and 2-hour post-meal value** to see how much spike is occurring. This is what turns an A1c into an actionable picture without imposing a monitoring burden.
- **The two questions that find the unsafe hypoglycemia plan:** where do you keep your test supplies, and what floor do you live on? Then: if you're low in the middle of the night, what do you actually do?
- **Rescue supply placement.** The guest: glucose tablets at the bedside. Valdez's practice: caregivers place sugar packets, gel caps, or tablets in **every room — the bathroom, by the toilet, by the sink** — because someone may step out of the shower feeling unwell and there is no predicting where it happens.
- **Teach-back before any correction scale** — give a specific scenario and require the patient to produce the arithmetic.
- **Three-day glucose profile** (fasting plus pre/post each meal) as the minimum substitute for CGM when deciding whether the problem is fasting or prandial. Applies both in pregnancy and in steroid-induced hyperglycemia.
- **CGM practicalities:** sensors worn on the arm for **10 or 14 days** depending on the product; either a receiver, which pairs easily and is small, or a phone, which adds clinic cloud upload and family follow-apps with alerts. Clinic staff can do the initial download and pairing; once connected it largely runs itself.

## Errors and Misconceptions

- **Congratulating an older multimorbid patient on an A1c under 6.5% without asking how it was achieved.** Families arrive high-fiving; the guest sees someone confused by this correction almost daily. The A1c has been the target of years of messaging and now reads as unambiguously good news.
- **Assuming the danger of diabetes runs in one direction.** High blood sugar damage is universally known; hypoglycemia's cognitive and fall harms are not.
- **Escalating insulin in an older patient whose doses already look large for their size**, without first asking whether the full dose is being injected. Deference and memory both work against an honest answer, so the question has to be asked in a way that does not require an admission.
- **Over-basalization** — treated here as a named, recurring error in both the geriatric and steroid cases: a large basal dose doing work it is poorly suited for, and in the steroid case doing work it cannot do at all.
- **Reaching for more basal insulin against steroid-driven postprandial excursions.** The physiology is prandial; the basal will not reach it.
- **Prescribing a correction scale to someone who cannot execute it.**
- **Assuming older patients cannot use CGM technology.** Named by the guest as an assumption he wants to push back on, with 90- and 95-year-old counterexamples.
- **Four sugar packets, not one.** And the colored packets are non-nutritive sweeteners, which will do nothing for a low.
- **Carrying metformin and sulfonylureas into pregnancy as the safe, familiar options** — the exact opposite of current guidance, and a reversal recent enough that many clinicians learned the old version.
- **Applying standard time-in-range (70–180) to a pregnant patient.** The upper bound is far too high; use the 63–140 range, and use glucose values rather than the TIR percentage.
- **Forgetting that insulin is renally cleared** when piling insulin onto a hyperglycemic patient with an acute kidney injury.
- **Starting an SGLT2 inhibitor around the time of an AKI** rather than waiting for renal function to settle.
- **Forgetting NPH exists** — an accessibility and cost advantage as well as a pharmacologic one, and the specific tool for a prednisone profile.
- **Reaching for a GLP-1 agonist to control an acute steroid-driven glucose surge**, when the titration schedule means it cannot work in time.
- **Using NPH for dexamethasone.** The flat, long profile calls for glargine instead.

## Case Examples

### Case 1 — Manny: deintensification in an older adult

A 78-year-old veteran with type 2 diabetes diagnosed 15 years ago, hypertension, and mild cognitive impairment, brought in by family after an ED visit for a fall. EMS measured a random glucose of **72 mg/dL** at the scene. In the ED: BP 145/63, **GFR 53, A1c 6.3%.** Daughter and son-in-law manage his medications and note he does not always take them as directed when left alone. Medications: **metformin, glimepiride, empagliflozin, losartan, atorvastatin, tamsulosin.**

The analysis: the A1c is not the problem — the glimepiride is. The same A1c on metformin and empagliflozin alone would be acceptable, because neither carries meaningful hypoglycemia risk. The plan is to back off the glimepiride, relax the targets to 100–150 fasting and under 180 postprandial, move the empagliflozin to the morning, and set up a rescue-glucose plan that does not involve walking downstairs at night. Pioglitazone is raised as the replacement worth considering in this specific patient: he is male, has no heart failure, and the bone-loss risk is the main caution, which is less concerning in a man with normal bone density — with possible stroke and cognitive benefits on top.

**Case 1 variant** — the same man instead on **glargine 50 units daily plus aspart 15 units before meals**, self-administered once or twice a day depending on what he remembers, always 15 units because that is the number that stuck. The teaching points: the insulin requirement is real (15 years of beta cell loss), but the doses are large for a thin older person and the first suspicion should be that they are not being fully taken. Recalculate from **0.2–0.3 units/kg**, hold the mealtime insulin or reduce it to the largest meal only, and reduce the basal — an A1c of 6.3% on 50 units of glargine is over-basalized.

### Case 2 — Penelope: gestational diabetes at 26 weeks

A 32-year-old, otherwise healthy, 26 weeks pregnant with her second child, diagnosed with gestational diabetes at 24 weeks after an abnormal glucose tolerance test. No complications this pregnancy or the last.

Targets: **fasting 70–95, 2-hour postprandial 100–120, A1c under 6%.** First-line treatment is lifestyle — nutrition counseling and regular moderate exercise — and many gestational cases are controlled that way. If not, insulin, with the profile checked first because gestational diabetes tends to spike postprandially out of proportion to fasting, making rapid-acting insulin the reasonable first agent. Folic acid 400–800 mcg daily. Postpartum: a 50% chance of type 2 diabetes within five years, insulin continued through breastfeeding, then weight loss — GLP-1 if affordable, never topiramate.

### Case 3 — Vanessa: steroid-induced hyperglycemia with CKD

A 53-year-old woman with lupus, CKD, hypertension, obesity (**BMI 33**), and type 2 diabetes, seen after rheumatology increased her to **prednisone 20 mg daily** following a hospitalization for cellulitis and AKI. Discharge labs: **A1c 9.2%, GFR 43.** Since discharge her CGM shows labile and high sugars, **350s to 400s**, with nocturnal hyperglycemia. Medications: **glargine 40 units twice daily, aspart 15 units before meals, olmesartan, methotrexate, folic acid, prednisone 20 mg, allopurinol, furosemide.**

Three valid strategies, in the order the guest would try them:

1. **Dial up the aspart** — a 20% increase (3–5 units per meal), or 5–6 units given the starting A1c, since the steroid effect is predominantly postprandial and this uses insulin the patient already knows.
2. **Convert the basal to NPH** — 80 units of glargine is over-basalized at this BMI *and* poorly matched to the problem. Two-thirds in the morning with the steroid, one-third at dinner, keeping the aspart. The NPH peak does work the flat glargine cannot.
3. **Add NPH on top of the existing regimen** at the time of the steroid dose — mostly an inpatient maneuver, since three insulins at home is a lot to ask.

Throughout: watch for insulin stacking from the renal impairment. Once the acute phase settles, a GLP-1 agonist fits the postprandial physiology well, and an SGLT2 inhibitor fits the CKD and the steroid-driven fluid gain — but not during or immediately around the AKI.

## Conflicts with Established Positions

**No direct conflicts identified.** Nothing here contradicts the committed positions carried elsewhere (anticoagulation, NSAIDs, sulfonylureas, clonidine/hydralazine, hospice threshold, thickened liquids, fall rate, EOL symptom management). Several reinforce strongly; two need a decision rather than adoption.

**Reinforcements worth carrying forward:**

- **Sulfonylureas unsafe in LTC.** This episode is close to a full evidence-and-mechanism brief for that position. The beta cell mechanism explains *why* the class is categorically wrong in someone who cannot sense or treat a low: it bypasses glucose sensing entirely, so insulin release is unrelated to how low the glucose already is. Case 1 is the position's worked example — glimepiride, A1c 6.3%, a hypoglycemic fall. This converges with the same argument arriving from the falls direction in [[Curbsiders 503 - Falls]] (glipizide, A1c 6.5%, an 82-year-old), giving the position two independent derivations.
- **Fall rate — target injurious falls specifically.** Hypoglycemia is a modifiable cause of exactly the falls worth targeting, and the intervention (deintensify) is a medication change rather than an environmental one. This is a better-leveraged fall intervention than most of what fall programs actually do.
- **Hospice requires a clinical or social need beyond eligibility.** The guest's hospice glycemic reasoning is orthogonal to eligibility but shares the underlying logic: the justification for continuing to manage glucose at end of life is a *symptom* (glycosuria, wetting, diaper burden, caregiving load), not a number and not life extension. That is the same shape of argument as the existing position and worth quoting into it.
- **Polypharmacy and deprescribing.** Glimepiride here is a textbook single-target deprescribe — a specific harm already realized, no offsetting benefit at the current A1c, and a family argument (cognition) already available. Cleanly parallel to the donepezil example carried in the falls note.
- **End-of-life symptom management as patient-centered.** The "let him have a taste of ice cream" framing under What Matters Most is the same reasoning that rejects reflexive protocolized EOL interventions.

**Position adopted — the substitution rule:**

- **If there is a compelling reason to use a sulfonylurea, use a meglitinide instead.** This settles the question the episode forces — whether "discontinue sulfonylureas" means discontinue that *class* or that *mechanism* — without softening the existing position. Sulfonylureas stay off the table; the rule routes the rare justified case to the shorter-acting member of the same mechanism, where the hypoglycemia and weight-gain penalties are smaller. Its force comes from the fact that a sulfonylurea usually persists on a list through inertia rather than necessity (Patrick's observation) — so making the bar explicit changes something real: continuing one has to be justified rather than merely inherited. This is consistent with the guest, who when asked directly for a favorite sulfonylurea had none and redirected to meglitinides as "the sulfonylurea for an older person."
- **Two caveats the episode does not supply, because the guest was reasoning in an outpatient frame.**
  - **Meal coupling only helps if the medication pass is coupled to meals.** Meglitinides are dosed with each meal and held when the meal is skipped — that coupling is exactly what makes them safer, but it depends on administration actually tracking intake, which in a facility it frequently does not. A meglitinide given on a fixed schedule to a resident who did not eat reproduces the hazard the substitution exists to avoid. The rule should therefore travel with an explicit hold-if-no-intake instruction, or it inherits the risk it was meant to shed.
  - **If the compelling reason was cost, the substitution does not solve it.** Where formulary access is what is keeping a sulfonylurea on a list, meglitinides are potentially more expensive and do not answer it. The guest's workaround — a prior authorization arguing that a sulfonylurea is a poor choice in this specific older patient — is worth recording as the standard script, and in his experience it works.

**Checked against the diabetes agent hierarchy** (committed position: GLP-1 agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, metformin and DPP-4 inhibitors first; thiazolidinediones second; insulin when needed; sulfonylureas and meglitinides only when backed into a corner):

- **Pioglitazone's second-tier placement is supported by this episode, and the exclusions are the work.** The guest uses it quite a bit in patients not right for intensive insulin and not safe for sulfonylureas. The case for it (low hypoglycemia risk, stroke reduction in IRIS, possible delay of cognitive decline, MASH benefit, once-daily, cheap) and the case against it in this population (fluid retention with heart failure as an absolute exclusion, bone loss in a population with high fracture risk) are both strong, and the guest's own risk-stratification — less concern in a male with normal bone mineral density — is exactly the kind of individualization a handbook chapter would need to spell out rather than gesture at. Note the adjacency to the existing **diltiazem and verapamil contraindicated in HFrEF** position: same structure, different class, and a heart-failure medication-exclusion list may be the right home for both. What the hierarchy still needs spelled out is the pair of exclusions — heart failure as absolute, bone mineral density as the stratifier — rather than a general second-tier endorsement.
- **The hierarchy's last tier already contains its own ordering.** "Sulfonylureas and meglitinides when backed into a corner," plus the substitution rule above, resolves to: within that tier, a meglitinide, dosed with meals and held when a meal is not taken. Sulfonylureas have no remaining indication a meglitinide does not serve better.
- **Insulin sitting outside the tiers is consistent with the episode's central physiologic claim.** Beta cell function is lost across time, so far enough into type 2 diabetes insulin is not a ranked choice among alternatives — it is the only agent that replaces what is missing. "When needed" is the right formulation, and the episode supplies three situations that define *needed*: long-duration type 2 with beta cell failure, pregnancy, and an acute corticosteroid surge where nothing else titrates fast enough.

**The frailty exclusion and the DPP-4 placement turn out to be the same point:**

- **A top-tier GLP-1 position still needs a stated frailty exclusion.** Under Mobility in the 4Ms the guest is explicit: the older, frail, thin patient cannot afford to lose weight, and some GLP-1-associated loss is muscle. In a facility, unintended weight loss is also a tracked quality measure, so the exclusion has an administrative dimension on top of the clinical one. The exclusion needs writing down alongside the position, the way the TZD position needs its heart-failure exclusion written down.
- **DPP-4 inhibitors are what that exclusion leaves you, which is why they belong in the first tier.** They are weight-neutral and well tolerated in older adults. That is not a weaker version of a GLP-1 — it is the agent that fits precisely where a GLP-1 is contraindicated, which makes the two complementary rather than ranked against each other. The apparent tension between a top-tier GLP-1 and a top-tier DPP-4 dissolves once the tier is read as *safety in this population* rather than potency.
- **A 0.4–0.5% drop is worth more than it looks, because of what the counterfactual is.** At 9%, the harm being averted is symptomatic hyperglycemia, glycosuria, and infection risk — not microvascular endpoints over decades — and the realistic alternative in this population is insulin, with its hypoglycemia risk and administration burden, or nothing. Getting a resident from 9% to 8.5% without starting insulin is a win on both axes. This is the stronger form of the guest's own concession that a DPP-4 "might help a little bit enough to stop using a higher risk agent."
- **The class is not uniform on either axis that matters, so the position names agents rather than the class.** On renal handling, linagliptin is primarily non-renally eliminated and needs **no dose adjustment at any eGFR, including dialysis**; sitagliptin, saxagliptin and alogliptin are renally dosed — sitagliptin in three steps (100 mg at eGFR ≥45, 50 mg at 30–<45, 25 mg below 30), which is the initiation caution the guest describes rather than a toxicity. On heart failure, saxagliptin (SAVOR-TIMI 53) and alogliptin (EXAMINE) carry hospitalization-for-heart-failure signals, while sitagliptin (TECOS) and linagliptin (CARMELINA) were neutral. **Saxagliptin and alogliptin are excluded on that basis; sitagliptin and linagliptin are the two live options.**
- **The committed position: generic sitagliptin is the preferred DPP-4 on cost, renally adjusted. Where renal function fluctuates, pursue a prior authorization for linagliptin.** Linagliptin remains brand-only — generics FDA-approved since 2021 but none commercially available, exclusivity to December 2026, earliest formulation patent May 2027 — and the gap is roughly an order of magnitude: generic sitagliptin averages ~$310 retail and as little as ~$36 with a discount card, against ~$626 and ~$278 for linagliptin *(verified 2026-09-12; the one fact here with an expiry date)*. The no-adjustment advantage is real but narrow, and it earns the PA in the resident for whom a missed adjustment step is a realistic failure mode — low, fluctuating, or unreliably measured eGFR. This resolves the guest's own formulary-freebie reasoning in a way he could not have, since he was discussing sitagliptin before it went generic.
- **CAROLINA is the prior-authorization script.** Linagliptin versus glimepiride, median 6.3 years: cardiovascular outcomes neutral, but hypoglycemia **10.6% versus 37.7%**, with a modest weight difference favoring linagliptin. The DPP-4-over-sulfonylurea preference is not extrapolated from mechanism — it was tested directly against the specific sulfonylurea most often found on an LTC medication list, over a duration long enough to matter. Those two numbers are the strongest thing to put in front of a payer.
- **Medicaid coverage cannot be stated generally, and for most LTC residents Medicaid is not the payer anyway.** Under the Medicaid Drug Rebate Program a participating manufacturer's drug is covered in essentially every state, but preferred-list placement — and therefore whether a PA is required — is set state by state: Oregon lists sitagliptin and generic as preferred with **linagliptin non-preferred, PA required**, while New York's NYRx lists both as preferred. More to the point for this project, most LTC residents are dual-eligible, so **Part D is the primary drug payer** and the governing document is the plan formulary the LTC pharmacy dispenses from. Discount-card pricing is a cash price outside the benefit and is not reachable through Part D.
- **The failure mode worth naming.** A 0.5% gain is a win when the counterfactual is insulin or nothing. It becomes a problem when it substitutes for closing a gap it cannot close: linagliptin does not take a resident from 9% to target, and adding it should not end the question of why the number is 9% in the first place.

- **Handbook — cross-reference from *Polypharmacy and Deprescribing*:** sulfonylurea discontinuation as a worked deprescribing case, and the "is the dose actually being taken" question as a general technique for any large dose in a thin older patient.

Where the review concludes an agent of this mechanism is still needed, the substitution rule applies: a meglitinide rather than a sulfonylurea, dosed with meals and held when a meal is not taken.
