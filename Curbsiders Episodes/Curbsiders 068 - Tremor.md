## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Movement disorder specialist Andres Deik walks through history, exam, and stepwise pharmacologic management of tremor for a primary care audience, organized around distinguishing essential tremor, Parkinsonian (rest) tremor, and drug-induced (enhanced physiologic) tremor.

## Pearls

- A tremor is a rhythmic oscillation of a body part — not necessarily the hand; it can affect anywhere from the face to the feet.
- First branch point on any tremor: rest vs. action. Action tremor subdivides into intention (amplitude increases as the patient reaches a target), postural (emerges just from holding a position), and isometric (emerges while sustaining a contraction against resistance, e.g. gripping something — the "gym tremor").
- A re-emerging tremor is a postural equivalent of a rest tremor: no tremor on immediately raising the arms, but one emerges after a few seconds of holding the position. This is different from a true postural tremor, which starts immediately on assuming the posture.
- History gives away roughly 70% of the diagnosis. Establish a specific onset timeline (tremor since the 20s vs. new onset at 65 point to very different diagnoses) and which activities provoke it (drinking coffee, handwriting, buttoning a shirt, brushing teeth).
- Rest tremor is usually described by patients as annoying but not disabling early on; action tremor patients tend to present because it's already interfering with function.
- Drug-induced tremor is an enhanced physiologic tremor: postural greater than intention, high-frequency/low-amplitude. In severe cases it can "spill into rest," but the postural component still dominates — that pattern is what argues against a Parkinsonian process even when a rest component is present.
- Essential tremor is alcohol-responsive in a meaningful subset of patients (a glass of wine or beer calms it) — a useful history clue, though it doesn't always pan out.
- Essential tremor has a strong autosomal dominant family history pattern and is far more common than Parkinson's disease; absence of family history doesn't rule out a familial condition (low penetrance, relatives who died before the condition would have declared itself).
- Parkinsonism is misdiagnosed in ~10% of cases even by movement disorder specialists (based on pathologic correlation at autopsy) and ~20% by non-specialists. The most common cause of misdiagnosis is an incomplete exam — someone sees a rest component and stops looking for postural/intention components. The other driver: "Parkinson's disease" is pathologically heterogeneous (alpha-synuclein vs. tau pathology, multiple genes), and some patients only phenoconvert to a different diagnosis (e.g. progressive supranuclear palsy) after years of looking like classic Parkinson's.
- Everybody has some physiologic tremor. The threshold for treatment is functional: if it bothers the patient, treat it; if it doesn't, just follow over time.
- About 70% of YouTube videos depicting "movement disorders" actually show patients with a psychiatric condition, not an organic movement disorder — worth knowing before pointing a patient or family member to video resources.

## Clinical Framework

**Rest vs. action, and the three action subtypes:**
- Rest tremor: present at rest, improves or resolves with activation of the limb. Classic for Parkinsonian tremor.
- Postural tremor: emerges immediately on holding a position against gravity (e.g. arms outstretched).
- Intention tremor: amplitude increases as the patient's hand approaches a target (finger-to-nose). The defining feature of essential tremor when present.
- Isometric tremor: emerges during sustained muscle contraction against resistance.
- Re-emerging tremor: a postural tremor that only appears after a latency period — functionally a rest tremor's postural equivalent, and distinguished from true postural tremor by that delay.

**Tremor frequency as a discriminator:** Parkinsonian tremor is slow, roughly 3–7 Hz. Essential tremor is faster, roughly 7–10 Hz.

**Essential tremor vs. enhanced physiologic tremor** can be genuinely hard to distinguish in younger patients (tremor onset is bimodal — teens/20s or 65+, uncommon in the 30s–40s), because both can present as a high-frequency, low-amplitude tremor more prominent in posture than intention. When it's ambiguous, secondary causes (hyperthyroidism, caffeine, medications) must be ruled out — by definition, essential tremor's intention component should predominate once fully expressed, but early on that may not yet be apparent.

## Drug and Dosing Detail

**Essential tremor — pharmacologic ladder:**
- First line: beta-blockers, propranolol specifically. Nadolol and atenolol are good alternatives if propranolol isn't tolerated. **Metoprolol does not work well for tremor** — the guest actively switches patients off metoprolol onto propranolol when their cardiologist or primary care physician agrees. This is a meaningful negative pearl: a patient already on metoprolol succinate for another indication who develops tremor needing treatment will likely need a different beta-blocker added or substituted, not just a dose change.
  - Elderly-specific titration (the guest is deliberately conservative in patients 65+, both because of age and because lower doses are often sufficient): start propranolol 10 mg daily → 20 mg three times daily → convert to long-acting propranolol 80 mg → 120 mg. Beyond 120 mg the guest reports getting palpitations himself and would add a second agent rather than push the dose further. Some patients achieve tremor control at 10 mg total daily dose.
  - Contraindications/relative contraindications to beta-blockade: asthma, bradycardia, hypotension — move to second line.
- Second line: primidone. Starting dose 50 mg at night, titrate by 50 mg increments weekly, up to 750 mg if tolerated and needed — though when it works, it usually works around 200 mg, so escalation rarely needs to go much higher. Added benefits: helps with sleep (relevant in a population with high rates of insomnia) and is long-acting, which supports adherence. **Caution:** meaningful drug interaction burden, specifically flagged with warfarin — may need dose adjustment.
- Third line: topiramate, roughly 100–200 mg daily in younger patients. **Caution in patients over 65:** cognitive side effects make it harder to use safely in that population, so reserve it for younger patients when possible.
- **Gabapentin: not effective for essential tremor** per this specialist ("like water… nothing bad happens to them, but also nothing good happens"). Causes sedation without benefit; he doesn't reach for it and stops it when patients arrive already on it for tremor.
- **Clonazepam (or other long-acting benzodiazepines):** used by some, and the guest does use it in select patients, but flags the tension explicitly — tremor worsens with anxiety in general, so treating with a benzodiazepine may really be treating anxiety rather than the tremor itself. Abuse-potential/epidemic-of-misuse concerns apply as usual.

**Drug-induced tremor — specific culprits:**
- Beta-2 agonist inhalers (ask specifically in patients with asthma).
- Systemic steroids — can increase tremor amplitude.
- Bupropion: significant tremor risk described above roughly 300 mg; the guest has seen it clearly at 450 mg.
- SSRIs — not a uniform class effect in this guest's experience: sertraline and fluoxetine are specifically called out as commonly associated (fluoxetine even at low doses); less emphasis on other SSRIs.
- Lithium and antipsychotics (see case example below).
- Older/rarely-used stimulants (xanthines and similar) — mentioned as a now-uncommon cause.

## Diagnostic and Exam Detail

**History:** onset timeline (specific, not vague); activities that provoke the tremor (ADLs — coffee, handwriting, buttons, brushing teeth); full medication list including recently added or changed drugs; alcohol responsiveness; family history (with the caveat above about absence not ruling anything out); task specificity (a tremor present only with one specific activity, e.g. writing or playing an instrument, raises suspicion for a dystonic tremor rather than essential tremor or Parkinsonian tremor).

**Exam protocol, in sequence:**
1. Patient seated, arms resting on armrests. Use distraction (serial subtraction, months of the year backward) to unmask a rest tremor — lying the patient down is rarely needed but is the fallback if distraction doesn't work.
2. Raise arms in front of the body ("Superman"/outstretched position) to assess postural tremor. For subtle tremor: lay a piece of paper across the hands to visualize amplification, or have the patient hold a laser pointer aimed at a wall.
3. "Wing"/chicken-wing position — elbows flexed, hands extended, fingertips almost touching — brings out postural tremor further.
4. Finger-to-nose maneuver assesses the intention component.
5. Tone testing (cogwheel rigidity) at elbows, wrists, knees, ankles — suggestive of Parkinson's disease when present.
6. Rapid alternating movements (finger tapping, opening/closing the hand, pronation/supination) — a decrement in amplitude over repetitions is bradykinesia, the defining feature of a Parkinsonian syndrome. Takes about 30 seconds and is described as highly diagnostic.
7. Mouth/jaw: ask the patient to talk (e.g. describe their commute) and observe. Parkinson's tremor can involve jaw, lips, or tongue and is a rest phenomenon — it lessens when the mouth is opened (relaxing the muscles of mastication unmasks the underlying rest tremor). Essential tremor's oral involvement, when present (uncommon), instead shows up as tremulous cheeks/lips specifically with talking — an action phenomenon, the opposite pattern.
8. Handwriting: have the patient write a standard phrase in script. Parkinson's produces micrographia (progressively shrinking letters). Essential tremor produces macrographia (large, bold letters — a compensatory strategy, since smaller writing worsens the visible tremor).
9. Freehand spiral drawing: hold the pen distally, approach the paper without resting the hand on it. In essential tremor, a large, jagged, star- or sea-urchin-shaped spiral with visible coarse tremor as the hand approaches the paper. In Parkinson's, a small, tight spiral with fine fluctuations, without the coarse jagged lines. Often diagnostic on its own.

**Imaging/biomarkers:** No reliable biomarker distinguishes a typical Parkinsonian syndrome from an atypical one during life; PET patterns exist in research settings but don't change management and are hard to justify to insurers. A DAT scan (dopamine transporter scan) images the integrity of the dopaminergic system and reliably distinguishes essential tremor (normal) from any Parkinsonian syndrome (abnormal) when the exam is equivocal — but it cannot distinguish between the different causes of Parkinsonism.

## Errors and Misconceptions

- Diagnosing Parkinson's from a rest component alone without completing the rest of the exam (postural/intention testing) is the most common cause of misdiagnosis — described as more common than genuine diagnostic difficulty.
- Assuming any beta-blocker will do for essential tremor — metoprolol specifically underperforms and shouldn't be the default choice.
- Reaching for gabapentin for tremor — no evidence of benefit per this specialist's experience, only sedation.
- Confusing a re-emerging tremor (postural equivalent of a rest tremor, delayed onset) with a true postural tremor (immediate onset) — they look similar but have different diagnostic implications.
- Trusting patient-facing YouTube videos of "movement disorders" at face value — a large majority reportedly show psychiatric rather than organic movement conditions.

## Case Examples

A 70-year-old man with bipolar disorder on lithium and an atypical antipsychotic presented with a disabling postural tremor interfering with eating and dressing, without Parkinsonian features (no slowness, rigidity, or facial masking). Because the pattern and medication list pointed to a drug-induced tremor, the specialist's first move was addressing the cause rather than the symptom: he coordinated with the patient's psychiatrist to reduce the lithium dose. The tremor improved but remained disabling at the lower amplitude, at which point propranolol was added and controlled it without triggering mood destabilization. Teaching points: take a genuinely thorough medication history including doses, address the causative drug first when it's identifiable and safe to adjust, and treat the residual symptom pharmacologically only once the underlying cause has been addressed as far as it safely can be — sometimes (as with lithium and bipolar disease) the causative drug can't simply be stopped, and symptomatic treatment of an iatrogenic effect is the reasonable compromise.
