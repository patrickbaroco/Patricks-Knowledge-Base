## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Endocrinologist Jeff Colburn covers continuous glucose monitoring end to end — what the devices are, how to order them, who qualifies since the 2023 coverage change, and how to read the output — then works five glucose patterns and the medication change each one calls for. The operational detail is unusually concrete: sensor wear times and monthly quantities, which apps, where on the arm and why, alarm thresholds, the interstitial delay and what it means for a falling glucose, and the insulin-stacking hazard that CGM introduces. The pattern half supplies an explicit order of operations — lows first, then basal, then prandial — and the physiology behind why reducing insulin in someone with an A1c of 8.6 can lower the A1c.

## Pearls

- **The A1c-to-glucose conversion worth memorizing: 126 equals an A1c of 6**, because 126 is the fasting threshold at which diabetes is diagnosed. Then **add 30 per A1c point** — 156 is 7, 186 is 8, 216 is 9. An A1c of 8.6 implies an average glucose near 200.
- That conversion is what exposes a discordance. Fasting sugars of 100–150 do not produce an A1c of 8.6, so either the A1c is unreliable or glucose is being missed — and what is being missed is almost always postprandial.
- **The glucose most people never see is the postprandial number.** This is why A1c improves in people who simply wear a CGM without any change in therapy: they watch what a large carbohydrate meal does and change what they eat.
- **In older and high-risk adults the time-in-range target is >50%, not >70%, with time below 70 mg/dL held under 1%.** The widely quoted >70% figure is the general-adult target; the same consensus sets a separate, more conservative pair for this group that prioritizes reducing lows over holding range.
- **Time in range beats A1c on three counts.** It responds far faster to an intervention — A1c is a three-month capture, time in range is the current sensor period. It reflects **glycemic variability**, which A1c cannot: a glucose sitting at 126 day and night and a glucose swinging between the 60s and 250s can produce the same A1c of 6. And it is **not distorted by the physiologic and pathologic factors that distort A1c** — for example iron deficiency anemia, where red cells circulate longer, accumulate more glucose, and falsely raise the A1c.
- "It's the only time in medicine we like a flat line."
- **Sensors have their own interferents, even though they escape the A1c's.** High-dose vitamin C is the named example; manufacturers maintain and update interference lists per device.
- **Accuracy has improved enough to change what the devices are for.** Measured by MARD, older sensors were good only for pattern recognition. Modern devices are accurate enough that **the FDA permits patients to dose based on the displayed number**, not just read trends.
- **Do not tell patients to throw out the fingerstick meter.** When symptoms do not match the reading, the fingerstick is the arbiter.
- **CGM measures interstitial fluid, not blood** — in equilibrium with blood but roughly **10 to 15 minutes behind.** When glucose is flat the delay is irrelevant. When it is falling fast, the displayed number is stale and the real glucose is already lower.
- **The fix for the delay is to raise the low alert, not to watch more closely.** For someone who drops quickly, set the low alarm at 90 rather than waiting for 70, so the alert arrives while there is still time.
- **The high alarm defaults to 250, and that default drives people to abandon the device.** A patient who is running above 250 most of the day gets alarmed constantly, and puts the device in another room. Temporarily raise the high threshold while the glucose is being brought down.
- **CGM introduces an insulin-stacking hazard that fingersticks did not.** Fingerstick testing is naturally spaced four to six hours apart around meals, which roughly matches rapid-acting insulin's duration, so stacking is self-limiting. A patient watching a CGM sees 250 an hour after breakfast, corrects, sees it still high an hour later, corrects again — all before the breakfast dose has finished working. Named by the guest as one of his real concerns about the devices.
- **Rapid-acting insulin: onset about 15 minutes, peak about 1 hour, duration about 4 hours.** That four hours is the number that makes stacking a hazard.
- **Address lows before anything else.** Stated as priority one of pattern management: fix the hypoglycemia and reduce the regimen before attempting any other change. Then basal needs. Then prandial needs.
- **Reducing insulin in someone with an A1c of 8.6 can lower the A1c**, by two mechanisms: the patient eats a large amount of sugar to correct each low, which spikes them afterward; and the counter-regulatory hormone surge produces roughly **24 hours of insulin insensitivity**, so glucose runs higher for a full day after every low event.
- **Letting the body correct a low on its own is not acceptable**, though patients ask. Every counter-regulatory hormone involved is damaging: hypoglycemia is a stress test on the heart, and recurrent episodes carry brain damage and dementia risk.
- **Intense exercise raises glucose for the first 30 minutes or so, then lowers it beyond about an hour — and insulin sensitivity stays elevated for up to 5 hours afterward.** Hypoglycemia can arrive long after the workout ends.
- **The pre-exercise snack should be a slowly-digested carbohydrate with protein and fat** — half a banana with peanut butter is the worked example, or a protein bar. **Not Gatorade or straight sugar**, which spike and then crash. The point is a snack the patient deliberately does *not* cover with insulin.
- **Both of that patient's drugs may be aimed where the problem isn't.** Metformin lowers hepatic glucose output and basal insulin covers the fasting state — so a patient on metformin plus basal with a perfect fasting glucose and a 9% A1c has a regimen pointed entirely at the wrong part of the day.
- **A rising bedtime glucose is the tell that the problem is prandial**, accumulating across the day from meals.
- **Breakfast is often the worst meal**, for two independent reasons: morning cortisol makes us maximally insulin resistant, and breakfast foods skew carbohydrate-heavy.
- **The bedtime-to-morning drop is how you detect over-basalization** without waiting for a low. Someone who goes to bed at 250 and wakes at 170 is falling a long way overnight — bring the bedtime number down to 180 and he will wake near hypoglycemia.
- **Uniformly high glucose is the easy pattern; mixed highs and lows is the hard one.** Trainees present a uniformly high tracing as distressing — the guest calls it "a great pattern," because the action is obvious. Mixed patterns are hard because you have to work out which lows are driving which highs.
- **Twice-daily basal is usually unnecessary.** Splitting is for very high doses — above about **80 units of glargine, which is as far as the pen dial goes.**
- **Sulfonylureas: weight gain, no end-organ benefit beyond glucose lowering.** The guest's stated reason they remain in use is that a person does not have insurance that covers the better products.
- **Sulfonylurea + basal insulin is a combination where the sulfonylurea is doing the prandial job.** The guest is not a fan and calls it a stopgap while a patient is educated or referred; a patient who needs a sulfonylurea probably needs mealtime-dosed insulin instead.
- **Mealtime insulin is deferred but "not a bad guy."** For a patient high all the time, the profession often waits too long, and adding one intervention can beat adding several medicines. Once controlled you can leave it, or add a GLP-1 or SGLT2 inhibitor later and back the mealtime insulin off.
- **Not everyone starting prandial insulin needs three doses a day.** Start with the largest meal. Basal plus one mealtime dose is still only two injections.
- **The patient whose glucose does not budge no matter what you add is an adherence question, not a dosing question** — cost, administration or storage, which people are often embarrassed to raise.
- **Metformin extended release can technically be taken as the full 2000 mg at once**, though some get GI upset. And the clinical difference between 1500 mg and 2000 mg is small enough to trade against pill burden.
- **Diabetes distress is a reason to simplify.** Diabetes is a lot of work and intrusive to eating; the guest treats "fewer pills" as a legitimate clinical goal rather than a concession.

## Clinical Framework

### The order of operations for reading any CGM tracing

1. **Hypoglycemia.** Fix the lows and reduce the regimen before attempting anything else.
2. **Basal needs.** Assessed primarily by the overnight trajectory — the bedtime-to-morning change, not the morning number alone.
3. **Prandial needs.** Only after the first two.

### The five counter-regulatory responses to hypoglycemia, in order

1. Beta cells stop producing insulin.
2. Alpha cells release glucagon, prompting hepatic glycogenolysis.
3. Growth hormone release.
4. Cortisol release — the hormone that wakes us in the morning, and which raises glucose.
5. Adrenal epinephrine and norepinephrine — the shakiness and high-alert state that usually wakes a sleeping patient.

Responses 4 and 5 are why nocturnal hypoglycemia often wakes people. When it does not, the patient rides through it and is found only by a CGM. The clinical consequence of the surge is roughly **24 hours of insulin insensitivity afterward.**

### How to read the display, in sequence

1. **Summary statistics** on the front page: time in range, time low and very low, time high.
2. **The stacked overlay** — every day of the sensor period superimposed, showing the pattern by time of day.
3. **Individual daily graphs** — which reveal things the overlay hides, such as weekends differing from weekdays.

The limiting factor is not the data but the context. Patterns without events are hard to read, which is why journaling meals, exercise and remembered lows matters. Absent a journal, walk the patient through a usual day. The guest turns the screen toward the patient and reads the line with them, making it a discussion rather than a pronouncement.

### Time-in-range targets — two different target sets

**As stated by the guest, for a general adult population:**

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Time in range, 70–180 mg/dL | **>70%** |
| Time low | **<3%** |
| Time very low | **zero if achievable** — "those are dangerous" |
| Time above range | **<30%** |

Mnemonic offered: everyone remembers an A1c target of 7; remember time in range 70%.

**For older and high-risk adults the targets are different, and substantially more
conservative.** The 2019 international consensus that produced the >70% figure also produced a
separate set for this group, which trades range attainment for hypoglycemia protection — a
much looser range target paired with a much tighter floor:

| Metric | General adult | **Older / high-risk adult** |
|---|---|---|
| Time in range, 70–180 mg/dL | >70% | **>50%** |
| Time below 70 mg/dL | <4% | **<1%** |
| Time below 54 mg/dL | <1% | *(subsumed in the above)* |
| Time above 180 mg/dL | <25% | — |
| Time above 250 mg/dL | <5% | **<10%** |

Battelino et al., *Diabetes Care* 2019;42(8):1593–1603,
[10.2337/dci19-0028](https://doi.org/10.2337/dci19-0028). The consensus panel's stated
rationale for the older-adult set is the elevated risk of severe hypoglycemia in this group,
which makes **reducing lows the priority and range attainment secondary.**

**Which set applies is the single most consequential choice in a CGM program for an older
population.** The >70% figure is the one in general circulation and the one quoted throughout
this episode; adopting it as a facility target would push toward tighter glycemic control than
is appropriate for the population, using a metric that looks objective while doing it. Note
the direction of each change: the range target *falls* from 70% to 50%, and the hypoglycemia
ceiling *tightens* from 4% to 1%. A program that borrowed only the first half would be worse
than one that used neither.

### Choosing an agent for postprandial hyperglycemia

Guest-endorsed ranking for postprandial effect: **GLP-1 > SGLT2 inhibitor > DPP-4 inhibitor**, with prandial insulin as the later option.

- **GLP-1 agonists** act by decreasing appetite through central nervous system effects on food-seeking behavior, lowering hepatic glucose output, and slowing GI transit — the last being where most side effects come from. Newer dual agents add GIP receptor activity (tirzepatide); semaglutide and dulaglutide also named.
- **SGLT2 inhibitors** lower the renal threshold for reclaiming glucose, so the kidney offloads more after a spike — but with **less postprandial impact** than a GLP-1.
- **DPP-4 inhibitors** are mentioned as an option but flagged as unlikely to lower a 9% A1c enough on their own.
- Both GLP-1 and SGLT2 carry weight loss plus cardiovascular and renal benefit; using both together helps considerably where insurance allows.

## Drug and Dosing Detail

### The devices

Two systems, described as very comparable and converging. The guest states no relationship with either company.

| | Dexcom | Abbott FreeStyle Libre |
|---|---|---|
| Generation current at recording | G7 | Libre 3 |
| Sensor wear | **10 days** | **14 days** |
| Sensors per month | **3** | **2** |
| Apps required | **two** — the G7 app plus **Clarity** (the cloud portal) | **one** — FreeStyle Libre 3 |
| Applicator | has a firing button | press against skin to fire |

- **Components:** the sensor adheres to the skin; the **transmitter is now integrated into the sensor** in current versions, having previously been a separate piece; the display is either a smartphone or a handheld reader.
- **Size:** about two stacked quarters.
- **Smartphone is preferred over a reader** where possible — no extra device to charge or lose, data uploads to the cloud so the clinic can pull it, and follow-on apps let a family member watch continuously and receive high and low alerts. Named use cases: an older parent with cognitive difficulty, and a child with diabetes.
- **Water:** wear it through the shower and swim with it. Scrubbing over it will take it off.
- **Pain:** the applicator resembles a single-serve coffee pod. It is held against the arm, a needle inserts a catheter and withdraws, leaving the catheter to sense glucose via a small chemical reaction. The patient never sees a needle. The guest applies these to about 15 new patients a week and reports no one has ever called it painful.

**Placement technique — the specific pearl:** apply to the **back of the upper arm over the triceps**, with the arm raised in a **"flexing your bicep" position and kept loose.** Do not turn the arm over to reach the site: turning stretches the muscle, and **the catheter can kink** when the arm returns to neutral.

**Ordering:**
- **Sensors come from the pharmacy** like any prescription. Write 3 per month for Dexcom, 2 per month for Libre.
- **A reader, if needed, usually comes through DME** rather than pharmacy.
- The guest writes monthly; up to three months is possible but pharmacies are cautious about large quantities, partly because rapidly increasing use has created supply constraints.
- **Cash purchase is becoming feasible** — described as not exorbitant — so a patient without coverage can buy a month to see what is happening.

### Coverage, as described in the episode

- **April 2023: CMS changed the eligibility criteria.** Previously a patient had to be on a **multiple daily injection** regimen — multiple rapid-acting doses plus a basal. Now **a single daily basal injection qualifies.** Most commercial payers follow Medicare and Medicaid.
- This is the change that moves CGM out of subspecialty clinics, because the qualifying patient is no longer the complex multiple-injection user.
- **Ongoing requirements:** seen by the prescriber **3 months after initiation**, then evaluated **every 6 months.** The stated rationale is that the payer wants a clinician actually evaluating the glucose pattern, not only the patient inspecting it.
- Loose accompanying language that the patient should be able to navigate the technology and understand the training.
- **Patients on no insulin at all were not covered**, though the guest notes evidence that their A1c improves simply from wearing the device.

*(See Conflicts for the current coverage standard and an additional qualifying pathway the episode does not mention.)*

### Setup, and the real barrier

Both systems walk the patient through installation with pictures and require allowing the Bluetooth pairing. The guest's observation is that **the device is not the hard part — creating and remembering a password for a new account is**, and this is true of people of every age, not only older ones. Family members frequently get involved for this step specifically.

**Clinic-side:** the practical hurdle is workflow. Set up the **provider portal as a practice or group rather than individually**, so that data is available to whichever clinician the patient sees rather than channeled to one; the manufacturers' representatives will help configure a group portal. With a portal, data can be pulled from the cloud and pasted into the note. Without one, you can still scroll the patient's own app together.

### Insulins named

- **Rapid-acting (aspart and others):** onset ~15 minutes, peak ~1 hour, duration ~4 hours.
- **Glargine:** the pen dials to a maximum of **80 units**, which is the practical trigger for splitting a basal dose twice daily. Below that, twice-daily basal is usually unnecessary.
- **Degludec:** designed for a longer half-life and duration of action, so **dose timing can be moved around**, with **less peak effect particularly at high doses.** Mostly used in type 1 but usable in anyone.
- **Detemir:** being discontinued by its manufacturer at the time of recording — see Conflicts. The guest's response is to convert to a once-daily basal, which simplifies the regimen anyway.

### Metformin

- **Maximum 1000 mg twice daily, 2000 mg total.** Extended release can technically be taken as the full 2000 mg at once, though some patients get GI upset.
- **The clinical difference between 1500 mg and 2000 mg is small** in the guest's experience, despite guidelines calling for the maximal tolerated dose — a reasonable trade against pill burden.
- **Mechanism:** decreases hepatic glucose output, so the effect is mostly on fasting glucose. The guest notes **DeFronzo's position that metformin is not an insulin sensitizer**, contrary to how it is usually taught.

### Sulfonylureas

- **Mechanism restated:** the drug "poisons the little potassium channel" that lets the beta cell sense glucose. Instead of the cell assessing the glucose and responding, it simply releases insulin.
- **The effect is prolonged specifically in renal failure and in older people** — the two reasons nocturnal hypoglycemia shows up on a CGM in these patients.
- They must be taken with food.
- **Management of sulfonylurea-associated nocturnal hypoglycemia:** get off the drug, or drop the evening dose.
- **Glipizide XL in an older person with CKD is explicitly not the guest's preferred choice.**
- **Summary position:** weight gain, no end-organ benefit beyond glucose lowering, and — in the guest's words — the only current reason to use one is that the patient lacks insurance coverage for the better products.

### Hypoglycemia treatment

**Rule of 15:** 15 g of carbohydrate — **four glucose tablets, or four ounces of juice or regular soda** — then wait 15 minutes and retest. The CGM can be used for the recheck.

Williams's observation, endorsed: patients keep the worst possible food at the bedside and overcorrect — half a sleeve of cookies. Colburn's response is that it is genuinely hard to eat a small amount while hypoglycemic, which is part of why preventing the low beats treating it.

## Diagnostic and Exam Detail

- **Professional (blinded) CGM** as a distinct tool: the sensor is applied in clinic and worn typically 14 days, **the patient cannot see the readings**, and the office downloads the data afterward. The stated rationale is that it is safer early on for some patients — they cannot react to glucose readings they are not yet equipped to interpret or act on. Appropriate when the patient is not on a regimen they could titrate themselves.
- **Personal CGM** is the prescribed, patient-facing version and the subject of most of the episode.
- **Structured fingerstick testing remains a reasonable starting point** for a patient on once-daily insulin who dislikes testing — a couple of days of paired readings before committing to a device.
- **Alarm configuration is a clinical decision, not a default.** Set the low alert above 70 — 90 is the guest's example — for anyone who drops quickly, to compensate for the interstitial lag. Raise the high alert temporarily above the 250 default for anyone running high enough that the alarm would fire constantly.
- **Journaling** is what converts a tracing into a diagnosis: meals, exercise timing, and any remembered symptomatic lows. The guest's example of what journaling recovers — "I was low at this time and I'd been cutting the lawn a long time."
- **Type 1 antibodies** are worth considering in a patient presenting younger than the typical type 2 diagnosis; the average age of type 2 diagnosis is given as 49.

## Errors and Misconceptions

- **Reading an A1c without converting it to an average glucose**, and therefore not noticing that the reported fingersticks cannot produce that A1c.
- **Believing an A1c reflects control.** It cannot show variability: 126 around the clock and swings from the 60s to the 250s produce the same number.
- **Trusting a rapidly changing CGM number.** The 10–15 minute interstitial lag means a falling glucose is already lower than the screen shows.
- **Leaving the high alarm at its 250 default** in a patient who lives above 250, which produces alarm fatigue and abandonment of the device.
- **Stacking rapid-acting insulin** because the CGM makes a high number visible an hour after the meal dose, before that dose has finished working.
- **Treating a low by letting the counter-regulatory system handle it.** Patients ask; the answer is no.
- **Escalating therapy in a patient with a high A1c and unrecognized nocturnal lows**, when the lows are part of what is producing the high A1c.
- **Aiming the regimen at the wrong part of the day** — metformin plus basal in a patient whose fasting glucose is already fine.
- **Judging basal adequacy from the morning number alone** rather than from the overnight drop, which is how over-basalization hides behind two hyperglycemic values.
- **Splitting basal insulin twice daily** when the dose does not require it.
- **Using a sulfonylurea to do a prandial job** alongside basal insulin.
- **Deferring mealtime insulin too long** in a patient who is uniformly hyperglycemic — the mirror-image error to over-treating.
- **Telling patients to discard the fingerstick meter** once they have a CGM.
- **Assuming the technology barrier is the device.** It is account creation and password management.
- **Setting up a provider portal individually rather than as a practice**, so the data follows one clinician instead of the patient.
- **Escalating doses in a patient whose glucose never moves**, without checking whether the medication is being taken, afforded, and stored.

## Case Examples

### Mr. H — nocturnal hypoglycemia behind a high A1c

A 58-year-old man, 100 kg, with type 2 diabetes, obesity, hyperlipidemia, and CKD 3a without albuminuria. On **glargine 35 units nightly and metformin XR 750 mg, two tablets daily.** **A1c 8.6.** Dislikes fingersticks; fasting glucose 100–150 when he checks; reports no lows.

The A1c-to-glucose conversion is what makes the case: 8.6 implies an average near 200, which those fasting numbers cannot produce. CGM shows glucose above 180–200 after lunch and at bedtime, **a dip to 65 overnight**, and fasting values of 100–130.

The move is to **reduce the basal insulin despite the 8.6** — and to ask whether progressing CKD is prolonging the insulin's effect. The A1c often falls afterward, through overcorrection eating and the 24-hour post-hypoglycemic insulin insensitivity. Daytime highs are addressed only after the lows are fixed.

### Mr. H again — exercise-associated hypoglycemia

The same man joins a gym and works out most afternoons around 3 p.m. He has had to stop several workouts for nausea and dizziness, and has not checked his glucose during them.

Exercise physiology explains it: intense exercise raises glucose for the first half hour, lowers it past an hour, and leaves insulin sensitivity elevated for **up to five hours afterward.** Management is to reduce the basal insulin — he may simply need less now — and, where the timing of exercise is unpredictable and the insulin is already on board, to add a **pre-exercise snack combining a slow carbohydrate with protein and fat** that is deliberately not covered with insulin.

### Ms. J — postprandial hyperglycemia with a good fasting glucose

A 42-year-old woman, 90 kg, with obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, and type 2 diabetes. On **metformin XR 2000 mg daily and degludec 25 units each morning.** Bedtime glucose **180–250**, fasting **80–130**, no post-meal checks, **A1c 9%.**

Both agents target fasting glucose, and the fasting glucose is fine — the regimen is aimed at the wrong part of the day. The rising bedtime number is the tell. CGM identifies which meals are responsible; breakfast is a common culprit, for the cortisol and the carbohydrate content. Options are a GLP-1, an SGLT2 inhibitor, possibly a DPP-4 (unlikely to be sufficient at 9%), or prandial insulin started at the largest meal. At 42, below the typical age of type 2 onset, type 1 antibodies are worth a thought.

### Ms. J, alternate pattern — fasting hyperglycemia with a good bedtime

Same patient and regimen, but bedtime glucose **under 180–200** and fasting **150–200**, with the same 9% A1c.

Now glucose is rising overnight, so the basal is insufficient — increase the degludec, or the metformin if it is not maxed. Ask whether she is snacking at night. A GLP-1 or SGLT2 inhibitor is still reasonable despite their mostly-prandial action.

### Mr. V — hyperglycemia everywhere

A 71-year-old man, 110 kg, with type 2 diabetes, hypertension, obesity, and **CKD 3b with a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio of 60.** On **glipizide XL 10 mg daily and detemir 10 units twice daily**; cannot tolerate metformin. Glucose in the 200s–300s whenever checked, with two weeks of fingersticks showing both pre- and postprandial hyperglycemia.

The teaching frame: this is the *easy* pattern. Three changes follow. **Detemir is coming off the market**, so convert to a once-daily basal — and twice-daily basal was unnecessary at this dose anyway. **Glipizide XL in an older person with CKD is the wrong drug**, and paired with basal insulin it is doing the prandial job that insulin or a newer agent should do. And before increasing the basal, **look at the bedtime-to-morning drop**: going to bed at 250 and waking at 170 is a long fall, and bringing the bedtime number to 180 would put him near hypoglycemia by morning.

Preferred additions given the CKD: an SGLT2 inhibitor, then a GLP-1 — treating the comorbidity and the prandial glucose at once. Mealtime insulin comes later, because it has no end-organ benefit, causes weight gain, and carries hypoglycemia risk. Two sequences are offered as equally valid: fix the basal, add the newer agents, then mealtime insulin; or add basal, add mealtime insulin, and pursue the other agents as prior authorizations and finances allow. Where finances foreclose the newer agents, the fallback is glipizide with more meals plus a once-daily basal, with explicit hypoglycemia counseling and a requirement to eat with the drug.

## Conflicts with Established Positions

**No direct conflicts identified** with the committed positions carried elsewhere. One target in the episode is the wrong one for an older population and needs substituting rather than adopting, two facts are time-stamped, and the operational material needs substantial translation before it drives a facility program.

**Reinforcements worth carrying forward:**

- **Sulfonylureas.** The guest's position is flat: weight gain, no end-organ benefit beyond glucose lowering, prolonged action specifically in renal impairment and in older people, and glipizide XL named as the wrong choice in an older person with CKD. He also names his reason they persist — lack of insurance coverage for better agents. That sits alongside rather than against the recorded position that they persist through inertia; both mechanisms can operate, and a deprescribing conversation that addresses only one will fail against the other.
- **The diabetes agent hierarchy gains a potency axis.** The hierarchy is ordered by safety; this episode supplies the complementary ordering for **postprandial effect — GLP-1 > SGLT2 > DPP-4.** The two orderings are compatible and answer different questions.
- **The DPP-4 failure mode is independently confirmed.** The recorded caution is that a DPP-4 does not take a resident from 9% to target. The guest reaches the same conclusion unprompted about a 9% patient: a DPP-4 is an option but "probably not going to get the A1c down enough."
- **Deintensification gains its physiology.** The recorded position that a low A1c in an older adult is a warning sign is supported here by the mechanism that makes reducing insulin *lower* the A1c: overcorrection eating, plus roughly 24 hours of insulin insensitivity after each counter-regulatory surge.
- **Over-basalization gains a detection method.** Previously this was recognized by an implausible dose-to-A1c combination. The bedtime-to-morning drop is a direct measurement, and it identifies the problem in a patient whose numbers are hyperglycemic at both ends.

**The substitution that matters most for an older population:**

- **Time in range >70% is the general-adult target, not the older-adult one — the substitution is made in Clinical Framework above.** The guest's figures (TIR >70%, time low <3%, time very low zero) are the general-population targets. For older and high-risk adults the same consensus specifies **TIR >50%, time below 70 mg/dL <1%, and time above 250 mg/dL <10%** (Battelino et al., *Diabetes Care* 2019, [10.2337/dci19-0028](https://doi.org/10.2337/dci19-0028)). Nothing in the episode is wrong; it simply is not addressed to this population. A facility program that set >70% as its quality target would be pushing toward tighter control than the deintensification position supports.

**Time-stamped, both now resolved:**

- **Detemir is gone.** The guest says it is coming off the market and that the last month of availability is imminent. Novo Nordisk discontinued it in the US at the **end of 2024**; the advice to convert to a once-daily basal is now simply the required action, not a forecast.
- **The coverage criteria have a pathway the episode does not mention.** The April 2023 change described is accurate, and current Medicare policy holds the standard as **insulin-treated OR a history of problematic hypoglycemia** — with problematic hypoglycemia defined as documented recurrent level 2 events (glucose <54 mg/dL) despite prior medication adjustment, or at least one level 3 event requiring third-party assistance (CMS Policy Article A52464). **That second limb is a coverage route for a resident who is not on insulin at all**, and it is the more relevant limb for an older population where documented severe lows are the reason to want a sensor in the first place. Device generations named in the episode (G7, Libre 3) are as of April 2024 and have not been re-verified; the 10-day versus 14-day wear distinction is the operationally durable part.

**Translation needed before this drives a facility CGM program:**

- **Almost every operational assumption here is a patient-self-management assumption**, and in a facility each one has a different owner. The patient owns the smartphone and the account; sees and responds to the alarm; journals meals and exercise; treats based on the displayed number; looks at the screen with the clinician. In a facility those map to the nurse, the med pass, the CNA, the dietary record and the medical director — and none of that reassignment happens automatically. The episode is a useful *inventory of what has to be reassigned*, which is a different thing from a protocol.
- **Alarm management becomes a unit workload question rather than a patient-annoyance question.** The failure mode described — the patient puts the alarming device in another room — becomes, in a facility, alarm fatigue distributed across staff, and the fix the guest gives (temporarily raising the high threshold) is an order someone has to write, document, and revisit as the glucose comes down.
- **The stacking hazard changes shape rather than disappearing.** In the episode the risk is a patient self-correcting repeatedly. Where correction is driven by a sliding scale on a fixed schedule, CGM visibility could prompt more frequent correction against the same four-hour rapid-acting duration. The countermeasure is the same and the responsible party is different.
- **Coverage in LTC is settled: CGMs are covered for residents** (Patrick, 2026-09-12). Neither the Glucose Monitors LCD (L33822) nor its Policy Article (A52464) addresses residential setting, so the coverage documents themselves do not answer the question — but it is answered in practice, and a program does not need to resolve it first. Two eligibility limbs are worth knowing: **insulin-treated**, which since April 2023 includes a single daily basal injection, **or a history of problematic hypoglycemia** — documented recurrent level 2 events (glucose <54 mg/dL) despite prior medication adjustment, or at least one level 3 event requiring third-party assistance. **The second limb reaches residents on no insulin at all**, and is often the more relevant one here, since documented severe lows are usually the reason to want a sensor.
- **The episode does not address sensor adhesion or skin integrity**, which is a live consideration for 10 to 14 days of continuous adhesive wear in anyone with fragile skin. A gap rather than an error, and worth resolving from another source.

## Downstream Opportunities

- **Protocol — the primary deliverable here: a facility CGM program.** This episode supplies most of the clinical content and a complete list of what has to be decided locally. The protocol needs: **older-adult time-in-range targets (TIR >50%, time below 70 mg/dL <1%, time above 250 mg/dL <10%), not the general ones**; who owns alarm response and at what thresholds; how the low alert is set above 70 to compensate for interstitial lag; how correction insulin interacts with continuous visibility; and what gets documented at the 3-month and 6-month coverage checkpoints. Worth writing the problematic-hypoglycemia eligibility limb into the protocol explicitly, since it reaches residents on no insulin and is easy to overlook.
- **Protocol — CGM ordering reference:** Dexcom 3 sensors monthly at 10-day wear, Libre 2 sensors monthly at 14-day wear, sensors through pharmacy, reader through DME, group provider portal rather than individual.
- **Protocol — professional (blinded) CGM as a distinct diagnostic order.** For a resident whose A1c and fingersticks disagree, or where the question is diagnostic rather than therapeutic, a 14-day blinded wear answers it without handing anyone a number to react to. This may be the easier first step for a facility than personal CGM, because it requires no alarm policy and no real-time response pathway.
- **Handbook — *Diabetes Management in Older Adults*:** the A1c-to-glucose conversion, the order of operations, the bedtime-to-morning drop as the over-basalization test, the counter-regulatory sequence and the 24-hour insulin insensitivity, and the regimen-aimed-at-the-wrong-time-of-day framing.
- **Handbook — *Hypoglycemia*:** the five counter-regulatory responses, why riding out a low is not acceptable, the rule of 15 with its exact quantities, and the observation that overcorrection is predictable rather than a patient failing.
- **Handbook — cross-reference from *Chronic Kidney Disease*:** prolonged sulfonylureas and prolonged basal insulin in declining renal function, as the two mechanisms behind unexplained nocturnal lows.
- **Newsletter:** "Why your A1c looks fine when your sugars aren't" — variability, the flat-line line, and the two tracings that produce the same number.
- **Newsletter:** "126 equals six" — the conversion, as a single usable tool for talking to patients in the units they actually think in.
- **Bedside Notes candidates:**
  - *"The number on the screen is fifteen minutes old"* — interstitial lag, why it only matters when glucose is moving, and the one-line fix of raising the low alert.
  - *"Look at the drop, not the number"* — bedtime-to-morning as the over-basalization test, in a patient whose readings are high at both ends.
  - *"Lower the insulin, lower the A1c"* — the counterintuitive result with both mechanisms.
  - *"The alarm you turned off"* — the 250 default, alarm fatigue, and a device abandoned for a fixable reason.
