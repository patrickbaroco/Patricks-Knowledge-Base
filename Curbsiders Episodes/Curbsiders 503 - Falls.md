## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Geriatrician Alyson Michener and physical therapist Suzanne Zukoski work a single hospitalized fall case — an 82-year-old with dementia, atrial fibrillation on apixaban, osteoarthritis, and diabetes — through the Geriatric 5Ms, then through a PT's bedside evaluation, disposition decision, and the three evidence-based arms of fall prevention. The distinctive material is the PT half: the exact bedside sequence, what each maneuver is actually testing, how assistance levels are graded and documented, and the specific home and kitchen modifications that come out of a home safety evaluation. Also here: the case against the phrase "mechanical fall," a medication analysis with two bradycardia culprits, and the current negative position on vitamin D.

## Pearls

- A fall is coming to rest unintentionally at a lower surface — the ground or the floor. That is the whole definition.
- **"Mechanical fall" should be retired.** The term has no agreed meaning: some clinicians use it to signify a fall without syncope, others use it differently, and in practice it functions as a stopping point — the clinician feels a diagnosis has been reached and the evaluation ends there. Every fall deserves a workup whether or not syncope occurred. (Guest's stated position and teaching practice.)
- "Slip" and "trip" are more useful words than "mechanical," because each implies a mechanism, and mechanism shapes the differential.
- Syncope is one possible cause of a fall, not a parallel category. The two differentials overlap. Pre-syncope makes the boundary blurrier still, and a patient with dementia often cannot say which happened — so the classification frequently cannot be made and should not gate the workup.
- Falls are a geriatric syndrome: a common clinical problem in older adults that is multifactorial by nature, arising from some combination of age-related physiologic change, chronic disease, and social or environmental factors. About a quarter of older adults fall each year.
- Geriatric syndromes do not respond to the organ-based history that medical training drills. This is why learners find falls stressful — they are searching for the one cause, and there is rarely one cause.
- **A single prior fall is the strongest predictor of future falls.** Ask about prior falls in every older adult, whatever the presenting complaint.
- Geriatric syndromes cluster. A patient presenting with one almost always has others.
- **About half of falls are never reported to a physician.** Patients assume falling is a normal part of aging, or fear that disclosing it will cost them their independence. Both assumptions have to be addressed directly for the history to be reliable.
- Assume a medication is responsible until proven otherwise. (Guest's explicit teaching rule for residents.)
- Dementia is a common and under-addressed fall risk factor. It is often noticed and then left alone, because clinicians assume nothing can be done about it. It contributes through three distinct mechanisms: impaired safety awareness, poor executive function, and altered visuospatial function.
- Depression is an independent risk factor for falls in its own right, separate from the medications used to treat it.
- Delirium from an early, still-occult infection — a URI or UTI in its first days — is a recurring path to a fall. This is the reason to ask specifically about any change in health in the days or weeks before the fall.
- A physical therapy consult is a diagnostic study, not an exercise session. The PT evaluation generates findings — orthostatic response across three positions, unsupported sitting balance, graded assistance levels, gait mechanics — that are not otherwise obtained.
- **An assistive device is not necessarily permanent.** The common assumption, by patients and clinicians, is that once a walker is issued it is issued for life. The safest gait pattern is a normal step-through gait, and returning a patient to it — walker to cane to nothing — is the goal wherever the rehab potential exists. (PT's stated position.)
- Screening balance tests answer the question "is this patient at elevated risk?" A patient already hospitalized for a fall has answered that question. Their value is in the outpatient setting, for risk stratification. (Geriatrician's framing.)
- Recommending "more exercise" or "try tai chi" without specifying a program is not a plan. Name the setting (home vs. public), the format (individual vs. group), and the supervision level (supervised vs. unsupervised), matched to the patient's fall risk.
- Balance and strength training carry the strongest evidence of any single fall-prevention intervention, and are recommended for the whole spectrum — patients who have never fallen and are at low risk, and patients recovering from a major fall alike.
- Tai chi has an evidence base across depression, low back pain, and fall prevention, appears in guidelines for each, and is close to harm-free — and is badly underused relative to that profile. (Host's observation, offered as a prompt to refer more.)
- Disposition disagreements are a Matters Most problem. A therapy recommendation is a recommendation; when a patient declines a facility, the work is to understand the objection and then make the home as safe as it can be made.

## Clinical Framework

**The Geriatric 5Ms applied to falls.** The 5Ms are used here as the substitute for an organ-based approach — a way to enumerate contributors when there is no single cause to find.

- **Multimorbidity** — the chronic conditions in play (in this case dementia, diabetes, osteoarthritis, neuropathy); prior falls; sensory impairment, both visual and auditory; and any recent change in health in the days-to-weeks preceding the fall.
- **Medications** — see the drug section below.
- **Mind** — the three D's: dementia, depression, delirium.
- **Mobility** — baseline mobility and any change from it; assistive device use; the physical space the patient must navigate; and function, meaning ADLs and IADLs. What was the patient physically doing at the moment of the fall is a mobility question.
- **Matters Most** — home environment, family and informal supports, and the patient's own goals. This is also the M that governs disposition disagreements.

**The three evidence-based arms of fall prevention.** Everything with real evidence behind it falls into one of these three:

1. **Multifactorial risk factor assessment and management** — enumerate every fall risk factor the patient has, then address each one systematically. Roughly 25% relative risk reduction in meta-analyses, with the caveat that this is a bundle of heterogeneous interventions rather than a single one, which makes it hard to measure cleanly. Indicated for the highest-risk group: anyone hospitalized for a fall or with a major fall-related injury, anyone with one or more falls in the past year, and anyone unsteady on their feet.
2. **Exercise** — specifically balance and strength training. Strongest evidence of the three. Recommended for all patients regardless of risk level.
3. **Home modifications** — roughly 23% relative risk reduction in meta-analyses.

**Levels of physical assistance, as PT grades and documents them.** These are proportions of the work done by the therapist, and they are the operative language in a PT note:

- **Independent / supervision** — no physical assistance.
- **Minimal assistance ("min A")** — about 25% therapist, 75% patient.
- **Moderate assistance ("mod A")** — about 50/50.
- **Maximal assistance ("max A")** — about 75% therapist, 25% patient.

Each is further specified as requiring one person or two. **The threshold that matters for disposition is the transition from supervision to minimal assistance** — the point at which the patient requires physical assistance to move safely.

**Rehab intensity levels.** Skilled nursing facility (also called subacute rehab) is documented as **less than three hours per day of inpatient skilled therapy**. Acute inpatient rehab is **more than three hours per day**. This is the operative distinction in the PT's recommendation.

**Detail on arm 1 — the assessment is also the script for the conversation.** Once the multifactorial risk assessment is complete, go through what was found, and pair each finding with what is being recommended about it. This does two things at once: it makes the plan concrete, and it directly contradicts the belief that falling is simply what happens with age and nothing can be done — which is the belief driving the roughly half of falls that never get reported.

**Detail on arm 2 — what the exercise prescription looks like.**

**Tai chi**, specifically the *Tai Chi for Arthritis and Fall Prevention* program (the PT is a certified instructor), is the named exercise program with evidence behind it. It trains movement together with conscious awareness of where the body is in space during weight shifting, giving it a mind component alongside the physical one. It is fully scalable: start seated if standing is not tolerated; progress to standing with a walker while minimizing the stepping components and working the arms and standing tolerance; then walker to cane, and cane to nothing where possible.

**The exercise-prescription ladder** for a patient like this one: home exercise supervised by a physical therapist immediately post-discharge → outpatient physical therapy → a class at a senior center → an online video. Match the rung to current fall risk and independence, and name a specific program rather than a category.

**Detail on arm 3 — what a home modification actually consists of.**

- **Occupational therapists perform home safety evaluations**, and they can be ordered at discharge for a patient going home. The OT visits, evaluates, and makes recommendations — including for durable medical equipment, which has evidence behind it: grab bars, shower chairs, stair glides.
- **CDC STEADI** is the named resource, and its one-page home safety checklist is printable and can be handed to a patient or family to walk their own home with.
- **Kitchen reorganization by height.** Items used most often go at the easiest level to reach; items used least go on the lowest and highest shelves. This directly spares the knees and back in a patient with knee osteoarthritis. People resist rearranging a kitchen they have organized the same way for decades — and are consistently pleased with it a week later.
- **Sit down to chop and prep** rather than standing through it.
- **Slide, don't carry.** Move plates along the counter to the point nearest the table, so the carrying distance is a turn rather than a walk across the room. Carry one item with two hands rather than two items with one hand each.
- **Area rugs** — particularly at the base of the sink and under the microwave.

## Drug and Dosing Detail

**High-risk medication classes for falls:**

- Psychoactive medications broadly — antidepressants, antipsychotics, benzodiazepines. Most appear on the Beers list.
- Opioid analgesics.
- Loop diuretics — classically associated with falls.
- **Any medication, via over-treatment.** Antihypertensives when the blood pressure target is lower than it needs to be; diabetes medications when the glycemic target is tighter than it needs to be. The risk here is not the drug class but the target being aimed at. (Guest's framing, offered as the more useful way to think about it than a fixed high-risk list.)

**Over-the-counter and unlisted agents to ask about by name:**

- **Diphenhydramine.** It is bought over the counter, so it does not appear on the medication list, and patients frequently take it alongside a second-generation antihistamine they were prescribed — loratadine plus diphenhydramine is the common pattern. It has to be asked about specifically, every time. Essentially no older adult should be on it. (Host's practice point, endorsed by the guest.)
- **Cannabinoids — CBD and THC in any form.**
- **Alcohol.** Ask quantity, not just yes/no; a ten-ounce glass of wine is a meaningful dose against a background of polypharmacy and impaired balance. A partner or family member present in the room will often supply the real answer.

Seeing the patient at home — the medicine cabinet, the countertop — surfaces these agents directly rather than by report. (Guest practices home-based primary care and flags this as an advantage of the setting, not a requirement.)

**The medications on this case's list, and how they were read:**

Apixaban, amlodipine, metoprolol, metformin, glipizide, citalopram, donepezil.

- **Metoprolol and donepezil are the two that stand out**, both for bradycardia, in a patient with documented heart rates in the 50s on 48 hours of telemetry.
- **Donepezil causes bradycardia** — severely enough in reported cases to lead to pacemaker placement. It is also frequently not doing much, and carries its own side effect burden, which makes it a defensible first deprescribing target here on both counts at once.
- **Metoprolol** can be backed off depending on the indication it is being carried for.
- **Glipizide with a hemoglobin A1c of 6.5%** in an 82-year-old is the over-treatment pattern described above — a sulfonylurea achieving a target tighter than this patient needs.

**Vitamin D — negative pearl.** Vitamin D does not prevent falls and should not be given for fall prevention in all comers. There is some thought that supplementing an older adult who has a genuinely low level may help, but the optimal level is itself debated. Supplement a low level because it is low, not as a falls intervention. (This is a stated change from earlier practice, when vitamin D was expected to do far more than it does.)

**Protein and diet — no useful evidence for falls.** What evidence exists addresses sarcopenia, and it has not been tied directly to fall outcomes.

**Anticoagulation after a fall.** The default in the hospital is to continue it, absent an immediate contraindication. The decision to stop is nuanced and belongs to a clinician who knows the patient: it turns on overall trajectory, the patient's goals, and their tolerance for competing risks. HAS-BLED and CHA₂DS₂-VASc can both be calculated, but the numbers are not the useful part of this particular decision. What the inpatient team owes the outpatient team is notification that a fall occurred and confirmed follow-up. (Guest's explicit practice: she does not attempt this conversation in the hospital with a patient she does not know.)

**Exercise dosing for fall prevention** (host's summary of the strength-training literature, not the guest's prescription): two to three sessions per week, at 50–80% of one-repetition maximum, six to twelve repetitions per set, accumulating roughly 50 total hours of training — on the order of a twelve-week program.

## Diagnostic and Exam Detail

**History questions the PT asks that the medical history usually misses:**

- How many steps to get into the house.
- **Which side the railing is on — right or left.** This matters whenever there is an upper extremity fracture or hemiplegia, and it is almost never asked.
- Is the bedroom on the first or second floor; is there a railing on the stairs to the second floor.
- Is there a stair glide. **The presence of a stair glide is itself a finding** — it means mobility difficulty predated this fall.
- Bathroom setup: walk-in shower or tub, shower chair, grab bars present or absent.
- **The two highest-risk locations in a house are the stairs and the bathroom.**
- Reconstruct the mechanics of the fall itself in the room where it happened: was she carrying two plates at once when she could have carried one item with two hands, or kept a hand on the counter? Are there area rugs — at the base of the sink, under the microwave?

**The PT bedside evaluation, in sequence.** Each step is doing a specific job; the sequence is built around a patient who has not been out of bed since arriving on the floor.

1. **Blood pressure supine.** Orthostatics get done whether or not they were ordered — this became standing practice in the medically complex older adult.
2. **Ask the patient to sit at the edge of the bed.** Two things are being observed simultaneously: whether the patient can follow the direction at all, and whether the transfer has to be broken into components (roll, legs over the side, push up to sitting) or can be done as one movement without prompting.
3. **Sitting unsupported, hands in the lap.** Then lift one leg against gravity and hold it, then the other. **This tests strength and balance in one maneuver** — the leg lift is a strength test, but whether the patient reaches for the bed or lists to the side while doing it is the balance finding.
4. **Focused sensory exam** where neuropathy is in the history, plus a look at what is on the patient's feet.
5. **Blood pressure again after one to two minutes sitting**, with a symptom check.
6. **Sit to stand with a rolling walker** — front wheels, positioned in front of the patient before the attempt. Grade the assistance required.
7. **Blood pressure again standing.** How far to go from here is decided in the moment on how the patient looks: a few steps to a chair, to the door of the room, or out into the hallway.

**Gait observation:**

- How antalgic is the gait.
- **Can the patient fully extend both knees during stance?** Walking with the knees flexed under load indicates range-of-motion and strength deficits — and is separately a very inefficient way to walk, which matters in a patient with limited energy to spend.

**Standardized balance and mobility tests** — Berg Balance Scale, Five Times Sit to Stand, 30-Second Sit to Stand, Timed Up and Go:

- The PT generally does **not** run these on an initial evaluation of a cognitively impaired patient. The Berg in particular asks for a stack of complex directions — stand on one foot, tap the feet on a step stool, pick a pen up off the floor — and a patient who gets flustered and frustrated at that stage will not participate later. Establishing trust outranks getting a score on day one. They can be added at a subsequent session, or used up front in a patient who is clearly higher-functioning.
- **Timed Up and Go** is, in principle, a test performed without an assistive device. It can be done with one as long as that is documented.
- Five Times Sit to Stand is the PT's preferred test when the patient can do it.

**Targeted workup after a fall.** The testing follows the history and exam rather than running as a fixed panel:

- **EKG and telemetry** where there is a rhythm history — here, atrial fibrillation, to see whether the rhythm or rate changed in a way that could have contributed.
- **Echocardiogram only if there were prodromal cardiac symptoms** such as chest pain. It is not indicated for every fall, even in a patient with significant cardiac history.
- **Focused musculoskeletal exam** of any painful or previously operated joint, with PT for the gait detail.
- **Chart review of dementia status** — how advanced, and could it plausibly have contributed.
- **Hemoglobin A1c plus inpatient glucose values** to establish whether diabetes is over- or under-controlled, particularly when the fall history is unobtainable.

## Errors and Misconceptions

- **"Mechanical fall" as a diagnosis.** The single error the episode is built around: the term ends the evaluation without explaining anything.
- **Leading the witness on history.** "You didn't hit your head, right?" and its variants are asked in a form that invites the reassuring answer, which conveniently spares the clinician a new problem to work up. Ask it neutrally. (Host's observation about his own precepting.)
- **Treating dementia as a fall risk factor that cannot be acted on.** It gets recognized and then skipped over.
- **Not asking about diphenhydramine** — or about cannabinoids and alcohol — because they are not on the medication list.
- **Believing that falls are a normal consequence of aging.** Held by patients, and the direct cause of the roughly half of falls that go unreported.
- **Assuming an assistive device is permanent.**
- **Running screening balance tests on a patient already hospitalized for a fall.** The screening question is already answered; the tests are for outpatient risk stratification.
- **Giving vitamin D for fall prevention.**
- **Expecting a PT consult to be an exercise session** rather than an evaluation that produces diagnostic findings.
- **Deciding anticoagulation in the hospital**, with a patient the team does not know, on the basis of risk scores.

## Case Examples

**"Faye L. Down," 82 years old** — the episode's single worked case, carried from ED presentation through hospital course, disposition, and primary care follow-up.

*Background:* type 2 diabetes with neuropathy and retinopathy; osteoarthritis, status post right total knee replacement in 2021; hypertension; atrial fibrillation on apixaban; anxiety; mild dementia. Lives in a two-story Philadelphia row home with her adult son, who works outside the home during the day. He moved in a year ago, noticed cognitive changes, took her to her PCP, and has since taken over some IADLs out of concern about complex tasks — but she still cooks. Home: five steps to enter, half bath on the first floor, full bathroom on the second with a walk-in shower, a shower chair, a shower curtain, and no grab bars; a stair glide inside; a borrowed rollator used only for longer distances outdoors.

*The fall:* heating frozen dinners for herself and her son in the kitchen; her feet went out from under her; she found herself sitting on the floor. No definite loss of consciousness, no known head strike, no recall of the events around the fall. Her son heard a thump from upstairs and found her on the floor. She got up with assistance. Her cardiologist had instructed evaluation after any fall given anticoagulation, so they called 911.

*ED and hospital:* CBC and BMP at baseline, chest x-ray unremarkable, head CT with no acute abnormality but chronic ischemic white matter changes. Acetaminophen for pain, admitted for fall workup. On the floor: heart rates in the 50s, **negative orthostatics**, right knee pain with ambulation, bruising over the buttocks. EKG showed atrial fibrillation; 48 hours of telemetry showed atrial fibrillation with rates sometimes into the 50s. Hemoglobin A1c 6.5%, other labs unremarkable.

*PT evaluation:* minimal assistance (25%) for bed mobility, transfers, and ambulation with a rolling walker for 16 feet. Mobility limited by aggravated osteoarthritis, neuropathy, and fear of falling.

*Disposition reasoning:* she requires physical assistance to move safely; her son is out of the house all day and reachable only by phone; the stair glide shows mobility difficulty predating this admission; there are five steps between her and every follow-up appointment. Recommendation: skilled nursing facility for less than three hours daily of skilled therapy — an hour of PT and an hour of OT — with speech therapy available on evaluation, which in the rehab setting addresses cognition and not only swallowing. On return home, PT plus OT for home safety and community mobility.

*Identified fall risk factors, as the assessment and plan should list them:* bradycardia; multiple contributing medications; dementia; osteoarthritis; possibly over-tight diabetes control; home environment factors. The documented etiology of the fall is this list, and each item then gets a stated intervention.

*Outpatient follow-up:* returns to the office with her son, ambulating with the rolling walker with a steady gait.

## Conflicts with Established Positions

**No direct conflicts identified.** Nothing here contradicts the committed positions carried elsewhere (anticoagulation, NSAIDs, sulfonylureas, clonidine/hydralazine, hospice threshold, thickened liquids, fall rate, EOL symptom management). Several reinforce, and one needs translation before it is usable in LTC.

**Reinforcements worth carrying forward:**

- **Fall rate.** This episode is the evidence base for that position, with the numbers attached: multifactorial risk factor assessment and management ~25% relative risk reduction, home modification ~23%, and balance-and-strength exercise carrying the strongest evidence of the three. Any fall-rate commitment can now cite which arm it is pulling on.
- **Sulfonylureas.** Glipizide with an A1c of 6.5% in an 82-year-old is exactly the pattern the sulfonylurea position exists to prevent, and this episode supplies the fall-specific argument for it — over-treatment is a fall mechanism, not only a hypoglycemia mechanism. That is an additional, independent line of reasoning for the same conclusion.
- **Anticoagulation.** The default of continuing anticoagulation after a fall, and of making any stop decision in an ongoing relationship rather than in an acute admission, aligns with the existing position and adds a procedural point: what the acute setting owes is notification and confirmed follow-up, not a decision.
- **Polypharmacy and deprescribing.** Donepezil as a first deprescribing target — justified simultaneously by a specific adverse effect (bradycardia) and by limited efficacy — is a clean worked example of the two-reasons-at-once pattern that makes a deprescribing conversation persuasive to a family.
- **Vitamin D.** The negative position on vitamin D for fall prevention converges with the mega-bolus dosing finding carried in [[Curbsiders 534 - Supplements in Primary Care]]. Together they close off vitamin D as a fall intervention from both ends: it does not help at ordinary doses, and it harms at large intermittent ones.

**Translation needed before this is usable in LTC** — flagged as a gap in applicability rather than an error:

- **The risk stratification collapses.** "Highest risk" here means hospitalized for a fall, a fall in the past year, or unsteadiness on the feet. In a long-term care population nearly every resident meets at least one criterion, so the stratification adds nothing and the entire population qualifies for multifactorial assessment. The useful consequence is that LTC should skip the screening tests altogether and go straight to the intervention arms — which is the opposite of how fall-risk scoring is typically deployed in facilities.
- **The exercise arm has no default delivery vehicle in LTC.** Every option offered — outpatient PT, a senior center class, a home video — presumes a community-dwelling patient. In a facility the equivalent is restorative nursing, and a seated tai chi program is one of the few named, scalable, evidence-supported options that fits.
- **The home modification arm becomes an environmental modification the resident does not control.** Grab bar placement, rug removal, and reach heights are facility decisions. This makes the OT home safety evaluation's content more valuable in LTC than the process — the checklist items translate; the visit does not.
- **The three-hour rule is a hospital discharge distinction**, not an LTC one, and does not transfer.

## Downstream Opportunities

- **Handbook:** *Falls in LTC* — the 5Ms structure gives the chapter its spine, and the medication section, the risk factor enumeration, and the intervention arms with their effect sizes are all directly usable. The LTC translation notes above should be written into the chapter rather than left implicit.
- **Handbook:** a *Working with Physical and Occupational Therapy* section — the assistance-level definitions (min/mod/max A, one vs. two person), the three-hour rehab intensity distinction, and what a PT evaluation actually contains are exactly the things physicians misread in therapy notes. This is genuinely novel material for a physician-facing handbook.
- **Handbook:** cross-reference from *Polypharmacy and Deprescribing* (donepezil and bradycardia; over-treatment as a fall mechanism) and from *Dizziness and Vertigo in LTC* (orthostatics before anything else).
- **Newsletter:** "Stop saying mechanical fall" — short, counterintuitive to a general audience, and a clean argument about how language forecloses thinking.
- **Newsletter:** the kitchen modifications — reorganize by height, sit to chop, slide the plate instead of carrying it — are immediately actionable, family-facing, cost nothing, and require no clinician.
- **Newsletter:** "Your walker isn't forever" — the rehab-potential point, which patients rarely hear.
- **Newsletter:** the diphenhydramine-plus-loratadine pattern as a household medicine cabinet audit.
- **Protocol:** a **post-fall evaluation protocol** — the neutral history questions (no leading the witness), orthostatics in three positions, medication review with the named high-risk classes plus the OTC and substance questions, and the targeted rather than reflexive workup.
- **Protocol:** a **post-fall risk factor checklist** that produces the enumerated list the assessment and plan should contain, with a required intervention paired to each item.
- **Protocol:** an **environmental safety walkthrough** for resident rooms, adapted from the CDC STEADI home safety checklist.
