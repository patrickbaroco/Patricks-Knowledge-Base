## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Kerry Reynolds, who directs the first dedicated inpatient service for severe immunotherapy toxicity, teaches recognition and management of immune-related adverse events (irAEs) from immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) through three cases: grade 3 colitis on ipilimumab plus nivolumab that proves steroid-refractory, pembrolizumab-associated hypophysitis presenting as fatigue and hyponatremia, and the myositis–myocarditis–myasthenia ("Triple M") overlap syndrome. The episode is strongest on timing of onset by organ, a head-to-toe screening panel, steroid dosing and taper rules, prophylaxis during prolonged steroids, and the evolving evidence on rechallenge.

## Pearls

- Ask every patient with cancer whether they have ever received immunotherapy or an immune checkpoint inhibitor, and obtain the oncology history. Patients on immunotherapy routinely describe it as "chemotherapy," and the start date is the single most useful piece of history for judging whether a new symptom could be an irAE.
- ICIs activate the immune system; chemotherapy suppresses it. The inflammation that produces a cancer response can also inflame any healthy organ, and the presentations resemble the autoimmune diseases internists already know.
- Over 15 ICIs are now FDA approved, and more than 50% of patients with cancer are eligible for one. Memorizing agents is unnecessary. Look up the specific drug, and establish whether it was given as monotherapy or in combination: two ICIs together carry a higher risk of toxicity.
- Most irAEs present within the first six months of therapy, but late events are common. In a Mass General Brigham series of 795 irAE hospitalizations, 14% occurred 6 to 12 months after ICI initiation and 10% after more than a year.
- irAEs can occur after the ICI has been stopped. The monoclonal antibody remains bound to T cells, so a patient who finished therapy months earlier is still at risk.
- Think head to toe, and when one irAE is found, look for others.
- Diarrhea after an ICI may not originate in the gut. ICI thyroiditis can cause profound hyperthyroid diarrhea, and new-onset celiac disease occurs after ICI exposure. TSH, free T4, and tissue transglutaminase IgA belong in every ICI diarrhea workup.
- Approximately 60% of patients with ICI colitis respond to steroids alone; 30–40% need second-line immunosuppression. Plan for second-line therapy at presentation by sending hepatitis B serologies and a T-SPOT (interferon-gamma release assay for tuberculosis) on day one, so infliximab is not delayed days later waiting on results.
- In grade 3 colitis that has not improved on IV steroids by about three days, move to second-line immunosuppression rather than waiting longer.
- Second-line therapy for ICI colitis is highly effective: remission in roughly 88–89% with either infliximab or vedolizumab. Unlike inflammatory bowel disease, ICI colitis usually resolves; conversion to a chronic course is rare.
- Any glucocorticoid course longer than four weeks at more than 20 mg/day of prednisone (or equivalent) requires prophylaxis: *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis, GI protection, and bone protection.
- Check a baseline calcium before adding calcium supplementation at discharge. Patients with cancer may already be hypercalcemic.
- Retrospective melanoma data suggest that patients who develop a severe irAE and cannot continue immunotherapy have progression-free and overall survival comparable to those who continue. A severe toxicity does not, by itself, mean the cancer treatment has failed.
- Do not rechallenge with an ICI while the patient is on more than 10 mg/day of prednisone. Steroids at that dose are expected to blunt the immune activation the ICI depends on.
- A fatigued patient on an ICI should get a morning cortisol, a CK, and a troponin in addition to the CBC, metabolic panel, and TSH. CK and troponin are the tests most often missed.
- Obtain a baseline troponin before starting an ICI. A troponin of 55 ng/L during an illness is uninterpretable without one and is reassuring if it was 55 at baseline.
- A TSH with reflex testing misses central hypothyroidism, because the TSH can be normal or low. Order TSH and free T4 together in any patient on an ICI.
- Adrenal insufficiency after an ICI is almost always secondary, from hypophysitis. Primary adrenalitis is rare enough to be case-reportable. ACTH distinguishes the two.
- Ipilimumab-associated hypophysitis and PD-1/PD-L1-associated hypophysitis are different diseases. Ipilimumab produces a more fulminant picture with headache, pituitary enlargement on MRI, and loss of both the ACTH and TSH axes; PD-1/PD-L1 agents mostly produce isolated central adrenal insufficiency with a preserved thyroid axis.
- Treat hypophysitis with physiologic glucocorticoid replacement. High-dose steroids do not rescue pituitary function, and in melanoma, patients given high-dose steroids for hypophysitis had worse progression-free and overall survival than those given replacement doses.
- Most ICI endocrinopathies are permanent: thyroid dysfunction, hypophysitis, and type 1 diabetes generally require lifelong hormone replacement or insulin.
- Unrecognized adrenal insufficiency can surface as an adrenal crisis under surgical stress. Diagnosing it before surgery matters most in patients receiving neoadjuvant immunotherapy.
- Recent glucocorticoid premedication for chemotherapy confounds cortisol and ACTH testing. Establish the timing of the last steroid dose before interpreting either result.
- Diplopia and muscle weakness after an ICI should be treated as an oncologic emergency.
- When myositis is suspected, check CK and troponin together; Reynolds says them as a single test name ("troponin-CK"). The heart is a muscle, and myositis, myocarditis, and a myasthenia-like syndrome cluster as the Triple M overlap.
- In suspected Triple M, call respiratory therapy first for negative inspiratory force (NIF) and forced vital capacity (FVC). Neuromuscular junction involvement can progress to respiratory failure quickly in a patient who is still talking comfortably.
- A normal ejection fraction does not exclude ICI myocarditis. In the first large myocarditis series, 51% of patients had a normal EF.
- ICI myocarditis occurs in about 1% of patients, but mortality for the full overlap syndrome reaches 40–60%.
- Cardiac and neurologic irAEs are the two most fatal categories, and both are treated with pulse-dose methylprednisolone, 1 g daily for three to five days.
- No biomarker currently predicts who will develop an irAE. Urinary CXCL9 is an investigational marker that distinguishes ICI acute interstitial nephritis from other causes of acute kidney injury.
- Four sets of irAE guidelines exist; the newest is the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guideline dedicated to immune-related adverse events, which is free online and includes condensed summary slides.
- Most patients on immunotherapy do well, and the benefit is measured in real survival gains. The task for the non-oncologist is recognition; management detail can be looked up.

## Clinical Framework

### Mechanism, briefly

ICIs release inhibitory "brakes" on T cells, targeting PD-1 (nivolumab, pembrolizumab), PD-L1 (atezolizumab), or CTLA-4 (ipilimumab). The resulting immune activation drives tumor response and, in a minority of patients, inflammation of healthy tissue. CTLA-4 is expressed on the pituitary gland, which may explain ipilimumab's high rate of hypophysitis as a direct on-target effect.

### Timing of onset by organ system

Median time from ICI initiation, as given by the guest:

| Organ system | Median onset |
|---|---|
| Skin | Earliest; after one or two doses |
| Cardiac (myocarditis) | About 4 weeks |
| Neurologic | About 6 weeks |
| GI (colitis) | About 6–8 weeks |
| Endocrine, liver, renal | Within about 4 months |

Most events fall within six months, but 14% of irAE hospitalizations in the Mass General Brigham series (Sienna Durbin and colleagues, 795 cases) occurred at 6–12 months and 10% beyond one year. The reason for delayed presentations is not understood. Events can occur after therapy has ended.

### Severity grading: CTCAE

irAEs are graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), which is free and publicly available. For diarrhea, stool frequency above the patient's baseline:

| Grade | Definition |
|---|---|
| 1 | Fewer than 4 stools/day above baseline (guest: "about four") |
| 2 | 4–6 stools/day above baseline |
| 3 | 7 or more stools/day above baseline |
| 4 | Life-threatening; for example, ICU-level hypotension from hypovolemia or severe hypokalemia |

The treatment goal in colitis is return to below grade 1.

### Steroid-refractory definitions (SITC)

The Society for Immunotherapy of Cancer (SITC) definitions are the ones Reynolds encourages clinicians to anchor on:

- **Steroid-unresponsive:** never improves on steroids.
- **Steroid-resistant:** improves, then worsens again on steroids.
- **Steroid-dependent:** improves but cannot be tapered; the course becomes chronic.

### Head-to-toe screen for the patient on an ICI who "isn't doing well"

Before attributing fatigue, nausea, or weakness to chemotherapy, disease progression, insomnia, or depression, screen for the common irAEs by organ:

| Target | Test | What it detects |
|---|---|---|
| Pituitary (hypophysitis) | Morning cortisol; TSH **and** free T4; ACTH if cortisol low | Central adrenal insufficiency, central hypothyroidism |
| Thyroid | TSH, free T4 | Thyroiditis (hyper- then hypothyroid phases) |
| Pancreas | Glucose on metabolic panel | New-onset type 1 diabetes (up to ~1%) |
| Kidney | Creatinine | ICI nephritis |
| Liver | Liver enzymes | ICI hepatitis |
| Blood | CBC, hemoglobin | Hemolytic anemia, other cytopenias |
| Skeletal muscle | CK | Myositis |
| Heart | Troponin (compare to baseline); ECG | Myocarditis |
| Skin | Skin examination | Dermatitis |
| Nervous system | Neurologic examination | Neuropathy, myasthenia-like syndrome |

### irAEs that travel together

- Endocrine and cutaneous irAEs overlap most often (Semenov, *Lancet Oncology*).
- In Jarushka Naidoo's work on co-occurring irAEs, thyroiditis paired with many others: pneumonitis, hepatitis, dermatitis. Reynolds notes this may partly reflect how common thyroiditis is.
- The one overlap syndrome that must not be missed is Triple M: myositis, myocarditis, and a myasthenia-like syndrome.

### Patient education at ICI initiation

The first visit at Reynolds's institution runs about the length of this episode (roughly an hour). Some disease centers add a separate teaching visit with a nurse or advanced practice provider; collaborating centers use pharmacists. The content: ICIs activate rather than suppress the immune system; any organ can be affected; events range from mild to serious; and patients should call for any new symptom or significant change, specifically worsening diarrhea, worsening dyspnea, or a progressive rash, rather than waiting for the next scheduled visit, which may be three weeks away.

### Multidisciplinary model for severe irAEs

Severe cases require oncology, hospital medicine, cardiology (often cardio-oncology), and neurology in active communication. In Triple M, neurology follows weakness and respiratory status while cardiology follows troponin and rhythm, and decisions about escalation depend on both.

### Guidelines

Four guideline sets exist. The newest is the NCCN guideline on management of immunotherapy-related toxicities, free online.

## Drug and Dosing Detail

### Checkpoint inhibitors named in the episode

- **Nivolumab (Opdivo)**: PD-1 inhibitor.
- **Pembrolizumab (Keytruda)**: PD-1 inhibitor.
- **Atezolizumab (Tecentriq)**: PD-L1 inhibitor.
- **Ipilimumab (Yervoy)**: CTLA-4 inhibitor. Higher risk of colitis than PD-1 agents, and hypophysitis in upwards of 10% of patients, particularly at higher doses.
- **Combination therapy** (ipilimumab plus nivolumab) carries a higher overall irAE risk than monotherapy.
- **Trend:** ICIs are increasingly given in combination and in earlier lines of therapy, including neoadjuvant regimens. KEYNOTE-522 for early-stage triple-negative breast cancer combines pembrolizumab with carboplatin and paclitaxel, followed by doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide, before surgery.

### ICI colitis

**First line: glucocorticoids**
- Steroids remain the mainstay of treatment in 2026.
- **Grade 3:** IV methylprednisolone **1–2 mg/kg/day**, to gain control quickly.
- Empiric steroids before endoscopy are acceptable when suspicion is high and endoscopy is not quickly available. The cost is diagnostic: if the patient then fails to improve, the lack of a tissue diagnosis makes escalation to a biologic harder to justify.

**Escalation**
- Escalate at about **three days** without improvement on IV steroids in a grade 3 patient.
- **Infliximab** (TNF-alpha inhibitor): the usual first choice at Mass General Brigham. Some patients need a single dose; others need the standard induction at **day 0, week 2, and week 6**; a few need longer courses.
- **Vedolizumab** (alpha-4 beta-7 integrin inhibitor; blocks T-cell trafficking into the gut).
- **Comparative data (guest-cited study, 184 patients):** remission about **89% with vedolizumab** and **88% with infliximab**. Vedolizumab was associated with shorter steroid courses, fewer hospitalizations, and shorter length of stay, but slower time to clinical response and much higher cost. The study also examined whether patients failing one agent responded to the other.
- **Pre-biologic screening:** hepatitis B serologies and T-SPOT, sent at presentation.

**Steroid taper** (practice habit; Reynolds stresses there is no standard)
- Reduce by about **10 mg every 5–7 days**; "about 10 mg every five days" is the common shorthand for many irAEs.
- After infliximab with a good response, aim to be off steroids within **4–6 weeks**.

### Prophylaxis during prolonged glucocorticoids

Threshold: **more than 4 weeks** at **more than 20 mg/day** prednisone equivalent.

| Prophylaxis | Usual agent | Nephrology concern | Alternative | Counterpoint |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PJP | Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole | Higher incidence of ICI nephritis | Atovaquone | Infectious diseases: atovaquone is less effective PJP prophylaxis |
| GI protection | Omeprazole (PPI) | PPIs cause acute interstitial nephritis and increase ICI nephritis risk | Famotidine | PPIs are more effective GI protection |
| Bone | Calcium (with vitamin D) | n/a | n/a | Check baseline calcium; withhold if hypercalcemic |

Reynolds sides with nephrology when she can. Taranto chose kidney protection for a patient with possible ICI acute interstitial nephritis.

### Hypophysitis and central adrenal insufficiency

- **Physiologic replacement:** hydrocortisone **15–25 mg/day** in divided doses, or prednisone **5 mg once daily** for simplicity.
- **Sick but not in adrenal crisis at presentation:** double the replacement dose for the first couple of days.
- **Adrenal crisis:** standard stress-dose glucocorticoid treatment.
- **Avoid high-dose steroids:** they do not rescue pituitary function and were associated with worse progression-free and overall survival in melanoma.
- **Central hypothyroidism alongside central adrenal insufficiency:** start hydrocortisone before levothyroxine. Thyroid replacement first accelerates cortisol clearance and can precipitate adrenal crisis.
- **Sick-day rules, stress dosing, and a medical alert bracelet** are required. The surgical team must know about the diagnosis and have an explicit perioperative steroid plan.
- **Duration:** expect lifelong replacement.

### Triple M overlap (myositis, myocarditis, myasthenia-like syndrome)

- **Pulse-dose methylprednisolone 1,000 mg/day for 3–5 days** once the diagnosis is made (for example, a positive cardiac MRI with the overlap syndrome). The same pulse dosing applies to many severe neurologic irAEs.
- **IVIG** is added, usually at neurology's recommendation, when respiratory status is compromised or the myositis or myasthenia-like component is significant.
- **Transition** to oral prednisone **1–1.5 mg/kg/day**. Rising troponin or CK during the transition means steroids alone are insufficient.
- **Mycophenolate** can be added in a stable patient (normal EF, no arrhythmia), but takes up to about **two weeks** to work.
- **Faster escalation** for patients needing urgent intervention: abatacept (a CTLA-4 agonist that "puts the brakes back on" the checkpoint) and JAK inhibitors were used in the rechallenge case below. Other escalation options in the literature include rituximab and plasmapheresis. Active research is under way, given mortality above 40%.

### Rechallenge

- Prerequisites: prednisone **below 10 mg/day** and the toxicity substantially improved.
- Decision depends on restaging scans and whether other cancer therapy is available.
- **Recurrence risk on rechallenge after a grade 3 event: at least about 30%** (data vary).
- After combination-therapy toxicity, consider rechallenge with the PD-1 agent alone, dropping ipilimumab, which carries the higher colitis risk.
- **Investigational:** rechallenge with concurrent biologic, giving infliximab or vedolizumab alongside the ICI.
- After severe myocarditis or neurologic toxicity, rechallenge is rare even at expert centers.

### Negative pearls

- High-dose glucocorticoids for hypophysitis: no benefit to the pituitary and a cancer-outcome signal of harm.
- Pituitary MRI is not routinely needed for PD-1-associated hypophysitis without mass-effect symptoms.
- CT abdomen does not rule out ICI colitis; absence of bowel wall thickening does not change the need for endoscopy.

## Diagnostic and Exam Detail

### ICI colitis

- **Stool studies:** *C. difficile*, stool culture or bacterial PCR panel, and viral testing including adenovirus and COVID-19.
- **Fecal calprotectin** as an inflammatory marker.
- **TSH and free T4:** hyperthyroid phase of thyroiditis can cause profound diarrhea.
- **Tissue transglutaminase IgA:** new-onset celiac disease after ICI.
- **Endoscopy with biopsy** (colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy): the diagnostic standard, providing histopathologic confirmation.
- **CT abdomen/pelvis:** often obtained, rarely diagnostic. Indicated specifically when there is abdominal pain or fever, both atypical for ICI colitis, to exclude abscess or another complication.
- **Severity markers at presentation:** stools above baseline, nocturnal diarrhea, blood, tachycardia, hypokalemia, rising creatinine.
- **Hepatitis B serologies and T-SPOT** at presentation, in anticipation of biologic therapy.

### Hypophysitis and adrenal insufficiency

- **Morning cortisol:** ideally drawn between **6 and 10 AM**, the window allowed by the endocrine consensus guideline. A morning cortisol **below 3 mcg/dL** is highly concerning for adrenal insufficiency.
- **ACTH:** distinguishes secondary (low or inappropriately normal ACTH) from primary (markedly elevated ACTH) adrenal insufficiency.
- **TSH with free T4:** in central hypothyroidism, TSH is normal or low and free T4 is low. Reflex testing that runs T4 only when TSH is abnormal will miss it.
- **Hyponatremia** is a common accompanying finding (129 mmol/L in the case).
- **Timing of recent glucocorticoid premedication** must be known before interpreting cortisol or ACTH. When the interval is short, coordinate with endocrinology on when testing is valid and whether to treat empirically in the meantime.
- **Pituitary MRI indications:** symptoms of mass effect (headache, visual change); posterior pituitary involvement, namely arginine vasopressin (AVP) deficiency, which is atypical for ICI hypophysitis; or florid multiple anterior pituitary hormone deficiencies. Its purpose is to exclude a sellar metastasis or another atypical cause; confirming pituitary inflammation adds little.

### Triple M overlap

- **Exam:** proximal muscle weakness (difficulty rising from a chair), bilateral ptosis (from inflammation of the ocular muscles), diplopia.
- **CK and troponin** together.
- **ECG.**
- **Respiratory therapy at the bedside for NIF and FVC.** These are measured in non-ventilated, conversant patients. The phrenic nerve innervates the diaphragm; poor diaphragmatic excursion lowers NIF and FVC. A concerning NIF changes triage: the patient may not belong on a general medical or oncology floor and needs closer neurologic monitoring.
- **Markers of a very sick patient:** CK above 8,000, troponin above 5,000, poor NIF.
- **Echocardiogram:** does not exclude myocarditis. In Tomas Neilan's original series, 51% of patients with ICI myocarditis had a normal EF; in the cardiac MRI cohort, about 60% of patients with confirmed myocarditis had a normal EF.
- **Cardiac MRI** is part of the diagnostic workup, but a normal study does not exclude myocarditis.
- Serial troponin, CK, BNP, and echocardiography are used to monitor treatment response and, in the rare rechallenge, recurrence.

### Investigational

- **Urinary CXCL9** (Shruti Gupta, Meghan Sise, and colleagues; urine proteomics): distinguishes ICI acute interstitial nephritis from other causes of AKI. Not clinically available.
- Other areas of biomarker discovery: baseline and serial immune profiling, genetics and HLA type, the microbiome, and organ-specific molecules.

## Errors and Misconceptions

- **Accepting "chemotherapy" as the oncology history.** Patients on ICIs often call it chemotherapy. Ask specifically about immunotherapy and check the treatment record.
- **Assuming the risk window has closed** because the patient is beyond six months of therapy or has stopped the ICI.
- **Attributing fatigue, nausea, and mild hyponatremia to chemotherapy, depression, or progression** without first screening for irAEs.
- **Omitting CK and troponin** from the screen of a weak or fatigued patient on an ICI.
- **Ordering TSH with reflex** and missing central hypothyroidism.
- **Interpreting a cortisol or ACTH without knowing when the last dexamethasone premedication was given.**
- **Treating hypophysitis with high-dose steroids** in hopes of rescuing the pituitary.
- **Starting levothyroxine before hydrocortisone** in combined central hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency.
- **Missing adrenal insufficiency before surgery** in a patient on neoadjuvant immunotherapy; the first sign may be refractory intraoperative hypotension.
- **Waiting a week before escalating** steroid-refractory grade 3 colitis. Three days is the threshold.
- **Failing to send hepatitis B and T-SPOT at presentation**, then delaying infliximab while results return.
- **Relying on CT** to rule out ICI colitis.
- **Being reassured by a normal ejection fraction** in suspected ICI myocarditis.
- **Evaluating myositis without assessing respiratory mechanics.** A conversant patient can still be close to respiratory failure from neuromuscular junction involvement.
- **Adding calcium at discharge without checking for hypercalcemia** in a patient with cancer.
- **Defaulting to TMP-SMX and a PPI for steroid prophylaxis** without weighing ICI nephritis risk.
- **Rechallenging while the patient is on more than 10 mg/day of prednisone.**
- **Telling patients that a severe irAE means their cancer treatment failed.**

## Case Examples

### Case 1: Grade 3 ICI colitis, steroid-refractory

A 66-year-old man with metastatic renal cell carcinoma on ipilimumab plus nivolumab called the after-hours line seven weeks into treatment with four days of watery diarrhea: eight stools a day above baseline, crampy abdominal pain, a few streaks of blood, and nocturnal bowel movements. Loperamide had done nothing. In the emergency department he was afebrile, with a heart rate of 108 and blood pressure 112/70; potassium was 3.3 mmol/L and creatinine 1.4 mg/dL from a baseline of 1.0.

Reynolds weighed three features at once: combination therapy, onset at seven weeks squarely in the GI window, and grade 3 severity (more than seven stools above baseline, nocturnal). The workup included a full infectious stool panel with viral testing, TSH and free T4, tissue transglutaminase IgA, and CT given the abdominal pain, followed by endoscopy with biopsy confirming ICI colitis. He was started on IV methylprednisolone 1–2 mg/kg/day. Five days later he still had eight bowel movements a day; Reynolds would have escalated at three. He received one dose of infliximab, improved within two days, and was discharged on a prednisone taper of 10 mg every five to seven days, targeting discontinuation in four to six weeks, with PJP prophylaxis, GI protection, and calcium and vitamin D.

At two-week follow-up, still tapering, he and his family asked whether the steroids and infliximab had ruined his cancer treatment. Reynolds's counseling: retrospective melanoma data suggest similar survival even when immunotherapy cannot be resumed after a severe irAE; rechallenge is possible once prednisone is below 10 mg/day, with a recurrence risk of at least about 30%; and resuming nivolumab alone without ipilimumab would lower the colitis risk.

### Case 2: Pembrolizumab-associated hypophysitis with central adrenal insufficiency

A 60-year-old woman with early-stage triple-negative breast cancer was midway through the KEYNOTE-522 neoadjuvant regimen (carboplatin, paclitaxel, and pembrolizumab, then doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, and pembrolizumab). After six doses of pembrolizumab she developed significant fatigue, mild nausea, and mild diarrhea. TSH and potassium were normal; sodium was 129 mmol/L; white count was mildly low with delayed neutrophil recovery. The head-to-toe screen was otherwise normal except for a morning cortisol of 1.8 mcg/dL.

The preserved TSH fit the PD-1 pattern of isolated central adrenal insufficiency. Reynolds wanted an ACTH to confirm a pituitary source; a low or inappropriately normal value would do so. Without headache, visual symptoms, or AVP deficiency, pituitary MRI was not required. Treatment was physiologic replacement (hydrocortisone 15–25 mg/day divided, or prednisone 5 mg daily), with sick-day rules, stress-dose education, a medical alert bracelet, and explicit communication with her surgeon before her planned operation.

A second patient illustrated the stakes. A woman with breast cancer on neoadjuvant immunotherapy went for lumpectomy and developed persistent, profound hypotension in the operating room that continued post-operatively and delayed her discharge. The surgical team called to ask whether her drug could be responsible. She had undiagnosed adrenal insufficiency, unmasked as adrenal crisis by surgery.

### Case 3: Triple M overlap syndrome

A 71-year-old woman with metastatic non-small cell lung cancer received her first cycle of carboplatin, pemetrexed, and pembrolizumab three weeks earlier. She presented with four days of exertional dyspnea and intermittent vague chest heaviness, and that day had trouble rising from a chair and new double vision. She assumed it was the treatment; her family brought her in. Vital signs were normal. Examination showed bilateral ptosis and proximal muscle weakness.

Reynolds's sequence: CK and troponin together, ECG, and a call to respiratory therapy for NIF and FVC before anything else, because neuromuscular junction involvement determines whether the patient can safely stay on a general floor. CK above 8,000, troponin above 5,000, and a poor NIF would mark her as gravely ill. Echocardiography and cardiac MRI followed, with the caveat that a normal EF is not reassuring. Once the overlap was confirmed, she would receive methylprednisolone 1 g daily for three to five days, IVIG for the neuromuscular component, and then oral prednisone 1–1.5 mg/kg/day, watching for a troponin or CK rebound that would signal the need for mycophenolate or faster-acting agents. Reynolds would rarely, if ever, rechallenge.

### Rechallenge after grade 4 myocarditis (published case)

Joe-Elie Salem and colleagues (*JACC: CardioOncology*) reported a patient in their 70s with metastatic squamous cell cancer who developed grade 4 myocarditis and myositis with markedly elevated CK and troponin, heart block, and a need for non-invasive ventilation. The patient was rescued with steroids, abatacept, and a JAK inhibitor, and the tumor responded so well that there was no progression for a year. At progression, facing death within weeks to months, the patient consented to rechallenge with serial troponin, CK, BNP, and echocardiography. Three weeks after a single dose, the same syndrome recurred. It was rescued again with the same three agents, and the tumor responded again. Reynolds offers it as evidence of how little is known about who can be rechallenged and whether concurrent immunosuppression should be on board.

## Conflicts with Established Positions

**No direct conflicts identified.** Nothing in this episode contradicts the committed positions on anticoagulation, diltiazem and verapamil in HFrEF, NSAIDs, sulfonylureas, clonidine and hydralazine, the hospice threshold, thickened liquids, fall rate, end-of-life symptom management, CGM targets, or the diabetes agent hierarchy.

**Points of contact worth carrying forward:**

- **Diabetes positions.** ICI-associated type 1 diabetes (up to about 1%) is a cause of new hyperglycemia that falls outside the agent hierarchy entirely: insulin-deficient from onset and permanent. A resident on an ICI who develops new or abruptly worsening hyperglycemia should not be started down the oral-agent tiers before this is considered.
- **PPI as default GI prophylaxis.** The nephrology preference for famotidine during prolonged steroids in ICI patients is a specific, reasoned exception to reflexive PPI co-prescription with steroids. It aligns with general deprescribing skepticism about PPIs but is not yet a committed position.

**Gaps the episode leaves that matter for the LTC population**, flagged because they follow foreseeably from applying the episode's recommendations in a facility:

- **Atovaquone versus TMP-SMX in older adults.** The nephrology-versus-infectious-diseases debate is framed around ICI nephritis. In an older adult, TMP-SMX also carries hyperkalemia risk (with ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or spironolactone) and a warfarin interaction, which would push further toward atovaquone.
- **Sick-day rules where the patient is not the one who acts.** Stress dosing and sick-day rules are taught to the patient. In a facility, recognition of intercurrent illness and the decision to stress-dose belong to nursing and the covering clinician, which requires the adrenal insufficiency diagnosis to be prominent on the medication administration record and in transfer paperwork.
- **Atypical presentation of hypophysitis in older adults.** Fatigue, nausea, and mild hyponatremia are nonspecific in any patient, and in a frail older adult they are easily attributed to baseline decline, a new medication, or poor intake.
- **Triple M presenting as falls or functional decline.** Proximal weakness and difficulty rising from a chair may reach an LTC clinician as a fall or a change in transfer status before diplopia or ptosis is noticed.

## Downstream Opportunities

- **Handbook — Polypharmacy and Deprescribing / Medication Reconciliation:** ICIs are administered at an infusion center and are often absent from the facility medication list, while the patient or family may call them "chemotherapy." Admission reconciliation should capture the agent, monotherapy versus combination, and the start and last-dose dates, since irAE risk persists after therapy ends.
- **Handbook — Endocrine (adrenal insufficiency, thyroid):** central adrenal insufficiency from hypophysitis; morning cortisol below 3 mcg/dL; ACTH to localize; TSH with free T4 rather than reflex TSH; hydrocortisone before levothyroxine; replacement-dose rather than high-dose steroids; permanence of ICI endocrinopathies.
- **Handbook — Hyponatremia:** secondary adrenal insufficiency as a cause in any patient with ICI exposure.
- **Handbook — Diabetes:** ICI-associated type 1 diabetes as a cause of abrupt new hyperglycemia.
- **Handbook — Glucocorticoid stewardship:** the >4 weeks at >20 mg/day prophylaxis threshold, the TMP-SMX/atovaquone and PPI/famotidine trade-offs, baseline calcium check, and the 10 mg every 5–7 days taper convention.
- **Handbook — Weakness, Falls, and Functional Decline:** Triple M as a cause of new proximal weakness; CK and troponin together; respiratory mechanics before reassurance.
- **Handbook — Post-acute care of patients with cancer:** the head-to-toe irAE screen as a ready-made table for a resident on active or recent immunotherapy.
- **Bedside Notes — strong candidates:**
  - *"The patient says chemo"* — why the oncology history now has to ask about immunotherapy by name, and why the risk window outlasts the treatment.
  - *"The TSH that looks normal"* — reflex TSH testing misses central hypothyroidism.
  - *"Troponin-CK"* — weakness after a checkpoint inhibitor, and why a normal ejection fraction is not reassuring.
  - *"Hydrocortisone first"* — the ordering rule in combined central hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency.
  - *"When the treatment works and the patient gets sick"* — the melanoma data suggesting outcomes are preserved after severe irAEs; useful for family-facing communication.
- **Protocol — irAE alert on admission:** for any resident with ICI exposure, a flag on the medication record listing red-flag symptoms (diarrhea, dyspnea, progressive rash, weakness, diplopia, fatigue with hyponatremia or hypotension) and the instruction to contact oncology the same day rather than waiting for the next visit.
- **Protocol — adrenal insufficiency in residents:** stress-dose standing orders, sick-day triggers owned by nursing, medical alert documentation, and inclusion in every transfer packet and pre-procedural communication.
- **Protocol — irAE screening panel:** an order set for the resident on an ICI with new nonspecific decline: CBC, CMP, TSH with free T4, morning cortisol (6–10 AM), CK, troponin, ECG.
