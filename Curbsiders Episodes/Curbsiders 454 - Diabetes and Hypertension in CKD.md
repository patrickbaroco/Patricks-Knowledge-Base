## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Nephrologist Joel Topf takes one patient from CKD 4 through heart failure to hemodialysis, and the blood pressure and diabetes decisions change at every step. The distinctive material is the reasoning about what to stop worrying about: GFR is not a patient-oriented outcome, intradialytic blood pressures do not predict anything, and RAS inhibition should not come off when GFR crosses 30. Also here: the Kidney Failure Risk Equation with worked numbers and the specific percentages that trigger a dialysis-modality conversation and access placement, a full case for cystatin C, the chlorthalidone dose-conversion argument, the finerenone-versus-steroidal-MRA distinction, and a glycemic philosophy that flips from tight control before dialysis to relaxed control after it.

## Pearls

- **Chronic kidney disease is not just a GFR.** Albuminuria is badly under-recognized, and classification needs all three: etiology, GFR, and albuminuria.
- Building the case for diabetic kidney disease as the etiology: significant proteinuria, a long history of it, and other end-organ damage from the diabetes.
- **The Kidney Failure Risk Equation beats the KDIGO heat map for talking to patients.** The heat map gives a *relative* risk against a low-risk reference patient — "four times the risk of progression" — without telling you the baseline. Topf's analogy: buying four lottery tickets quadruples your odds and you still will not win. Relative risk is not useful when baseline risk is low. The KFRE instead returns an absolute two-year and five-year probability of kidney failure, and **patients understand a percentage in a way they do not understand "high risk"** — a phrase they hear about everything and cannot act on.
- **Topf's two action thresholds, both on two-year risk:** at **20%**, start the dialysis modality conversation — "if they said there was a 20% chance of rain, I'd carry an umbrella." At **40%**, place the access and start building the fistula.
- **KDIGO 2024 — the first CKD guideline update since 2012 — sets referral by risk rather than by GFR:** a **3–5% five-year risk of kidney failure warrants nephrology referral regardless of where the GFR sits.**
- The counter-case that makes the point: an 80-year-old with an eGFR of 48 and 12 mg/g of albuminuria is CKD 3b by category and **is not going anywhere.** That creatinine will stay stable and she will never progress. Topf does not need to see her — and gets to be the hero who tells her she will never need dialysis.
- **Cystatin C is prime time**, and now part of the guidelines. Topf's framing: cardiology went LDH → CK → CK-MB → troponin → troponin I → high-sensitivity troponin, while nephrology stayed on creatinine the entire time. This is the first meaningful change.
- **Cystatin C makes the race question disappear rather than solving it.** Some cystatin C calculators still offer a race field; flipping it changes nothing, because it is simply not related. (Topf uses the 2021 race-free creatinine equations and is emphatic that removing race was right — his point is that not needing the variable at all is better than adjusting for it.) It also sidesteps the gender problem, in equations built on a binary that does not hold.
- **Because cystatin C is not muscle-dependent**, amputations, bodybuilders, creatine supplementation and people of small stature all stop being problems.
- **The interpretive shortcut that makes it easy to adopt: a normal cystatin C is roughly the same number as a normal creatinine.** If a creatinine of 1 reads as fine and 1.5 as starting to get iffy, read cystatin C the same way. There are no new numbers to learn.
- **Cystatin C rises with inflammation** — clinicians working in ICUs with septic patients find it unhelpful there. That is its main limitation.
- In large epidemiologic studies cystatin C is a **better predictor of death, cardiac disease, hospitalization and kidney failure** than creatinine-based eGFR.
- **It takes only 0.2–0.3 mg/dL of creatinine to move a fit older woman from an eGFR of 65 to 48**, reclassifying her as CKD 3 — at which point, in Topf's experience, patients look at a scale that runs one to five and say "I'm halfway to dialysis." He regards the 1-through-5 staging as a genuine problem that generates a lot of unnecessary fear.
- **GFR is not a patient-oriented outcome.** Being focused on it at the expense of blood pressure is missing the forest for the trees; losing a couple of points of GFR to get the pressure down is an acceptable trade, and it is part of the job to explain that this number moves up and down as a biological variable.
- What blood pressure control actually buys, as Topf lists it to patients: the heart, stroke prevention, dementia prevention, and erectile dysfunction prevention.
- **Losartan is not a good once-daily ARB** — its half-life is two to three hours.
- **Do not peel off the ACE inhibitor or ARB when GFR crosses 30.** This is his single strongest take-home. STOP-ACE randomized patients to hold or continue as GFR fell below 30; continuing did not accelerate time to dialysis. Consistent retrospective data agree, and found **more cardiac events in the patients whose ACE inhibitor was stopped.**
- Potassium ceiling for continuing RAS inhibition: Topf goes into the **low 5s** — his lab's upper limit of normal is 5.4, and "if there's no H next to it, I'm good with it." He volunteers that this is not sophisticated.
- **Potassium binders work better than he expected.** He was skeptical when they arrived, assuming he could manage potassium other ways; in his hands they are far more effective at controlling hyperkalemia than diuretics. The obstacle is cost — a few hundred dollars a month to keep someone on a cheap generic ACE inhibitor.
- **The evidence gap on binders is real and named:** DIAMOND was flummoxed by COVID. What exists is data that binders let people stay on RAS inhibition and MRAs. What does not exist is data that staying on those drugs then reduces outcomes. Topf: "it is a leap of faith — a leap I'm comfortable making, but it is a gap in the evidence for sure."
- **Below a GFR of about 40, an SGLT2 inhibitor stops improving A1c** — there is not enough glucose being filtered to produce meaningful glycosuria. Every cardiorenal benefit persists. These drugs were introduced as antihyperglycemics and stop being antihyperglycemics in advanced CKD.
- **EMPA-KIDNEY patients who progressed to dialysis stayed on study drug**, and post hoc those on empagliflozin did better than matched placebo controls *on dialysis*. Topf: "kind of blows my mind for a drug that's supposed to work in the kidneys — but the evidence is the evidence." The LIFESTYLE trial is now randomizing dialysis patients to dapagliflozin or placebo.
- **The kidneys are gluconeogenic organs, and they metabolize insulin.** Losing them means losing a significant share of gluconeogenesis *and* prolonging insulin's half-life — two independent reasons hypoglycemia risk rises on dialysis.
- **Blood pressures taken in the dialysis unit do not predict cardiovascular outcomes.** A patient sitting in a chair at 6 a.m. about to have a 14-gauge needle placed in a fistula reads 160/100, and that is understandable rather than prognostic; a reading taken after three liters came off in three hours is equally unrepresentative.
- **The blood pressure that counts is the one the internist takes on a non-dialysis day.** Topf: "I'm drowning in blood pressure readings. None of mine make a difference. The one that you grab is the one that's going to help that patient."
- **Most hypertension on dialysis is volume-dependent, so the first move is the dry weight, not the medication list.** Get enough fluid off and many patients come off most of their antihypertensives — "hard to do, but that's kind of the promised land."
- **Spironolactone works in anuric dialysis patients**, in unselected patients rather than only those with high aldosterone-to-renin ratios — and, strangely, still causes hyperkalemia, thought to be via inhibited **colonic potassium clearance.**
- **Midodrine to get through dialysis is a terrible prognostic sign** — usually end-stage heart failure, and outcomes are poor. It is still used, because if fluid cannot be removed the patient enters a death spiral of increasing interdialytic gains.
- **Once a patient is on dialysis, the main thing tight glycemic control was protecting against has already happened.** Risk goes up, reward goes down, so the targets relax.
- Sulfonylureas are "the bad old days" — real hypoglycemia risk, and a falling A1c in advanced kidney disease is a warning sign rather than an achievement.
- **The elderly diabetic patient on dialysis does not have a long lifespan**, and routine preventive care — Topf names colonoscopy — may not make sense. "They're kind of like hospice patients, but they're not."

## Clinical Framework

### Classifying CKD — three axes, not one

1. **Etiology** — diabetic, hypertensive, or a glomerulonephritis. Built from the history: duration, degree of proteinuria, other diabetic end-organ damage.
2. **GFR** — and note whether it is creatinine- or cystatin C-based.
3. **Albuminuria** — the axis most often omitted, and the one that determines trajectory.

### The Kidney Failure Risk Equation, with the episode's worked numbers

Inputs at kidneyfailurerisk.com: age, sex, region, eGFR, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio. Optional additions: corrected calcium, bicarbonate, albumin, phosphate. Output: two-year and five-year probability of kidney failure.

| Same patient: 58-year-old man, North America, eGFR 28 | 2-year risk | 5-year risk |
|---|---|---|
| uACR **75** mg/g | 6% | 17.9% |
| uACR **750** mg/g | 16.3% | 42.7% |

The albuminuria alone moves the five-year risk from roughly one in six to roughly one in two. This is the argument for why a GFR-only classification is inadequate.

**What the numbers trigger:**

| Two-year risk | Action |
|---|---|
| — | *(KDIGO 2024: refer to nephrology at a 3–5% **five-year** risk, regardless of GFR)* |
| **20%** | Begin the dialysis modality conversation |
| **40%** | Place the access — start building the fistula |

**Host referral practice, for comparison:** Watto refers all CKD 4. Williams refers CKD 4, or significant albuminuria with CKD 3 — or, honestly, significant albuminuria at any stage.

### Where the evidence stops

A recurring structural point across the episode: **the pivotal trials excluded GFR below 30.** Heart failure GDMT, ARNIs, and most antihypertensive outcome data all have this hole. Topf's stated reasoning for extrapolating anyway: nothing about these patients suggests their physiology or their heart failure is different, so he makes the leap — and names it as a leap rather than pretending the evidence exists. The exceptions, where advanced-CKD data actually exist, are worth knowing precisely because they are so few: **CLICK** (chlorthalidone in stage 4), **STOP-ACE** (continuing RAS inhibition below 30), **EMPA-KIDNEY** (initiation down to eGFR 20), and **Agarwal's atenolol-versus-lisinopril trial** in dialysis.

### The glycemic philosophy inverts at dialysis

- **CKD 4 and CKD 5 not yet on dialysis → tight control.** Good data that tight glycemic control slows progression of kidney disease, and this is the outcome patients care most about: none of them want dialysis and they will do a lot to avoid it. Partner with the patient on it — but move them onto the safer agents first.
- **On dialysis → relax.** The microvascular complication that tight control was protecting against has already arrived, so the reward side collapses; meanwhile the risk side rises through lost renal gluconeogenesis and prolonged insulin half-life. Do not let sugars run really high, but stop being fastidious.

### The DCCT caution Topf attaches to tight control

DCCT (NEJM, 1992) reduced albuminuria with tight control in type 1 diabetes and became the basis for tight control in everyone. But **there was no mortality reduction initially — it took about 15 years of follow-up to appear.** Among the outcomes the trial tracked were **motor vehicle accidents, and the deaths of passengers in them**, because hypoglycemia was killing people in crashes. Topf: "I don't know if the reduction in albuminuria was worth this." The endocrinologists were eventually proven right, at a real cost — and the argument for the newer agents is precisely that the cost no longer has to be paid.

## Drug and Dosing Detail

### Stepwise blood pressure escalation in CKD 4

The case: 160/100 on maximally tolerated losartan, carvedilol and hydrochlorothiazide, eGFR 28, secondary workup negative. Topf's sequence — **stepwise across visits, not all at one visit:**

1. **Switch hydrochlorothiazide to chlorthalidone, milligram for milligram.** 25 mg HCTZ becomes **25 mg chlorthalidone**, which he acknowledges is "a sneaky way to double their dose," and he doses it **every day** so it accumulates. He reports improved blood pressure control from that change alone.
   - **The CLICK trial (Agarwal)** is why he reaches for chlorthalidone specifically: effective blood pressure control in **CKD stage 4** with uncontrolled hypertension. It stands alone — there is essentially no comparable data for other antihypertensives in advanced CKD.
   - **He has less confidence than he used to, because of the Diuretic Comparison Project**, which randomized patients on HCTZ to switch to chlorthalidone and found no outcome difference. **His critique: DCP used equivalent doses** — 25 mg HCTZ to 12.5 mg chlorthalidone — which is not the conversion he performs. The milligram-for-milligram conversion is the intervention; a dose-equivalent swap is not.
   - Caution: chlorthalidone is "a pretty big gun" and lowers potassium and magnesium.
2. **Switch losartan to a longer-acting ARB** — half-life two to three hours makes it a poor once-daily agent. Topf favors **telmisartan**.
3. **Add a long-acting dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker.** He prefers **nifedipine** in his hands, citing useful dose granularity at **30, 60 and 90 mg**.

**Indapamide** — raised by Watto as an alternative: available in **1.25 mg and 2.5 mg**, so easier to titrate than chlorthalidone, which comes as a 25 mg tablet that is crumbly and hard to halve ("chlorthalidone dust"). Topf's response: the reasoning and logic are sound and indapamide is fine; he reaches for chlorthalidone because CLICK is the trial that exists in this population.

### RAS inhibition below GFR 30 — and managing the potassium instead

- **Continue it.** STOP-ACE plus consistent retrospective data; stopping was associated with more cardiac events.
- **Potassium ceiling: low 5s** (his lab's ULN is 5.4). Watto notes a prior guest allowing stable patients to ride at ≤5.5.
- **Work-arounds rather than discontinuation:** diuretics, and potassium binders. Binders outperform diuretics in his hands.
- **The cost problem:** a few hundred dollars a month for a binder to preserve access to a generic ACE inhibitor. The failure mode he describes: the patient who runs out of the binder and proudly keeps taking the ACE inhibitor — exactly the wrong half to continue, and a predictable consequence of how the pairing is explained.
- **The evidence gap:** DIAMOND was disrupted by COVID. Binders demonstrably enable continuation; whether that continuation improves outcomes has not been shown.

### SGLT2 inhibitors

- **Strong indication** in CKD with heart failure: renal outcomes, heart failure outcomes, and mortality. "A clean win."
- **EMPA-KIDNEY initiated down to eGFR 20** — the lowest studied.
- **Continue through dialysis.** In EMPA-KIDNEY, starting dialysis was not a reason to stop study drug, and those patients did better than matched placebo controls. The **LIFESTYLE trial** is testing dapagliflozin versus placebo in dialysis patients directly.
- **No A1c benefit below GFR ~40** — insufficient filtered glucose. All other benefits remain.
- **EMPA-KIDNEY enrolled patients with no proteinuria at all.** The primary outcome showed no difference; the secondary outcome of **GFR slope** did show slowed progression. Topf's read: slope is a continuous variable and therefore easier to move than a dichotomous outcome (dialysis initiated, or a 40% GFR drop) in a group with few events. His sense is that they work across the span of kidney disease, more slowly and subtly in less sick patients.
- **A caution on the albuminuria-benefit gradient:** Watto suggests worse albuminuria means more benefit. Topf pushes back — it may be an artifact, since those patients accrue more outcomes and therefore show a bigger effect simply by being higher risk. Compounding it, these trials are short and **have been stopped early because results were so impressive**, which further penalizes detection of subtler benefit in lower-risk patients.
- **Tolerability is why adoption changed.** Topf: "they're really innocuous... it's an easy drug to prescribe, I'm not getting any blowback from my patients." Williams describes the old pattern where cardiology deferred to nephrology deferred to primary care and the patient never got the drug; now whoever thinks of it first starts it.
- **Euglycemic DKA** — Watto has seen it in hospitalized patients, but rarely relative to how many people are on these agents.

### ARNIs

Topf extends the low-GFR argument to ARNIs: excluded from the pivotal trials, but he uses them in low-GFR patients **and in patients on dialysis.** Besides hypotension they are well tolerated, and **there are data showing less hyperkalemia than with ACE inhibition or ARB alone.**

### Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists — the one he is nervous about

- He uses spironolactone and eplerenone in CKD 4 and 5, but **his frequency of hyperkalemia is very high.**
- Practice: check the potassium before starting, guarantee follow-up, counsel the patient explicitly. "The worst thing is calling that patient at eight or nine o'clock at night and saying you gotta go to the ER. I hate doing that — and these are the drugs that do it to me the most."
- **Position it as the last thing added**, once everything else is in place, framed to the patient as carrying real risk.
- **If the potassium is already 5, do not start it.** Stated flatly.

### Finerenone is not a heart failure drug — the distinction, with a 2025 update

- **As stated in the episode:** finerenone has an indication for **increased cardiovascular risk**, not for heart failure, and it is **not part of heart failure GDMT.** For a heart failure patient, use **eplerenone or spironolactone.**
- Where finerenone fits: the patient **without** heart failure who has cardiovascular risk — a smoker, established atherosclerotic disease. "Great drug for that, saves lives, also slows down the progression of diabetic kidney disease."
- **FIDELIO** was the kidney outcomes trial; **FIGARO** was the cardiovascular outcomes trial.
- **Both enrolled only patients with a potassium below 4.8.** Topf finds this striking — a potassium in the normal fours being disqualifying — and notes that **even with that restriction roughly 20% of patients developed hyperkalemia**, while investigators were deploying diuretics and potassium binders to manage it.
- Watto adds that finerenone lowers blood pressure less than the steroidal MRAs, and is used mainly for CKD with diabetes and proteinuria.
- **Time-stamp:** see Conflicts. The FDA approved finerenone for heart failure with **LVEF ≥40%** in July 2025, after this recording. The statement above remains correct for HFrEF.

### Glucose-lowering agents in advanced CKD

- **Sulfonylureas:** "the bad old days." Real hypoglycemia risk, and a falling A1c in advanced CKD should worry you rather than please you.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists and DPP-4 inhibitors:** "these are amazing drugs and we don't see this hypoglycemia with them."
- **Metformin:** do not initiate below GFR 30; with a GFR in the 20s, stop it in most patients.
- **GLP-1 agonists now have renal outcome data — the FLOW trial**, released the month before this recording. **Semaglutide 1 mg weekly** versus placebo, with a reduction in the classic composite of **40% GFR decline, death, or dialysis** — "exactly the kind of compelling data we've seen with the SGLT2 inhibitors." Topf flags that 1 mg is a relatively low dose relative to what many patients now receive for weight loss or diabetes. Other agents have shown albuminuria improvements; he is **not yet willing to call it a class effect**, but suspects others will follow. "That's a big deal trial, FLOW."

### Blood pressure agents on hemodialysis

- **Topf's working list:** a lot of calcium channel blockers, a lot of ACE inhibitors, some beta blockers — **usually carvedilol rather than atenolol**, because heart failure frequency in this population is "through the roof" and he wants to stay on GDMT. Plus spironolactone in selected patients.
- **Atenolol** — UpToDate lists it as first choice for dialysis patients, which surprised Watto. Topf's response is the most nuanced passage in the episode: "atenolol is usually the punching bag of blood pressure medications — if you want your blood pressure medication to look good, just compare it to atenolol." **But there is an RCT.** Agarwal again, and a clever design: atenolol accumulates in dialysis patients, so it was given as **directly observed therapy, 100 mg in the dialysis unit**, against lisinopril. It was **underpowered** with few patients, but showed a **survival advantage for atenolol**. Mechanistically plausible, since dialysis patients die of **sudden cardiac death** and beta blockers are good for that. "There's a reason UpToDate didn't just make that up." Skepticism persists because of the power problem, and the area is badly under-investigated.
- **Spironolactone on dialysis** — works despite absent kidney function, in **unselected patients** rather than only those with high aldosterone-renin ratios, with good blood-pressure lowering. **Still causes hyperkalemia**, believed to be via inhibition of **colonic potassium clearance**; avoid in the minority of dialysis patients who run hyperkalemic.
- **Dry weight before drugs.** Most of this hypertension is volume-dependent. Lowering the dry weight is the highest-yield intervention; patients resist because the push down causes cramps and discomfort. Get through it and many come off most of their antihypertensives.
- **Midodrine** — used to get hypotension-prone patients through treatment. A terrible prognostic marker, usually end-stage heart failure. Used anyway, because failure to remove fluid produces a death spiral of escalating interdialytic gains.

## Diagnostic and Exam Detail

- **Order a cystatin C on essentially every new CKD patient** to check for a discrepancy with creatinine. Most of the time the two correlate.
- **Interpret cystatin C on the creatinine scale** — the normal values are roughly the same, so no new reference points are required.
- **The three populations where cystatin C repeatedly changes the answer:** young, healthy, very athletic patients with an elevated creatinine (often compounded by creatine supplementation); fit older adults with more muscle than average for their age and sex; and anyone where muscle mass is atypical — amputees, people of small stature.
- **Where it fails:** significant inflammation. Cystatin C rises with it, and ICU clinicians with septic patients find it unreliable.
- **Blood pressure measurement on dialysis patients:** the unit's readings — start, end, every five minutes during treatment, twelve sessions a month — are essential for delivering dialysis safely and useless for guiding chronic therapy. **Use non-dialysis-day readings:** ambulatory monitoring is ideal (every 20 minutes awake, every 30 asleep), home readings are also very good, and an office reading on a non-dialysis day is the practical version.
- **Holding antihypertensives on dialysis days is not universal and should not be reflexive.** The standard advice — hold before dialysis, resume after the pressure recovers — applies to patients who actually have intradialytic hypotension. Topf is explicit: patients who tolerate dialysis without hypotension can simply take their medications beforehand. Individualize, and coordinate with the nephrologist.
- **What the internist uniquely holds:** the non-dialysis-day reading. Williams adds the practical counterpart — dialysis unit visits are a black box to the internist, the reports that come back are often unintelligible, and the right move is to call the nephrologist rather than adjust unilaterally, because you cannot see what is happening the other half of the week.

## Errors and Misconceptions

- **Classifying or referring on GFR alone.** The 80-year-old with an eGFR of 48 and trivial albuminuria is not going to progress; a 58-year-old with an eGFR of 28 and 750 mg/g has a coin-flip risk at five years. The same GFR category carries wildly different meanings.
- **Communicating risk in relative terms when the baseline is low.** "You're at high risk" is a phrase patients hear about everything and cannot act on; a fourfold increase over a negligible baseline is still negligible.
- **Peeling off the ACE inhibitor or ARB when the GFR crosses 30.** Named explicitly as misguided, with both trial and observational evidence against it, and a cardiac-event penalty for doing it.
- **Over-indexing on GFR at the expense of blood pressure.** GFR is a biological variable and not a patient-oriented outcome; blood pressure control prevents the events that matter.
- **Treating losartan as an adequate once-daily ARB.**
- **Converting hydrochlorothiazide to chlorthalidone at dose-equivalence.** Topf's reading of why the Diuretic Comparison Project was negative — the conversion that produces benefit is milligram for milligram, not potency for potency.
- **Using dialysis-unit blood pressures to guide chronic antihypertensive therapy.** They exist to deliver dialysis safely. They do not predict cardiovascular outcomes.
- **Holding all antihypertensives on all dialysis days reflexively**, when the hold is only indicated for patients who actually develop intradialytic hypotension.
- **Expecting an A1c response to an SGLT2 inhibitor below a GFR of 40.** There is not enough filtered glucose. The drug is still doing its job; the A1c is simply the wrong way to see it.
- **Stopping an SGLT2 inhibitor because the patient started dialysis.**
- **Treating finerenone as heart failure GDMT** — see the update in Conflicts, which narrows but does not eliminate this error.
- **Starting an MRA when the potassium is already 5.**
- **Assuming spironolactone cannot work in an anuric patient** — and, conversely, assuming it cannot cause hyperkalemia in one.
- **Reaching for another antihypertensive before adjusting the dry weight** in a hypertensive dialysis patient.
- **Celebrating a falling A1c in advanced kidney disease.** Watto: "I get worried." Williams: "My stomach drops."
- **Continuing routine cancer screening in the elderly diabetic dialysis patient** without weighing the prognosis.
- **Relying on cystatin C in a significantly inflamed or septic patient.**

## Case Examples

### Mr. A, followed across three stages

**Stage 1 — CKD 4 with uncontrolled hypertension.** A 58-year-old man with CKD, hypertension and type 2 diabetes, averaging **160/100** over six months on maximally tolerated **losartan, carvedilol and hydrochlorothiazide.** No symptoms. Secondary workup complete and negative: renin and aldosterone, pheochromocytoma excluded, renal ultrasound without stenosis. **eGFR 28.** The concern posed to the guest is the familiar one — that pushing harder on the blood pressure will cost kidney function.

The answer reframes the question: the GFR is not the outcome, and a couple of points of it is an acceptable price. Chlorthalidone replaces hydrochlorothiazide milligram for milligram, losartan becomes telmisartan, and a long-acting dihydropyridine goes on next — stepwise, across visits. The albuminuria, absent from the stem, is the missing variable that would have determined how urgently any of this matters.

**Stage 2 — CAD with MI, CABG, and class II heart failure on eplerenone.** Now the heart failure GDMT question, in a zone every pivotal trial excluded. SGLT2 inhibition is the clean win — renal, cardiac and mortality benefit, initiable to an eGFR of 20 and continuable onto dialysis. ARNI is reasonable by extrapolation. The MRA is where the nervousness lives: already on eplerenone, so the question becomes surveillance rather than initiation — and had the potassium been 5, Topf would not have started it at all. Finerenone is explicitly *not* the substitute here, because this is heart failure and finerenone's indication was cardiovascular risk.

**Stage 3 — on hemodialysis.** Blood pressure running 140s–150s over 80s–90s on non-dialysis days, with episodes of intradialytic hypotension. The pile of unit readings is discarded as prognostically useless; the non-dialysis-day readings become the target. Because this patient *does* have intradialytic hypotension, the dialysis-day hold applies to him specifically. Dry weight is the first lever, medications the second. And the diabetes philosophy inverts: the tight control that was justified at CKD 4 to protect the kidneys is no longer buying what it was buying, while lost renal gluconeogenesis and prolonged insulin half-life have made it more dangerous.

## Conflicts with Established Positions

**No direct conflicts identified.** Nothing here contradicts the committed positions carried elsewhere (anticoagulation, NSAIDs, sulfonylureas, clonidine/hydralazine, hospice threshold, thickened liquids, fall rate, EOL symptom management), or the diabetes agent hierarchy. Several reinforce it materially, one pearl has been overtaken by events, and two need translation.

**Reinforcements worth carrying forward:**

- **Sulfonylureas unsafe in LTC.** A third independent derivation, after the hypoglycemia mechanism in [[Curbsiders 483 - Diabetes in Older Adults, Pregnancy, and Corticosteroid Use]] and the injurious-fall mechanism in [[Curbsiders 503 - Falls]]. Here the argument is renal: **the kidneys are gluconeogenic and they metabolize insulin**, so advanced CKD independently raises hypoglycemia risk and prolongs the effect of any hypoglycemic agent. The three arguments are independent of one another, so a resident who meets any of the three conditions is covered by at least one of them.

**Population data, from outside the episode, on how far the renal argument reaches.** In a cross-sectional study of 794 residents across 82 geographically representative US nursing homes, **CKD (eGFR <60 by MDRD) was present in about 50%**; within that group, 47.6% were stage 3a, 39.3% stage 3b, and 13.2% stage 4/5 — so **roughly a quarter of all residents were stage 3b or worse** (McClellan et al., *JAMDA* 2010, [10.1016/j.jamda.2009.07.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jamda.2009.07.003), via PubMed). A common but not universal finding, in other words: substantial, and not a majority. Worth pairing with the cystatin C material above, because the measured prevalence depends heavily on the equation used — in 522 long-term care residents, the proportion with eGFR <60 ranged from **58.2% by CKD-EPI to 79.1% by BIS1** (Paparazzo et al., *Front Med* 2022, [10.3389/fmed.2022.870835](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmed.2022.870835), via PubMed). Caveats before reuse: the US figures are creatinine-based and from 2009 data, and the second cohort is Italian.
- **The diabetes agent hierarchy, with CKD-specific boundaries added.** Topf's ranking matches it — GLP-1s and DPP-4s as "amazing drugs" without hypoglycemia, SGLT2 inhibitors as a clean win, sulfonylureas as the bad old days. What the episode adds are the constraints the hierarchy did not yet carry: **metformin is out below GFR 30** and should be stopped in most patients in the 20s; **SGLT2 inhibitors stop being glucose-lowering agents below GFR 40** while retaining every other benefit; and **GLP-1s now have a renal outcome trial (FLOW)** rather than only cardiovascular and metabolic data.
- **The DPP-4 carve-out for linagliptin.** The committed position reserves linagliptin for low, fluctuating, or unreliably measured eGFR. This episode is the population where that lives: CKD 4 with a creatinine-based GFR the guest immediately questions, a recommendation to recheck with cystatin C, and an explicit endorsement of DPP-4 inhibitors as a safe class in advanced kidney disease. The cystatin C material also sharpens the trigger — **a discrepancy between creatinine- and cystatin C-based GFR is itself a reason to treat the eGFR as unreliable**, which is exactly the clinical impression the carve-out rests on.
- **Oral clonidine and hydralazine contraindicated for chronic hypertension.** Williams's aside — "no one likes hydralazine, except for the occasional surgeon for a PRN" — is the position stated as common sense, and the guest's stepwise escalation reaches for chlorthalidone, a longer-acting ARB and a dihydropyridine without either drug appearing.
- **Deintensification, and the low A1c as a warning.** The "patient celebrating a low A1c" moment recurs almost verbatim across two episodes — the family high-fiving in 483, "my stomach drops" here. Two different specialists reaching the same reflex is worth more than either alone.

**Overtaken by events — a time-stamped pearl:**

- **Finerenone now has a heart failure indication, for LVEF ≥40%.** Recorded June 2024, the guest's statement that finerenone "does not have an indication for heart failure" was correct. The **FDA approved finerenone for heart failure with LVEF ≥40% on 15 July 2025**, on the strength of FINEARTS-HF. **The guest's clinical advice nonetheless still stands for the case he was discussing:** the approval does not cover HFrEF, so for a patient with reduced ejection fraction a steroidal MRA — eplerenone or spironolactone — remains the GDMT agent, and finerenone is not a substitute. What has changed is the blanket statement, not the recommendation. Any downstream use of this note should carry the narrowed version: *finerenone is not a substitute for a steroidal MRA in HFrEF; in HFmrEF and HFpEF it now has an indication of its own.* **(Verified 2026-09-12.)**

**Translation needed before this is usable in LTC:**

- **"Add a calcium channel blocker" means a dihydropyridine.** The guest reaches for nifedipine, and the class as he uses it is amlodipine-and-nifedipine. A downstream chapter that mines "use a calcium channel blocker in CKD hypertension" into an LTC population sitting on the existing **diltiazem and verapamil contraindicated in HFrEF** position would be importing a real hazard. The recommendation should be written as *dihydropyridine* every time it is carried out of this note.
- **The prognosis point is adjacent to the hospice position, not an instance of it.** Topf's closing observation — that the elderly diabetic dialysis patient may not warrant a colonoscopy, and is "kind of like a hospice patient, but not" — is about prognosis-based deprescribing of preventive care, not about hospice eligibility. It does not contradict the requirement for a clinical or social need beyond eligibility; if anything it illustrates why that requirement exists, since the reasoning that limits screening is not the same reasoning that qualifies someone for a benefit. Worth carrying as a separate principle rather than folded into the hospice position.
- **Hyperkalemia is worth treating as a named LTC problem, not just a nephrology one.** In 100% Medicare fee-for-service data covering 4,562,231 patients with a long-term care stay, hyperkalemia prevalence was **14.7%** across the study period and **7.1%** during or within 14 days before the index LTC stay, with a **48.3% recurrence rate**. Propensity-matched, patients with hyperkalemia were **2.2 times more likely to be hospitalized**, with higher mortality (**30.8% versus 21.5%**) (Neuenschwander et al., *Adv Ther* 2023, [10.1007/s12325-022-02420-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12325-022-02420-x), via PubMed). This gives the guest's "manage the potassium rather than stop the drug" position a cost side: the potassium management has to actually happen, because the hyperkalemia it is working around carries a measurable hospitalization and mortality penalty in this setting.
- **The dialysis material applies wherever a facility cares for a resident on hemodialysis.** Almost everything in that section is executed by facility staff rather than by the nephrologist: the non-dialysis-day blood pressure, the dialysis-day medication hold, and the observation that intradialytic readings are not a reason to change anything. The framing that the internist holds the only prognostically meaningful blood pressure translates directly to the facility holding it.

**Sharpens an existing open item:**

- **FLOW cuts against the GLP-1 frailty exclusion, without resolving it.** The open item notes that a top-tier GLP-1 position needs a frailty, sarcopenia and weight-trajectory exclusion. A renal outcome trial gives GLP-1s a hard indication in exactly the population where CKD and frailty overlap most, which raises the cost of applying that exclusion broadly. The tension is now sharper rather than softer: the resident with CKD 4 and diabetes has the strongest possible indication and, frequently, the strongest contraindication.

## Downstream Opportunities

- **Handbook — new chapter candidate: *Chronic Kidney Disease in LTC*.** The three-axis classification, the KFRE with its referral and access thresholds, the cystatin C material, the RAS-inhibitor continuation position, and the potassium-management ladder give the chapter its spine. The single most useful import is the reframing that GFR is not the outcome.
- **Handbook — *Hypertension in LTC*:** the stepwise escalation (chlorthalidone milligram-for-milligram, longer-acting ARB, dihydropyridine), with the losartan half-life point and the chlorthalidone-versus-indapamide practicalities.
- **Handbook — new section candidate: *The Resident on Dialysis*.** The episode supplies the operational core: which blood pressures count, when to hold antihypertensives and when not to, dry weight before drugs, midodrine as a prognostic marker, and what the facility should be communicating to the nephrologist.
- **Handbook — *Hyperkalemia*:** the potassium ceiling for continuing RAS inhibition, binders versus diuretics with the cost reality, the MRA initiation rule, and the colonic-clearance mechanism in anuric patients.
- **Handbook — cross-reference from *Diabetes Management in Older Adults*:** the CKD boundaries on the agent hierarchy (metformin below 30, SGLT2 glycemic ceiling at 40), the tight-then-relaxed philosophy across the dialysis transition, and the renal hypoglycemia mechanism.
- **Handbook — *Laboratory Interpretation*:** cystatin C — when to order, how to read it on the creatinine scale, the three populations where it changes the answer, and the inflammation caveat. Converges with the creatine artifact carried in [[Curbsiders 534 - Supplements in Primary Care]]; together they make a complete short section on when a creatinine-based eGFR should not be believed.
- **Handbook — *Prognosis and Preventive Care*:** the colonoscopy point, generalized — preventive interventions whose benefit horizon exceeds the resident's prognosis.
- **Newsletter:** "The low A1c that should worry you" — now supported by two episodes and two specialties, which makes it the strongest recurring hook this project has accumulated.
- **Newsletter:** "Your kidney stage sounds worse than it is" — the one-through-five scale, the 0.2 mg/dL that reclassifies someone, and the KFRE as the number that actually answers the question patients are asking.
- **Newsletter:** "Don't stop the ACE inhibitor" — counterintuitive, specific, and with a named trial behind it.
- **Bedside Notes candidates, in descending order of fit:**
  - *"The blood pressure that counts is the one you take"* — twelve readings a month that predict nothing, and one office reading that does. A clean example of a measurement's value depending entirely on when it is taken.
  - *"Read cystatin C like a creatinine"* — one sentence that makes a new test adoptable, plus the muscle-mass populations where it changes the answer.
  - *"Four lottery tickets"* — relative risk against a low baseline, and why "high risk" fails as patient communication.
  - *"The passengers"* — DCCT tracked the deaths of passengers in car accidents. A short, sobering piece on what tight glycemic control cost before the safer agents existed.
  - *"Milligram for milligram"* — why the chlorthalidone conversion is the intervention, and how a dose-equivalent swap produced a negative trial.
- **Protocol — dialysis resident blood pressure documentation:** obtain and record blood pressure on non-dialysis days, transmit those readings to nephrology, and explicitly exclude dialysis-day and intradialytic readings from chronic medication decisions.
- **Protocol — dialysis-day medication hold:** applied only to residents with documented intradialytic hypotension, with the hold and the post-dialysis resumption both written into the order rather than left to the pass.
- **Protocol — RAS inhibitor continuation standard:** an explicit statement that a GFR crossing 30 is not an indication to discontinue, with the potassium threshold, the monitoring interval, and the binder-versus-diuretic escalation named.
- **Protocol — MRA initiation checklist:** potassium below 5 to start, baseline and scheduled follow-up potassium, patient counseling on the risk, and initiation only after the rest of the regimen is in place.
