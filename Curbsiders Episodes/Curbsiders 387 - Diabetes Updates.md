## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Endocrinologist Marie McDonnell works a single case — a 39-year-old whose A1c jumps from 6.4% to 9.6% — into a full approach to type 2 diabetes: how to avoid missing adult-onset autoimmune diabetes, how to set an A1c target and why the ACCORD-based reading of the evidence is weaker than it looks, and how to choose among the newer agents. The distinctive practical material is a detailed metformin extended-release conversion, an explicit A1c threshold below which she will not start an SGLT2 inhibitor, and a worked set of decision rules for prescribing a GLP-1 in someone with a pancreatitis history.

## Pearls

- **50% of what used to be called juvenile diabetes now presents in people over 18**, and the guest believes that threshold is moving toward 25. Adult-onset autoimmune diabetes that progresses rapidly to insulin deficiency is more common than it used to be.
- **Don't use a magic A1c number to decide about insulin — listen to the patient.** "A lot of patients have an A1c of 9 and they feel fine. Actually, that's the biggest problem in diabetes." A patient telling you she feels unwell is the signal.
- **Nothing you do at the first visit will make or break the situation.** Start a therapy, send the labs, and bring the patient back quickly. The decision that matters is the follow-up interval.
- **Weight loss plus polyuria together is insulin deficiency**, and it is the one presentation that makes insulin the right initial therapy in a patient who otherwise looks like type 2.
- **The dramatic A1c rise has a name and an analogy.** Beta cell failure behaves like the Starling curve in heart failure: you push the failing system until at some point it falls off the curve and compensation stops. It happens in both type 1 and type 2 — a steep rise is classic type 2, but in a young person the concern is type 1 "hiding under the cloak of type 2."
- **Insulin is not the right medicine for type 2 diabetes unless it is really advanced** — with the exception of relieving insulin deficiency, **which can be transient in type 2.** "My favorite thing to do is take insulin away."
- **"Metformin is never wrong, unless it is completely contraindicated."** Do not start below an eGFR of 30.
- **Cardiovascular risk is a legitimate tiebreaker toward a GLP-1.** Run an ASCVD or AHA 2018 risk score; **above roughly 13–15% ten-year risk**, an agent that changes that risk deserves consideration.
- **Before escalating, look for a reversible contributor** — juice, soda and milk are the three the guest names first, and **a recent steroid injection into a joint** is the one that is easy to miss entirely.
- **The ACCORD reading that drives the 7–8% guidance is weaker than it is usually presented.** ACCORD randomized older adults to <6.5% versus <8% and found increased mortality — but on post hoc analysis **the mortality was not related to hypoglycemia**, and **the people who actually achieved an A1c below 6.5% had the lowest mortality.** It was the patients in the tight-control arm who *could not get there* who died.
- **It is how you get there.** Metformin and lifestyle normalizing an A1c is a different clinical situation from 150 units of insulin a day producing the same number.
- The cardiovascular outcome trials for the newer agents were **deliberately designed not to show a difference in glucose control**, so glucose would not be a factor — and they showed benefit within a few years with barely any A1c separation, where older trials pushed A1c hard for 10 to 20 years and struggled. "We're in some sense focusing too much on the wrong thing."
- **The guest's own framing of targets: below 7.5% is "the safe zone" — you will not have rapid progression of disease.** The best place, if achievable without undue burden, is as low as you can get.
- **True remission off medication is achieved by something like 0.1% of people.** A good number, though, reach well below 6.5% on a GLP-1 or on lifestyle plus metformin.
- **Time spent in the normal range may matter independently.** The Diabetes Prevention Program's 15-year follow-up suggests the more time spent with an A1c in the normal range — they looked below 6% — the lower the likelihood of a complication. So even after diagnosis, getting there and spending time there is worth something.
- **There is no data at all on people who achieve remission-level glucose and what happens to them over a life.** No study exists.
- **Where tight control still earns its keep is mostly neuropathy.** For a patient whose neuropathy is progressing aggressively, once B12 deficiency and excessive alcohol are excluded, better glucose control does appear to stabilize it — and it is disabling. **Retinopathy tends to be stable in the 7–7.5% range**, and for kidney disease the SGLT2 inhibitors are "like magic," dropping microalbuminuria precipitously even at an A1c of 7.4%.
- **Roughly 50% of prescriptions that patients fill are not being taken within a year.** The guest attaches this to every conversation about escalating therapy.
- **"The pill box is full and she's doing it right" is not verification.** The guest's own case: a patient had been taking glimepiride the entire time after it had been stopped, because insulin had been started and the sulfonylurea was meant to come off.
- **An A1c over 10%, even in the first year, probably does damage** — the guest treats this as reason to move, without endorsing a numeric threshold for insulin.
- **Metformin extended release only comes in 500 mg tablets**, which patients dislike, but the dose does not change and the maximum effective dose remains 2000 mg/day. The package insert permits 2550 mg; "you don't get much more after 2000."
- **The extended-release tolerability evidence is thinner than the practice suggests.** The only randomized evidence is for **Glumetza**, a distinct micronized brand formulation — not for generic metformin ER. "Metformin is so old, nobody's going to actually study it." In her practice the switch helps **60–70% of the time**; for the rest, "you just have to say goodbye to metformin."
- **Get the A1c below 9% before starting an SGLT2 inhibitor.** At higher A1c the glycosuria drives genital mycotic infection and polyuria hard enough to be genuinely miserable.
- **Ask about yeast infection before prescribing an SGLT2 inhibitor.** "Women suffer in silence with yeast infections their whole life and they might not connect it to their diabetes." A useful technique: describing the side effect usually prompts the patient to disclose that they already have one.
- **The early GLP-1 agonists are not the same class as the current ones.** The agents homologous to Gila monster venom (exenatide) have never shown cardiovascular benefit. **Liraglutide was the first to show it**, then semaglutide and dulaglutide.
- **Semaglutide's stroke risk reduction is the standout**, and the guest reaches for it specifically in patients with a prior TIA or otherwise elevated stroke risk — while noting herself that it could reflect the population studied.
- **Only liraglutide and semaglutide permit genuinely slow titration**, because their pens can be manipulated in a more nuanced way.
- **A history of pancreatitis is not an absolute contraindication to a GLP-1.** The overall pancreatitis risk is very low and has not borne out statistically in the trials.
- **Hypertriglyceridemia is a reason to prescribe a GLP-1, not to avoid one.** "You're treating the problem with the GLP-1." The guest has never seen recurrent pancreatitis in a hypertriglyceridemic patient whose triglycerides she lowered this way.
- **The thyroid cancer signal is detection bias.** Patients on a GLP-1 are more likely to get a thyroid ultrasound, which finds a micropapillary cancer — small, nearly benign, no survival impact — some 10–15% of the time. **Advanced thyroid cancer has never been seen in the GLP-1 trials.**
- **There is no increased risk of pancreatic cancer, even with a family history** — no reason to withhold a GLP-1 on that basis.
- **GIP agonism appears to let the GLP-1 component work with fewer GI side effects** — the mechanistic reason tirzepatide is better tolerated than its potency would predict, based on animal data.
- **The weight loss gap between obesity and diabetes trials may be about the counselling, not the drug.** In a weight-loss trial nobody cares about the A1c, so participants get extensive diet advice and learn how to eat; in diabetes trials "they're just given the med."

## Clinical Framework

### A1c targets, stratified

| Patient | Target |
|---|---|
| Young, healthy, few comorbidities | **<7%, and ideally <6.5%** |
| Moderately sick, some cardiovascular or microvascular complications | **<7.5%** — UKPDS showed <7.5% much better than >8% |
| Older, sicker, limited life expectancy | **Prevent the complications of hyperglycemia**; the number recedes |

Once a patient is around 7.5%, the guest's emphasis shifts from the number to agent selection: statin adherence, an SGLT2 inhibitor if there is kidney disease, a GLP-1 if there is heart disease. "It's less about the A1c at that point."

### Working up the steep A1c rise

1. **Family history of diabetes** — does the story make sense as familial type 2?
2. **GAD65 antibody** (glutamic acid decarboxylase 65) — the most prevalent islet cell antibody in autoimmune diabetes. Send it and get it out of the way.
3. **C-peptide with a simultaneous glucose**, non-fasting. Not mandatory up front; reserve it for the patient who is not responding to the next step. **A low C-peptide despite a high glucose is the suspicious combination.**
4. **Catabolic symptoms** — weight loss and polyuria together — which are the clinical read on the same question.
5. **Reversible contributors** — juice, soda and milk intake; a recent intra-articular steroid injection.

### The three goals the agent has to serve

The guest returns to this as the organizing idea for agent choice: current guidelines ask for **weight loss and glucose control at the same time in every patient with obesity and diabetes**, and the agent should also deliver **organ protection**. Potency matters, but an agent that serves all three "really pays off."

### Where the newer-agent evidence actually points

The argument the guest and hosts build together, worth keeping intact because it changes how a target is justified:

- Older trials pushed A1c down hard over 10–20 years and struggled to demonstrate outcome benefit.
- The SGLT2 and GLP-1 cardiovascular outcome trials showed benefit in under five years **with the arms deliberately matched on glucose control**, so the benefit could not be attributed to A1c.
- Therefore the A1c is a poor proxy for what the treatment is doing, and the combination that matters is good glucose control *achieved with* an agent that carries independent benefit — "a dual effect that we're all trying to achieve now."

## Drug and Dosing Detail

### Metformin

- **Never wrong unless contraindicated.** Do not initiate below eGFR 30.
- **Maximum effective dose 2000 mg/day.** The package insert permits 2550 mg; the incremental benefit above 2000 mg is minimal.
- **Extended release is the default in pharmacist-run titration programs** — they start there and skip immediate release entirely, because of GI intolerance.
- **Cost is no longer a reason to avoid ER.** Generics are tier 1 and priced the same as immediate release — **provided you do not prescribe Glumetza**, a brand micronized metformin the guest calls "completely unaffordable."
- **The conversion changes the formulation, not the dose.** 1 g twice daily stays 1 g twice daily. ER comes only as 500 mg tablets, so that is two tablets twice daily, which patients dislike until they see the tablet is smaller.
- **All four tablets can technically be taken at once. The guest does not do this** — in her experience the effect is not truly 24 hours, so she splits it into two and two despite that sounding counterintuitive for an extended-release product.
- **Evidence caveat, volunteered:** the randomized tolerability evidence belongs to Glumetza, which is a different micronized formulation, not to generic metformin ER. The ER switch nonetheless works in her hands **60–70% of the time.**
- **Nighttime titration** — start 500 mg at night, increase at night, move to daytime once tolerance develops — is endorsed, with the note that **ER allows faster titration**, so the 250 mg-at-night approach is rarely necessary.

### SGLT2 inhibitors — the A1c threshold

- **Policy: get the A1c below 9% before starting one.** The guest breaks this rule occasionally, but the side effect at higher A1c is predictable enough to plan around.
- **Why:** glycosuria is proportional to hyperglycemia, so at a very high A1c both the genital mycotic infection risk and the polyuria are much worse. The polyuria at that level is "really miserable."
- **Severity worth knowing:** the guest has seen "pretty serious perineal tinea" — a patient can require **antifungal treatment for months** if the drug is started at the wrong moment. The problem occurs in men and women, and is more often volunteered by women.
- **Ask about current yeast infection before prescribing.** The worst move is starting an SGLT2 inhibitor in someone who already has one, and patients frequently do not connect the two.

### GLP-1 receptor agonists

- **Potency is comparable to insulin**, with weight loss on top — the reason the guest reaches for one in a symptomatic, very hyperglycemic patient with obesity.
- **Class effect is not uniform.** Exenatide and the other early agents homologous to Gila monster venom have not shown cardiovascular benefit and are distinct from the current generation. **Liraglutide showed it first**, followed by semaglutide and dulaglutide.
- **Semaglutide for stroke risk** — the reduction in the semaglutide trials is described as very impressive, and the guest selects it for patients with a prior TIA or elevated stroke risk.
- **Slow titration is only really possible with liraglutide (daily) or semaglutide**, because of how finely those pens can be adjusted.
- **Oral semaglutide** is the option for a patient who will not inject — "probably the best idea if she can afford it."
- **Introducing the injection:** keep the device physically available in the room so it can be produced casually rather than ceremonially, and show manufacturer instructional videos.

### GLP-1 and pancreatitis — the decision rules

Pancreatitis etiology in patients with diabetes, as the guest breaks it down: **roughly half gallstone or gallstone sludge plus hypertriglyceridemia, about 20% alcohol, and the remainder idiopathic.** The idiopathic group is the one that warrants caution.

**Do not prescribe:**
- A **family history of pancreatitis** suggesting a heterozygous **CFTR** mutation — not cystic fibrosis, but a real pancreatitis predisposition, and not uncommon. "Family history is gold, if you have it."
- **Ongoing alcohol use.**
- **Pancreas divisum.**
- **Personal history of pancreatic cancer**, or a **pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor.**

**Prescribe, going slowly:**
- A single prior episode in a patient with gallstone risk factors — likely gallstone sludge.

**Prescribe without hesitation:**
- After **cholecystectomy** — "full speed ahead."
- **Hypertriglyceridemia**, including familial hypertriglyceridemia with triglycerides in the thousands. These patients respond well, and the GLP-1 treats the cause. Note the bidirectional relationship the hosts raise: uncontrolled diabetes raises triglycerides, so the two travel together.

### GLP-1 and malignancy

- **Thyroid:** retrospective studies show more thyroid cancer *found* in GLP-1 users, which the guest attributes to detection bias — these patients are more likely to get an ultrasound, which finds micropapillary cancers that do not affect survival in perhaps 10–15% of cases. **Advanced thyroid cancer has not appeared in the trials.**
- **Pancreas:** no increased risk of pancreatic cancer, **even with a family history**, and no reason to withhold therapy on that basis.

### Tirzepatide

- **Six dose steps**, designed so the patient starts low and goes slow — which the guest reads as a deliberately patient-centred design.
- **The GIP mechanism:** dual GIP/GLP-1 agonism appears to let the GLP-1 component do its work **with fewer GI side effects**, based on animal data. "It's really the GIP allowing the GLP-1 to do its job without hurting the patient."
- **Weight loss of 20–30%** in a fair number of patients in the obesity trials, **less in the diabetes trials** — as is always the case.
- **At recording, the cardiovascular outcome data were not yet available**, and the guest says explicitly that she wants to see them before being confident. See Conflicts.

### DPP-4 inhibitors

Used specifically in the older patient with cognitive impairment where the alternatives are poor:

- **"They can sometimes help to move an A1c from 9 to in the 8 range, and maybe protect somebody from more aggressive consistent hyperglycemia."**
- **"They're safe, obviously, in advancing kidney disease."**
- **"They're not potent."** The guest is explicit that when a DPP-4 inhibitor is not going to be enough, she moves on rather than persisting with it.

### Sulfonylureas — two scenarios where the guest uses them

Both are reached only when the alternatives are foreclosed:

1. **The older patient with cognitive impairment** where a DPP-4 inhibitor will not move the number enough. **Low-dose glimepiride**, chosen over insulin because insulin "was going to change her life, it was going to change her family's life" and "it puts her at risk" — someone has to come and inject a patient who does not live with family.
2. **The very hyperglycemic patient who cannot access a GLP-1 and is too high for an SGLT2.** A **temporary sulfonylurea with metformin ER** to make progress on the A1c. "I like the temporary sulfonylurea even in a patient with obesity who cannot access the GLP-1 and is too high for the SGLT2. **It works.**"

## Diagnostic and Exam Detail

- **GAD65 antibody** as the single screening test for adult-onset autoimmune diabetes in a patient whose A1c rises steeply.
- **C-peptide with a simultaneous non-fasting glucose**, held in reserve for the patient who does not respond as expected.
- **A cardiovascular risk score** (ASCVD or AHA 2018) run explicitly to inform agent choice, with **13–15% ten-year risk** as the region where it starts to drive the decision.
- **A direct question about current genital mycotic infection** before starting an SGLT2 inhibitor, framed by describing the side effect first so the patient volunteers it.
- **Adherence verification that goes past the pill box.** The guest's position is that a family's confident report is not evidence, and that discovering a stopped medication still being taken is common enough to check for actively.
- **Family history in two places:** for diabetes, to confirm the type 2 story; and for pancreatitis, to surface a possible CFTR heterozygote before prescribing a GLP-1.

## Errors and Misconceptions

- **Using a threshold A1c — 10%, or any number — to decide when to start insulin**, rather than reading the patient's symptoms and catabolic state.
- **Missing adult-onset type 1** in a patient whose obesity makes type 2 the comfortable story.
- **Reading ACCORD as a straightforward argument for loose targets.** The mortality signal was not hypoglycemia-mediated, and the patients who reached <6.5% did best; it was failure to reach target in the intensive arm that marked risk.
- **Judging a regimen by the A1c it produces rather than by how it produces it.**
- **Abandoning metformin for GI intolerance without trying extended release** — and assuming the patient who "doesn't feel good on it" is still taking it.
- **Prescribing Glumetza** when generic metformin ER is tier 1 and priced like immediate release.
- **Assuming extended release means once daily.** The guest's experience is that the effect does not reliably last 24 hours.
- **Starting an SGLT2 inhibitor at a very high A1c**, producing predictable and sometimes prolonged genital mycotic infection and miserable polyuria.
- **Starting an SGLT2 inhibitor without asking about a current yeast infection.**
- **Treating a prior pancreatitis episode as an absolute contraindication to a GLP-1** — and, conversely, withholding a GLP-1 from a patient with hypertriglyceridemia, for whom it is treatment.
- **Withholding a GLP-1 for a family history of pancreatic cancer**, where there is no increased risk.
- **Treating the thyroid cancer association as causal** rather than as surveillance bias.
- **Assuming all GLP-1 agonists carry cardiovascular benefit** — the early agents do not.
- **Accepting a full pill box as evidence of adherence.**

## Case Examples

### Ms. J — the steep rise, then the escalation

A 39-year-old woman with hypertension, dyslipidemia, class 3 obesity and new-onset diabetes. **A1c 6.4% for two years, now 9.6%.** Symptomatic with polyuria and generally feeling unwell, after a period of holiday eating and reduced activity.

The red flags are her age and the steepness of the rise; the reassuring features are the class 3 obesity and, if present, a family history. The plan is to send a **GAD65**, consider a C-peptide with glucose, look for a reversible contributor, and treat — with a **GLP-1 receptor agonist** given the potency needed and the obesity, or metformin with lifestyle change, both of which the guidelines support. The decision that matters most is bringing her back soon.

**At follow-up several months later**, she has not managed the lifestyle changes, has gained weight, is not tolerating metformin, and her **A1c is now 11%**. She still wants to avoid injections.

Three moves follow. **Metformin extended release**, because she is probably skipping the immediate-release doses whether or not she realizes it. **Not an SGLT2 inhibitor yet** — at 11% the glycosuria would make her miserable and she very likely already has a yeast infection. **Oral semaglutide** if she can afford it; and if cost forecloses that, a **temporary sulfonylurea alongside metformin ER** to bring the number down while other options are arranged.

**Resolution:** she starts a weekly GLP-1, tolerates it, titrates monthly, attends a diabetes education program, and reaches an **A1c of 7.4%.**

### The patient still taking the stopped drug

An older patient with cognitive impairment whom the guest had transitioned to insulin because of polyuria and weight loss — the end of the natural history of type 2 — with **glimepiride stopped** at that transition. The family reported the pill box was full and correctly taken. She had in fact been **taking the glimepiride the entire time**, alongside the insulin. The guest offers it as the ordinary case rather than a remarkable one: "nonadherence or screwing up the meds is so incredibly common that we just want to make sure we understand that they are taking what we're prescribing."

## Conflicts with Established Positions

**One genuine conflict, one important refinement to how a position is argued, and one trial that has since reported.**

**The conflict — sulfonylureas:**

- **This guest actively recommends sulfonylureas in two scenarios**, and both fall squarely inside the population the committed position covers. First, **low-dose glimepiride in an older patient with cognitive impairment** when a DPP-4 inhibitor will not move the A1c enough — chosen over insulin explicitly because insulin would change the patient's and family's life and "puts her at risk," a real consideration when nobody lives with the patient to inject her. Second, a **temporary sulfonylurea with metformin ER** when cost forecloses a GLP-1 and the A1c is too high to start an SGLT2 inhibitor.
- **Her reasoning is exactly the "backed into a corner" structure the hierarchy already anticipates** — she reaches for the drug only when the better options are genuinely unavailable, and she names the insulin risk that makes the corner real. What the hierarchy adds is the step she does not take: **the substitution rule says that when there is a compelling reason to use a sulfonylurea, the answer is a meglitinide.** Both of her scenarios are compelling-reason cases, and in both the rule would change the drug without changing the reasoning. Meglitinides are not considered anywhere in the episode.
- **Worth carrying forward from her, separately from the drug choice:** the articulation of *why* insulin is not a free alternative in this population. "It was going to change her family's life... it puts her at risk" is the clearest statement in any of these episodes of the burden side of the insulin decision, and it belongs in the deprescribing argument regardless of which agent replaces it.

**The refinement — how the relaxed-target position should be argued:**

- **Do not lean on ACCORD.** The guest's post hoc account is that the mortality signal **was not related to hypoglycemia**, and that **the older patients who actually achieved an A1c below 6.5% had the lowest mortality** — it was patients randomized to tight control who could not get there who died. Read that way, ACCORD is evidence about the hazard of *pushing hard against a patient who cannot respond*, not evidence that a low A1c is itself dangerous.
- This matters because it does not weaken the relaxed-target position; it relocates its support. The argument for relaxed targets in an older population rests on **hypoglycemia harm and treatment burden** — the material in [[Curbsiders 483 - Diabetes in Older Adults, Pregnancy, and Corticosteroid Use]] and [[Curbsiders 454 - Diabetes and Hypertension in CKD]] — not on ACCORD mortality. A chapter that cites ACCORD loosely is citing the weakest available support for a well-supported position.
- The hosts add the complementary point: ACCORD's intensive arm got there on insulin and sulfonylureas, without access to agents that are both potent and cardioprotective.

**Reinforcements:**

- **DPP-4 inhibitors — a third independent confirmation, in almost the same words.** The committed position places them in the first tier on safety rather than potency, with the explicit failure mode that a DPP-4 will not take a patient from 9% to target. This guest reaches for them in precisely the target population (cognitive impairment, advancing kidney disease), gives the same three-part characterization — **can move an A1c from 9 into the 8 range, safe in advancing kidney disease, not potent** — and moves on when that is not enough rather than persisting. After Colburn in [[Curbsiders 433 - CGMs and Glucose Pattern Management]], that is two endocrinologists independently describing the position's logic.
- **Metformin below eGFR 30** — consistent with the renal boundary already recorded.
- **Adherence verification** — the glimepiride case is a medication reconciliation failure of the kind the reconciliation material already covers, with the added detail that a confident family report is not evidence.
- **The three-goals framing** (weight, glucose, organ protection, simultaneously) is the clinical rationale underneath the agent hierarchy's first tier, stated by a different specialist.

**Time-stamped — now resolved:**

- **Tirzepatide's cardiovascular outcome data have reported.** The guest says explicitly that she likes the drug but wants to see the cardiovascular outcomes before being confident. **SURPASS-CVOT** (13,299 patients with type 2 diabetes, BMI ≥25 and established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, median 4 years) compared tirzepatide against **dulaglutide — an active comparator with established cardiovascular benefit, not placebo.** Three-point MACE occurred in **12.2% versus 13.1%**: **noninferiority met (P = 0.003), superiority not met (P = 0.09).** Presented at EASD and published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* on 18 December 2025. The precise reading is that tirzepatide is established as cardiovascularly safe and comparable to a proven agent, not as superior to one. *(Verified 2026-09-12.)*

**Applicability note:**

- **The SGLT2 <9% rule is new to this project and interacts with the CKD position.** The renal indication for an SGLT2 inhibitor is strongest in exactly the patients who may also be very hyperglycemic. This guest's rule implies a **sequence**: bring the glucose down first, then start the SGLT2 inhibitor for its renal and cardiac benefit. That sequencing is not currently carried anywhere in the committed positions and is a candidate for addition.
- **The genital mycotic infection caution may land harder in a facility**, where a resident with cognitive impairment cannot reliably report a yeast infection, the "ask first" step therefore fails, and the polyuria interacts with continence and skin integrity. The rule and the pre-prescription question both need a different owner than the patient.
