## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Vestibular neurologist David Hale (Johns Hopkins) gives a timing-and-triggers framework for undifferentiated dizziness — acute vestibular syndrome, episodic (provoked vs. spontaneous), and chronic — and walks through the bedside exams that go with each bucket: Dix-Hallpike and supine roll for BPPV, HINTS Plus for acute continuous vertigo, and the diagnostic criteria and treatment of PPPD for chronic non-spinning dizziness. Heavy on exam technique, terminology, and the case against chronic meclizine.

## Pearls

- Do not build the differential from how the patient describes the sensation. Clinicians use "dizziness" (impaired spatial awareness without false motion) and "vertigo" (sensation of false motion when no motion is occurring) precisely; patients use dizziness, vertigo, and imbalance interchangeably. The description is not reliable enough to hang a diagnosis on.
- Categorize instead by **timing** and **triggers**, then layer on associated symptoms. Three buckets: acute vestibular syndrome, episodic (subdivided into provoked/triggered vs. spontaneous), and chronic.
- Sorting the patient into a bucket does two jobs at once: it generates the differential *and* it tells you which physical exam to perform. The exams are bucket-specific and are uninterpretable outside their bucket.
- The review of systems is four separate screens, not one: the "dangerous D's" (diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia) for a central neurologic cause; a migraine ROS (headache, light sensitivity, sound sensitivity, visual aura); an ear ROS (hearing loss, especially fluctuating); and a cardiac ROS (palpitations, arrhythmia symptoms) — a neurologist still asks about the heart.
- The differential for **episodic, provoked/positional** dizziness is narrow: BPPV is number one, orthostatic hypotension is number two.
- The differential for **episodic, spontaneous** dizziness centers on TIA, vestibular migraine, and Ménière's disease. Associated symptoms are what separate them.
- TIA is typically brief and does not repeat over and over. A patient with the same episodes recurring over days, weeks, or months is somewhat reassuring against TIA — though isolated dizziness *can* be the sole presentation of a TIA, so vascular risk factors still weight the assessment.
- A useful question to sort continuous from episodic when the history is muddy: "When you are sitting completely still and not moving, are you still dizzy?" The acute vestibular syndrome patient says the sensation of motion is still there regardless; the episodic patient often realizes it settles when they stop moving.
- Screen every non-acute dizzy patient for BPPV regardless of what the history suggests. Missing a condition you could diagnose and treat in the room during the same visit is the avoidable error.
- BPPV diagnosis requires **both** the characteristic nystagmus **and** reproduction of the patient's symptoms. When a patient has symptoms without nystagmus, the first question is whether the nystagmus was missed — it can last only a few seconds, and visual fixation suppresses it.
- Hearing loss accompanying an acute vestibular syndrome is now treated as a **red flag for stroke**, not as a reassuring label of "labyrinthitis." The labyrinth is supplied by the anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA), so an AICA stroke can produce an exam that otherwise localizes peripherally, plus hearing loss. This is what turned HINTS into HINTS Plus. (Exception: an obvious alternative explanation for the hearing loss — purulent ear discharge, vesicles suggesting Ramsay Hunt syndrome.)
- For a peripheral-localizing HINTS exam, every component must be peripheral — it is an AND, not a majority vote. A single central finding means central until proven otherwise.
- Chronic vestibular suppressant use is its own harm: it can produce chronic dizziness and hinders vestibular compensation. Meclizine should be a two-to-three-day course at most, and ideally avoided in favor of an antiemetic.
- Persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (PPPD) is a functional neurologic disorder and accounts for roughly **20% of patients seen in subspecialty vestibular neurology clinics**. It requires a preceding trigger — commonly an untreated or badly managed acute vestibular event.
- Giving the patient a name for their condition and walking them through the diagnostic criteria is itself therapeutic in PPPD; these patients have usually cycled through multiple clinicians and specialties without a diagnosis.
- Vestibular physical therapists are described as expert in essentially all of the above and substantially underutilized. The Vestibular Disorders Association maintains a directory at vestibular.org.

## Clinical Framework

**The three-bucket sort (timing first, then triggers):**

1. **Acute vestibular syndrome (AVS)** — acute, sudden-onset, *continuous* dizziness/vertigo/imbalance, often with nausea and vomiting, that started abruptly and is still happening in front of you. → Use HINTS Plus. Central (ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke) vs. peripheral (vestibular neuritis) is the question.
2. **Episodic vestibular syndrome** — discrete recurring episodes. Subdivide immediately:
   - *Provoked/triggered* (typically positional): BPPV, orthostatic hypotension.
   - *Spontaneous/unprovoked*: TIA, vestibular migraine, Ménière's disease. Vestibular migraine and Ménière's episodes are expected to run somewhat longer than a TIA-length event.
   - → Use Dix-Hallpike + supine roll, plus orthostatic vitals.
3. **Chronic dizziness/vertigo** — → Evaluate the three balance systems (see below); consider PPPD and MdDS once mimics are excluded.

**Exam-to-bucket pairing (the part that is easy to get backwards):**
- Dix-Hallpike and supine roll are for the *episodic/chronic* patient, **not** the AVS patient. In AVS the patient is dizzy regardless of how you move them, so positional testing is the wrong exam and cannot be interpreted.
- HINTS Plus is only valid in the patient with acute-onset *continuous* dizziness. Applied outside that setting the results do not make sense.

**The three systems required for balance** (the organizing schema for chronic dizziness): vision, proprioception/sensation, and the vestibular system. The chronic-dizziness exam is a check that each of the three is working as it should, plus exclusion of neurologic mimics — bilateral vestibulopathy (bilateral vestibular loss) and cerebellar ataxia in particular.

**PPPD diagnostic criteria — the name is the mnemonic:**
- **Persistent** — present more days than not for three months.
- **Postural** — worse when upright and moving.
- **Perceptual** — provoked by visual/perceptual stimuli: busy or crowded places, grocery stores, traffic, scrolling patterns on a phone screen.
- **Dizziness** — characteristically *non-spinning*: rocking, bobbing, or swaying.
- Plus a **preceding trigger**. Proposed mechanism (guest's framing of the current understanding): a form of brain miswiring after the triggering event that leaves the patient visually dependent rather than vestibular-dependent.

**PPPD vs. mal de débarquement syndrome (MdDS):** both present as chronic non-spinning rocking/bobbing/swaying and can look very similar. MdDS is distinguished by its required trigger — onset within a couple of days of exposure to **passive motion** (boat, train, or even a long car ride). It is the normal "getting your land legs back" sensation, except that it persists all day, every day, and does not resolve.

## Drug and Dosing Detail

**Meclizine — the negative pearl of the episode.**
- Acceptable use: a very small prescription, a couple of days' worth, taken only if symptoms are extremely severe. In vestibular neuritis, "two or three days" is the stated ceiling, with perhaps some kept on hand for a particularly bad day.
- Preferred alternative for BPPV: **avoid meclizine if you can and give an antiemetic instead** — whichever antinausea agent you normally use. The goal is specifically to let the patient tolerate being put through the repositioning maneuver without nausea stopping them.
- The harm of chronic use: chronic vestibular suppressant use can itself lead to other forms of **chronic dizziness** and can hinder recovery back to normal by blunting central vestibular compensation. The guest explicitly names chronic meclizine (in a vestibular neuritis patient never referred to vestibular PT) as a route into PPPD.
- Hosts corroborate the common failure mode of inheriting a patient who has been on meclizine in perpetuity for unclear reasons.

**SSRIs / SNRIs for PPPD.**
- Used to treat the chronic dizziness itself, not only comorbid mood symptoms.
- Dosing: **quite low doses, often starting at half of the lowest available dose**, because these patients frequently have medication sensitivity. Titrate up slowly to let the patient become comfortable with the medication. (Guest's practice habit; no specific agent named.)

**Vitamin D.**
- In patients with **repetitive/recurrent episodes of BPPV**, check a vitamin D level. Low vitamin D has been **associated** with recurrent BPPV — the guest is explicit that this is an association, not established causation. Presented as the one lab worth adding in that specific scenario.

**Nothing pharmacologic for BPPV itself.** The treatment is the repositioning maneuver; drugs are only for symptom tolerance.

## Diagnostic and Exam Detail

### Dix-Hallpike (tests the posterior canals)

- **Principle behind both positional tests:** the goal is to align the canal being tested with the plane of the earth — i.e. make that canal parallel to the ground.
- The posterior canal is oriented roughly like the earlobe, or like a big pair of elephant ears sticking out from the head.
- **Technique (right side):** turn the head about **45°** to the right → lay the patient down flat → tilt the head back about **20–30°** so the posterior canal lines up with the ground → ask the patient to keep their eyes open → observe for nystagmus. Repeat on the left.
- **Expected finding:** posterior canal BPPV produces an **upbeat torsional nystagmus** — a mixed nystagmus.
- **Terminology:** name the torsion by the direction it is turning — "with torsion toward the patient's right ear" or "toward the patient's right shoulder." Avoid "clockwise" and "counterclockwise."
- **Positioning workarounds** (for exam tables that are heavy and pushed against a wall): stack pillows under the patient's shoulder blades so the head hangs off the edge of the pillow rather than off the end of the table. Failing that, a reasonably mobile patient can scoot forward in a chair and lean back over the chair's edge with the head tilted backward — often enough to get an adequate Dix-Hallpike position seated.

### Supine roll test (tests the horizontal canals)

- **Technique:** patient flat on their back → tuck the chin in **20°** → turn the head all the way to the right (tests the right horizontal canal), then all the way to the left.
- **Expected finding:** horizontal nystagmus, either **geotropic** (beating toward the earth — right-beating with the head right, left-beating with the head left) or **apogeotropic** (the opposite pattern). The two patterns reflect whether the otoconia are on the canal side or the cupula side.
- Horizontal canal BPPV has many variants and many corresponding maneuvers. Practical advice: master the posterior canal and you will diagnose and treat the majority of BPPV.

### Diagnosing BPPV — the nystagmus caveat

- Textbook answer: you need **both** the characteristic nystagmus and reproduction of the patient's symptoms.
- Practical caveat: set yourself up to actually see it. Nystagmus can be present for only a few seconds, and **patients can suppress vertical and horizontal nystagmus with visual fixation**. Without fixation removed, you may see only the mild torsional component, or nothing.
- **Frenzel goggles** remove visual fixation and are used in the guest's clinic for this reason.
- **Best yield is early morning** — the patient has been lying down overnight letting the otoconia collect, and gets their symptoms rolling over on waking. By the time they reach an acute appointment later that day, or an office visit weeks later, it may be gone.
- **"Subjective" or type 2 BPPV** (symptoms without observable nystagmus) is debated within the vestibular field. If the history is convincing but the exam is negative, reasonable options: refer to vestibular PT with instructions to keep the appointment if symptoms return and cancel if they don't; or empirically perform an Epley on the right *and* the left, which is generally safe. Do **not** empirically perform horizontal canal maneuvers — those get confusing and things can get mixed up.

### Epley maneuver (treatment, posterior canal)

- Go **straight from a positive Dix-Hallpike into the Epley** without sitting the patient back up — the first stage of the Epley is identical to the Dix-Hallpike position.
- Technique emphasis: one **"really good" Epley** — hold the patient's head in each position for a **full minute** and move through the maneuver slowly.
- Follow-up: referral to vestibular physical therapy, or home Epley instructions (canal-specific) for patients capable of doing it themselves. Linking the patient to a reliable video of the modified home Epley is a reasonable adjunct.
- Learning it: practice on a friend or family member to get the feel, and keep a bookmarked video from a reliable source to refresh before you do one.

### Explaining BPPV to patients

- Explain what the vestibular system is *before* starting the exam, so the maneuvers make sense as they happen.
- Use a visual — a 3D inner ear model, or just a picture pulled up on a phone. Point to where the crystals live (the otolith organs — utricle and saccule) and where they get stuck.
- Analogy: the crystal in the thick canal fluid is like snow in a snow globe that has been shaken up. While it swirls it hits all the motion sensors and causes dizziness; once it settles at the bottom of the canal the symptoms stop — which is why the episode is brief, on the order of 30 seconds.

### HINTS Plus (acute vestibular syndrome only)

**H — Head impulse test** (tests CN VIII / the vestibulo-ocular reflex)
- Patient sits upright and fixes on the examiner's nose. Move the head **quickly** through only **10–15°**.
- **No catch-up saccade** (eyes stay on the nose throughout) = normal VOR = **concerning for central** in the AVS patient.
- **Catch-up saccade present** (eyes fall off the target with the head turn, then correct back) = **peripheral localizing**.
- Terminology: do not say positive or negative — nobody knows which is which. Document as "catch-up saccade present with head impulse test" or "no catch-up saccade with head impulse test."
- The guest notes he practiced this on every patient he saw for a full fellowship year before feeling comfortable with it; it is the component that most requires deliberate practice and video review.

**N — Nystagmus**
- Give the patient a small target (the guest uses the end of a reflex hammer) and hold each position for at least **10–15 seconds**: straight ahead (looking for spontaneous nystagmus), full left, full right, full up, full down.
- Identify the direction of the **fast phase**.
- **Peripheral pattern: horizontal and unidirectional** — e.g. left-beating in primary gaze, still left-beating on left gaze, right gaze, up gaze, and down gaze. Mnemonic offered: "horizontal is peripheral."
- **Central patterns:** anything else. Specifically **direction-changing** nystagmus (left-beating on left gaze, right-beating on right gaze), **pure vertical** nystagmus, or **pure torsional** nystagmus.

**TS — Test of skew** (vertical eye misalignment)
- Patient upright, fixing on a **distant** target across the room. Alternate-cover: cover one eye with your hand for **2–3 seconds**, then move to the other eye, back and forth.
- Watch the **just-uncovered** eye for a **vertical** refixation movement. A vertical misalignment in the setting of AVS is a **skew deviation** = **central localizing**.
- Horizontal misalignments are common and can be entirely normal — not what you are looking for.
- A skew deviation is usually obvious, and the patient will typically report vertical diplopia. You should not have to squint to convince yourself of a tiny vertical movement.

**Plus — Hearing**
- New sudden-onset hearing loss at the time of sudden-onset dizziness, in the absence of an obvious ear cause, is a **red flag for a central (AICA) stroke**.

**Interpreting the whole exam**
- Report it as a **peripheral-localizing** or **central-localizing** HINTS exam, not positive/negative.
- Peripheral requires **all** of: catch-up saccade present **and** horizontal unidirectional nystagmus **and** no vertical eye misalignment **and** normal hearing.
- **Any single finding in the central column** → treat as central localizing until proven otherwise.

### Imaging and next steps for a central-localizing exam

- A central-localizing HINTS exam should be treated as a stroke until proven otherwise: if the patient is in the ED and within a thrombolytic window, involve the stroke team; otherwise follow the local stroke pathway.
- MRI does not have to happen the same day, and access varies by institution.
- **Timing caveat:** an MRI obtained within the first **24 hours** may not show the infarct, raising the question of whether a repeat at **72 hours** would change the picture. Whether to repeat is a nuanced risk-benefit discussion, usually with neurology involved.

### Vestibular neuritis vs. labyrinthitis

- Labyrinthitis is the same process **plus acute-onset hearing loss**. But the practice shift is to stop lumping hearing loss in as reassuringly "labyrinthitis" and instead treat it as the red flag described above.

### Phone triage and referral routing

- Sudden-onset, continuous, new dizziness in a patient with vascular risk factors → **emergency department**, unless you can see them yourself promptly.
- History suggestive of BPPV (episodic; provoked by lying down or rolling over in bed) → **vestibular physical therapy**, before a neurology appointment.
- Dizziness with associated hearing change → **ENT**, before neurology.
- Dizziness with headache → **neurology** (vestibular migraine).
- These are general buckets; all three specialties see a mix and share patients.

### Workup before referring chronic dizziness

- An **MRI** is helpful before or at referral — part of making a PPPD diagnosis is confirming nothing else is going on.

## Errors and Misconceptions

- **Over-weighting the patient's description of the sensation.** "Spinning" vs. "lightheaded" vs. "off balance" is not a reliable discriminator; patients have not read the textbook definitions.
- **Using the wrong exam for the bucket.** Dix-Hallpike during an acute vestibular syndrome is uninterpretable (the patient is dizzy with any movement). HINTS outside of acute continuous dizziness is equally uninterpretable.
- **"Positive" / "negative" head impulse or HINTS exam.** Nobody knows which is which; the terminology invites the wrong action. Describe the finding (catch-up saccade present/absent) and localize (peripheral/central).
- **Requiring a majority of HINTS components to be central before worrying.** One central finding is enough.
- **Concluding "no nystagmus, therefore no BPPV."** More often the nystagmus was missed — it can be seconds long and is suppressible by visual fixation.
- **Reading hearing loss with acute vertigo as benign labyrinthitis.** AICA territory stroke does this.
- **Assuming isolated dizziness cannot be a TIA.** It can, though a pattern of many repeated identical episodes over weeks argues against it.
- **Leaving patients on meclizine indefinitely.** Chronic vestibular suppressant use causes chronic dizziness and blocks compensation; it is a named pathway into PPPD.
- **Delaying vestibular PT until symptoms improve.** The referral should go in as soon as the patient can get an appointment; the therapist calibrates intensity to where the patient is, and earlier start means better recovery.

## Case Examples

**Case 1 — episodic, provoked (BPPV).** Kathy, 65, with hypertension, hyperlipidemia, migraines, and osteoporosis, presents with a week of dizziness interfering with her grocery store job. History: room-spinning sensation, triggered by turning her head, lasting about a minute, with nausea, no hearing loss. Episodic + clear positional trigger narrows the differential to BPPV vs. orthostatic hypotension. Orthostatic vitals normal; right Dix-Hallpike produces upbeat torsional nystagmus with reproduction of her vertigo. Managed with an in-office Epley proceeding directly out of the Dix-Hallpike position, a home Epley video, and a vitamin D level given recurrent episodes.

**Case 2 — acute vestibular syndrome (vestibular neuritis).** Joe, 50, with hypertension and type 2 diabetes, presents with one day of sudden-onset, persistent vertigo, nausea, vomiting, and gait instability. Negatives: no headache, no neck pain, no hearing change, no visual change, no dysarthria, no dysphagia, no sensory change, no weakness, no falls. Positive: recent cold. Despite the reassuring ROS, the vascular risk factors mean the exam decides it. HINTS Plus shows a catch-up saccade with head impulse and otherwise meets peripheral criteria including normal hearing → vestibular neuritis, no hospital transfer. Counseling: a one-time episode caused by inflammation of the nerve connecting the brain to the ear, producing low vestibular function on one side and an imbalance between the two sides; expect some dizziness and imbalance for weeks to months; vestibular PT will train the other side to pick up the slack. Meclizine limited to two to three days.

**Case 3 — chronic, non-spinning (PPPD).** Joan, 30, with depression and anxiety with panic disorder, presents with three months of daily dizziness that began after a panic attack on a crowded bus. Described as swaying, as if on a boat, without spinning, present most of the day, worse walking through traffic or grocery stores and when more anxious. Orthostatic vitals, Dix-Hallpike, and HINTS Plus are all normal. Meets all four PPPD criteria with an identifiable precipitating event. Management: deliver and explain the diagnosis; vestibular PT or other engaging movement; low-dose SSRI/SNRI started at half the lowest dose; CBT or health psychology for the functional and mood impact.

## Conflicts with Established Positions

None identified. Nothing here contradicts the LTC Handbook's committed positions (anticoagulation, NSAIDs, sulfonylureas, clonidine/hydralazine, hospice threshold, thickened liquids, fall rate, EOL symptom management).

Two points **reinforce** rather than conflict with existing positions and are worth carrying forward as such:
- The case against chronic meclizine (chronic dizziness, blunted vestibular compensation) is an independent, mechanistic argument that sits alongside the standard anticholinergic/Beers-criteria argument against it in older adults. Both point the same direction; the vestibular argument is the more persuasive one for a patient or family who feels the drug "helps."
- Orthostatic hypotension as the number-two cause of positional dizziness is directly relevant to any position on antihypertensive intensity and deprescribing in frail older adults — a dizzy LTC resident deserves orthostatics before a vestibular workup.
