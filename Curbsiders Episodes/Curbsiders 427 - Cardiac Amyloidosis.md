## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Heart failure/transplant cardiologist Michelle Kittleson works through cardiac amyloidosis as a diagnosis hiding inside routine HFpEF: the extracardiac red flags that should raise suspicion, the strict sequence of tests required to sort AL from ATTR without killing a patient on a false-positive scan, tafamidis and the access problem that comes with it, and — the part most relevant to a primary care or long-term care panel — why standard heart failure guideline-directed medical therapy is actively harmful in these patients and should be stopped.

## Pearls

- HFpEF is a diagnosis of exclusion. There is no sign that waves and says "I'm HFpEF." Before accepting it, ask whether it is even the heart's fault — protein-wasting kidney disease, a liver not making protein, and portal hypertension all produce dyspnea and edema with a normal ejection fraction.
- Once it is the heart's fault, the next question is whether this is garden-variety HFpEF (older, female, hypertension, diabetes, coronary disease, chronic kidney disease) or an underlying process with disease-directed therapy behind it. Stopping at "HFpEF" forecloses treatment that could make the patient feel better, stay out of the hospital, and live longer.
- Amyloidosis used to be a medical school fact you learned and forgot because it did not change management. Two developments in the past decade changed that: non-invasive diagnosis with bone scintigraphy, and a therapy that works. It now matters to make the diagnosis.
- Amyloid fibrils do not deposit only in the heart. The extracardiac deposits are the clinical clues — seemingly unrelated findings that combine into a unifying diagnosis.
- In a case series of patients hospitalized with decompensated HFpEF who were all tested, roughly 15% had ATTR amyloidosis. In a separate case series of patients with severe aortic stenosis presenting for transcatheter valve implantation, roughly 15% had concomitant ATTR amyloidosis. The two conditions coexist in older people, and there is no reason to treat only one of them.
- A "rare disease" quoted at a handful of cases per million cannot also be 15% of hospitalized HFpEF. The reconciliation is that the disease is underdiagnosed, not rare — heart failure symptoms are the tip of the iceberg.
- "Left ventricular hypertrophy" is the wrong phrase for an echo report. Say **increased LV wall thickness**. Thickness may come from muscle (hypertrophy) or from infiltration into muscle; writing "LVH" silently amputates the differential.
- The single most useful starting point is an increased LV wall thickness you cannot explain. If there is no hypertension and no antihypertensives, that is the tingle on the back of the neck.
- Pronounced intolerance to a vasodilating antihypertensive — a patient started on amlodipine who returns a week later near-syncopal on standing — is a clue to amyloid autonomic dysfunction, not just an overshoot.
- Carpal tunnel syndrome above age 60 is not common. Bilateral carpal tunnel above 60 is a red flag. In case series of patients undergoing carpal tunnel or lumbar spinal stenosis surgery whose specimens went to pathology, amyloid deposition was present and preceded cardiac manifestations by six to seven years.
- Biceps tendon rupture (the Popeye sign) belongs on the same list of tendon/canal deposits.
- AL and ATTR are two distinct diseases in demographics, tempo, and treatment — not two flavors of one. ATTR is a disease of people over 60, more typically 70s–80s, whether wild type or variant. AL can present at any age and often presents acutely: thick walls, bad heart failure, restrictive physiology, cardiogenic shock.
- Macroglossia and periorbital purpura are unique to AL. The orthopedic findings — carpal tunnel, biceps tendon rupture, spinal stenosis — are classic for ATTR. Neuropathy occurs in both.
- Median survival of untreated AL amyloidosis is under a year. An abnormal light chain result in a patient with a thick heart is a same-week problem, not a next-available-appointment problem.
- Male sex carries higher risk than female for amyloidosis in both wild type and variant forms. Present hypertension argues *against* the diagnosis, because it supplies an alternative explanation for the thick walls.
- Only about 30% of patients meet the classic definition of low voltage on ECG in one case series. Do not write off the diagnosis because voltage is not low — look instead for **voltage discordant with wall thickness**, including voltage that is merely normal when the walls are thick enough that it should not be.
- The V122I variant is present in 3.4% of Black Americans. That does not mean 3.4% will develop ATTR amyloidosis — penetrance and expressivity are variable — but it is a meaningful piece of family and social history when assembling the diagnosis.
- Amyloid patients live in a narrow therapeutic window of euvolemia: slightly wet and they are severely short of breath, slightly dry and they faint. Diuretic therapy here is not set-it-and-forget-it.
- Guest's framing of the division of labor: in AL, the hematologist is in charge and the cardiologist is a foot soldier managing volume. In ATTR, the cardiologist drives the bus.
- Guest's own rule, offered on trial interpretation: the road to bad outcomes is paved with plausible pathophysiology, surrogate endpoints, and wishful thinking.

## Clinical Framework

### What amyloidosis is

A protein the body produces naturally folds into abnormal configurations, forms fibrils, and deposits into tissues. The two forms found in the heart:

- **AL** — immunoglobulin light chain. A plasma cell dyscrasia on the spectrum from MGUS at one end to multiple myeloma at the other.
- **ATTR** — transthyretin (historically called prealbumin), a transport protein that circulates as a stable tetramer until it destabilizes into fibrils.

### The two ATTR subtypes

- **ATTRv (variant, formerly "mutant," ATTR-M)** — a mutation in the *TTR* gene.
- **ATTRwt (wild type)** — no mutation; the normal protein misbehaves. Formerly called senile cardiac amyloidosis; the term was retired because it hurt patients' feelings.
- Either may be suffixed **-CM** for cardiomyopathy in the literature (e.g. ATTRwt-CM).

### Where the fibrils go — the red-flag map

**Cardiac:** increased LV wall thickness, HFpEF-pattern heart failure, restrictive physiology, persistent troponin elevation with normal coronaries, atrial fibrillation (highly prevalent), AV block and pacemaker requirement, coexisting aortic stenosis.

**Neurologic:** sensory peripheral neuropathy; autonomic dysfunction presenting as orthostatic lightheadedness, erectile dysfunction, or dramatic intolerance of vasodilating antihypertensives.

**Musculoskeletal / orthopedic (ATTR-typical):** carpal tunnel syndrome, especially bilateral and over age 60; lumbar spinal stenosis; biceps tendon rupture (Popeye sign). These may precede cardiac manifestations by six to seven years.

**AL-specific:** macroglossia, periorbital purpura.

### Risk scoring

An ATTR cardiomyopathy risk score published in *JAMA Cardiology* 2022 (first author Davies, senior author Redfield) assigns points across clinical factors: age (60–70, and men over 80 score heavily), male sex, absence of hypertension, increased wall thickness, and relative wall thickness of the septum and posterior wall. It produces a numeric sense of pretest suspicion rather than a yes/no.

### The diagnostic algorithm — order matters

1. **Monoclonal protein screen first, always.** Three tests together: serum immunofixation electrophoresis, urine immunofixation electrophoresis, and serum free light chains (kappa/lambda ratio).
2. **If any is abnormal:** hematology now, and tissue diagnosis. Do not proceed to scintigraphy as if it settles anything.
3. **If all are negative:** technetium-99m pyrophosphate (Tc-PYP) scintigraphy.
4. **Negative monoclonal screen + positive Tc-PYP = ATTR amyloidosis, no biopsy needed.**
5. **Then genetic testing** for *TTR*, to separate variant from wild type — which determines cascade screening of first-degree relatives and eligibility for variant-only therapies.

The sequence is not stylistic. About 10% of patients with AL amyloidosis have a false-positive Tc-PYP scan. Labeling one of them ATTR on the strength of a scan alone, in a disease with under one year of median untreated survival, is a fatal error.

## Drug and Dosing Detail

### ATTR — tafamidis (the only approved option)

- **Mechanism:** stabilizes the TTR tetramer so it does not destabilize into fibrils. It prevents progression; it does not reverse deposits already present.
- **Indication:** FDA approved for any patient with transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis and symptoms — wild type or variant alike.
- **Evidence — ATTR-ACT, *New England Journal of Medicine* 2018:** 440 patients randomized to tafamidis vs. placebo. **13% absolute** (not relative) risk reduction in mortality at 30 months; 30-month mortality 43% → 30%.
- **Two features of the trial that change how it is used:**
  - Survival curves did not separate until **18 months**. This follows from the mechanism — halting progression takes time to show up. A patient with a short horizon may not live to the benefit.
  - On subgroup analysis (hypothesis-generating, as always), **the less sick the patient, the greater the benefit** — consistent with a drug that freezes you where you are. Earlier is better.
- **Monitoring and side effects:** effectively none. The guest has never had a patient stop it for a side effect; no liver, kidney, rash, or GI signal; nothing in the trials. Described as a set-it-and-forget-it medication.
- **Formulations:** the 61 mg single-capsule form and the 80 mg form given as 20 mg capsules have equivalent efficacy. Patients have specifically asked the guest to prescribe the 20 mg capsules so they can take fewer than prescribed and stretch a fill further — a workaround worth recognizing as a marker of cost distress rather than nonadherence.
- **Cost and access:** quoted at **$225,000 per year** at launch. Prior authorization paperwork is described as onerous enough on its own to justify referral to a specialist; the guest's office employs a nurse whose job is essentially tafamidis paperwork (36 signatures in one January day, for fewer than 36 patients). Getting it affordable is a negotiation between insurer and manufacturer patient assistance programs. Some patients cannot afford it.

### ATTR — diflunisal (the fallback)

An NSAID with some TTR-stabilizing properties. Never rigorously tested in a randomized trial in cardiomyopathy. Offered as an alternative when tafamidis is unaffordable.

### ATTR — the silencers

Mechanism sits alongside stabilization in a tidy three-option pathophysiologic scheme: **stop producing the protein (silencers), stabilize it (tafamidis), or clear it from tissue (antibodies).**

- **Agents:** patisiran, inotersen, vutrisiran, eplontersen — mRNA-directed agents that stop TTR production. Subcutaneous or intravenous. Vutrisiran is the most convenient at every three months subcutaneously.
- **Indication as of this episode:** **variant ATTR with polyneuropathy only.** Shown to reduce progression of neuropathy; that is what they are approved for. **Not approved and not shown to benefit the cardiomyopathy.**
- **APOLLO-B:** patisiran added on top of tafamidis (which could not be withheld, being approved), placebo-controlled, primary endpoint six-minute walk distance at 12 months. Walk distance declined in both arms — it is a progressive disease — but declined less with patisiran. **The FDA was not impressed and did not approve it.** The guest agrees with that decision: an IV infusion costing tens of thousands of dollars every three weeks so that at one year the patient walks slightly farther than they otherwise would have, but still less than before, is not obviously worth the squeeze.

### ATTR — on the horizon at the time of the episode

A monoclonal antibody that binds only to fibrils and triggers antibody-mediated phagocytosis — the "suck it out of tissue" arm of the scheme. Phase 2 trial in the *New England Journal of Medicine* 2023; follow-up Tc-PYP scans and MRIs looked better after therapy, i.e. it appears capable of reversing deposition rather than merely halting it. Phase 3 pending at the time of recording.

> [!note] Since this episode aired (February 2024) — not from the episode
> Several of the open questions above have resolved, and anyone mining this note for current practice needs them:
> - **Acoramidis** (a second TTR stabilizer) was FDA approved in November 2024 for ATTR-CM on the strength of ATTRibute-CM.
> - **Vutrisiran** was FDA approved in March 2025 for ATTR-CM based on HELIOS-B, which used hard endpoints (all-cause mortality and recurrent cardiovascular events). This is exactly the "harder endpoints, results due 2024–2025" trial the guest was waiting on, and it read out positive — so the silencer class is no longer neuropathy-only.
> - **Eplontersen** remains under study for cardiomyopathy in CARDIO-TTRansform.
> - The anti-fibril antibody (ALXN2220, formerly NI006) is in phase 3 (DepleTTR-CM), not approved.
>
> The episode's framing — tafamidis as the sole option — is therefore historically accurate but out of date. Everything else in this note about diagnosis, red flags, and GDMT cautions stands unchanged.

### AL — hematologist-directed

Plasma-cell-directed therapy is the hematologist's call: daratumumab, cyclophosphamide, bortezomib, dexamethasone (DaraCyBorD) and related regimens. The cardiologist's job during that is optimizing fluid balance. If free light chains become undetectable, the heart may begin to improve — measured as reduced diuretic requirement and less congestion — but on a lag of six to nine months or longer, and only maybe.

### Guideline-directed medical therapy — the deprescribing pearl

This is the highest-yield section for a primary care or long-term care panel, because it inverts the reflex.

**Why:** a restrictive cardiomyopathy has a **fixed stroke volume**. In a normal HFrEF heart, vasodilation raises stroke volume and cardiac output improves. With a fixed stroke volume, vasodilation cannot be answered by a bigger stroke volume, so the heart compensates with rate. These patients do not tolerate vasodilation, and they are rate-dependent for cardiac output.

- **ACE inhibitors / ARBs / ARNI / vasodilating antihypertensives (e.g. amlodipine):** frequently poorly tolerated. Guest's practice: if a patient arrives on one and is tolerating it, do not rock the boat — but at **any** excuse (symptomatic orthostasis, worsening kidney function), stop it.
- **Beta blockers: stop them.** Blunting heart rate in a fixed-stroke-volume patient is directly harmful, and beta blockers have no role in HFpEF anyway. Guest's observation: patients arriving on a high-dose beta blocker and feeling terrible think she is a miracle worker when she stops it.
- The general instruction: let the patient be your guide. Tafamidis is the thing that reduces hospitalization and improves survival; the rest of the regimen exists to make them feel well, and should be subtracted freely when it does not.

### Anticoagulation

**Anticoagulate atrial fibrillation in amyloidosis regardless of CHA₂DS₂-VASc score** — thromboembolic risk is high and is not captured by the score. Guest's list of three conditions where the score does not apply and the answer is simply a DOAC: **cardiac amyloidosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and a bioprosthetic valve** (mechanical valves being on warfarin regardless).

### Symptomatic management

- **Sensory neuropathy:** in variant ATTR, refer to neurology for a silencer, which reduces progression. In wild type, there is no disease-directed option for the neuropathy — fall back on gabapentin, pregabalin, tricyclic antidepressants, duloxetine.
- **Autonomic dysfunction:** midodrine, fludrocortisone, droxidopa, abdominal binders, compression hose.
- **Diuretics:** no fixed dose. The therapeutic window between "short of breath" and "faints" is narrow, and the guest explicitly hands titration to the patient — asking which they prefer, a little swelling or a little lightheadedness, and having them adjust accordingly.

### Supplements and unproven agents

Doxycycline/TUDCA, curcumin, resveratrol, and green tea come up from patients. The guest's position: no evidence any of it works, and taking them mostly makes expensive urine. She does not fight patients who want to take them, but they are not a substitute for tafamidis in anyone who can access it.

## Diagnostic and Exam Detail

### History

Specifically hunt for: absence of hypertension and antihypertensive use in a patient with thick walls; carpal tunnel syndrome (age at onset, bilaterality); lumbar spinal stenosis; biceps tendon rupture; erectile dysfunction; lightheadedness on standing; near-syncope after starting a vasodilator; family history (and note when it is unavailable — an adopted patient loses that channel entirely).

### Physical exam

- **Orthostatic vitals.** A patient with a normal seated blood pressure who reports wooziness on standing should have documented orthostatics; a significant systolic and/or diastolic drop in an otherwise healthy 68-year-old is a finding requiring explanation, not a normal variant.
- **Murmur.** A systolic ejection murmur raises aortic stenosis, which coexists with ATTR often enough (about 15% of severe AS patients presenting for TAVI in one series) that finding one does not excuse you from looking for the other.
- **JVP and volume assessment**, as in any heart failure evaluation.
- The guest is explicit that no single history item, exam finding, or lab establishes the need to look — it is the pattern assembled across all three.

### ECG

Classic teaching is low voltage from infiltration. Reality: about 30% meet the strict low-voltage definition in one case series. The more useful finding is **voltage discordant with wall thickness** — voltage that is normal, or merely lower than the walls would predict.

### Laboratory

BNP will likely be elevated. Troponin may be elevated and may stay elevated with angiographically normal coronaries — resist filing this under "demand ischemia," a wastebasket nobody can define; fibril deposition injures myocytes and releases troponin.

None of this shows up on a CBC or basic metabolic panel. The diagnosis requires specifically ordering the right tests.

### Monoclonal protein screen

**SPEP and UPEP are not sufficient — they are not sensitive enough.** Order **serum immunofixation electrophoresis, urine immunofixation electrophoresis, and serum free light chains (kappa/lambda)**. All three.

### Biopsy, when required

Surrogate sites — fat pad, bone marrow — are convenient but **less sensitive, especially for ATTR**. A negative fat pad biopsy does not end the workup. If the monoclonal screen is abnormal and tissue is required, the patient may ultimately need biopsy of the **affected organ** (heart or kidney), up to and including endomyocardial biopsy, before AL can be excluded.

### Technetium-99m pyrophosphate scintigraphy

- A bone-avid nuclear medicine tracer study. The read is relative brightness of heart versus bone:
  - **Grade 2** — heart equal to bone.
  - **Grade 3** — heart brighter than bone.
  - Grade 2 or 3 is positive; grade 0–1 is not. Guest: it is not rocket science — which one is brighter — but she will have a cardiac radiologist overread an equivocal study.
- **Never interpret it outside the context of the monoclonal protein screen.** ~10% false positive rate in AL.

### Genetic testing and cascade screening

- Straightforward relative to other cardiomyopathy panels: one gene, well-established variant pathogenicity, and known genotype–phenotype patterns (which variants tend toward neuropathy vs. cardiomyopathy, often with geographic clustering). The guest does not consider a genetic counselor essential for the proband here, in contrast to a dilated cardiomyopathy panel of hundreds of genes where variants of uncertain significance are guaranteed and pre-test counseling matters.
- **Cascade screening (per the 2023 ACC Expert Consensus Decision Pathway on cardiac amyloidosis, which the guest chaired):** a first-degree relative who tests gene-positive begins screening with **echocardiogram ± biomarkers ten years before the age at which the index patient first manifested symptoms** — the ten-year buffer accounting for diagnostic delay. If the proband was diagnosed at 75, screening starts at 65.
- **The hard part is the asymptomatic young gene carrier.** Offspring who test positive in their 30s and 40s have decades of nothing to do, and the guest describes real anxiety burden — every skipped beat becomes a question. There is no established management for asymptomatic carriers beyond a calendar reminder decades out. She names this as a genuine problem of too much information.

### Cardiac MRI — when it is *not* needed

- **Not required to diagnose amyloidosis.** If the monoclonal screen is negative and the Tc-PYP is positive, the diagnosis is made and an MRI is a waste of a stretched health system's resources.
- **Where it earns its place:**
  - A genuinely broad differential — a younger or atypical patient where hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, sarcoidosis, hemochromatosis, pericardial constriction, or idiopathic restrictive cardiomyopathy are all live. MRI images muscle and pericardium well and sorts those.
  - A patient with tissue-proven AL amyloidosis elsewhere, where the hematologist needs to know whether the heart is involved because it changes the chemotherapy regimen — cardiac involvement established without another biopsy.

## Errors and Misconceptions

- **Stopping at HFpEF.** It is a diagnosis of exclusion, and treating it as an endpoint is how a treatable infiltrative cardiomyopathy goes unfound.
- **Writing "LVH" on an echo report.** Increased wall thickness is the accurate phrase; "hypertrophy" asserts a mechanism and prunes the differential before it is earned.
- **Believing amyloidosis is rare.** It is underdiagnosed. If you do not think of it, you will not look for it.
- **Requiring low voltage on ECG.** Only ~30% have it by strict criteria.
- **Ordering SPEP/UPEP as the monoclonal screen.** Not sensitive enough — immunofixation of serum and urine plus free light chains is the correct order.
- **Ordering the Tc-PYP scan first because it is easier.** A positive scan without a negative monoclonal screen is not a diagnosis. Ten percent of AL patients have false-positive scans, and misassigning an AL patient to ATTR — median untreated survival under a year — is, in the guest's words, killing the patient.
- **Stopping at a negative fat pad biopsy.** Surrogate sites are insensitive, particularly for ATTR; the affected organ may need to be biopsied.
- **Reflexively getting a cardiac MRI.** Ambient enthusiasm for cardiac MRI does not make it a specific test for amyloidosis, and it is unnecessary once the two-test algorithm is complete.
- **Applying CHA₂DS₂-VASc to atrial fibrillation in amyloidosis.** Anticoagulate regardless; thrombus risk here does not track the score.
- **Applying HFpEF/HFrEF guideline-directed medical therapy reflexively.** Vasodilators and beta blockers are poorly tolerated to harmful in a fixed-stroke-volume, rate-dependent patient.
- **Calling a persistently elevated troponin with clean coronaries "demand ischemia"** and filing it away, rather than asking what is injuring the myocytes.
- **Attributing carpal tunnel in a 68-year-old manual laborer to repetitive use** without noticing that new carpal tunnel over 60 is itself atypical.

## Case Examples

**The episode's running case (built by the hosts, not a real patient):** a 68-year-old man seen for a transitions-of-care visit after a first hospitalization for dyspnea, discharged with a diagnosis of heart failure. Echo showed increased LV wall thickness, EF 55%, grade 2 diastolic dysfunction. Rate-controlled atrial fibrillation. Troponin 0.5 with a normal coronary angiogram. Treated with IV furosemide and started on an SGLT2 inhibitor; discharged on furosemide 40 mg.

History added on the second pass: no prior hospitalizations since a childhood tonsillectomy, no hypertension, no antihypertensives, on aspirin for primary prevention only. Wears wrist braces for pain at work as a carpenter. Adopted — no family history available.

Exam: asymptomatic on the day, blood pressure 120/80, heart rate 70, regular pulse, faint systolic ejection murmur at the right sternal border, JVP ~6 cm. Reports feeling woozy standing up quickly.

The assembled pattern: unexplained wall thickening without hypertension, persistent troponin elevation with clean coronaries, atrial fibrillation, carpal tunnel at an atypical age, and orthostatic symptoms with a normal seated blood pressure. Worked up as scripted — negative immunofixation, positive Tc-PYP — he lands on ATTR amyloidosis without a biopsy.

**The specialist's real-world illustration of urgency:** a community colleague phones about a patient with heart failure, a markedly abnormal kappa/lambda ratio, and a very thick heart. The guest's response is to see the patient that week, get the hematologist to see them that week, obtain tissue, and start treatment — explicitly refusing to let it queue behind routine referral timelines.

## Conflicts with Established Positions

Two genuine tensions with LTC Handbook committed positions, both worth resolving explicitly rather than absorbing silently:

- **CONFLICT (partial) — anticoagulation.** The guest's position is categorical: anticoagulate atrial fibrillation in cardiac amyloidosis regardless of CHA₂DS₂-VASc, because thromboembolic risk is high and score-independent. Any handbook position that routes AF anticoagulation decisions through CHA₂DS₂-VASc, or that leans toward withholding in low-score or frail residents, needs a stated amyloidosis exception (alongside hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and bioprosthetic valve, which the guest groups with it). This is a carve-out to the framework, not a repudiation of it — but it has to be written down or it will be lost.
- **CONFLICT — diflunisal as an NSAID.** Diflunisal is offered here as the fallback TTR stabilizer for patients who cannot afford tafamidis. It is an NSAID, and it would be used chronically, in older adults with heart failure, frequently on anticoagulation — precisely the population an NSAID-avoidance position exists to protect. The handbook's NSAID position should not be quietly overridden by an amyloidosis chapter. Practically, the conflict is smaller than it looks: diflunisal has never been tested in a randomized cardiomyopathy trial, so the evidence supporting the exception is thin, and as of 2025 a second stabilizer (acoramidis) exists as an alternative access pathway.

**Reinforcing rather than conflicting:**

- The stop-the-beta-blocker, stop-the-vasodilator instruction is an independent, mechanistic argument for exactly the deprescribing the handbook already argues for on other grounds. In an amyloid patient the argument is stronger than the usual one — it is not "the benefit is small in this population," it is "the physiology runs backward here."
- Orthostatic hypotension and the narrow euvolemia window are directly relevant to fall risk. An amyloid resident is a patient for whom the fall-prevention and blood-pressure-target positions collide, and the resolution favors accepting swelling over accepting lightheadedness.
- The tafamidis 18-month time-to-benefit is a clean, quantified instance of the time-to-benefit reasoning already used for hospice thresholds and for deprescribing preventive medications — a resident whose prognosis is under 18 months gets no survival benefit from a $225,000-a-year drug.

## Downstream Opportunities

- **Handbook — new chapter or section candidate: *Heart Failure with Preserved Ejection Fraction in LTC*, with cardiac amyloidosis as its central "don't stop here" case.** The diagnostic algorithm is short enough to reproduce in full, and the GDMT-subtraction pearl is the kind of counterintuitive, high-consequence guidance a handbook exists to carry.
- **Handbook — cross-reference from *Polypharmacy and Deprescribing*:** amyloidosis is a named indication to *stop* beta blockers and ACE inhibitors/ARBs, with a mechanism (fixed stroke volume, rate dependence) rather than a risk-benefit shrug. One of the cleanest deprescribing arguments available.
- **Handbook — *Anticoagulation*:** add the three score-independent conditions (cardiac amyloidosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, bioprosthetic valve) as an explicit exception block.
- **Handbook — *Falls*:** orthostatic hypotension from amyloid autonomic dysfunction, and the diuretic window that trades dyspnea against syncope.
- **Handbook — *Laboratory Interpretation*:** the persistently elevated troponin with clean coronaries; why SPEP/UPEP is the wrong monoclonal screen.
- **Handbook — time-to-benefit material:** the 18-month curve separation in ATTR-ACT is a concrete, citable example for whatever section handles prognosis-matched prescribing.
- **Newsletter / Bedside Notes candidates,** in descending order of fit:
  - *"Carpal tunnel is a cardiac symptom"* — the six-to-seven-year lead time between tendon deposits and heart failure. Counterintuitive, short, and immediately changes what a clinician asks.
  - *"The heart failure drugs you should stop"* — beta blockers and vasodilators in amyloidosis, built on the fixed-stroke-volume mechanism.
  - *"Don't order the easy test first"* — the Tc-PYP false positive and why sequence matters. A general lesson about test ordering wearing an amyloid costume.
  - *"Stop writing LVH"* — the echo-report language pearl, small and portable.
  - *"One in eight"* — amyloidosis inside routine HFpEF; a rare-disease framing that does not survive contact with the case series.
- **Protocol — unexplained wall thickening trigger:** a standing rule that increased LV wall thickness without hypertension, or new bilateral carpal tunnel over 60, prompts the monoclonal screen. Cheap, specific, and does not require the ordering clinician to hold the whole algorithm in their head.
- **Protocol — amyloidosis medication review:** an admission/annual review flag that, for a resident carrying an amyloidosis diagnosis, checks for beta blocker, ACE inhibitor/ARB/ARNI, and vasodilating calcium channel blocker, and confirms anticoagulation is in place if AF is present regardless of score.
