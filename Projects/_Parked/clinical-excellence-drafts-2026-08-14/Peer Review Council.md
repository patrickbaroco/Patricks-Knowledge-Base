## Context

Patrick chairs the Peer Review Council. Where the [[Clinical Quality Council]] sets standards, this body reviews practice against them and turns what it finds into education and improvement rather than into discipline.

This is the higher-risk of the two councils and the one where a bad first six months is hard to undo. Physicians and APCs decide within about three cases whether peer review here is a learning system or a punishment system, and that judgment doesn't get revised.

Draft charter below.

---

## The gating question: privilege

Before a single case is reviewed, get a written answer from legal and compliance:

**Does peer review conducted by Eventus — a multi-state clinical group, not a hospital medical staff — carry statutory peer review privilege in each of the six states?**

This is not a formality. Peer review protection statutes were largely written around hospital medical staff structures, and their extension to physician groups, and to APCs specifically, varies considerably by state. The answer determines:

- Whether review documentation is discoverable in malpractice litigation
- How findings can be written down, and in what detail
- Whether peer review records can be referenced in credentialing or performance conversations
- Whether reviews need to be structured differently in different states

Get it in writing. Structure the entire process around the most restrictive of the six.

> [!warning]
> The realistic outcome is partial or uncertain protection in at least some states. Design for that: findings written to be defensible if read aloud, review focused on the care process rather than characterizing the clinician, and no informal side channel where the real opinions live.

## Draft charter

### Purpose

Review individual cases against enterprise standards, identify patterns in clinical practice, and convert what's learned into education, pathway changes, and improvement initiatives. Not a disciplinary body.

### The founding commitment

Write it into the charter, say it in the first meeting, and repeat it every time:

**Peer review here exists to improve care, not to build files on people.** Referral to peer review is not an accusation. The output of a review is normally a learning point, sometimes a pathway gap, occasionally a coaching conversation, and rarely — with a separate and explicit escalation path — a performance matter.

The separate escalation path matters. If peer review and performance management run through the same door, every review is heard as a performance review. Keep them structurally distinct: define in the charter exactly what triggers escalation out of peer review and to whom, and make that a narrow, high-threshold list.

### Case selection methodology

Agree this *before* any case is reviewed. Selection methodology chosen after seeing cases looks like selection to fit a conclusion.

Four sources, with a target mix:

| Source | Share | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Random sample** | ~40% | The credibility anchor. If most cases are random, being selected carries no implication |
| **Trigger-based** | ~30% | Defined objective triggers: unexpected death, readmission within 72h of a visit, transfer against pathway, sentinel medication events |
| **Referred** | ~20% | Facility, colleague, or family-initiated. Requires a written intake so referral isn't a back channel |
| **Focused review** | ~10% | Cases pulled because the metric pack flagged a pattern worth understanding |

Volume target: enough to establish rhythm, not so much it becomes unfunded work. Somewhere around 8–12 cases per month across the enterprise is a reasonable starting point — calibrate after three months. Note the existing Airtable RTH case review workflow already runs ~19+ cases/month with structured data; worth deciding whether this council builds on that infrastructure rather than starting fresh.

### Review process

1. **Blinded first pass** where practical — reviewer sees the case, not the clinician's name. Not always achievable in a small market, but the attempt itself signals intent
2. **Structured review instrument**, same every time. Standard of care met / not met is a poor primary output; better: was the clinical reasoning defensible given what was knowable at the time, and would a different process have changed the outcome
3. **Clinician response invited** before any finding is finalized. Non-negotiable — review without response is judgment, not peer review
4. **Council discussion and finding**
5. **Feedback to the clinician**, delivered by their Regional CMO rather than centrally
6. **Theme extraction** — the part that matters at enterprise scale

> [!tip] Design the instrument around cognitive process
> Given the clinical reasoning material already in the vault — [[Premature Closure]], [[Anchoring Bias]], [[Diagnostic Momentum]], [[Search Satisficing]], [[Cognitive Autopsy]] — there's an opportunity to build a review instrument that classifies *how* reasoning went wrong rather than only *that* an outcome was bad. That reframes review from blame toward diagnosis, produces themes that map directly onto teachable content, and is a genuinely differentiated approach for a post-acute group. It is also the single most defensible use of the clinical reasoning work as institutional method rather than personal scholarship.

### Membership

Smaller than the CQC. Six to eight.

- Chair — Patrick
- Three to four rotating physician/APC reviewers, rotated on a fixed term so the burden and the perspective both circulate
- Regional CMO of the market under review, for that case only
- Quality function representative, non-voting, for documentation and follow-through

Rotation is deliberate: a fixed reviewer panel becomes a priesthood, and a rotating one spreads the culture of review through the clinician body faster than any education module.

### Cadence

Monthly, 60 minutes, week 3 of the operating loop. Recommend starting **every other month** for the first six months — a shorter runway per meeting is better than a full calendar with thin cases while methodology is still being calibrated.

### Outputs

- Case findings and clinician feedback
- **A quarterly theme report to the [[Clinical Quality Council]]** — this is the real product. Individual cases inform; patterns change standards
- Education requests to Will Sampson
- Pathway gap identifications back to the standards work

### Failure modes

- **Becomes a disciplinary tribunal.** Guard: 40% random sampling, separate escalation path, and repeated explicit framing
- **Becomes toothless.** Guard: quarterly theme report with named recommendations to CQC, tracked to closure
- **Findings never reach anyone.** Guard: feedback delivered by Regional CMO within two weeks, every time
- **The privilege question stays unanswered.** Guard: it's the next action on this note and it blocks the first review

## Decisions

- Privilege question is answered in writing before the first case is reviewed
- Case selection methodology is fixed in advance; ~40% random
- Peer review and performance management are structurally separate with a narrow, written escalation path
- Reviewer panel rotates on fixed terms
