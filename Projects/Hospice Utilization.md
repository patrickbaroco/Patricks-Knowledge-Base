## Context

Hospice utilization is the first concrete clinical initiative to come out of the ACO data, and it is the one Sandra Jurani explicitly wants **physician-driven**. It hangs off the [[Clinical Quality Framework]] umbrella.

It arrives now because the ACO quality measures stopped being the interesting problem. **2026 is the first year Eventus has achieved all of them** — never happened before. Those measures were always the gimmes: CMS picked objective, simple, non-controversial things with generous exclusions. With them in hand, the ACO can move to targets that actually change care, and Sandra named two: **hospice utilization** and **inpatient readmissions**.

Sandra can see the problem in her monthly data but not diagnose it. Every month the same two names surface as the highest certifiers — **Dr. Bengali and Dr. Begalia** *(names transcription-uncertain; confirm)* — with a disproportionate hospice census spread across a disproportionate number of facilities, and patients who have been on hospice **for years**. What she doesn't have is the mechanics: how certification and recertification actually work, who controls whether a patient stays certified, who decides when someone graduates, and what the APC's role is in caring for a hospice patient.

**The core problem is not that we use too much hospice. It is that we don't own it.** Patients get onto hospice without an Eventus provider involved, and once there, nobody at Eventus sees the recertification paperwork again.

## The mechanism (why patients disappear onto hospice)

This is the part worth writing down precisely, because everything else follows from it.

1. Medicare **requires** the hospice to ask the family who they want as the attending of record. **It is not enforced.**
2. So the hospice lists its own medical director as attending, and that medical director signs the initial certification.
3. The record then says the patient has **no primary care provider** — so nobody else signs a certification.
4. **Eventus never sees another piece of hospice paperwork.** The hospice recertifies indefinitely, on its own judgment, with no external check.

Predatory hospices accelerate this by marketing directly to the facility (*constantly bringing food and lunches*) and, in some cases, calling families themselves — *wouldn't it be great if your mom had an extra CNA* — so the referral never routes through a clinician at all. **Gentiva** *(transcript "Genteva")* is named as notorious for taking our patients; smaller local outfits are worse. This has happened to Patrick's own patients.

**The regulation that makes it fixable:** only a physician can certify or recertify, and the primary care physician is required only on the *initial* certification, and only if there is one. **If the primary care provider is an NP, the certifying physician is the hospice's physician — but the NP remains the attending.** So who signs the initial form matters much less than whether the NP knows it is being signed, referred the patient, remains the ongoing provider, and can pull the patient off hospice when appropriate. It also matters for revenue: **an NP can only bill Medicare if listed as the attending.**

## What appropriate use looks like

- **SNF.** There should be some, but limited to cases where hospice contributes real medical expertise. Patrick's test: *if it matters to me that they call me overnight or on the weekend rather than the triage team, because this is complicated, that's a use for hospice.* Otherwise the facility can manage it.
- **Assisted living.** Should be — and in the current data is — the disproportionate share. That is correct, not a finding. A resident with a stage 3 wound is getting regulatory-marginal for the AL; hospice is what lets them stay in the building instead of moving to long-term care. Legitimate.
- **Length of stay is the discriminator, not volume.** A building can run high hospice utilization across a year and be entirely appropriate if length of stay is short. The signal of a problem is the long tail — and years-long hospice enrollment is the clearest marker.
- **The recert nobody catches.** Common legitimate path: patient aspirates, gets pneumonia in the hospital, and the AL will only take them back on hospice. Three months later they look great — the acute event never signalled terminal decline. **That recertification is where our providers should be discharging them, and today nobody is looking.**

## The operating model

**Own the referral, and choose the partner.**

The pitch to a facility or a hospice, in Patrick's framing: *"We realize us coming in and saying there's going to be less hospice is threatening to you. What I want is less bad hospice. If you're providing good hospice, you'll have more referrals, and the bad hospice will have fewer. Doesn't that sound nice?"*

This works because it is genuinely a win-win. A reputable hospice **wants** short length of stay — long stays draw regulatory scrutiny — and will happily discharge a patient who no longer qualifies in exchange for a reliable stream of appropriate referrals from a partner in long-term care. It also makes the AL conversation easy: *if they need hospice to stay in the building, great, I'll sign off and support it — but it's this hospice, and I put in the referral.*

Around the valley, most of the facilities Eventus covers already use one of **two hospices that don't do the predatory thing**, with a few exceptions. Naming them is step one.

**Who owns what:**

- **Physician — the relationship.** Establishes and maintains the hospice partnership at the facility level, and signs. Not day-to-day management: in AL the physician is in the building maybe **once a quarter**.
- **APC — the patient.** The NP is the attending, should be **listed** as the attending, and meets the hospice nurse weekly or biweekly to walk the census. The hospice nurse will happily take that meeting (they're hoping to find someone else eligible), and the relationship is what makes *"Mrs. Jones looks great, I think she needs to be discharged"* a conversation rather than a fight.
- **Everyone.** Hospice is a service ordered by the treating provider for a patient who **remains our patient**. *"Nobody just materializes on hospice."* This has to reach the home health people on the back end too.

**"We just have to own it, because if we don't own it, hospice will be very happy to own it — and they don't care about the ACO one bit."**
