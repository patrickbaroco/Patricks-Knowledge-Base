# Stewardship

Eventus should be exacting about performance and generous about people.

Our mission is to care better for people in long-term care so they can live with greater dignity and wellbeing. Our patients are often frail and unable to advocate for themselves, and they deserve excellent care. We hold ourselves and our clinicians to high standards of competence, integrity, and accountability, and we expect people to do the right thing even when it is difficult.

The people who do this work have chosen to spend a meaningful part of their lives with us. That choice creates obligations on our part that go beyond compensation and contract. We owe our clinicians the resources to be outstanding, working conditions that respect their lives outside medicine, and an investment in their professional growth. If we insist on excellence, we have an obligation to help make excellence possible.

Loyalty runs both ways, and leadership carries the greater share. We never ask for more loyalty than we are prepared to show. Our people can trust that we will deal with them fairly, listen to them seriously, tell them the truth, and give them the benefit of the doubt when the facts are uncertain. Disagreeing with leadership, negotiating for better terms, and choosing to leave are not acts of disloyalty. They are rights in a free relationship, and we respect them as such.

Loyalty is a consequence of the culture we create, not a prerequisite for belonging to it.

Grace governs how we treat people, and standards govern what we expect of them. Neither diminishes the other. When someone falls short, we start by learning what happened, distinguishing fact from interpretation, and deciding what needs to change. We hold people accountable for their conduct without turning judgments about actions into judgments about character.

The standards we expect of our clinicians apply equally to leadership. We are as willing to acknowledge our own mistakes, receive difficult feedback, and change our behavior as we expect our people to be. Authority increases our obligation to be accountable.

Our commitment to people endures through disagreement, disappointment, and departure. Someone who leaves Eventus does not become our adversary. We wish them well, speak of their contributions with respect, and examine honestly what we might have done better.

We are a professional community built on mutual obligation. We can expect a great deal of our people without claiming to own them, and we can compete hard for business without treating competitors or former colleagues as enemies. We are aligned around our mission without demanding conformity of thought.

The test of these principles comes when our interests are threatened: when someone is struggling, angry, critical of leadership, or on the way out. How we behave in those moments reveals our culture.

If we remain faithful to our obligations when doing so is difficult, we earn the trust and credibility to ask extraordinary things of our people in service of our patients.
