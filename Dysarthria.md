Dysarthria is a disorder of the **mechanical, motor execution** of speech — difficulty or failure to articulate — as distinct from [[Aphasia]], a disorder of the cognitive/linguistic processes of language itself. Because dysarthria reflects a problem anywhere along the pathway from cortex to muscle, it localizes far less specifically than aphasia does on its own, but the specific *pattern* of articulatory failure can meaningfully narrow that pathway.

## Localization by level

**Distal articulatory apparatus (individual cranial nerve weakness).** Weakness of a specific muscle group produces a specific, nameable pattern:

- **Labial dysarthria** — facial (CN VII) weakness impairing lip closure, causing difficulty with **"p" and "b"** sounds. A patient with bilateral facial weakness (e.g., from Guillain-Barré syndrome) may be unable to produce these sounds at all and can learn to substitute phrasing that avoids them.
- **Lingual dysarthria** — tongue (CN XII) weakness, causing difficulty with **"l" and "t"** sounds (the tongue tip against the teeth/alveolar ridge).
- **Guttural dysarthria** — pharyngeal/laryngeal (CN IX/X) weakness, causing difficulty with **"g" and "k"** sounds.

Bedside testing of individual sounds ("pa-pa-pa," "ta-ta-ta," "ga-ga-ga" or "ka-ka-ka") screens each of these three cranial-nerve territories in turn.

**Coordination (cerebellar / "scanning" dysarthria).** A coordination problem — classically cerebellar, and perhaps most familiarly demonstrated by acute alcohol intoxication — produces its own distinctive pattern: **scanning dysarthria**, in which speech volume and tempo fluctuate irregularly, as if a radio dial were being turned up and down while the person speaks. Individual sounds may be entirely normal in isolation, so testing a **complex syllable sequence** — "pa-ta-ka" repeated rapidly, or a deliberately complicated phrase like "Methodist Episcopal" — is more sensitive for revealing a higher-order coordination deficit than single-sound testing alone.

**Upper motor neuron (corticobulbar tract / spastic dysarthria).** The corticobulbar tract — the upper motor neuron pathway descending from cortex to the cranial nerve nuclei (*"bulbar"* referring to *la bulbe*, French for the brainstem/medulla) — can itself be the site of the lesion, producing a spastic dysarthria. Because most cranial nerve nuclei (with the notable exception of the lower face and tongue, which receive relatively more contralateral-predominant innervation) receive *bilateral* corticobulbar input, a unilateral corticobulbar lesion often produces a milder or absent dysarthria, and prominent spastic dysarthria more often implies a bilateral corticobulbar process (pseudobulbar palsy) than a single unilateral lesion.

## Non-neurologic mimics

Not every "dysarthria" reported by a family member is neurologic. Berkowitz's own recalled example: mistaking an elderly patient's dry mouth and missing dentures, on early-morning pre-rounds, for a new stroke — resolved entirely by a sip of water and reinserted dentures. Simple mechanical causes should be actively excluded, especially in an inpatient setting, before pursuing a full neurologic workup.

## Related conditions on the same differential

**Spasmodic dysphonia** — a laryngeal dystonia in which speech is interrupted by spasms of the vocal cords — is neither a classic dysarthria nor an aphasia, but belongs on the differential for "impaired speech" as a distinct, focal dystonic phenomenon.

## Putting it together

Bedside dysarthria localization proceeds in roughly this order: (1) confirm this is dysarthria and not aphasia or confused speech (test comprehension and repetition — both should be intact in isolated dysarthria); (2) test individual sounds (pa/ta/ga) to screen CN VII, XII, and IX/X function; (3) test a complex syllable sequence (pa-ta-ka, or a complicated phrase) to screen for a higher-order coordination or spastic component that single-sound testing can miss; (4) integrate with the rest of the cranial nerve and cerebellar exam to localize further.
