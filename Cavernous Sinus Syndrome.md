The cavernous sinus is one of the most anatomically dense — and most frequently tested — structures in clinical neurology: a paired venous sinus flanking the pituitary gland and sphenoid sinus that packs an unusual number of critical structures into a small space, and is the site of a genuinely unusual anatomic fact worth remembering on its own: **it is the only place in the body where an artery runs directly through a venous structure** — the internal carotid artery (and the surrounding oculosympathetic plexus) passes straight through the cavernous sinus rather than alongside it.

## What runs through it

- **Cranial nerve III** (oculomotor)
- **Cranial nerve IV** (trochlear)
- **Cranial nerve V1 and V2** (ophthalmic and maxillary divisions of the trigeminal nerve — *not* V3, the mandibular division, which exits separately through the foramen ovale)
- **Cranial nerve VI** (abducens) — notably, CN VI runs free-floating *within* the sinus itself, immediately adjacent to the carotid artery, rather than embedded in the lateral wall alongside III, IV, V1, and V2; this makes it, in some accounts, the cranial nerve most vulnerable to cavernous sinus pathology
- **Oculosympathetic fibers** (postganglionic sympathetics traveling with the carotid) — cavernous sinus involvement can therefore produce a Horner syndrome alongside the ocular motor and sensory findings
- **The internal carotid artery** itself

## What does NOT run through it

**Cranial nerve II (the optic nerve) does not pass through the cavernous sinus.** This is the single most important negative finding to check on exam: a patient with a cavernous sinus process should have **preserved visual acuity**, distinguishing it from the more anterior **orbital apex syndrome**, which does include the optic nerve (see [[Multiple Cranial Neuropathy Localization]] for the full comparison table of skull-base syndromes). A patient with painful ophthalmoplegia, V1/V2 sensory loss, *and* visual loss should raise suspicion for a lesion extending anterior to the cavernous sinus into the orbital apex, not a pure cavernous sinus process.

## Clinical presentation

The classic cavernous sinus syndrome is a **painful ophthalmoplegia**: pain (from V1 involvement, and often from the inflammatory or compressive process itself) combined with some combination of ptosis, diplopia, and impaired eye movements (from III, IV, and/or VI), plus facial sensory loss in the V1/V2 distribution, and — if the oculosympathetic fibers are involved — a partial Horner syndrome superimposed on the picture (which can be difficult to fully appreciate given the concurrent CN III ptosis). Proptosis and chemosis can occur if venous outflow is sufficiently obstructed. Visual acuity should remain normal.

## Causes

- **Tolosa-Hunt syndrome** — idiopathic granulomatous inflammation of the cavernous sinus, a diagnosis of exclusion classically presenting with painful ophthalmoplegia that responds rapidly (often within hours) to corticosteroids; imaging may be initially normal and only show focal cavernous sinus wall enhancement on repeat imaging with clinical recurrence. See [[CPS 351 - Headache, Ptosis, and Diplopia from Tolosa-Hunt Syndrome|CPS 351]] for a full case, including the diagnostic pitfall of an initially entirely normal MRI/MRA and lumbar puncture.
- **Cavernous sinus thrombosis** — thrombosis specifically of the cavernous sinus is an uncommon cause of cerebral venous sinus thrombosis relative to the other dural venous sinuses (superior sagittal, transverse, sigmoid); see [[Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis]] and [[CPS 193 - Impaired Speech and Broca's Aphasia from Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis|CPS 193]] for the broader venous sinus anatomy and the relative frequency of thrombosis at different sites.
- **Carotid-cavernous fistula** — an abnormal connection between the carotid artery and the cavernous sinus, producing pulsatile proptosis, an audible bruit, and chemosis alongside the ocular motor findings, from arterialized venous pressure.
- **Pituitary tumor or pituitary apoplexy** — mass effect or acute hemorrhagic/ischemic expansion of a pituitary adenoma can compress the adjacent cavernous sinus structures, typically with visual field involvement (bitemporal hemianopsia from optic chiasm compression) as an additional clue pointing to the sella rather than a pure cavernous sinus process.
- **Neoplastic infiltration** — nasopharyngeal carcinoma, lymphoma, and skull-base metastases can infiltrate the cavernous sinus directly.
- **Infectious** — bacterial or fungal (e.g., invasive fungal sinusitis in immunocompromised patients) infection can spread into the cavernous sinus, typically with a more toxic clinical picture and a identifiable contiguous source.

## Bedside approach

1. Confirm the pattern: ophthalmoplegia (III/IV/VI) plus V1/V2 sensory loss, with pain.
2. Check visual acuity specifically — normal acuity supports cavernous sinus/superior orbital fissure; visual loss pushes the localization more anterior, to the orbital apex.
3. Look for a partial Horner syndrome, which can be subtle in the presence of a CN III-related ptosis.
4. Consider the tempo and systemic context: an afebrile, subacute, steroid-responsive course with normal initial imaging favors Tolosa-Hunt; an acute, prothrombotic-risk-factor-laden presentation favors cavernous sinus thrombosis; a pulsatile, bruit-associated presentation favors carotid-cavernous fistula; a slowly progressive course with visual field loss favors a pituitary mass lesion.
