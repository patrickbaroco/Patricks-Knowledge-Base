## Physiology (background)

Parathyroid hormone is the body's minute-to-minute calcium thermostat, secreted by the parathyroid glands under the control of a calcium-sensing receptor: a falling calcium drives PTH up, a rising calcium shuts it down. It raises serum calcium through three levers — mobilizing calcium from bone via osteoclast activation, increasing distal tubular calcium reabsorption (while wasting phosphate) in the kidney, and stimulating renal [[1-Alpha-Hydroxylation|1-α-hydroxylation]] of 25-OH vitamin D to calcitriol, which increases gut calcium absorption.

The three hyperparathyroid states are distinguished by the calcium–PTH pairing:

- **[[Primary Hyperparathyroidism|Primary]]** — autonomous over-secretion (adenoma or hyperplasia): high calcium *with* a high or inappropriately normal PTH.
- **[[Secondary Hyperparathyroidism|Secondary]]** — an appropriate response to hypocalcemia (classically CKD or vitamin D deficiency): low or normal calcium with a high PTH.
- **[[Tertiary Hyperparathyroidism|Tertiary]]** — autonomous secretion that develops after prolonged secondary stimulation: high calcium with a high PTH.

## The PTH fork in hypercalcemia

- **PTH is the first fork of the hypercalcemia workup — it splits PTH-mediated from non-PTH causes.** A suppressed PTH moves you to the non-PTH arm and excludes both primary and tertiary hyperparathyroidism in a single step; the reflex to send it is correct. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])
- **PTH is exquisitely sensitive to calcium and shuts off fast.** It "doesn't take time to shut off," so a suppressed PTH in the face of a high calcium means the gland is behaving *appropriately* — the driver of the calcium is somewhere outside the parathyroid axis. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])
- **Read the PTH trend, not just the snapshot.** In the case, PTH ran 115 when the calcium was low (an appropriate secondary response), then 65, and finally suppressed at 9.4 against a lab floor of 10–12 — "something shifted." The trajectory shows an intact, reactive axis being turned down by a calcium that some *other* process is driving up. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])
- **A rising PTH after lowering the dialysate calcium bath confirms responsiveness.** Pulling calcium off releases the brake and PTH climbs back — the axis is working, which is itself evidence the primary problem is non-parathyroid. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])

## Interpreting PTH against calcium

| Calcium | PTH | Points toward |
|---|---|---|
| High | High or inappropriately normal | Primary or tertiary hyperparathyroidism; FHH |
| High | Low / suppressed | Non-PTH causes — malignancy/[[PTHrP]], granulomatous, ingestion, immobilization, vitamin D excess |
| Low / normal | High | Secondary hyperparathyroidism (CKD, vitamin D deficiency) |

- The key subtlety: in the setting of hypercalcemia, an "inappropriately normal" PTH still counts as *PTH-mediated* — a normally functioning axis should have suppressed it. Only a genuinely low PTH cleanly moves you to the non-PTH arm.
