# Cary Trainor Connect — Clinical Governance, 2026-09-03

3–4pm, Teams. Cary Trainor (COO). Invite agenda read *Committee input · CSS · Operating to the Clinical Model*; the hour went entirely to committee input. Source: meeting transcript, ~60 minutes.

**Bottom line: she's in.** *"I love it. I'm super pumped about this, and I want to support it."* No substantive objection to any of the three committees. One caution, one deliverable, and a route to approval.

## The case, as delivered

Opened with the same frame used with Sandra that morning — *a lot of things we kind of do, and not many things we really do* — and made it concrete with the CGSC.

**The CGSC evidence, which did most of the persuading.** Patrick now owns the antipsychotic and anxiolytic reduction measures, met with the person who runs the data, and asked to see where they stand. **The September number is the same as the January number.** Not moved, not worsened — unchanged, to the point where *"it's almost surprising there wasn't just more random variability in the graph."* Which would only be surprising if anything had been done to move it.

Cary's response: **"You don't have to convince me. I'm in the exact same place as you, exactly the same place."** She had also stopped attending the CGSC. Her diagnosis of why it fails: 30 minutes, too many people, and — Patrick's addition — *an agenda per meeting but no agenda in the sense of a mission*: nothing that says *this thing, by December*.

**The three committees, as explained:**

- **Quality Committee** — takes priorities from the ACO and from generally accepted quality gaps, then *defines the measure* (not "we want high quality" — *A1c under nine, what percentage should be under nine*), publicizes it, builds the education that teaches both the measure and how to hit it, and audits to it. So we never again reach September to find the January number.
- **Peer Review Committee** — the thing that has teeth. Credentialing and privileges; restriction of privileges by a federally-defined peer review committee for more than 30 days is reportable to the National Practitioner Data Bank. *"It sets a clock of 30 days while keeping it all out of HR and under a clinical umbrella."*
- **Clinical Governance Council** — the piece Cary was fuzziest on, and the explanation that landed: a peer review committee produces **findings and a recommendation**, deliberately staffed without supervisors so it is answering a purely clinical question — **and somebody has to approve that recommendation.** In a hospital that's the medical executive committee. Here it's the Council. It is also the *"idiot check"* on quality initiatives — the body closest to the board and to operations, making sure what gets picked aligns with the Eventus mission rather than being *"a bunch of random docs and NPs picking pet projects."* And it's the entity that reports clinical quality upward, board-packet style.

## What Cary pushed on

**1. What's the difference between the Quality Committee and the primary care / psychiatry committees?**

Answered as departments. Primary care and psych each own their own priorities and measures — *"I can't and should not be coming up with the psych stuff, that's not my specialty."* They work it out in their own committee and bring it to Quality; Quality builds the education and the audit around it. This is precisely the CGSC failure: everyone hashing out anxiolytics one week and transitions of care the next. **Bidirectional** — Quality (fed by the ACO) hands down priorities and asks the departments *what would you choose as a measure for this*, then discusses and ratifies. *"Hopefully it'll be pretty short because it'll be good ideas, well thought out, well structured, and then we rubber stamp it and move on to how we design an audit for that."*

**2. With only two disciplines, do we need the department layer at all?** — Cary's sharpest structural question, since adding wound care or palliative isn't the company's plan.

Answered on participation: the department layer is what brings the broader clinical community into the decisions rather than leaving them to a handful of distinguished roles. And on time: *"every time I get on one of those CGSC things and they're like, we're going to talk about antipsychotic stuff, I just know that's going to be Jennie Byrne and Williams, and it's okay, I'll sit here."* Cary accepted this — **"I think the value of having that other layer is then there's more participation from the broader clinical community."**

**3. Membership — who's on what.** She had read the memo and was still fuzzy, and said so more than once. Clarified: department chairs sit on Quality (bringing subject-matter expertise and awareness of their departments), plus distinguished physicians as they roll out, plus APCs, plus **someone from education**. Council membership is designed for **more at-large members than leadership** — everyone technically eligible, falling off over time, so the structure doesn't launch by booting people out. The problem it anticipates: *"if we started adding a lot more regional CMOs, you wouldn't want every regional CMO on that committee."*

**4. Isn't peer review worse than HR?** — the best question she asked. If NPDB reporting follows you and quitting Eventus doesn't, peer review is the *harsher* escalation, not the softer one. Will clinicians hear "they're starting a peer review committee" and leave?

Two-part answer. **First, familiarity:** most people here will ask *what is that*; the ones who know it are hospital-trained and, being decent providers, mostly never touched it. Cary's own read, unprompted and worth keeping: **"Maybe that's a good thing, because then people who don't want it — that's a red flag."** **Second, and this is what the non-reportable tier is for:** the program is built so most people's experience of peer review is *this doesn't have to be a big deal, do this education, we'll follow up, and we're done* — sealed on completion, like a juvenile record. **"Most people's experience with peer review will be the same as the conversations they're currently having, except that at the end of that conversation there is a 'but if you don't.' Right now we don't have the 'but if you don't' part."**

**5. What's actually in scope?** — is it only clinical decisions, or also non-participation?

- **In scope:** quality broadly. **Note closures are a quality problem** — the worked example being seeing a patient Wednesday when the NP saw them Monday and there's no note: *"I don't know what happened, I don't know what she did, I don't know what she was thinking. I'm starting from scratch, and that's poor quality."* Named case: **Paul Tompkins, 100-odd open notes**, already pulled out of the field, every opportunity given, and the remaining tool is *"stop, or I'll say stop again."* Also in scope: a physician disengaged from fixing ACO measure failures — because CMS picked nothing controversial or subtle and gave plenty of outs, so **failing that standard is a quality problem whether or not shared savings exist.**
- **Out of scope:** getting someone to do an **ACO visit** for a patient not otherwise assigned to them. That's a systems problem, already being solved, not a peer review matter. (If it *is* their assigned patient, different story.)
- **Untouched:** **Jeff's billing and coding work stays exactly where it is.** Coding is compliance and operations — financial, not quality. Auditing and monitoring there doesn't change at all.

Side note delivered in passing, on the note-closure culture: Eventus's policy is *the most lenient Patrick has ever worked under* — his own career has run on same-day closure with 15% of pay tied to a 90% threshold, and a 24-hour standard elsewhere. The perception that Eventus is strict comes from people who have never worked in a health system, because **no health system has loose note-closure policies.**

## Where the departments meet the org chart

**Education pairs with Quality, hand in glove** — *"you don't have quality if you don't have good education"* — and they are not synchronized today. The live example: the ACO gets the fourth Wednesday of each month for its own education slot, and **nobody is jumping up and down to attend the ACO education meeting.** It should be dissolved into specialty-specific didactics instead: if heart failure is the six-month initiative because it drives return-to-hospital, then the Wednesdays track heart failure, AKI, and so on. Someone from education therefore sits on the Quality Committee.

**Signature standards audits go away.** Easy to game at best, a very weak signal for bad quality — *"if you can't manage to pass that, then we have big problems. You need to learn how to use a text expander."* Replaced by real quality audits run through the Quality Committee.

**The other forums get called what they are.** The experienced clinician roundtable is, in practice, the place to ask random questions — which is fine, and maybe should just be a monitored Teams channel, but nobody knows what it currently is. Name it and let it be that.

## Education and the handbook — the biggest unplanned outcome

This started as a question about distribution and turned into the COO adopting the handbook.

**The clinical pathways don't work, and there is now a number.** Patrick polled the Virginia APCs: **19 respondents, 3 knew where the pathways were at all, and exactly 1 had ever looked at more than one.** Not their fault — the pathways are PDFs on the wiki, which is hard to reach and miserable to read on a phone, which is where people actually look. (Cary needed a moment to place what a clinical pathway even was, then recognized them as the one- or two-page write-ups under clinical best practice — Patrick's UTI one, Monica's Coumadin one.)

**The distribution model:** stop hoping for one channel to work. *"Right now our mission is heart failure. We should just bombard people with heart failure. It's going to be hard to not come across some education about heart failure today."* Seminars, Thursday meetings, newsletters (which get read, because they're short), a one-page "doorway version" of each chapter, and an **iPhone app** — Will says it isn't rocket science, and Patrick's content is just text and links, so it's easier than what Will already built for his family's vacation. None of that coordination exists today.

**Cary wants the handbook to be the ground truth.** She asked directly how to get her hands on the work in progress. The shape both landed on:

- The handbook becomes **the resource** — *this is your resource, all of our NPs, all of our docs*.
- Chapters become **PowerPoints for the Wednesday seminars**.
- The **regulatory / operations / orientation section becomes new hire orientation.** Existing sections named: long-term care ecosystem, the ACO, MDS and care planning, CMS star ratings, quality measures, the F-tags clinicians encounter most, survey readiness for clinical staff, the clinician's role, prehospitalization. (Cardiology is furthest along; GI is barely started — *"you can also tell what my interests are"*.)

**Cary wants to blow up new hire training, and this is why.** There is currently **no orientation to long-term care whatsoever** — she has been through the training twice, and by day three of a three-day program clinicians are still using the wrong terms for the type of facility they're about to walk into. Patrick's matching example: a PA who has worked in this space **for years** asking *"What is Part A?"* Cary's model is BCG, where training arrived the exact week you hit the problem it addressed — phone-and-tools at the start, client skills at month three, managerial at month six. *"We have such an opportunity there."* What she likes about the chapters specifically: **they're written for someone with no background.**

Practical friction: everything is in Markdown in Patrick's personal Obsidian, and most chapters have never been read by anyone. He offered to put them online. **Cary already has the table of contents** and it's roughly current (falls has since been added).
