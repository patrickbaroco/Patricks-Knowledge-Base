A fasciculation is the visible or palpable, spontaneous, brief contraction of a single motor unit — twitching muscle fibers without any voluntary effort, produced by ectopic depolarization somewhere along the lower motor neuron rather than by the muscle fibers themselves. Isolated fasciculations provoke outsized anxiety, especially among healthcare workers, because they are the presenting feature most people associate with ALS ([[RLR 190 - A Twitch in the Bicep- A Hospitalist Diagnoses His Own Benign Fasciculation Syndrome|RLR 190]]) — yet the overwhelming majority of fasciculations are benign, and the bedside task is less about naming the twitch than about localizing it and checking it against the company it keeps.

## Localizing the twitch: fasciculation is a lower motor neuron sign

The starting framework — co-developed by Reza Manesh and Aaron Berkowitz and taught explicitly in [[RLR 190 - A Twitch in the Bicep- A Hospitalist Diagnoses His Own Benign Fasciculation Syndrome|RLR 190]] and [[RLR 148 - Nocturnal Leg Cramps That Weren't Painful- A Meningioma Presenting as Focal Seizures with Todd's Paralysis|RLR 148]] — walks any complaint of involuntary muscle movement back along the chain from muscle to neuromuscular junction to neuron, since irritation anywhere along that chain can produce a twitch. Fasciculations specifically localize to the **lower motor neuron**: the anterior horn cell, its root, the plexus, or the peripheral nerve axon supplying that motor unit. They are never a muscle-disease phenomenon — as [[CPS 344 - Bilateral Lower Limb Weakness and Kennedy Disease|CPS 344]] puts it, reflexes are preserved in myopathy (unless the muscle is too weak to move the joint) while fasciculations, especially of the tongue, are a lower-motor-neuron sign a myopathy simply cannot produce. That single discriminator — reflexes and fasciculations together — is what lets an exam pull a case out of "muscle" and into "nerve/anterior horn" territory before any test is ordered.

## Fasciculation and its mimics

Before building a differential, confirm the patient is describing a true fasciculation and not one of several lookalikes — a distinction [[RLR 148 - Nocturnal Leg Cramps That Weren't Painful- A Meningioma Presenting as Focal Seizures with Todd's Paralysis|RLR 148]] frames with a dedicated "fasciculation and its mimics" teaching sheet, and [[RLR 190 - A Twitch in the Bicep- A Hospitalist Diagnoses His Own Benign Fasciculation Syndrome|RLR 190]] opens every case with:

| Phenomenon | What it looks/feels like | Localization |
|---|---|---|
| Fasciculation | A brief, visible ripple or twitch under the skin, involving a single motor unit, not crossing a joint | Anterior horn cell, root, plexus, or peripheral nerve (lower motor neuron) |
| [[Myoclonus\|Myoclonus]] | A sudden, lightning-fast jerk that *does* move a joint | Cortex, subcortex, or spinal cord |
| Myokymia | A continuous, undulating muscle ripple (classically eyelid) rather than a discrete twitch | Peripheral nerve hyperexcitability (benign eyelid myokymia) or, rarely, a central process such as MS or Isaac syndrome |
| [[Tremor]] | A rhythmic, oscillatory, sinusoidal movement | Central (basal ganglia, cerebellum, thalamus) far more often than peripheral |
| Muscle spasm/cramp | A sustained, painful muscle contraction | Excited lower motor neuron/muscle fiber, usually benign |

By base rate, ordinary benign muscle contraction is overwhelmingly the most likely explanation for any of these — but a short list of "can't-miss" possibilities (ALS, electrolyte disturbance, stimulant/caffeine effect) is worth holding in mind before reassuring a patient ([[RLR 148 - Nocturnal Leg Cramps That Weren't Painful- A Meningioma Presenting as Focal Seizures with Todd's Paralysis|RLR 148]]).

## Benign fasciculation syndrome: a diagnosis of exclusion

[[RLR 190 - A Twitch in the Bicep- A Hospitalist Diagnoses His Own Benign Fasciculation Syndrome|RLR 190]] is built around a hospitalist's autobiographical case of exactly this diagnosis: a single bicep twitch that spread over weeks into random, painless fasciculations across both arms, both legs, the face, back, and abdomen — worse at night, unrelieved by stopping caffeine — with an essentially normal neurologic exam throughout and one self-limited episode of generalized fatigue and weakness. The case was diagnostically difficult precisely because it was a purely **positive** neurological phenomenon (a twitch gained) with no accompanying **negative** phenomenon (a deficit lost) to anchor a localization — most movement disorders are tractable because they arrive bundled with a deficit, and this one never did.

Benign fasciculation syndrome is common — lifetime prevalence estimates run around 70% — and is a diagnosis of exclusion confirmed by an EMG showing only occasional fasciculation potentials **without** electrodiagnostic evidence of motor neuron disease, neuropathy, or radiculopathy. It is most often tied to poor sleep, stress, anxiety, and caffeine, though any or all of these triggers can be absent, as they largely were in the RLR 190 case. It is markedly overrepresented as a self-diagnosis among healthcare workers, who know just enough about ALS to catastrophize a benign finding — a textbook [[Availability Heuristic]] exemplar, since vivid clinical exposure to ALS makes that association cognitively available far out of proportion to its true base rate. Reassurance, not treatment, is usually the intervention: in the RLR 190 case, relief tracked not to any medication but to the moment the patient received a normal EMG, and the fasciculations themselves persisted — the patient simply stopped noticing or caring about them once reassured.

## What actually distinguishes benign fasciculations from ALS

The instinct to fear ALS the moment a fasciculation appears outruns the evidence: [[RLR 148 - Nocturnal Leg Cramps That Weren't Painful- A Meningioma Presenting as Focal Seizures with Todd's Paralysis|RLR 148]] notes that patients who present with fasciculations but *no* weakness are unlikely to have ALS, since in typical ALS presentations weakness is the driving complaint and fasciculations are usually an incidental exam finding rather than the presenting symptom. The features that actually separate a benign process from a motor-neuron-disease one:

| Feature | Benign fasciculation syndrome | ALS / motor neuron disease |
|---|---|---|
| Weakness | Absent — exam normal | The driving complaint, not incidental ([[RLR 148 - Nocturnal Leg Cramps That Weren't Painful- A Meningioma Presenting as Focal Seizures with Todd's Paralysis\|RLR 148]]) |
| Atrophy | Absent | Present, often with an oddly distributed, asymmetric pattern ([[CPS 229 - Left Upper Extremity Weakness and Hirayama Disease\|CPS 229]]) |
| Reflexes | Normal | Mixed picture in the same limb — brisk reflexes, a positive Hoffman sign, or Babinski (upper motor neuron) alongside diminished/absent reflexes and atrophy (lower motor neuron); "everything goes up" is the upper-motor-neuron half of the pattern ([[CPS 312 - Frequent Stumbling and HTLV-1-Associated Myelopathy\|CPS 312]], [[CPS 317 - Generalized Weakness and Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy\|CPS 317]]) |
| Bulbar/tongue involvement | Absent | Tongue and perioral fasciculations, dysarthria, dysphagia can appear, though these also mark non-ALS anterior-horn-cell disease such as Kennedy disease ([[CPS 344 - Bilateral Lower Limb Weakness and Kennedy Disease\|CPS 344]]) |
| EMG | Occasional fasciculation potentials only, with normal nerve conduction studies and no denervation | Widespread active chronic denervation with incomplete reinnervation, often far exceeding what nerve conduction studies (which test the nerve as an inert wire) would suggest ([[CPS 317 - Generalized Weakness and Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy\|CPS 317]]) |

A mixed upper-and-lower-motor-neuron picture within a single limb — atrophy and fasciculations together with brisk reflexes, or absent reflexes with an upgoing toe — is one of the few patterns that should raise ALS specifically, rather than a pure lower-motor-neuron or pure upper-motor-neuron process alone ([[CPS 229 - Left Upper Extremity Weakness and Hirayama Disease|CPS 229]]).

## Anterior-horn-cell mimics worth knowing before landing on ALS or BFS

Several conditions produce fasciculations through a genuinely lower-motor-neuron mechanism but run a course distinct enough from ALS to change the prognosis entirely:

- **Cervical spondylotic myelopathy** can compress the ventral (motor) cord and cervical roots badly enough to produce widespread EMG fasciculations and chronic denervation that looks like ALS on paper — until normal nerve conduction studies and a structural cervical MRI redirect the diagnosis away from motor neuron disease ([[CPS 317 - Generalized Weakness and Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy|CPS 317]]).
- **Hirayama disease** (monomelic amyotrophy / cervical flexion myelopathy) is a rare, usually self-limited anterior-horn-cell process that can present with spreading twitching and oddly distributed hand wasting mimicking early ALS, but stays static in one limb over a year without bulbar or sensory involvement — and is unmasked only by a dynamic flexion cervical MRI ([[CPS 229 - Left Upper Extremity Weakness and Hirayama Disease|CPS 229]]).
- **Kennedy disease** (X-linked spinobulbar muscular atrophy) presents with facial and tongue fasciculations, areflexia, and retained strength alongside gynecomastia and an X-linked pedigree — a pure lower-motor-neuron syndrome, unlike ALS's mixed upper-and-lower pattern, that progresses over decades rather than years ([[CPS 344 - Bilateral Lower Limb Weakness and Kennedy Disease|CPS 344]]).
- Rarer central mimics of the fasciculation-like ripple — myokymia related to multiple sclerosis or peripheral-nerve hyperexcitability syndromes such as Isaac syndrome — round out the differential when a purely positive phenomenon resists an ordinary lower-motor-neuron explanation ([[RLR 148 - Nocturnal Leg Cramps That Weren't Painful- A Meningioma Presenting as Focal Seizures with Todd's Paralysis|RLR 148]], [[RLR 190 - A Twitch in the Bicep- A Hospitalist Diagnoses His Own Benign Fasciculation Syndrome|RLR 190]]).
