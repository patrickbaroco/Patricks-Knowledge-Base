Conjugate horizontal gaze deviation is one of the most useful — and most frequently misapplied — localizing signs in acute neurology, because the "direction rule" governing it **reverses** depending on whether the responsible lesion is hemispheric or brainstem, and has at least one further exception on top of that.

## The hemispheric rule

The **frontal eye field (FEF)**, in each cerebral hemisphere, normally drives the eyes toward the **contralateral** visual field. A destructive (ablative) hemispheric lesion — most commonly a large MCA stroke — silences the FEF on that side; the unopposed contralateral FEF then wins, and the eyes deviate **toward** the side of the lesion (and, since the lesion also typically involves the adjacent corticospinal tract, **away from** the resulting contralateral hemiparesis). An irritative lesion at the same location — a seizure focus in or near the FEF — instead hyperactivates that FEF, and the eyes deviate in the *opposite* direction: **away from** the seizure focus.

## The brainstem rule — the opposite of the hemispheric rule

At the brainstem level, the **paramedian pontine reticular formation (PPRF)** drives horizontal gaze **ipsilaterally**, not contralaterally — the left PPRF sends the eyes to the left, not the right. A destructive lesion of the left pons (which, being above the corticospinal tract decussation, still produces a *right*-sided hemiparesis, just as a hemispheric lesion would) knocks out the left PPRF's ability to drive gaze to the left, so the eyes deviate to the **right** — **away from** the side of the lesion. This is the exact opposite of the hemispheric-stroke gaze pattern, despite both producing a contralateral hemiparesis, and is one of the few bedside signs that can distinguish a hemispheric from a brainstem lesion purely from watching a patient's eyes and testing strength. This reversal was taught in detail in [[CPS 305 - Shallow Breathing and Locked-In Syndrome from a Vertebrobasilar Stroke|CPS 305]].

| Lesion location | Mechanism | Gaze deviation relative to the lesion | Gaze deviation relative to the hemiparesis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hemispheric, destructive (e.g., MCA stroke) | Unopposed contralateral FEF | Toward the lesion | Away from the hemiparesis |
| Hemispheric, irritative (seizure) | Hyperactive ipsilateral FEF | Away from the focus | Toward the (contralateral, ictal) weakness, if any |
| Brainstem/pontine, destructive | Loss of ipsilateral PPRF drive | Away from the lesion | Toward the hemiparesis |

## Exceptions worth knowing

- **Thalamic hemorrhage — "wrong-way eyes."** Thalamic hemorrhage can, unpredictably, produce gaze deviation that violates even the brainstem rule — a phenomenon first described by Miller Fisher and still not well mechanistically understood. It is uncommon enough that experienced clinicians may go an entire career without personally observing a case, but it is worth knowing the eponym and the fact that thalamic hemorrhage is the one lesion location where the usual rules may not reliably apply.
- **Post-ictal (Todd's) gaze deviation.** During a seizure, gaze deviates *away* from the ictal focus (per the irritative hemispheric rule above). In the post-ictal phase, however, the exhausted/refractory seizure focus can produce a **reversal** — gaze deviation *back toward* the (now post-ictal) focus — which can closely mimic a new, acute destructive stroke on the same side. An unwitnessed seizure with a post-ictal exam should always be on the differential for an apparent new-onset hemispheric stroke with gaze deviation.

## Related brainstem gaze syndromes

**One-and-a-half syndrome** combines an ipsilateral horizontal gaze palsy (from a PPRF or abducens-nucleus lesion — the "one") with an ipsilateral internuclear ophthalmoplegia (from an adjacent MLF lesion — the "half," since it only affects adduction on attempted contralateral gaze), leaving only contralateral eye abduction as the sole preserved horizontal eye movement. See [[CPS 379 - Sudden Diplopia and One-and-a-Half Syndrome from a Dorsal Pontine Stroke|CPS 379]] for a full case and the underlying MLF/PPRF anatomy, and [[Rule of Four (Brainstem Localization)]] for how the MLF fits into the broader medial-brainstem-structures framework.

## Bedside approach

1. Determine the direction of gaze deviation and the side of any hemiparesis.
2. If gaze deviates *toward* the lesion side and *away* from the hemiparesis → favor a destructive hemispheric lesion.
3. If gaze deviates *away* from a suspected lesion side and *toward* the hemiparesis → favor a destructive brainstem (pontine) lesion.
4. If the exam pattern doesn't fit either rule cleanly, consider thalamic hemorrhage ("wrong-way eyes") or an unwitnessed seizure with post-ictal Todd's-pattern gaze deviation before assuming the rules have simply failed.
