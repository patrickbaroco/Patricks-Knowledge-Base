Evergreen concept note. A heme-positive urine dipstick with few or no red cells on microscopy points to free hemoglobin or myoglobin — not true bleeding — a distinction that separates intravascular hemolysis from RBC hematuria. RLR / Clinical Problem Solvers teaching points are cited per episode; general clinical detail is scaffolding.

## The Core Distinction
The urine dipstick "blood" pad detects the **peroxidase activity of heme**, so it turns positive for **intact red blood cells**, **free hemoglobin**, and **myoglobin** alike. The decisive step is **microscopy**: a strongly heme-positive dipstick with **few or no RBCs** indicates **myoglobinuria or free hemoglobin (pigment)** rather than true bleeding ([[RLR 064 - Hematuria and Post-TAVR Perivalvular Leak|RLR 064]]; [[RLR 030 - Dark Urine and Delayed Hemolytic Transfusion Reaction|RLR 030]]). This "large blood, few RBCs" pattern is the classic tip-off to intravascular hemolysis or rhabdomyolysis ([[RLR 030 - Dark Urine and Delayed Hemolytic Transfusion Reaction|RLR 030]]).

## Sorting the Three Sources
- **True RBC hematuria** — dipstick positive, **RBCs present** on microscopy. Localize as anatomical bleeding (see [[Hematuria]]).
- **Free hemoglobin (intravascular hemolysis)** — dipstick positive, **RBCs absent**; **plasma is pink** (free hemoglobinemia) and haptoglobin is low. Suspect mechanical/shear hemolysis such as **[[Valve Hemolysis|valve hemolysis]]**, TMA, or transfusion reactions.
- **Myoglobinuria (rhabdomyolysis)** — dipstick positive, **RBCs absent**, but **plasma is clear** (myoglobin is small, not protein-bound, and cleared quickly) with a high CK.

## Confirmatory Testing
- **Plasma appearance and plasma free hemoglobin** — pink plasma and elevated plasma free hemoglobin favor intravascular hemolysis over myoglobin ([[RLR 064 - Hematuria and Post-TAVR Perivalvular Leak|RLR 064]]).
- **Haptoglobin** (low), **LDH** (high), **indirect bilirubin** (high), **schistocytes** on smear — the intravascular-hemolysis signature (see [[Intravascular Hemolysis]], [[Hemolysis]]).
- **Urine free hemoglobin** and an actual **RBC count** — measuring these lets you apportion how much "red urine" is bleeding versus hemolysis ([[RLR 064 - Hematuria and Post-TAVR Perivalvular Leak|RLR 064]]).
- **CK** to identify myoglobin.

## Why It Matters at the Bedside
When a patient has **both** a true bleeding source and intravascular hemolysis, attributing all the red urine to bleeding is a trap — you would miss the hemolysis entirely. Explicitly asking "are these red cells, or valve-sheared free hemoglobin?" separates the two mechanisms and can redirect the diagnosis toward a failing valve ([[RLR 064 - Hematuria and Post-TAVR Perivalvular Leak|RLR 064]]; see [[TAVR Perivalvular Leak]]). This mirrors the RLR 030 lesson that a dipstick reading "large blood" with scant RBCs should trigger the hemolysis pathway ([[RLR 030 - Dark Urine and Delayed Hemolytic Transfusion Reaction|RLR 030]]).
