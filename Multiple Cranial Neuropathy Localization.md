When a patient presents with dysfunction of more than one cranial nerve, the central localizing question is not "which nerves are affected" but **where along the neuraxis a single lesion could explain all of them at once** — and, just as importantly, whether the pattern is better explained by a process that isn't localizing to one anatomic site at all.

## The five-level framework

**1. Brainstem (nuclear or fascicular).** A lesion inside the brainstem affects a cranial nerve nucleus or its fascicle before the nerve exits the pia. Because brainstem structures are packed tightly together, a lesion here almost always produces "neighborhood" findings beyond the cranial nerve itself — most classically, contralateral long-tract signs (corticospinal, spinothalamic) alongside the ipsilateral cranial neuropathy, a crossed pattern that is the single strongest clue to a brainstem localization. See [[Rule of Four (Brainstem Localization)]] for the systematic version of this reasoning. Cranial nerve nuclei can also sit close enough to *each other* that a small lesion produces a "neighborhood sign" between two nerves without any long-tract involvement at all — as in [[CPS 199 - Diplopia and an Isolated Microvascular Sixth Nerve Palsy|CPS 199]], where the tight anatomic relationship between the CN VI nucleus and the looping CN VII fascicle around it means an isolated CN VI palsy *without* facial weakness argues specifically *against* a brainstem lesion (since a lesion large enough to hit the VI nucleus would likely also catch the VII fascicle looping around it) and localizes the process to the nerve itself, outside the brainstem.

**2. Subarachnoid space.** Once a cranial nerve exits the brainstem, it travels for some distance through the subarachnoid space, bathed in cerebrospinal fluid, before reaching its exit foramen. A process that infiltrates or inflames the CSF itself — most classically **leptomeningeal disease** — can pick off multiple cranial nerves as they each cross this shared compartment, without any single nerve's exit foramen or brainstem nucleus being the culprit. This is the mechanism behind the multiple, often asymmetric cranial neuropathies of leptomeningeal carcinomatosis (see [[CPS 237 - Slurred Speech and Leptomeningeal Carcinomatosis from Gastric Adenocarcinoma|CPS 237]]) and leptomeningeal lymphomatosis ([[CPS 269 - Headache and Leptomeningeal Lymphomatosis|CPS 269]]), as well as infectious and granulomatous meningitides. The diagnostic signature is enhancement layering in the subarachnoid spaces themselves (classically the cerebellar folia) on post-contrast MRI, rather than a discrete parenchymal or nerve-root mass.

**3. Skull base (named exit-foramina syndromes).** As cranial nerves leave the subarachnoid space, several travel together through shared bony foramina or dural compartments at the skull base, so a single compressive, infiltrative, or inflammatory lesion at one of these sites produces a *specific, named combination* of cranial neuropathies. This is the most classically "testable" level of the framework:

| Syndrome | Cranial nerves involved | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Orbital apex syndrome | II, III, IV, V1, VI | Optic nerve involved — visual loss is expected, unlike more posterior cavernous sinus lesions |
| Superior orbital fissure syndrome | III, IV, V1, VI | Same as orbital apex but *without* optic nerve involvement — visual acuity spared |
| Cavernous sinus syndrome | III, IV, V1, V2, VI (+ oculosympathetic fibers) | See [[Cavernous Sinus Syndrome]] for full detail. Optic nerve (CN II) does **not** pass through the cavernous sinus — preserved visual acuity is a key distinguishing feature |
| Jugular foramen syndrome (Vernet syndrome) | IX, X, XI | Dysphagia, dysphonia, absent gag, sternocleidomastoid/trapezius weakness |
| Petrous apex syndrome (Gradenigo syndrome) | V, VI | Classically from petrous apicitis complicating otitis media — facial pain plus abducens palsy |
| Internal auditory canal syndrome | VII, VIII | Facial weakness plus hearing loss/vestibular dysfunction, without trigeminal or cerebellar involvement |
| Cerebellopontine angle (CPA) syndrome | V, VII, VIII (+ ipsilateral cerebellum) | See [[CPS 339 - Multiple Cranial Neuropathies and a Cerebellopontine Angle Vestibular Schwannoma|CPS 339]] — the CPA is technically just outside the skull base foramina proper, but is grouped here because it produces its own well-defined named combination, classically from vestibular schwannoma or meningioma, and adds ipsilateral cerebellar signs (the cerebellum sits immediately adjacent) to the cranial nerve triad |

The single most useful discriminator among these syndromes at the bedside is simply **which nerves are, and are not, involved** — a patient with painful ophthalmoplegia and V1/V2 sensory loss but preserved visual acuity localizes to the cavernous sinus or superior orbital fissure, not the orbital apex; a patient with ipsilateral facial weakness, hearing loss, and cerebellar ataxia but no trigeminal involvement points toward the internal auditory canal rather than the CPA proper.

**4. Distal target organ.** Rarely, a disease process that primarily affects a *type of tissue* rather than a location can appear to cause "multiple cranial neuropathies" by simultaneously affecting several structures the cranial nerves innervate — a mitochondrial myopathy affecting the extraocular, facial, and bulbar muscles, for example, or a neuromuscular junction disorder affecting several muscle groups at once (see level 5 below, since these often overlap with the "fake-out" category).

**5. Non-localizing "fake-outs."** Several conditions produce a *pattern that mimics* multiple cranial neuropathies without any single anatomic lesion explaining them — these are worth screening out before committing to an anatomic localization at all:

- **Myasthenia gravis** — fatigable weakness of extraocular, bulbar, and/or facial muscles (neuromuscular junction, postsynaptic); no reflex changes, no sensory findings, and fatigability with sustained effort is the key discriminator.
- **Guillain-Barré syndrome / Miller Fisher syndrome** — peripheral, often bilateral and symmetric or near-symmetric cranial neuropathies (classically facial diplegia in GBS; ophthalmoplegia as part of the ataxia-areflexia-ophthalmoplegia triad in Miller Fisher, see [[CPS 263 - Diplopia, Numbness, and Miller Fisher Syndrome|CPS 263]]) accompanied by areflexia and, often, limb involvement or ataxia — the cranial nerves are being affected as peripheral nerves, not via any single shared anatomic compartment.
- **Botulism** — descending paralysis from neuromuscular junction blockade (presynaptic), classically with early, prominent pupillary involvement (unlike GBS, where pupils are typically spared) and bulbar/ocular findings preceding limb weakness.

## Reasoning approach at the bedside

1. Enumerate exactly which cranial nerves are involved, and — just as importantly — which *expected neighbors* are spared (e.g., is CN II involved alongside III/IV/V1/VI, or spared?).
2. Ask whether the pattern matches a brainstem "neighborhood," a named skull-base syndrome, or neither.
3. Look for long-tract signs (favors brainstem) or meningeal/CSF findings (favors subarachnoid space).
4. If the pattern doesn't fit any single anatomic site — especially if there's fatigability, symmetric bulbar/ocular involvement with areflexia, or a rapidly descending pattern — actively consider the non-localizing fake-outs before continuing to search for a single structural lesion.
