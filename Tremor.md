How to characterize a tremor at the bedside and map it to a cause. Built from the CP Solvers Neurology VMR (Aaron Berkowitz's movement-disorder method).

## Characterize first, then localize

In [[Movement Disorder Approach|movement disorders]], decide what the movement is before deciding where it's coming from. First name it: a **tremor** is a rhythmic, oscillatory, roughly sinusoidal movement, unlike the lightning jerk of [[Myoclonus|myoclonus]], the flowing dance of [[Chorea|chorea]], or the sustained co-contraction of [[Dystonia|dystonia]]. Then read four parameters:

- **Activation** (the single most useful axis): does it occur at **rest**, with **posture** (arms outstretched), with **action/intention** (finger-to-nose, crescendo toward a target), or **isometrically** (sustained contraction against resistance, e.g. gripping something, the "gym tremor")? A **re-emergent tremor** looks postural but isn't: no tremor when the position is first assumed, then one appears after several seconds of holding it. That latency marks it as the postural equivalent of a rest tremor, not a true postural tremor (immediate onset), and it still counts as a vote for a Parkinsonian process. ([[Curbsiders 068 - Tremor|Curbsiders 068]])
- **Frequency**: how fast, in Hz (low ≈ 2–5, high ≈ 8–18)? Lower frequency skews "central/cerebellar"; higher frequency skews physiologic/orthostatic.
- **Amplitude**: fine and low, or coarse and high?
- **Distribution**: hands, legs, head, jaw, voice; symmetric or asymmetric; and behavior on maneuvers (does tapping **entrain** or **distract** it, a functional sign?).

There is **no test** that names the tremor; the phenotype is the diagnosis. Once named, each tremor maps to a **four-column cause schema**: primary neurologic disease, structural/lesional, systemic condition, and drugs/toxins.

**Bedside tip: when a limb is confusing, look at a quieter body part.** A very high-amplitude tremor with relaxed, "flopping" hands can look choreoathetoid or mixed; because a patient **rarely has two distinct movement disorders**, cover the busy limb and read the legs (or vice versa). If the legs show a clear rest tremor oscillating at a similar frequency, the arms are usually the *same* tremor at higher amplitude, not a second movement disorder ([[CPS 447 - Psychiatric Disturbance, Tremor, and Wilson Disease|CPS 447]]).

**Common pitfall: stopping the exam early.** Even movement-disorder specialists misdiagnose Parkinsonism roughly 10% of the time (by autopsy correlation), and non-specialists roughly 20%. The leading driver isn't diagnostic difficulty but an incomplete exam: finding a rest component and stopping before testing posture and intention. Much of the work happens before the exam even starts: a specific onset timeline (tremor since the 20s vs. new onset at 65) and the activities that provoke it (coffee, handwriting, buttoning a shirt) reportedly carry ~70% of the diagnosis. ([[Curbsiders 068 - Tremor|Curbsiders 068]])

## Which tremor is which: frequency, amplitude, activation

| Tremor | Activation | Frequency (Hz) | Amplitude | Distinguishing features / associations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Parkinsonian (rest) | **Rest**; damps with action; re-emergent on posture | 4–6 | Low–moderate | Asymmetric, "pill-rolling"; with bradykinesia + rigidity. Parkinson disease, atypical parkinsonism, drug-induced parkinsonism |
| Essential | **Postural + kinetic** (action) | 4–12 (usually 6–8; slows with age) | Moderate (large when severe) | Bilateral, fairly symmetric; hands > head/voice; improves with alcohol; often familial. Essential tremor |
| Enhanced physiologic | Postural | 8–12 | Low (fine) | Fine, symmetric; anxiety, caffeine, hyperthyroidism, β-agonists, hypoglycemia, withdrawal, fever |
| Cerebellar (intention) | **Kinetic**: crescendo as it nears the target | 2–4 (low) | High | With dysmetria and other cerebellar signs; MS, stroke, degeneration, drugs/toxins |
| Holmes (rubral/midbrain) | **Rest + postural + kinetic** (all three) | < 4.5 (2–5) | High, coarse, irregular | Slow, disabling; midbrain/thalamic lesion (stroke, MS, trauma), often weeks–months after the lesion |
| Dystonic | Postural/kinetic; **position-dependent** (a "null point") | 4–7, irregular | Variable | Jerky/irregular, with dystonic posturing (e.g., head tremor in cervical dystonia) |
| Orthostatic | On **standing** (legs/trunk) | **13–18** (very high) | Low (often felt/heard, not seen) | Unsteadiness on standing, relieved by sitting or walking; EMG "helicopter" pattern |
| Functional | Variable | Variable; **entrains** to an external rhythm | Variable | Abrupt onset, distractibility, entrainment, variability; positive rule-in signs (John Stone) |
| Drug/toxin-induced | Usually postural/action (occasionally rest) | Often 6–12 | Variable | Lithium, valproate, SSRIs, β-agonists, amiodarone, tacrolimus, stimulants, mercury, heavy metals |

**Quick frequency ladder (low → high):** cerebellar / Holmes (2–5) < parkinsonian (4–6) < essential (6–8) < enhanced physiologic (8–12) < orthostatic (13–18).
**Amplitude rule of thumb:** cerebellar and Holmes are high-amplitude; essential is moderate (can be large); enhanced physiologic and orthostatic are low/fine.
**Activation shortcut, "which tremors have which":** *rest* → parkinsonian (Holmes also has a rest component); *postural* → essential, enhanced physiologic, dystonic; *kinetic/intention* → cerebellar; *all three* → Holmes; *on standing* → orthostatic; *entrains/distracts* → functional.

## Eliciting each component on exam

A fixed sequence surfaces rest, posture, intention, and bradykinesia in a couple of minutes ([[Curbsiders 068 - Tremor|Curbsiders 068]]):

1. **Rest**: patient seated, arms resting on the armrests; use distraction (serial subtraction, months of the year backward) to unmask a rest tremor patients otherwise suppress.
2. **Posture**: arms outstretched ("Superman"); for a subtle tremor, lay a sheet of paper across the hands or have the patient hold a laser pointer on the wall to amplify visible movement. A "chicken-wing" position (elbows flexed, hands extended, fingertips almost touching) brings out a postural tremor further.
3. **Intention**: finger-to-nose.
4. **Bradykinesia**: rapid alternating movements (finger tapping, hand opening/closing, forearm pronation/supination). A decrement in amplitude over about 30 seconds of repetition is bradykinesia, the defining feature of a Parkinsonian syndrome, distinct from tone testing (cogwheel rigidity at elbows, wrists, knees, ankles) though usually checked alongside it.
5. **Mouth/jaw**: have the patient talk (e.g. describe their commute) and watch the jaw, lips, and tongue. Parkinsonian oral tremor is a rest phenomenon that *lessens* when the mouth opens. Essential tremor's oral involvement (uncommon) is the opposite: tremulous lips/cheeks that appear *with* talking, an action phenomenon.
6. **Handwriting**: a standard phrase in script. Micrographia (progressively shrinking letters) points to Parkinson's; macrographia (large, bold letters, a compensatory strategy since smaller writing worsens the visible tremor) points to essential tremor.
7. **Freehand spiral**: pen held distally, hand not resting on the paper. Essential tremor produces a large, jagged, star- or sea-urchin-shaped spiral; Parkinson's produces a small, tight spiral with fine fluctuations and no coarse jaggedness. Often diagnostic on its own.

When the exam still leaves essential tremor and a Parkinsonian syndrome in play, a [[DAT Scan|DAT scan]] (dopamine transporter imaging) reliably separates the two: normal in essential tremor, abnormal in any Parkinsonism. It can't distinguish which Parkinsonian syndrome, though.

## Cause schema (four columns)

- **Primary neurologic disease**: [[Parkinsonism|Parkinson disease and atypical parkinsonism]], [[Essential Tremor|essential tremor]], dystonic tremor, cerebellar degenerations, [[Wilson Disease|Wilson disease]] (young patient, the wing-beating tremor).
- **Structural / lesional**: a cerebellar or midbrain lesion (stroke, MS, tumor, trauma) producing an [[Cerebellar Tremor|intention]] or [[Holmes Tremor|Holmes]] tremor. (The classic step-exam subthalamic-nucleus lesion causes hemiballismus, not tremor.)
- **Systemic**: [[Thyrotoxicosis|hyperthyroidism]], hypoglycemia, pheochromocytoma, and other hyperadrenergic states (enhanced physiologic tremor); hepatic/renal failure blur into [[Asterixis|asterixis]] and [[Myoclonus|myoclonus]] rather than true tremor.
- **Drugs / toxins**: lithium, valproate, SSRIs, β-agonists, amiodarone, tacrolimus/calcineurin inhibitors, stimulants, caffeine, and heavy metals.

## Distinguishing tremor from its mimics

- **[[Myoclonus|Myoclonus]]**: a sudden lightning jerk across a joint; irregular, not sustained or oscillatory. High-amplitude/rapid myoclonus (polyminimyoclonus) can *look* tremulous.
- **[[Clonus|Clonus]]**: rhythmic, but a sign of hyperreflexia (elicited, e.g., by sustained ankle dorsiflexion), not a spontaneous tremor.
- **[[Dystonia|Dystonic tremor]]**: irregular and position-dependent, with a "null point," accompanying dystonic posture; also suspect it when a tremor is strictly **task-specific** (present only with one activity, e.g. handwriting or playing an instrument) rather than general to posture or action.
- **[[Functional Tremor|Functional tremor]]**: diagnosed by positive signs (entrainment to a tapped rhythm, distractibility, variability, abrupt onset), a *rule-in* diagnosis, not a wastebasket.

## Examples from episodes

- **The full method: characterize by frequency/amplitude/activation, then the four-column schema.** A subacute distal tremor (rest + postural, non-intention) plus facial-brachial myoclonus in a young woman becomes an autoimmune encephalopathy; the tremor's *lack* of a cerebellar/intention quality and the toxidrome/thyroid checks are worked at the bedside. ([[CPS 247 - Tremors, Myoclonus, and Steroid-Responsive Autoimmune Encephalopathy|CPS 247]])
- **Tremor of an atypical-parkinsonian syndrome.** A coarse resting tremor with cogwheel rigidity in a patient whose dysautonomia and cerebellar signs point past Parkinson disease to multiple system atrophy. Parkinsonian tremor read in the context of the wider syndrome. ([[CPS 211 - Episodic Transient Loss of Consciousness and Multiple System Atrophy|CPS 211]])
- **A mixed, high-amplitude tremor + liver disease in a young adult → [[Wilson Disease|Wilson disease]].** A unilateral **rest + posture + action**, high-amplitude tremor (the "look at the legs" disambiguation applied) in a 20-year-old with hepatomegaly and psychiatric change; the broad activation range recalls the [[Holmes Tremor|rubral/Holmes]] pattern, and the classic Wilson descriptor is the proximal **wing-beating** tremor (arms held abducted). Tremor phenomenology intersected with new liver disease is the unifying clue. ([[CPS 447 - Psychiatric Disturbance, Tremor, and Wilson Disease|CPS 447]])
- **Drug-induced tremor, and treating the cause before the symptom.** A 70-year-old on lithium and an antipsychotic develops a disabling postural tremor with no Parkinsonian features (no slowness, rigidity, or masking); the pattern and medication list point to a drug-induced tremor. The lithium dose is reduced first: the tremor improves but stays disabling at lower amplitude. Only then is propranolol added for the residual symptom, since the causative drug can't simply be stopped. ([[Curbsiders 068 - Tremor|Curbsiders 068]])
