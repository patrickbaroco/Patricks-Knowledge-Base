The **"Rule of Four"** (popularized by neurologist Peter Gates) is a bedside mnemonic for localizing brainstem lesions using only two pieces of information: which cranial nerves are involved, and which structures on cross-section are affected. It trades some anatomic precision for speed — useful for a quick first-pass localization at the bedside, to be refined with more detailed anatomy once a general level is established.

## Rule 1 — cranial nerve level tells you midbrain, pons, or medulla

There are 12 cranial nerves. Nerves I and II are not brainstem structures at all (the olfactory and optic nerves are extensions of the forebrain). Of the remaining ten:

- **CN III and IV** — midbrain (an exception to the "four" pattern — only two nerves live here, which is itself a useful thing to remember: finding *only* an isolated CN III or IV problem, without V–VIII findings, points to the midbrain)
- **CN V, VI, VII, and VIII** — pons (four nerves)
- **CN IX, X, XI, and XII** — medulla (four nerves)

So: which cranial nerve is involved tells you, immediately, which of the three brainstem levels to focus on. In [[CPS 305 - Shallow Breathing and Locked-In Syndrome from a Vertebrobasilar Stroke|CPS 305]], for example, intact pupils and extraocular movements placed the midbrain as functioning, an intact face placed the pons as functioning, and **absent cough and gag reflexes** (CN IX/X, localizing to the medulla) placed the causative lesion at or below the medulla — a direct application of this rule using reflexes rather than imaging.

## Rule 2 — four "M" structures run medially

Four structures cluster near the midline at every level of the brainstem, and — with one exception — a lesion affecting them produces **contralateral** signs, because the relevant pathways have either already crossed (motor, sensory) or are about to cross (as they ascend/descend further):

- **M**otor pathway (corticospinal tract) — contralateral limb weakness
- **M**edial lemniscus — contralateral loss of vibration/proprioception
- **M**edial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF) — the exception to the contralateral rule; an MLF lesion causes an *ipsilateral* internuclear ophthalmoplegia (impaired adduction of the ipsilateral eye on contralateral gaze), since the MLF is carrying signal to the ipsilateral medial rectus subnucleus
- **M**otor nucleus and nerve of the cranial nerve at that level (III, IV, VI, or XII, depending on level) — produces an *ipsilateral* cranial nerve palsy, since the nerve exits on the same side it originates

A **medial** (paramedian) brainstem lesion, then, classically produces a combination of an **ipsilateral cranial nerve palsy** (from the motor nucleus/nerve) together with a **contralateral hemiparesis and/or contralateral loss of vibration/proprioception** — a "crossed" syndrome. This is one of the most important brainstem localizing patterns and is the exam finding that most reliably distinguishes a brainstem lesion from a hemispheric one.

## Rule 3 — four "S" structures run laterally

Four structures cluster more laterally at every level, and a lesion affecting them tends to produce **ipsilateral** signs, since these pathways have not yet crossed at the level of the lesion:

- **S**pinocerebellar pathways — ipsilateral limb ataxia
- **S**pinothalamic tract — contralateral pain/temperature loss of the *body* (the one lateral structure that is, confusingly, contralateral — spinothalamic fibers have already crossed at the spinal cord level before ascending)
- **S**ensory nucleus of cranial nerve V (spinal trigeminal tract/nucleus) — ipsilateral pain/temperature loss of the *face*
- **S**ympathetic pathway (descending) — ipsilateral Horner syndrome

A **lateral** brainstem lesion classically produces ipsilateral ataxia, ipsilateral facial pain/temperature loss, an ipsilateral Horner syndrome, and — the one crossed element in this otherwise ipsilateral group — contralateral body pain/temperature loss. This crossed facial-versus-body sensory pattern (ipsilateral face, contralateral body) is itself a highly specific sign of a lateral brainstem (or high cervical cord) lesion.

## Putting it together

The practical bedside sequence is: (1) identify which cranial nerve(s) are involved to establish the level (midbrain/pons/medulla); (2) determine whether the accompanying long-tract and sensory findings are medial-pattern (crossed: ipsilateral CN palsy + contralateral weakness/proprioceptive loss) or lateral-pattern (mostly ipsilateral, with crossed facial-vs-body sensory loss); (3) combine both to arrive at a specific quadrant of brainstem cross-section, before confirming with imaging.
