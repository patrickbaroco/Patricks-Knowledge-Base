Rectus sheath hematoma is a spontaneous or trauma-provoked arterial bleed from the epigastric vessels into the rectus sheath, classically presenting as an abdominal-wall mass that respects the midline.

## Definition and Anatomy
A rectus sheath hematoma (RSH) is a collection of blood within the rectus abdominis muscle and its fibrous sheath, arising from rupture of the epigastric arteries or tearing of the muscle fibers.

- **Arterial source.** The bleed originates from the **inferior epigastric artery** (below the arcuate line, where it is most common and least tamponaded) or, less often, the **superior epigastric artery** ([[RLR 058 - Inguinal Bulge and Rectus Sheath Hematoma|RLR 058]]).
- **Midline boundary — the linea alba.** The rectus sheath is bounded in the midline by the **[[Linea Alba]]**, so a hematoma (and its overlying [[Ecchymosis]]) *cannot cross the midline*. A bulge or bruise that stops abruptly at the midline localizes the process to the sheath — the key exam sign in the episode ([[RLR 058 - Inguinal Bulge and Rectus Sheath Hematoma|RLR 058]]).
- **Arcuate line.** Below the arcuate (semicircular) line the posterior sheath is absent, so inferior epigastric bleeds can dissect freely into the pelvis and prevesical space, producing larger, sometimes hemodynamically significant hematomas.

## Risk Factors and Triggers
- **Anticoagulation** (warfarin, DOACs, heparin) is the dominant modern risk factor; the drug is the *vulnerability*, not the force ([[RLR 058 - Inguinal Bulge and Rectus Sheath Hematoma|RLR 058]]).
- **Cough / Valsalva / straining** provides the pressure trigger; patients often cannot recall the trivial event because it truly was trivial ([[RLR 058 - Inguinal Bulge and Rectus Sheath Hematoma|RLR 058]]).
- Other contributors: advanced age, female sex, pregnancy, abdominal-wall injections, recent surgery, chronic cough, and vigorous exercise.

## Presentation and Differential
- **Acute abdominal-wall pain and a palpable, non-reducible, tender mass**, frequently with overlying [[Ecchymosis]] that may track along fascial planes ([[RLR 058 - Inguinal Bulge and Rectus Sheath Hematoma|RLR 058]]).
- **Fothergill sign** — a palpable mass that remains fixed and does not cross the midline when the rectus is tensed (patient lifts head/legs), distinguishing an abdominal-wall mass from an intra-abdominal one.
- **Carnett sign** — pain that persists or worsens with abdominal-wall tensing, localizing pain to the wall rather than the viscera.
- Differential includes incarcerated hernia, abscess, abdominal-wall tumor/sarcoma, and intra-abdominal hemorrhage. The [[Anatomical Approach]] to a visible bulge (what substance, what plane) plus [[Point-of-Care Ultrasound|POCUS]] narrows it at the bedside.

## Diagnosis
- **CT with contrast** is the confirmatory test: it defines size, location relative to the arcuate line, and — critically — the presence or absence of **active extravasation** (contrast blush), which flags ongoing arterial bleeding and predicts need for intervention.
- Ultrasound can identify the hematoma at the bedside but is operator-dependent and less reliable for extravasation.
- Labs: CBC (baseline and trend for a falling hemoglobin), coagulation studies/INR to gauge reversibility.

## Management
Match the intervention to severity, not merely to the diagnosis ([[RLR 058 - Inguinal Bulge and Rectus Sheath Hematoma|RLR 058]]).

- **Conservative therapy** is first-line for most: rest, analgesia, cough suppression, and **holding anticoagulation**. The tight sheath frequently **self-tamponades**, so many RSH are self-limited.
- **Anticoagulation reversal** is guided by the *source of bleeding*: arterial bleeds (like RSH) tip toward **reversal**, whereas venous bleeds are more often simply observed — a specialist pearl that reorganizes the management branch ([[RLR 058 - Inguinal Bulge and Rectus Sheath Hematoma|RLR 058]]). See [[Anticoagulation Reversal]].
- **Transfusion / resuscitation** for hemodynamic compromise or a significant hemoglobin drop.
- **IR angioembolization** of the epigastric artery is reserved for hemodynamic instability, active extravasation on CT, expanding hematoma, or compressive complications (e.g., femoral nerve palsy from a large hematoma).
- **Surgical evacuation/ligation** is a last resort for failure of the above or compartment-type physiology.
- Downstream: reassess whether/when to re-anticoagulate (weigh [[CHA2DS2-VASc]] against bleeding risk); if re-anticoagulation is untenable in AFib, consider a [[Watchman Procedure]].
