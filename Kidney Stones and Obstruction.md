Interpreting an obstructing kidney stone — imaging choice, what hydronephrosis does and doesn't tell you, and when urgency escalates.

## How it operates

- **A contralateral asymptomatic stone is common.** Roughly 20–30% of patients imaged for a symptomatic stone have an unrelated, asymptomatic contralateral stone; a stone only causes symptoms once it obstructs.
- **Contrast CT barely reduces stone sensitivity.** A radiologist's rule of thumb: non-contrast CT is roughly 98% sensitive for clinically significant obstructing stones versus roughly 95% with contrast — a small enough tradeoff that a single contrast-enhanced study is often preferred over sequential non-contrast-then-contrast imaging (which doubles radiation) when contrast adds value elsewhere (e.g., evaluating for infection).
- **Hydronephrosis confirms obstruction, not urgency.** It tells you a given stone is symptomatic, not how urgently it needs intervention.
- **Size predicts spontaneous passage.** Stones under 10 mm are likely to pass with medical expulsive therapy and observation; urgency of urologic referral should escalate sharply whenever a stone causes AKI, particularly in a patient with reduced nephron reserve from an unrelated process.

## Examples from RLR episodes

- **Reduced reserve changes the urgency calculus.** A 6 mm obstructing stone would typically not even warrant a urology referral in an otherwise healthy patient — but because the contralateral kidney is already compromised by an underlying myeloproliferative neoplasm, every functioning nephron matters, and urology is consulted urgently for stenting.
