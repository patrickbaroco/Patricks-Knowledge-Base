## The alignment problem

Patrick's framing: *"We do a lot of things. We kind of do a lot of things. We don't really do a lot of things."* Quality, education and auditing exist but are not organized as one system.

The intended shape:

1. **Quality initiatives** anchored to ACO measures and enterprise-accepted, Eventus-wide quality targets.
2. **Education aligned to those initiatives** — folded into the didactics people already attend (psych, medicine, primary care), not standalone ACO meetings. Standalone ACO sessions should be rare: *"nobody's like, oh yeah, I can't wait to hear more about ACO measures."* Primary care providers show up for primary care didactics.
3. **Clinical pathways carrying the measures.** Current pathways "disappear into the wiki" — hard to find, hard to access. Written educational material should have the ACO measures tied into it.
4. **Systematic audits closing the loop** — feeding back on how initiatives are doing, how education is landing, and on the measures themselves (a measure may be the wrong one, or too easy, and should be retired for something better).

**Auditing method.** Scrap the current "signature standard" auditing — not meaningful. Move to actual clinical measures. Random chart audits plus compiled data (likely stitched from more than one source pre-Athena). **Athena will substantially expand what can be measured**; until then, deliberately target things that are easy to measure now.

## Physician accountability — the real gap

The sharpest problem in the meeting, and it is structural rather than individual.

- Education and feedback currently land almost entirely **on the APCs**. Where a collaborating/supervising physician isn't on board, the APC can't move, and there's no documented physician support.
- Dr. Patel's framing — *the physician guides the direction, the APC executes* — gets read by some physicians as **"all I have to say in my note is 'goal A1c less than 9,' and then I'm done."**
- **The correction: physicians own the outcome, not just the plan.** The physician writes the regulatory note, the APC writes the chronic care note, but they should be writing the same note in substance and both are accountable for the result.
- **Operationally:** when an audit flags a building — say, some threshold percentage of diabetics with A1c > 9 — the feedback meeting includes **the physician as well as the APC**. That does not happen today.

Sandra's worked example: more than half the year gone, an active diabetic with **no A1c drawn**, two physician visits with no acknowledgment that it's missing. Today the only person told is the APC. Her words: *"when we try to get physicians involved, sometimes they get crunchy and they're like, you can't tell me what to do"* — and her own read is that this is fair, **because Eventus never set an expectation of physician involvement in the first place.**

Setting that expectation is the deliverable.

## Peer review as the enforcement layer

## Hospice

The largest single thread; full working detail in [[Hospice Utilization]]. Summary of what was established here:

**Sandra's data view.** Every month the same two names come up as the highest certifiers — **Dr. Bengali** and **Dr. Begalia** *(sp? — likely garbled, confirm both)* — with a disproportionate hospice census across a disproportionate number of facilities. She has patients who have been on hospice **for years**. Her honest position: she can see it in the data but doesn't understand the mechanics — how certification and recertification work, who controls whether a patient stays certified, who decides when someone should graduate, and what role the APC plays in acute and chronic care for a hospice patient. **She explicitly wants this one physician-driven.**

**Patrick's read of her list.** It is disproportionately assisted living, which is *correct*. SNF hospice should exist but be limited to cases where hospice adds real medical expertise — his test: *"if it is important to me that they call me overnight or on the weekend, not the triage team, because this is complicated, that's a use for hospice."*

**AL is the harder case, and length of stay is the discriminator.** A building with high hospice utilization over a year can be entirely appropriate — a resident with a stage 3 wound is getting regulatory-marginal for the AL, and hospice is what lets them stay in the building instead of moving to long-term care. That's a legitimate use. The signal of a problem is not volume, it's **long length of stay**.

**The failure mode.** Predatory hospices market to the facility directly (*"they're always bringing food, lunches all the time"*), and some call families themselves — *wouldn't it be great if your mom had an extra CNA* — and the patient ends up on hospice **with no Eventus provider involved at all**. Gentiva *(transcript: "Genteva")* named as notorious for taking our patients; smaller ones worse. This has happened to Patrick personally.

**The attending-of-record mechanism, which is the crux.** Medicare requires the hospice to ask the family who they want as attending. **It is not enforced.** So the hospice lists its own medical director, that medical director signs the initial certification, the record says the patient has no primary care provider, and **Eventus never sees another piece of hospice paperwork** — they just keep recertifying indefinitely.

**The regulation that makes this fixable:** only a physician can certify or recertify, and the primary care physician is only required on the *initial* certification, and only if there is one. If the primary care provider is an NP, the certifying physician is the hospice's physician — **but the NP remains the attending.** So who signs the initial form matters far less than whether the NP knows it's being signed, referred the patient, stays the ongoing provider, and can pull the patient off hospice. (It also matters for billing: **an NP can only bill Medicare if listed as the attending.**)

**The principle:** hospice is a service provided *to our patients*, ordered by the treating provider. They remain our patients. **"Nobody just materializes on hospice."** Everyone at Eventus — APCs, physicians, and the home health people on the back end — needs to see it that way.

**Ownership — Sandra proposed, Patrick corrected.** Sandra's three-part read was (1) name preferred hospice partners by region/facility, (2) give the facility's physician ownership of the hospice relationship since they sign the certifications, (3) major APC education. Patrick agreed on 1 and 3, and corrected 2: in AL the physician is in the building maybe **once a quarter** and can't be on top of day-to-day. **The NP is the attending and should be listed as such**, meeting the hospice nurse weekly or biweekly to walk the census. Physician ownership is of the *relationship*, not the patients.

**The pitch to facilities and hospices** (Patrick's language, worth keeping): *"We realize us coming in and saying there's going to be less hospice is threatening to you. What I want is less bad hospice. If you're providing good hospice, you'll have more referrals, and the bad hospice will have fewer."* A reputable hospice wants short length of stay anyway — less regulatory scrutiny — and will happily discharge a patient who no longer qualifies in exchange for a reliable stream of appropriate referrals from a partner in long-term care. Win-win, and it makes the AL conversation easy: *if they need hospice to stay here, great, I'll support it — but it's this hospice, and I put in the referral.*

**"We just have to own it, because if we don't own it, hospice will be very happy to own it — and they don't care about the ACO one bit."**
