### Over-seed or kill-and-reseed? — the tall fescue problem (resolved 2026-08-30)

> [!warning] Over-seeding + no-mow does not work
> The existing lawn is almost certainly **turf-type tall fescue**, the default lawn grass in
> Virginia's transition zone. Tall fescue is a coarse, upright **bunch grass** that reaches
> **3–4 ft unmowed** and throws tall seed heads; fine fescue lays over at 6–8 in. Stop mowing
> a mixed stand and you get coarse clumps spiking out of a low mat — lumpy and uneven, worse
> than either grass alone. Shade makes it worse, since tall fescue thins and clumps harder in
> low light. **Over-seed and no-mow are mutually exclusive here.**

> [!note] The brown phase for a fescue conversion is ~4–6 weeks, not 18 months
> Nothing like the meadow. We are establishing a fast-germinating turfgrass immediately
> rather than dormant-seeding natives that sleep until spring. Spray ~Sept 5–6 → brown by
> mid-September → seed Sept 19–26 → germination in 10–14 days → **green again by early-to-mid
> October**, sod by spring. Virginia's fall fescue window runs roughly **Sept 1 – Oct 15**;
> earlier is better and we are at the front of it now.
> These zones need **one** knockdown, not two — two rounds are for the meadow, where slow
> natives cannot face competition. A turf renovation kills once and immediately establishes a
> competitive grass.

### Native alternatives (considered)
- **Pennsylvania sedge (Carex pensylvanica)** — the native answer for shade. 6–12 in., needs
  no mowing, tolerates full shade. **Not available as seed** (germination unreliable) — plugs
  only, 1 ft spacing for instant cover. But it spreads **3–8 ft/year by rhizome**, so
  scattered colonies at wide spacing will fill in over a few years. Realistic as accent
  colonies in gully openings or in front-zone spots too dark for fescue; not realistic as a
  0.47-acre carpet
- **Poverty oatgrass (Danthonia spicata)** — native, low, dry shade, poor soils. Seed is
  specialty and slow to establish. A purist option, not a practical one at this scale
- **Nothing at all** — a true eastern woodland floor is leaf litter. Still the right answer
  for any part of the gully under real canopy


### Clover in the fescue zones (added 2026-08-30)

**Shade:** white clover is full sun to part shade and "looks ragged in deep shade" — placing
it **below fine fescue** on the shade ladder. The **gully** (broken canopy) is fine. The
**front** could clear the fescue's 2-hour minimum and still be too dark for clover, so one
light count does not answer both questions.

> [!warning] Clover and true no-mow are in tension
> In a genuinely unmowed 6–8 in. fescue stand, **the fescue shades out the clover.** Clover
> persists in lawns partly *because* mowing keeps the grass short enough for it to compete.
> Commercial fescue+clover no-mow mixes are sold as **"0–2 mows per year," not never** — that
> once-or-twice cut is what keeps clover in the stand. Prairie Nursery already recommends a
> June and a fall mow, so this is likely fine. **If the plan is truly zero mowing, skip the
> clover.**

> [!note] Nitrogen — earlier caution refined
> "Fine fescue dislikes nitrogen" is about *applied synthetic* fertilizer. Clover supplies
> roughly **1 lb N/1,000 sq ft/yr** through root nodules — slow and biological. Fescue+clover
> is an established product category, not a contradiction.

**Rate discipline is what actually matters.** Clover seed is small — ~800,000 seeds/lb vs
~450,000 for fine fescue — so a tiny weight fraction delivers a large plant count. Too much
and clover dominates the establishment year and the low grass-textured look is lost.

- **~5% by weight, or ~0.25 lb per 1,000 sq ft**
- **Gully (9,882 sq ft): ~2 lbs**
- **Front (10,764 sq ft): ~2 lbs** — only if the light count supports it
- **Buy inoculated seed**, or add rhizobium inoculant. Without it there is no N fixation.

> [!warning] Mowing timing vs. the hives
> A June mow cuts the clover bloom off at its peak. Mow **after** bloom, or make fall the only
> cut. Worth deciding deliberately, since bee forage is the whole reason for the clover.

## Glyphosate

### Product
- Standard 41% glyphosate concentrate (e.g., Compare-N-Save, Ranger Pro, Glyphosate 4
  Plus, Hi-Yield Killzall, or similar — functionally interchangeable)
- **Bought: Hi-Yield Killzall, 41% glyphosate, double-surfactant formulation (already
  fully loaded — no separate surfactant needed).** Note for future reference: Killzall runs
  expensive per gallon (~$76/gal at DoMyOwn); Compare-N-Save or Ranger Pro 2.5-gal jugs are
  the same active ingredient + surfactant for roughly a third to a quarter of the per-gallon
  cost, if buying again.
- Avoid formulations marketed for brush/woody plants (e.g., triclopyr, "extended control")
  — standard grass/turf formulation is what's needed here

### Dosing principle
Glyphosate is dosed by **amount of product per acre**, not by spray-solution volume. The
water is only a carrier — mix enough solution to cover the area spraying to
wet-but-not-dripping (runoff wastes product and reduces kill). Get the per-acre **product**
rate right and the water volume can flex to the sprayer.

- Standard rate for killing existing lawn/pasture grass: **2–3 qt of 41% product per acre**

### Quantity for the drainfield only — ~0.26 acres (~11,485 sq ft)

#### Per application
- Product needed: **~0.5–0.8 qt** (2–3 qt/acre)
- Diluted solution needed: **~6–11 gallons**, depending on mix strength (1.5–2%)
- Backpack loads (4-gallon): **~2–3 loads**

#### For both applications (2x)

> [!warning] Don't over-apply
> An earlier draft of this note back-calculated from "300–400 sq ft per gallon of
> solution," which implies ~8–11 qt/acre — roughly 2–3× the normal rate and at/above most
> label maximums. More glyphosate does not mean a better kill; follow the per-acre product
> rate and the label.

> [!warning] Application conditions
> Spray while grass is **green and actively growing** (this is what the late-Aug / mid-Sept
> timing is about — see [[Meadow Timeline]]). No rain forecast for 24–48 hours, wind under
> 10 mph, early morning ideal. Avoid drought-stressed or frost-touched growth — the
> herbicide won't translocate and won't kill. Shaded front zone may green up/dormant on a
> different schedule than the sunny zones — check it separately before each application.
> Gully (part-sun) may also lag the fully sunny drainfield slightly.

> [!warning] Fruit trees and small trees — spray caution
> The drainfield's top strip has fruit trees, and the gully has many small trees. Glyphosate
> is generally safe around mature, established trees with hardened bark, but avoid drift or
> direct contact onto green bark, low limbs, suckers, foliage, or exposed roots/root flare —
> young or thin-barked trees are more vulnerable to uptake. Use a low-pressure setting or
> shield/spot-treat near trunks rather than broadcast spraying right up to the base, and
> avoid spraying on windy days near these areas in particular.

> [!note] Equipment
> Sprayer purchased: **4-gallon battery backpack sprayer**. Calibrate before first use: at
> a fixed nozzle/pressure setting, measure how much solution is actually applied over a
> known area, then set mix strength to deliver the per-acre product rate. Battery sprayers
> hold steadier pressure than manual pump units, so coverage is more consistent once dialed in.
