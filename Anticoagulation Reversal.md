Anticoagulation reversal is the decision — and the pharmacology — of counteracting an anticoagulant in a bleeding or high-risk patient, driven by the bleeding source, the drug, and the competing thrombotic risk.

## The Reversal Decision
Reversal is never automatic; it is a balance of the bleed against the clot the drug was preventing.

- **Stop first, then decide on reversal.** In a patient with a *known bleed and no known clot*, the immediate move is to hold the anticoagulant; active reversal is a separate, escalatory decision ([[RLR 058 - Inguinal Bulge and Rectus Sheath Hematoma|RLR 058]]).
- **Arterial vs venous source guides reversal.** A specialist pearl: **venous** bleeds are more often observed, while an **arterial** bleed (e.g., a [[Rectus Sheath Hematoma]] from the epigastric arteries) tips toward **reversal** ([[RLR 058 - Inguinal Bulge and Rectus Sheath Hematoma|RLR 058]]).
- **Severity and site.** Major criteria pushing toward reversal: hemodynamic instability, critical-site bleeding (intracranial, pericardial, airway, compartment), a significant hemoglobin drop, or need for urgent surgery.
- **Weigh thrombotic risk.** The indication being reversed (mechanical valve, recent VTE, high [[CHA2DS2-VASc]] AFib) sets the cost of reversal and shapes how quickly to re-anticoagulate.

## Warfarin (Vitamin K Antagonist) Reversal
Warfarin depletes functional factors II, VII, IX, X (and proteins C, S).

- **Vitamin K** restores synthesis but works over hours; **oral** is preferred for non-urgent reversal (reversed an INR of 2.3 in the episode), **IV** (slow) for urgent/major bleeding. Avoid over-correction if the patient will need re-anticoagulation ([[RLR 058 - Inguinal Bulge and Rectus Sheath Hematoma|RLR 058]]).
- **4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (4F-PCC)** is the agent of choice for *major/life-threatening* warfarin bleeding — rapid, low-volume factor replacement, dosed by weight and INR; give **with IV vitamin K** to sustain the effect.
- **FFP** is an alternative when PCC is unavailable, but is slower, volume-heavy, and less complete.

## DOAC Reversal
- **Dabigatran (direct thrombin inhibitor):** specific antidote **idarucizumab** (monoclonal antibody fragment); dialysis is an adjunct because dabigatran is dialyzable.
- **Factor Xa inhibitors (apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban):** **andexanet alfa** is the specific reversal agent; **4F-PCC** is a widely used off-label alternative when andexanet is unavailable or cost-prohibitive.
- **Timing matters:** last-dose timing and renal function determine residual drug effect; a distant last dose with normal clearance may need no reversal at all.
- Activated charcoal is reasonable for a very recent ingestion.

## General Supportive Measures and Aftercare
- **Local and mechanical control:** pressure, endoscopic/IR hemostasis, and source control (e.g., epigastric artery embolization for [[Rectus Sheath Hematoma]]).
- **Resuscitation:** transfusion, tranexamic acid in selected settings, and correction of coexisting coagulopathy (uremia, thrombocytopenia, dilution).
- **Re-anticoagulation planning:** decide *whether and when* to restart, balancing rebleeding against thrombosis; if anticoagulation is untenable in AFib, consider a [[Watchman Procedure]] (analogous to a temporary IVC filter for the patient who can't tolerate DVT anticoagulation) ([[RLR 058 - Inguinal Bulge and Rectus Sheath Hematoma|RLR 058]]).
