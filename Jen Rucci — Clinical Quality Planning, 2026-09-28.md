## Durham Ridge, and why North Carolina is different

North Carolina Medicaid funds assisted living on a scale no other state matches. Kentucky does it for a limited number of licensed beds; Rucci has found no other state like North Carolina. The buildings run on thin margins.

The complication is the IMD rule: a Medicaid-funded ALF cannot have more than half its residents carrying an SPMI diagnosis. North Carolina began enforcing it about ten years ago. Many of those ALFs closed, and in others dementia was charted onto residents to keep the building clear of IMD status, the mirror image of schizophrenia charted in SNFs to justify a medication. Rucci has rounded at Durham Ridge many times: *"There might be two patients in that facility that have dementia. It is a mental health facility."* Its outlier status says nothing about its NPs.

Her worry about the method as a whole: chasing buildings down rabbit holes and concluding the providers are doing a bad job, when the diagnosis is what's wrong.

A second-order prediction of hers worth testing. Because North Carolina diverts residents with SPMI to assisted living, its SNFs should carry less SPMI, and so less antipsychotic use, than other states' SNFs. *"I don't think we do, but we should."* Her state × setting breakout can check it.

My side: rates are never attributed to an individual, because the big outliers usually have a reason. Spark, a Hill Valley building we lost that I had for a decade, was where people deinstitutionalized from Western State eventually landed. Half the residents had been there, and the antipsychotic numbers sank a good facility's quality measures. The biggest outliers at least have meaningful systems issues, and some are Medicaid-policy problems we cannot fix. Virginia has its own version: auxiliary grants are infrequent, and once a screener finds a long-term care need the resident can essentially never move to an ALF, so some auxiliary-grant ALFs sit with empty beds.

Setting shapes the work too. North Carolina's Medicaid ALFs are mostly residents without family or POA, and the work is fast. In a nice Virginia CCRC, stopping atorvastatin in a 98-year-old with advanced dementia is a 45-minute conversation with a daughter. Population health across states and settings is hard for that reason.

## EQUiP mechanics

Rucci sits on the EQUiP changes, which are meant to be small tweaks to a program that is generally working, and this is one of them. Her request: **keep the point scale and weighting so the new rubric maps into EQUiP the same way.** Five questions instead of twenty is fine if the points reweight; Byrne did exactly that in her psychiatry revision.

As she recalls v2 (Josh can confirm): each component pays separately. Signature standards is its own component, graded out of 100, and pays only above 90. Last year's v1 averaged three 100-point components into one grade and paid in tiers.

The threshold sets the insulin standard. **At six reviewed charts, five of six is 83% and pays nothing.** I am comfortable requiring six of six. A comprehensive chronic care note on a resident receiving corrective insulin three times a day that does not say how much they receive is substandard. I want to run that past a few of my A students first to be sure it is not a cranky internist talking.

> [!warning] Conflicts with the drafted measure
> `Clinical Governance/equip-corrective-insulin-measure.md` specifies five charts per clinician per cycle with one allowance, an 80% bar that pays nothing under a 90 threshold. Today's discussion assumed six charts, all required. Reconcile the chart count and the allowance before the measure goes further.

The pass criterion is unchanged: the note states how much corrective insulin the resident actually received. Having corrective insulin is not a failure. I have a resident on dulaglutide 4.5 mg, metformin, an SGLT2 inhibitor and 80 units of insulin glargine, A1c 9.5, 15 units with meals, who drinks two liters of soda in the afternoon and needs correction to cover it. If he averages 10 extra units at every check, his basal or scheduled dose is wrong, and the note has to show someone looked. Clinically necessary, with a real workflow cost: the corrective doses live on the MAR in PCC, which is hard to read. A PA asked me where to find it.

## Objective and subjective, kept apart

Rucci pushed: if a note states 20 units of correction a day and nothing changes, what makes that acceptable? The plan is not scored for money because reasonable physicians differ (I correct once a day at dinner in frail elders; others correct every meal), and the reviewer is the supervising physician, who can take up the plan directly. Stating the number usually produces the change on its own.

Rucci's synthesis: **a narrow objective measure tied to dollars, plus a subjective supervising-physician review tied to nothing.** If the review were only objective, Jonna could do it. The reason to use physicians is that these providers need physicianizing. The measure's job is to put the clinician's reasoning on the table where the supervisor can assess it, for praise and rapport as much as for correction.

Her addition: a **hidden field on the reviewer form**, like the manager-only section of a UKG review, where the supervising physician can flag a referral to the quality committee. The subjective review becomes a record without being punitive, and gives a physician who noticed something a safe way to say so.

The escalation path we agreed on. The supervising physician handles "not wrong, but here's what I'd do." A gap larger than the supervisor can teach goes to the CQC, which decides whether it is an education gap or a danger. Danger (three hypoglycemia readmissions in a month) or a pattern that persists despite education goes to peer review. Repeated hypoglycemia on corrective insulin could become an individual rule later, with room for the brittle patient already followed by endocrinology. Blood glucose values sit under vital signs in PCC, so this is easier to audit than the MAR.

**No individual rate measures in EQUiP.** Rucci asked about per-provider rates of corrective insulin, or of antipsychotics in dementia. Rates belong to the quality committee at the system level. Per clinician they would punish APCs in North Carolina buildings with younger residents appropriately on antipsychotics, who have metabolic syndrome from them and need tight control.

## An individual rule for antipsychotics

From the outlier charts I read yesterday: almost every note says *prior GDR unsuccessful, continue risperidone 0.5 mg daily*. When was the attempt, and what failed? A hospitalization, or a nurse who thought the resident was marginally worse one day two weeks after the stop? **Candidate rule: document the date of the last GDR and its specific outcome.** Nobody is held to getting everyone off; the dot phrase stops working. Rucci likes it. **Run it past Byrne.**
