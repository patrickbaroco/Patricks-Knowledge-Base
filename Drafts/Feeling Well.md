# Feeling Well

*Working title. Bedside Notes, longer reflective issue.*

## Draft

“I feel well.” That is her report at every visit. At age 84, Ms. Andrews is a beloved figure in her assisted living. She is quick to smile and slow to complain. But her blood pressure is 154/78, and persistently so. She is started on 5 mg of amlodipine, but a month later her blood pressure is only modestly improved. She is started on 12.5 mg of hydrochlorothiazide. The following month, her blood pressure is 138/70 and she is pleased with the improvement. She feels well. Two months later, she falls on her way to lunch and is not able to stand. In the hospital, her sodium is 126. Her hip is surgically repaired and she is discharged to skilled care. Delirium from the hospital does not clear, and she makes little headway with therapy. She begins to fade, and a family meeting is scheduled. She returns to her assisted living on hospice and dies peacefully a few months later.

The word “patient” is derived from Latin, and means “one who suffers”. From the earliest days, the intent of medical practice has been to relieve suffering. 

---

## Notes

**Thesis.** Be honest with ourselves about what is in our power, and humble about the fact that trying to make a well person better can make them worse. This is not an argument for doing nothing.

**Structure considered**
1. Opening case: a resident who feels well, and a defensible prescribing cascade
2. The asymmetry: little room to feel better, some room to feel worse
3. History: the microbial triad; the London flush toilet as hinge
4. Honest accounting: we credit ourselves for wellness and attribute harms to frailty
5. What remains in our power: vaccines, anticoagulation for AF, time to benefit
6. Return to the opening resident

**Possible live questions**
- How do you tell, in the moment, whether you are healing or meddling?
- Why is it so hard for clinicians to leave a well person alone?
- Where did the maxim come from? What patient or mistake taught it?

## Sources

### Where the gains in life expectancy came from

**Cutler D, Miller G.** The role of public health improvements in health advances: the twentieth-century United States. *Demography*. 2005;42(1):1–22. [doi:10.1353/dem.2005.0002](https://doi.org/10.1353/dem.2005.0002)
Clean water (filtration and chlorination) accounted for nearly half of the total mortality reduction in major US cities in the early twentieth century, three quarters of the infant mortality reduction, and nearly two thirds of the child mortality reduction. The gains were concentrated in the young.

**Bunker JP, Frazier HS, Mosteller F.** Improving health: measuring effects of medical care. *Milbank Q*. 1994;72(2):225–58. [PMID 8007898](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8007898/)
Estimated the aggregate effect of medical care on life expectancy at roughly five years over the twentieth century, with a further potential of two years.

**Bunker JP.** Medicine matters after all. *J R Coll Physicians Lond*. 1995;29(2):105–12. [PMID 7595883](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7595883/)
Attributes three of the seven years of life expectancy gained since 1950 to medical care, and estimates an average of five years of partial or complete relief from the poor quality of life of chronic disease. A counterweight to the thesis: medicine's share grew in the chronic-disease era, mostly through treatment in middle and early old age.

**Bunker JP.** Low-cost therapeutic agents: uses and abuses. *Int J Technol Assess Health Care*. 1997;13(4):521–5. [doi:10.1017/s0266462300009995](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0266462300009995)
Estimates that drugs and vaccines account for about half of medicine's contribution to life expectancy and quality of life.

**Bunker JP.** The role of medical care in contributing to health improvements within societies. *Int J Epidemiol*. 2001;30(6):1260–3. [doi:10.1093/ije/30.6.1260](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/30.6.1260)
Later summary of the same body of estimates.

### The McKeown debate

**McKeown T.** *The Modern Rise of Population*. 1976. Also *The Role of Medicine: Dream, Mirage, or Nemesis?* 1976.
Argued that rising living standards, especially nutrition, drove the nineteenth-century decline in infectious mortality, with little role for medicine. Not yet consulted; verify edition and pages before citing.

**Szreter S.** The importance of social intervention in Britain's mortality decline c.1850–1914: a re-interpretation of the role of public health. *Soc Hist Med*. 1988;1(1). Verify pages.
Challenged McKeown's evidence and emphasized preventive public health measures and local authorities' response to urban congestion.

**Szreter S.** Rethinking McKeown: the relationship between public health and social change. *Am J Public Health*. 2002;92(5):722–5. [doi:10.2105/ajph.92.5.722](https://doi.org/10.2105/ajph.92.5.722)
Szreter's later restatement, focused on the political and social character of public health.

**Guha S.** The importance of social intervention in England's mortality decline: the evidence reviewed. *Soc Hist Med*. 1994;7(1):89–113. [doi:10.1093/shm/7.1.89](https://doi.org/10.1093/shm/7.1.89)
Finds weaknesses in both McKeown and Szreter. Diarrhoeal diseases persisted despite late-Victorian public health measures but appear to have become less lethal. The historiography remains contested.

### London: flush toilets, sewers, cholera

Verified from secondary accounts only:
- 1848: the Metropolitan Commission of Sewers required cesspools and house drains to be connected to sewers that emptied, unfiltered, into the Thames. The commission acted on miasma theory.
- Flush toilets, marketed to the public at the Great Exhibition of 1851, increased household wastewater, overwhelmed the old cesspools, and pushed more sewage into the river London drew its drinking water from.
- Bazalgette's intercepting sewers were completed around 1870. The final cholera epidemic, in 1866, reportedly struck the part of London where the sewers were not yet finished.

Two parables: a plausible mechanism, acted on confidently, made the population sicker; the sewers later worked even though the theory behind them was wrong.

TK: scholarly source. Suggested, not yet consulted: **Halliday S.** *The Great Stink of London: Sir Joseph Bazalgette and the Cleansing of the Victorian Metropolis*. 1999.

### Clinical frameworks

**Lee SJ, Leipzig RM, Walter LC.** Incorporating lag time to benefit into prevention decisions for older adults. *JAMA*. 2013;310(24):2609–10. [doi:10.1001/jama.2013.282612](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2013.282612)
Weighing an intervention's time to benefit against the patient's remaining life expectancy.

**Fries JF.** Aging, natural death, and the compression of morbidity. *N Engl J Med*. 1980;303(3):130–5. [doi:10.1056/NEJM198007173030304](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM198007173030304)
Average life span rose from 47 to 73 years while maximum life span did not, so survival curves became more rectangular. Proposes postponing the onset of chronic illness to compress morbidity into a shorter period near the end of life. Closest to what "feeling well for longer" means.
