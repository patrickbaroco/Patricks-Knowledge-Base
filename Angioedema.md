Angioedema by mechanism, trigger, and duration — and the acquired, autoimmune-driven form that can be mistaken for a purely allergic process.

## How it operates

- **Two mechanisms.** Histamine-mediated (mast-cell, typically itchy, may include urticaria, responds to antihistamines/epinephrine/steroids) versus bradykinin-mediated (non-itchy, often no urticaria, does not respond to antihistamines/epinephrine).
- **Triggers extend well beyond ACE inhibitors** — ARBs, opiates, antibiotics, topical anesthetics, and OCPs/progesterone should all be reviewed.
- **Duration is diagnostic.** Ordinary angioedema typically resolves within about three days; swelling persisting well beyond that, especially without lip predominance, should prompt reconsideration of the working diagnosis rather than simply extending the angioedema timeline.
- **Hereditary versus acquired matters for workup.** Hereditary angioedema reflects a genetic C1-esterase inhibitor deficiency; acquired forms can arise from autoimmune disease (notably lupus) and require a normal quantitative C1 inhibitor level to be interpreted cautiously — see [[Complement Levels]].

## Examples from RLR episodes

- **A false angioedema diagnosis that delayed the real one.** Persistent lip and facial swelling following a dental abscess is presumptively treated as angioedema in the ED; the swelling's persistence beyond the typical three-day window, plus a rash, low complements, and leukopenia, ultimately reveal systemic lupus erythematosus with acquired angioedema/serum sickness-type features rather than a primary allergic process.
