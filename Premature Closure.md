Evergreen concept note. Definition plus worked examples, drawn from the RLR / Clinical Problem Solvers series and elsewhere. Source episodes are cited per example.

## Definition

Premature closure is accepting a diagnosis before it has been fully verified — stopping the diagnostic process once a plausible answer is in hand, so that later data are never sought or are quietly folded into the accepted label. It is the "when the diagnosis is made, thinking stops" failure, and it is repeatedly identified in the diagnostic-error literature (Graber, Croskerry) as one of the most common contributors to missed and delayed diagnoses.

The closure can happen at different points: on a *diagnosis* (settling on SIADH), on a *comorbidity* (the documented UTI explains everything), on a *first positive test*, or — more subtly — on a *line of questioning* or a *disposition* that ends inquiry before the real problem surfaces.

## How it operates and its cousins

- **It is the downstream failure that [[Anchoring Bias|anchoring]] produces.** An anchor narrows the search; premature closure is the point at which the narrowed search is declared finished. The two are usually described together.
- **[[Search Satisficing|Search satisficing]] is the near-synonym.** Stopping as soon as one *satisfactory* answer is found — accepting the first sufficient explanation rather than the best or the complete one. Especially dangerous when a problem is multifactorial or when a second, co-occurring diagnosis is present.
- **[[Diagnostic Momentum|Diagnostic momentum]] carries it between clinicians.** Once a label is attached and handed off, each subsequent clinician inherits the closed frame and is less likely to reopen it.
- **A salient, sufficient finding is the classic trap.** A documented UTI, a positive syphilis test, an active outbreak — any finding that plausibly "explains it" invites the search to stop before a better or additional explanation is reached.

## Examples from RLR episodes

- **Closure on a line of questioning, not a diagnosis.** The bedside clinician became fixated on ruling out meningitis and dominated the encounter with that agenda, never leaving room for the patient to volunteer that he had abruptly quit six cups of coffee a day. Closure happened on the *task* of excluding meningitis, which crowded out open-ended history — the real diagnosis was caffeine-withdrawal headache. ([[RLR 001 - New Onset Headache|RLR 001]])
- **Closure on a known prior diagnosis.** A history of chronic pancreatitis, an ERCP stent, and an elevated alkaline phosphatase made a biliary/pancreatic explanation feel sufficient, and the search nearly stopped there despite two normal lipase values pointing away from it toward a primary gastric process. ([[RLR 002 - Nausea, Vomiting, and Hyponatremia|RLR 002]])
- **Closure on a single cause when the problem is multifactorial.** Hyponatremia is usually "and, not or" — Reza's insistence keeps thiazide, hypovolemia, and SIADH-like physiology all in play rather than letting the team settle on SIADH alone. The withheld thiazide made SIADH the tempting place to stop. ([[RLR 003 - Hypertension and Severe Hyponatremia|RLR 003]])
- **Closure on the first positive test / the outbreak.** Primed by an active mpox outbreak, "MSM + rash = mpox" was a tempting stopping point, and a positive syphilis test could easily have ended the search — but the team kept HSV, VZV, and secondary syphilis explicitly in play, which is how the mpox–syphilis co-infection was still caught. ([[RLR 004 - Rectal Pain and Proctitis|RLR 004]])
- **Closure on the obvious comorbidity.** With a documented UTI and stool impaction on the scan, "treat the UTI and disimpact" felt complete; tracing the timeline instead surfaced the iatrogenic trigger the salient findings would otherwise have masked. ([[RLR 005 - Abdominal Distention, Constipation, and Urinary Retention|RLR 005]])
- **Closure on the disposition — and the inverse, comfort with staying open.** The case was pre-labeled a simple G-tube swap, which nearly ended inquiry before the hypercalcemia was examined. The hosts then model the opposite discipline: ending in explicit disagreement about the cause and agreeing the uncertainty does not change management — separating *diagnostic* closure from the decision to act, rather than forcing a premature label. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])

## Examples elsewhere

- **The second fracture on the trauma film.** "Satisfaction of search" in radiology — spotting one abnormality and stopping — is the textbook version: a found lesion reduces detection of a second, co-existing one.
- **The transfer diagnosis.** A patient admitted "for CHF exacerbation" whose real problem is a PE that was never reconsidered — diagnostic momentum closing the case before arrival.
- **The convenient explanation for an abnormal lab.** Attributing a rising creatinine to "dehydration" and moving on, without the follow-up that would reveal obstruction or a nephrotoxin.

## Counter-moves

- **Reason in "and, not or."** Assume a problem may be multifactorial and keep contributors in play until each is actually excluded ([[RLR 003 - Hypertension and Severe Hyponatremia|RLR 003]]).
- **Treat a positive test as compatible-with, not proof.** Let the first positive coexist with a live differential rather than end the search ([[RLR 004 - Rectal Pain and Proctitis|RLR 004]]).
- **Trace the timeline / keep asking why.** When a salient comorbidity "explains it," ask what set that off — root-cause inquiry surfaces triggers the obvious finding masks ([[RLR 005 - Abdominal Distention, Constipation, and Urinary Retention|RLR 005]]).
- **Run a diagnostic time-out: "what else could this be, and what doesn't fit?"** Force one explicit alternative before committing.
- **When reassuring data arrive, reframe rather than keep testing within the closed frame** ([[RLR 001 - New Onset Headache|RLR 001]]).
- **Distinguish closing the diagnosis from deciding to act.** It is legitimate to leave a diagnosis unresolved when the management is set — that is disciplined tolerance of uncertainty, not premature closure ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]]).
