Dystonia is a hyperkinetic movement disorder produced by sustained or intermittent contraction of opposing muscle groups — agonist and antagonist co-contracting rather than reciprocally inhibiting one another — producing abnormal, often repetitive twisting movements or fixed postures. It belongs to the same phenomenology-first taxonomy as [[Tremor]], [[Chorea]], and [[Myoclonus]] within the [[Movement Disorder Approach]]: for hyperkinetic movement disorders, bedside description of the movement itself usually does more diagnostic work than anatomic localization, since nearly all of them trace back to disordered basal ganglia circuitry regardless of the exact structure involved ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]). Two phenomenological signatures separate dystonia from tremor and the other hyperkinetic movements: a posture-dependent **null point**, a position in which the abnormal contraction relents, and **task-specificity**, where the abnormal posturing surfaces only during a particular skilled action.

## Phenomenology: co-contraction versus oscillation

Where tremor is a rhythmic, oscillatory alternation between agonist and antagonist, dystonia is the opposite pattern at the muscle level — sustained, simultaneous **co-contraction** that locks a body part into a twisted posture rather than moving it back and forth. Cervical dystonia (torticollis, a sustained turning or tilting of the head) and writer's cramp are the two exemplars used to teach this contrast: torticollis shows the sustained-posture signature in a body part at rest, while writer's cramp shows its **task-specificity** — the abnormal co-contraction of hand and forearm muscles appears only during the specific skilled act of writing and is absent otherwise ([[CPS 247 - Tremors, Myoclonus, and Steroid-Responsive Autoimmune Encephalopathy|CPS 247]]). The **null point** is the corollary of this position-dependence at the level of the whole movement: because the abnormal contraction is tied to posture rather than being a fixed, unrelenting spasm, there is often a specific position in which it relents, which is why dystonic tremor — the oscillatory component that can ride on top of a dystonic posture — is itself irregular and position-dependent rather than a fixed-frequency oscillation (see [[Tremor]]).

## Classification: distribution and cause

Dystonia is classified anatomically by how much of the body is involved, and etiologically by whether it arises as a primary/idiopathic (often genetic) process or secondary to an identifiable structural, metabolic, or toxic cause.

| Distribution | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Focal | A single body region | Cervical dystonia (torticollis); writer's cramp |
| Segmental | Two or more contiguous regions | Cranial-cervical dystonia (e.g., blepharospasm extending into the neck) |
| Generalized | Trunk plus at least two other regions | Widespread childhood-onset dystonia |

Etiologically, the same three-bucket schema used across the hyperkinetic movement disorders — primary/neurologic, structural, and systemic/drug-toxin — organizes dystonia's causes as well ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]). A genetic, primary overlap syndrome is **myoclonus-dystonia (DYT11)**, in which dystonic posturing and myoclonic jerking coexist as a single inherited disorder rather than two separate movement disorders — an entity named directly in the differential of a young patient's mixed movement disorder ([[RLR 037 - Episodic Lightheadedness and Functional Neurological Disorder|RLR 037]]) and echoed in the "dystonia-myoclonus" overlap syndrome taught alongside dystonic tremor ([[CPS 247 - Tremors, Myoclonus, and Steroid-Responsive Autoimmune Encephalopathy|CPS 247]]). On the structural/systemic side, **[[Wilson Disease|Wilson disease]]** is the paradigm secondary cause in a young patient: its movement disorder "can be almost anything" — the classic wing-beating tremor, but also dystonia or parkinsonism — so a new dystonic posture in a teenager or young adult, especially alongside new liver disease or a psychiatric change, should raise Wilson disease rather than a primary dystonia ([[CPS 447 - Psychiatric Disturbance, Tremor, and Wilson Disease|CPS 447]]). Drug and toxin exposure is the other major secondary bucket: a case report of ertapenem-induced encephalopathy, read aloud as the clinching evidence for a beta-lactam antibiotic neurotoxicity syndrome, described altered mental status, hallucinations, and **dystonic symptoms** that resolved dramatically on discontinuing the drug — an acute, reversible, drug-induced dystonia rather than a primary or structural one ([[RLR 024 - Confusion and Ertapenem-Induced Encephalopathy|RLR 024]]).
