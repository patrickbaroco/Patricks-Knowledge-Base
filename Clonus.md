Clonus is a rhythmic, involuntary series of muscle contractions triggered by a sudden, sustained stretch of a muscle — most often elicited at the ankle by an abrupt, held dorsiflexion, or at the patella by a sharp downward push on the kneecap. It reflects loss of descending inhibition over the spinal stretch-reflex arc from [[Upper Motor Neuron vs Lower Motor Neuron|upper-motor-neuron]]/corticospinal-tract damage — hyperreflexia's rhythmic extreme rather than a separate finding, and it travels with brisk deep tendon reflexes, [[Spasticity|spasticity]], and an extensor plantar (Babinski) response as one package. This is exactly what separates it from a [[Tremor|tremor]]: clonus requires an examiner-applied stretch stimulus, appears only in response to that maneuver, and stops the instant the stretch is released, whereas a tremor is a spontaneous, self-sustaining oscillation present at rest, with posture, or with action — never dependent on someone else eliciting it.

## The bedside maneuver and what the response means

The classic test is sudden, sustained ankle dorsiflexion held against resistance; a positive response is a rhythmic beating of the foot into plantarflexion for as long as the stretch is maintained. A few beats that quickly fatigue out (unsustained clonus) can be a normal variant, particularly in anxious, hyperadrenergic, or simply brisk-reflex patients, and by itself carries little weight. Sustained clonus — conventionally ≥5 beats, or beating that persists as long as the examiner holds the stretch — is the pathological finding: it means the spinal reflex arc is no longer under adequate descending inhibitory control and should be read as part of the same hyperreflexic, upper-motor-neuron picture as brisk deep tendon reflexes and an up-going toe, as in the pure UMN leg syndrome — spasticity, hyperreflexia, clonus, bilateral Babinski, and a [[Hoffman Sign|Hoffman sign]] — that opened the localization in [[CPS 312 - Frequent Stumbling and HTLV-1-Associated Myelopathy|CPS 312]].

## Localizing value — a pyramidal sign like any other

Clonus carries the same localizing weight as the Babinski response, the Hoffman sign, spasticity, and hyperreflexia: it is a corticospinal-tract sign, not a peripheral one, so a positive, sustained clonus places the lesion somewhere in the brain or spinal cord above the level being tested — never in nerve, neuromuscular junction, or muscle. In [[CPS 312 - Frequent Stumbling and HTLV-1-Associated Myelopathy|CPS 312]], a chronic, painless, progressive **spastic paraparesis** with hyperreflexia, sustained ankle clonus, bilateral Babinski signs, and a left Hoffman sign — with normal sensation and cognition — localized cleanly to the spinal cord and drove the chronic-[[Myelopathy|myelopathy]] differential toward [[HTLV-1 Associated Myelopathy|HTLV-1-associated myelopathy]] (tropical spastic paraparesis), ultimately confirmed serologically. The absence of clonus is equally informative: in [[RLR 059 - Weakness and Numbness and Leptomeningeal Carcinomatosis|RLR 059]], a conflicting exam (downgoing toes, no Hoffman, no clonus) left the upper-motor-neuron question genuinely unsettled rather than pointing cleanly to a compressive myelopathy, and in [[RLR 044 - Recurrent Falls and Gluten Ataxia|RLR 044]] a normal reflex exam with no clonus and downgoing plantars helped keep the localization in the cerebellum rather than the corticospinal tract, consistent with gluten ataxia rather than a UMN process.

## A discriminator in hyperthermia-and-rigidity syndromes

Beyond structural localization, clonus is also a bedside discriminator among the toxidromes that cause rigidity and hyperthermia. In [[RLR 056 - One Life, So Many Dreams — Post-Operative Rigidity and Propofol-Induced Neuroexcitatory Syndrome|RLR 056]], reflexes and clonus were treated as the single most useful feature separating [[serotonin syndrome]] (where hyperreflexia and clonus are prominent, especially in the legs) from [[neuroleptic malignant syndrome|NMS]] (where reflexes are typically blunted) — a distinction sharper than tempo or trigger history alone, since onset-and-offset speed and setting can overlap between the two syndromes. The patient in that case had **no hyperreflexia or clonus**, which argued against serotonin syndrome and helped steer the diagnosis toward the rarer propofol-induced neuroexcitatory syndrome once neuroleptic malignant syndrome and malignant hyperthermia were also excluded on other grounds.
