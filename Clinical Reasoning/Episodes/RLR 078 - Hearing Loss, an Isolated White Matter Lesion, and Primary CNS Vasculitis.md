## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 47-year-old woman with progressive, predominantly unilateral sensorineural hearing loss is referred to ENT, then to neurology after an isolated, nonspecific right temporal white matter lesion is found. Guest discussant Aaron Berkowitz builds outward from the [[Anatomical Approach]] fact that hearing is bilaterally represented almost immediately in the brainstem, works through a short list of neurologic causes of hearing loss, and openly names his own anchor on [[Susac Syndrome]] before a new headache and progressive weakness force a brain biopsy that reveals primary CNS vasculitis — a disease he had never personally seen and had spent years teaching residents *not* to over-diagnose.

## Pearls

- Because auditory input becomes bilaterally represented almost immediately after the eighth nerve synapses in the brainstem, true bilateral hearing loss from a single unilateral central lesion is exceedingly rare; most hearing loss is peripheral (ear or eighth nerve), which is why it is a chief concern neurology rarely sees.
- Textbook triads and tetrads (Susac syndrome's hearing loss–retinal artery occlusion–white matter lesions; Wernicke encephalopathy's confusion–ataxia–ophthalmoplegia) are present in only a minority of real cases — absence of the full triad should not be used to exclude the diagnosis.
- An inflammatory but nonspecific CSF pleocytosis (single digits to low teens, elevated protein) provides orientation — "something is happening" — but rarely narrows a differential on its own; treat it as a starting point for further testing, not as a diagnosis.
- When stuck on a rare, hard-to-fit case, either a key piece of knowledge is missing or the wrong element of the presentation is being over-weighted — deliberately setting aside the most salient symptom (here, the hearing loss) and re-examining the remaining findings as their own differential can unstick the reasoning.
- Vascular imaging (CTA/MRA/digital subtraction angiography) is neither sensitive nor specific for primary CNS vasculitis, which affects small vessels below its resolution; a radiologist's "cannot exclude vasculitis" comment on a CTA should not be used to rule the diagnosis in, nor a normal CTA to rule it out.
- Primary CNS vasculitis is rare enough, and mimicked by enough more common diseases, that it should be a diagnosis of near-last resort — confirmed only by brain biopsy — and its diagnostic criteria (headache, inflammatory CSF, an abnormal MRI) are each necessary but individually nonspecific.

## Problem Representation

## Case Narrative

A 47-year-old woman presents to primary care with left-sided hearing loss; bedside testing suggests sensorineural hearing loss, and a subsequent audiogram shows severe left-sided and mild-to-moderate right-sided sensorineural hearing loss. By the time she is seen in ENT clinic, roughly three weeks into the illness, she has no other ear symptoms, a normal exam apart from the hearing abnormalities, and no relevant past medical history.

The guest discussant, a neurologist, works from anatomy: hearing loss is an unusual primary neurologic complaint because, unlike vision, weakness, or sensory pathways, the auditory pathway becomes bilaterally represented almost immediately after the eighth nerve synapses on the cochlear nuclei at the pontomedullary junction — enabling sound localization from the split-millisecond difference in arrival time at the two ears. Practically, this means a unilateral central lesion essentially cannot cause bilateral hearing loss; most hearing loss is peripheral (inner ear or eighth nerve), which is why these patients are usually managed by ENT rather than neurology. Free-associated neurologic causes, offered explicitly as rare "for fun" rather than as a working differential, include sudden sensorineural hearing loss (thought inflammatory, treated with steroids), HINTS-plus (cochlear infarction via the anterior inferior cerebellar artery), Susac syndrome (hearing loss, central retinal artery occlusion, and white matter lesions), vestibular schwannoma (unilateral), neurofibromatosis type 2 (bilateral), superficial siderosis, mitochondrial disease, a paraneoplastic syndrome from anti-Kelch-like protein 11 antibodies (associated with testicular cancer, causing hearing loss and brainstem encephalitis), and processes causing sequential multiple cranial neuropathies (leptomeningeal disease, sarcoidosis, skull base disease). True bilateral central hearing loss from bihemispheric lesions is described as something seen only once in over a decade of practice.

ENT gives bilateral intratympanic steroids without improvement and obtains an MRI protocoled for the internal auditory canals, which shows nothing to explain the hearing loss but incidental patchy T2/FLAIR signal; a dedicated brain MRI shows patchy T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in the subcortical white matter of the right perisylvian region and right anterior temporal lobe, without definite cortical involvement, possible subtle mass effect, and no abnormal parenchymal or leptomeningeal enhancement. The lesion is unilateral (right-sided only) and juxtacortical, without significant mass effect; reviewed "as a radiologist" — appearance alone, without clinical context — it most resembles progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML), prompting a question about the patient's immune status, though the clinical context (hearing loss, a young-ish, previously healthy woman) does not fit that pattern well. Susac syndrome is raised explicitly as a "knee-jerk" association to hearing loss plus a white matter lesion in neurology, alongside an acknowledgment that this lesion's location and character are not classic for it.

Systemic labs, including an immune workup, are unremarkable apart from IgG-positive/IgM-negative evidence of prior West Nile virus exposure; total spine MRI with and without contrast is normal. Lumbar puncture: opening pressure 19 cm H2O, 16 white cells with 96% lymphocytes, glucose 55, protein 47 — a mildly inflammatory but nonspecific profile. The discussant is explicit that CSF pleocytosis of this modest degree provides orientation ("something is happening") rather than a diagnosis, and that lumbar puncture is often obtained when a case is already diagnostically stuck rather than because it is expected to solve it; a systematic review of Susac syndrome is cited showing that only a low percentage of patients present with the full triad, and that headache — not even part of the classic triad — is actually more common at presentation than any single triad element. With hearing loss, a nonspecific white matter lesion, and now mild CSF inflammation, the discussant explicitly names anchoring on Susac syndrome while acknowledging awareness of doing so, and proposes a fluorescein angiogram to look for subclinical retinal vasculopathy, alongside a trial of high-dose steroids as both possibly therapeutic and mildly diagnostic, given the low anticipated harm once infection and lymphoma are not favored.

Weeks later, a "thunderstorm" hits: the patient develops severe headaches and new left-sided pyramidal weakness, prompting hospital admission. A CT venogram is normal; the LP is repeated and remains unrevealing, including negative cytology and negative serum/CSF autoimmune encephalitis antibody panels. Repeat MRI shows the same lesion, now worse, still without enhancement, with the lesion tracing along the white matter without dramatic mass effect. Systemic autoimmune and infectious workups, including RPR/syphilis studies presumed sent, remain unremarkable; JC virus PCR on CSF is specifically negative, arguing against PML despite the imaging resemblance. With the new headache, the discussant's differential shifts decisively: headache plus an inflammatory, evolving, nonspecific white matter lesion — with everything else worked up and negative — makes primary CNS vasculitis "maybe all that's left," despite the discussant's own long-standing teaching that this diagnosis is chronically over-suspected and almost never confirmed. Given the progression despite steroids and the lack of any other explanation, brain biopsy is recommended as the only way to secure a diagnosis; the discussant notes it is safer in skilled hands than its reputation suggests, and that vascular imaging (CTA/MRA/digital subtraction angiography) would not have been diagnostic, since it is neither sensitive nor specific for the small vessels involved in primary CNS vasculitis.

Brain biopsy reveals prominent perivascular and scattered intraparenchymal lymphocytic inflammation extending through vessel walls, without necrosis, granulomas, viral inclusions, or organisms — consistent with a small-vessel vasculitis; with rheumatologic and infectious causes excluded, this is primary CNS vasculitis. She is treated with steroids and cyclophosphamide as induction, then rituximab as maintenance, and is completely back to normal one year later.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The discussant explicitly names his own anchor in real time — "I'm anchored on a zebra, and it's always a wrong endeavor... I would acknowledge that I'm anchoring and be concerned that I'm anchoring, but I would continue to be anchored for lack of a competing hypothesis" — a rare, candid demonstration that awareness of a bias does not automatically dissolve it, and that anchoring can be a reasonable default when nothing better is available, provided it is held loosely and revisited as new data arrive.

The discussion of Susac syndrome's incomplete triads generalizes into an explicit critique of triad-based pattern matching: because the profession trains on prototypical mnemonics, it becomes psychologically difficult to weight an atypical presentation appropriately even while intellectually knowing that most real cases lack the full pattern (Wernicke encephalopathy's triad, cited as present in under 20% of cases, is offered as a parallel example).

A structured de-biasing technique is offered and used: take a multi-element presentation (hearing loss, headache, white matter lesion, inflammatory CSF), deliberately remove one element as a possible "red herring," and see what differential emerges from the remainder — then separately ask whether the differential for each individual element has been fully considered. This is presented as one of few reliable ways to pull out of an anchor once recognized, alongside actively trying to read the imaging "like a radiologist," blinded to the clinical story, to avoid letting the story bias visual interpretation.

The discussant frames rare-disease reasoning along a spectrum — common presenting commonly, common presenting atypically, atypical (rare) presenting typically, and atypical presenting atypically — and states that illness scripts built from personal experience work well for common diseases seen often enough to know their atypical presentations, but are nearly impossible to build reliably for a disease encountered once in a career, since that single case may itself have been atypical.

The presenting discussant separately reflects on his own general strategy for these situations, framing routine diagnosis as "a bow and arrow" (a single confident hypothesis) versus difficult cases as requiring "a fishing net" (broad, parallel testing without commitment to one diagnosis) — arguing that the deepest learning comes from cases where even the fishing net fails to catch the diagnosis, since those moments force a recalibration of approach rather than of a single fact.

## Teaching Discussion

Because the auditory pathway becomes bilaterally represented almost immediately in the brainstem (enabling interaural time-difference sound localization), a unilateral central lesion essentially cannot produce bilateral hearing loss; bilateral hearing loss from a neurologic cause requires either bilateral peripheral disease or a rare, large bihemispheric central lesion.

On MRI sequences, T1 shows anatomy (gray matter darker than white matter), T2 is the visual "negative" of T1 (CSF bright, white matter darker than gray matter), and FLAIR is a T2 sequence with the CSF signal subtracted out, making it easier to see periventricular or juxtacortical lesions that might otherwise be obscured by adjacent bright CSF; FLAIR is generally preferred supratentorially, while T2 can be more useful in the brainstem and posterior fossa.

CSF pleocytosis in the range of roughly 5–20 white cells with a modestly elevated protein is a nonspecific but real signal of inflammation (infectious, autoimmune, or neoplastic); it orients the workup without narrowing it, and lumbar puncture in a diagnostically stuck case often serves this orienting function rather than delivering a definitive answer.

Vascular imaging (CTA, MRA, or even digital subtraction angiography) is neither sensitive nor specific for primary CNS vasculitis, because the disease affects small vessels below the resolution of these studies; irregularities seen on CTA are far more often explained by atherosclerosis or other common causes than by vasculitis, so a radiologist's comment that findings are "concerning for vasculitis" should not be treated as diagnostic, nor should a normal study be used to exclude the disease.

Primary CNS vasculitis is diagnosed only by brain biopsy, and even biopsy is imperfect given the small tissue sample obtained; published case series and pathology-outcome studies ("Pathologist's Revenge") show that most patients worked up for suspected primary CNS vasculitis do not have it on biopsy, and that even patients treated empirically as "biopsy-negative vasculitis" often turn out, at autopsy, still not to have had it — reinforcing that the diagnosis should be reserved for cases where headache, inflammatory CSF, and an abnormal, evolving MRI are all present and every more common alternative has been excluded.
