## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A Neurology VMR built on a single word — **"[[Clumsiness|clumsiness]]"** — which the group first *decodes*: a patient may mean **[[Weakness|weakness]]** (motor/corticospinal), **[[Sensory Ataxia|numbness/impaired proprioception]]** (sensory), **[[Cerebellar Ataxia|incoordination]]** (cerebellar), or even **[[Apraxia|apraxia]]** — clumsiness being the *disconnection between the intended movement and the final product*. The patient is a **72-year-old woman with a 20-year, slowly progressive gait disorder** (falls → cane → wheelchair), now with clumsy hands and, over five years, slurred speech, without reported sensory loss or dysphagia. The **decades-long, indolent, symmetric** course points to a **degenerative or genetic** process, and the trio is drawn to the **cerebellum** (dexterity, gait, and a **[[Cerebellar Dysarthria|cerebellar/scanning dysarthria]]**) — while Berkowitz insists that **gait is a whole-nervous-system function** and most elderly gait disorders are **[[Multifactorial Gait Disorder|multifactorial]]** (a "pie chart" in which the task is finding a *reversible* big slice — [[Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy|cervical myelopathy]], [[Peripheral Neuropathy|neuropathy]], [[Parkinsonism|parkinsonism]]). What pulls *away* from those common causes is the 20-year tempo, the dysarthria, and the (near-)absence of sensory symptoms; the **"[[Shower Sign|shower sign]]"** (worse with eyes closed — Marty Samuels's "Romberg machine") is flagged as a possible **[[Romberg Sign|proprioceptive]]** clue. A **pedigree** clinches the frame: many wheelchair-bound relatives, an affected grandfather, and a **five-year-old grand-nephew** already in a wheelchair — an **[[Anticipation (Genetics)|anticipation]]** signature — with a non-skipping, **autosomal-dominant** pattern. The exam is floridly cerebellar (**scanning dysarthria, [[Dysmetria|dysmetria]], [[Dysdiadochokinesia|dysdiadochokinesia]], [[Intention Tremor|intention tremor]]**) but with **no [[Nystagmus|nystagmus]]**, mild spasticity/weakness (attributed to disuse from years in a wheelchair), and a **length-dependent sensory loss** — findings the group honestly notes they are "**[[Anchoring Bias|explaining away]]**." Had the course been *months*, the tempo would have opened **[[Paraneoplastic Cerebellar Degeneration|paraneoplastic]]/autoimmune, [[Gluten Ataxia|gluten]], [[Anti-GAD Ataxia|anti-GAD]], [[B12 Deficiency|B12]]/[[Vitamin E Deficiency|E]] and [[Nitrous Oxide|nitrous-oxide]]** causes. The closing humility: presented five years in, "everyone would have missed it."

## Pearls

- **"[[Clumsiness|Clumsiness]]" is a chief complaint to *decode*, not a diagnosis.** It can mean **[[Weakness|weakness]]** (motor, brain-to-muscle), **[[Sensory Ataxia|sensory loss / impaired proprioception]]** (why carpal tunnel patients "drop things" without weakness), **[[Cerebellar Ataxia|incoordination]]** (cerebellar or sensory ataxia), or **[[Apraxia|apraxia]]** — the disconnect between the intended act and the product. History is aimed at sorting *weakness vs numbness vs incoordination*, then adding **time course** and distribution.
- **[[Multifactorial Gait Disorder|Gait is a whole-nervous-system function]] — treat the elderly gait disorder as a pie chart.** Walking needs strength, sensation, proprioception, coordination, vestibular and visual input, and higher-order ([[Basal Ganglia|basal-ganglia]]/frontal) gait programming; most older patients have a *little* of everything ([[Peripheral Neuropathy|neuropathy]], [[Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy|spine disease]], [[Parkinsonism|parkinsonism]], visual loss, orthopedic issues). The reasoning task is finding the one **reversible** slice worth intervening on versus many small irreversible ones — the [[Center of Gravity|center-of-gravity]] judgment that guides (or restrains) spine surgery.
- **[[Illness Tempo|Time course]] reroutes a symmetric cerebellar syndrome.** A **20-year** indolent course says **degenerative or genetic**; the *same* symmetric pan-cerebellar picture over **months** would instead demand **[[Paraneoplastic Cerebellar Degeneration|paraneoplastic]]** (anti-Yo/Ri/Ma — ovarian/breast in women, testicular in young men), **[[Gluten Ataxia|gluten ataxia]]**, **[[Anti-GAD Ataxia|anti-GAD]]**, and nutritional/toxic causes (**[[B12 Deficiency|B12]]**, **[[Vitamin E Deficiency|vitamin E]]**, **[[Nitrous Oxide|nitrous oxide/whippets]]**).
- **The "[[Shower Sign|shower sign]]" flags sensory (proprioceptive) ataxia.** Being markedly worse with eyes closed — the shower as Marty Samuels's "**[[Romberg Sign|Romberg]] machine**" (slippery, dim, eyes shut) — points to large-fiber/[[Dorsal Column|dorsal-column]]/[[Dorsal Root Ganglion|dorsal-root-ganglion]] proprioceptive loss rather than pure cerebellar ataxia, which worsens far less with eye closure.
- **Read the exam that *doesn't* fit — and name the anchoring.** A florid cerebellar exam ([[Dysmetria|dysmetria]], [[Dysdiadochokinesia|dysdiadochokinesia]], [[Intention Tremor|intention tremor]]) with **no [[Nystagmus|nystagmus]]**, mild weakness/spasticity, and a length-dependent sensory loss should be held honestly: weakness is attributed to **disuse/deconditioning** (years wheelchair-bound), absent nystagmus to sparing of the flocculonodular lobe or the specific genetic form — but "explaining away" the misfits is a *dangerous exercise*, and a superimposed treatable disease (cervical stenosis, neuropathy) must stay on the table.
- **Genetics: [[Whole Exome Sequencing|whole-exome sequencing MISSES trinucleotide repeats]] — and [[Anticipation (Genetics)|anticipation]] is a clue.** For a repeat-expansion **[[Spinocerebellar Ataxia|spinocerebellar ataxia]]**, send a **targeted repeat/ataxia panel**, not just exome. An **autosomal-dominant** pedigree with a *five-year-old* already affected screams anticipation; here **ATXN7 (39 CAG) = [[Spinocerebellar Ataxia Type 7|SCA7]]**, a rare SCA whose hallmark **[[Retinitis Pigmentosa|retinal degeneration]]** and nystagmus are repeat-length-dependent (spared early in low-repeat, slowly-progressive disease).

## Problem Representation

A 72-year-old woman with a 20-year, slowly progressive gait disorder — beginning as clumsiness and falls, worsening from cane to wheelchair over the last decade — now with clumsy hands and five years of slurred speech, and dependence for most activities of daily living, without reported sensory symptoms or dysphagia. The decades-long, indolent, symmetric course and a striking family history (multiple wheelchair-bound relatives, an affected grandfather, and a five-year-old grand-nephew already affected — an autosomal-dominant, anticipation pattern) frame a hereditary cerebellar disorder. Examination shows a florid cerebellar syndrome (scanning dysarthria, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, intention tremor) without nystagmus, with mild spasticity and 3/5 weakness with reduced bulk (attributed to disuse from prolonged wheelchair use), normal reflexes, and a length-dependent small-fiber sensory loss. A remote MRI showed mild cerebellar atrophy. Because whole-exome sequencing misses trinucleotide repeats, a targeted ataxia panel was sent, revealing **ATXN7 with 39 CAG repeats — spinocerebellar ataxia type 7** — a rare SCA associated with retinal degeneration whose ocular findings are repeat-length-dependent and spared early in this slowly progressive, lower-repeat phenotype.

## Case Narrative

Maria presents a Neurology VMR whose entire opening move is decoding one word: **"[[Clumsiness|clumsiness]]."** Berkowitz has Andrea and Sridhara unpack what a patient might mean. Andrea starts with *what they can't do* — losing dexterity, dropping things, poor handwriting — which Berkowitz reframes as possibly **[[Weakness|weakness]]** (loss of motor function). Sridhara offers the unifying definition: clumsiness is **the disconnection between the intended motor act and the final product**, which can be **weakness** (corticospinal) or **[[Cerebellar Ataxia|ataxia]]** (spinocerebellar). Berkowitz adds a third and fourth reading: **[[Sensory Ataxia|sensory loss]]** (a numb hand loses the feedback the cerebellum needs — carpal tunnel patients "drop things" without weakness) and **[[Apraxia|apraxia]]** (usually noticed by a spouse or found on exam — intact strength, sensation, and coordination but absent skilled, learned actions). The initial branch point: **motor vs sensory vs cerebellar** (± apraxia), refined by asking about weakness, numbness/tingling, temperature sensation, and balance — plus **time course** and distribution, since a sudden, focal deficit summons the **[[Stroke|stroke]]** neurologist.

The **HPI**: a **72-year-old woman** with a **20-year** history of difficulty walking — first clumsy with frequent falls while "walking normally" ("why are you tripping?"), worsening over the last decade from **cane to wheelchair** — now with **clumsy hands** (dishes, dressing) and, in the last five years, **difficulty speaking**, dependent for most ADLs, with **no reported sensory abnormalities** and no dysphagia. Andrea reads a **degenerative cerebellar** disorder from the chronic, worsening course. Berkowitz agrees the **20-year tempo** signals a degenerative process with a **cerebellar feel** (dexterity, gait, and a probable **[[Cerebellar Dysarthria|cerebellar dysarthria]]**) — but pauses on a favorite teaching point: **gait is one of the hardest things to analyze because it uses the *entire* nervous system** (strength, sensation, proprioception, coordination, vestibular and visual input, and higher-order [[Basal Ganglia|basal-ganglia]]/frontal gait programming). Most older patients have a **[[Multifactorial Gait Disorder|multifactorial gait disorder]]** — a "pie chart" of a little neuropathy, a little spine disease, a little parkinsonism, some visual loss — and the job is to find the one slice big and **reversible** enough to be worth intervening on ([[Center of Gravity|center of gravity]]), versus a collection of small irreversible ones best managed with therapy and gait aids. He asks for the *common* cause of slowly progressive gait-plus-hand dysfunction at this age: Sridhara names **[[Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy|cervical spondylotic myelopathy]]** (with [[Peripheral Neuropathy|neuropathy]] and [[Parkinsonism|parkinsonism]] often coexisting). What pulls *away* from those: the **20-year** course, the **dysarthria**, and the **absence of sensory symptoms** (cervical myelopathy usually hits the dorsal columns → numbness).

Sridhara adds two clinical anchors: **genetic conditions can present in the 50s+** (variable penetrance, even without a family history), and the **"[[Shower Sign|shower sign]]"** — markedly worse with eyes closed (splashing water on the face, standing in the shower) — suggests a **[[Sensory Ataxia|sensory ataxia]]** component, since cerebellar ataxia worsens *less* with eye closure. Berkowitz elaborates with a tribute to his late mentor **Marty Samuels**, who called the shower the "**[[Romberg Sign|Romberg]] machine**" (slippery, dim, eyes shut) and taught "bathroom neurology" (Valsalva on the toilet opening a PFO to embolize; the shower unmasking [[Romberg Sign|Rombergism]] from large-fiber/[[Dorsal Column|dorsal-column]]/[[Dorsal Root Ganglion|DRG]] proprioceptive loss). The long, indolent, symmetric, system-specific course keeps him on **degenerative or genetic** disease — and he notes genetic diseases can present late, sometimes **unmasked by a medication** (e.g., a genetic myopathy revealed by a statin).

Asked for specific degenerative/genetic cerebellar causes, Sridhara lists **[[Fragile X-Associated Tremor Ataxia Syndrome|FXTAS]]** (a premutation carrier grandparent; tremor-ataxia-parkinsonism; family history of intellectual disability/cryptorchidism), the **[[Spinocerebellar Ataxia|spinocerebellar ataxias]]**, and **[[Friedreich Ataxia|Friedreich ataxia]]**. Andrea adds SCAs and a vascular cause. Berkowitz rounds it out — the SCAs (a long list; **[[SCA3|SCA3]]** enriched in the Azorean population he saw in Boston), FXTAS (rare; a middle-cerebellar-peduncle white-matter MRI signature), and **[[Multiple System Atrophy|MSA]]** (a Parkinson-plus syndrome; MSA-P parkinsonian, MSA-C cerebellar, both with autonomic dysfunction).

The **history** then all but seals it: past history only **hypertension** (labetalol, atorvastatin), and a **family history** of multiple wheelchair-bound relatives — an affected **grandfather** (clumsy, walker) and, strikingly, a **five-year-old grand-nephew already in a wheelchair**. Everyone smiles: this is a **hereditary neurologic disorder**, and the very young affected member hints at **[[Anticipation (Genetics)|anticipation]]**. Berkowitz cautions against **[[Anchoring Bias|anchoring]]** even here — the patient could carry the family disease *and* a superimposed treatable process (cervical stenosis, neuropathy) — and adds **[[Hereditary Spastic Paraplegia|hereditary spastic paraplegia]]** (simple vs complex) to the familial-gait differential, with a digression on cerebellar tone (Gordon Holmes's "The Cerebellum of Man"; **[[Pendular Reflexes|pendular reflexes]]** and the decreased-tone arm-swing sign). On the **pedigree**, Sridhara leans recessive (few affected), but Andrea reads **autosomal dominant** because it **doesn't skip generations** — the correct call for an SCA.

The **exam** is floridly cerebellar. Mental status and language are intact (naming >30 fruits/vegetables in a minute); the **speech is slow with an unusual separation of syllables and variable intonation** — **[[Scanning Speech|scanning dysarthria]]** ("sounds drunk," since acute alcohol hits the cerebellum). Cranial nerves show **no [[Nystagmus|nystagmus]]** and slightly slow saccades; motor exam shows **reduced bulk and slight spasticity in all four limbs with 3/5 strength** and no pronator drift; reflexes are **normal** (Achilles 1+, Babinski downgoing, Hoffman negative); sensory testing shows **length-dependent decreased light touch and pinprick to the mid-shin** with **preserved vibration, proprioception, and temperature**; and the cerebellar exam is unmistakable — **[[Dysmetria|dysmetria]]** (overshoot on finger-nose, unchanged by slowing or eye closure), **[[Dysdiadochokinesia|dysdiadochokinesia]]**, and heel-shin fragmentation. Gait and Romberg were not assessed.

Berkowitz teaches the exam's fine points — the **[[Intention Tremor|intention tremor]]** (a subset of *action* tremor unique to cerebellar disease, where the tremor worsens and overshoots as the target nears, unlike a precise essential/physiologic action tremor), and bedside tests (finger-chase; "draw a sharp square in the air" to expose corner overshoot). Then he **reads the misfits honestly**: the **absent nystagmus** (unexpected in prominent cerebellar disease — either too subtle to see or the flocculonodular/vestibular cerebellum spared by this condition); the **weakness/atrophy** (not cerebellar — attributed to **disuse and deconditioning** after years in a wheelchair, supported by the *normal reflexes* arguing against a primary motor/muscle disease); and the **length-dependent sensory loss** (small-fiber/anterolateral — part of the *spino*cerebellar disease, or an age-related/undiagnosed-diabetic neuropathy). He names the risk out loud: he too is **anchored on the cerebellum and "tossing away" what doesn't fit — a dangerous exercise** — and flags that cerebellum-plus-muscle would be a hard Venn diagram, while a **[[POLG|POLG]] mitochondrial** ataxia (a resident's recent case) can produce multi-system findings.

For teaching's sake, Berkowitz runs the **tempo counterfactual**: strip out the 20-year course and the family history, and a **symmetric pan-cerebellar syndrome over *months*** with normal imaging would put **[[Paraneoplastic Cerebellar Degeneration|paraneoplastic]]** disease "high on the differential" (anti-Yo/Ri/Ma; ovarian/breast in women, testicular in young men), alongside **[[Gluten Ataxia|gluten ataxia]]**, **[[Anti-GAD Ataxia|anti-GAD]]** (with the caveat of common low-titer false positives), **[[B12 Deficiency|B12]]** and **[[Vitamin E Deficiency|vitamin E]]** deficiency, **[[Nitrous Oxide|nitrous oxide/whippets]]** (functional B12 deficiency → subacute combined degeneration), and even **[[JC Virus Granule Cell Neuronopathy|JC-virus granule cell neuronopathy]]**. Sridhara offers a memorable heuristic: **ataxia + neuropathy → recessive ataxias; ataxia + spasticity/hyperreflexia → dominant ataxias.**

On workup, imaging would be expected to show **cerebellar (midline/vermian) atrophy** — best seen on the **sagittal midline** (prominent superior folia) — with incidental age/hypertension-related white-matter changes not to be blamed for the syndrome. Diagnosis needs **genetic testing**, and Berkowitz shares the practical pearl (and Sridhara the hard-won one): **[[Whole Exome Sequencing|whole-exome sequencing]]** is often the efficient choice — his own "adrenomyeloneuropathy" surprise, where a presumed HSP turned out to be **[[Adrenomyeloneuropathy|X-linked ALD/AMN]]** on exome — *but exome **misses [[Trinucleotide Repeat Disorder|trinucleotide-repeat]] expansions***, so a **targeted repeat/ataxia panel** is required for the SCAs.

Maria delivers the resolution in one aliquot. The only prior imaging, **15 years earlier**, had shown **mild cerebellar atrophy**; frustrated by an undiagnosed workup, the patient had declined further testing and returned now *only* for genetics after so many relatives were affected. A **targeted ataxia panel** returned **ATXN7 with 39 CAG repeats — [[Spinocerebellar Ataxia Type 7|spinocerebellar ataxia type 7]]**. She teaches the group about SCA7: one of the **rarest** SCAs, distinguished among them by **[[Retinitis Pigmentosa|retinal degeneration]]** (macular/RP — sometimes caught first by an *ophthalmologist*); with **repeat-length-dependent phenotypes** — lower repeats (as here, near the 36-repeat threshold) give a **slow, elderly-onset** course with **ocular findings and nystagmus spared until later** (explaining the missing nystagmus Berkowitz flagged), while larger repeats give **pediatric, fulminant** disease with prominent retinal involvement — and **anticipation** explaining the five-year-old relative. The reduced bulk/weakness was judged **disuse** (five years wheelchair-bound, no PT/OT), though rare case reports describe early muscle involvement.

The closing reflection is pure humility: retrospectively it was "easy" to call this cerebellar and hereditary, but the SCAs are **phenotypically heterogeneous** (neuropathy, hyperreflexia, spasticity, atrophy, fasciculations, autonomic features) and, in a slowly progressive low-repeat form, **"if she'd presented five years in, everyone would have missed it."**

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|Decoding the chief complaint]].** "[[Clumsiness|Clumsiness]]" is translated into **motor / sensory / cerebellar / apraxia** — with the insight that it names a *disconnection between intent and product* — so history is aimed at weakness vs numbness vs incoordination before any differential.
- **[[Localization × Time Course|Localization × time course]] (the VMR engine).** Cerebellar localization × a **20-year** tempo yields degenerative/genetic disease, and the explicit **counterfactual** (months instead of decades → paraneoplastic/autoimmune/nutritional) shows the same localization rerouting entirely on tempo.
- **[[Multifactorial Gait Disorder|Gait as a whole-nervous-system function]] + the [[Center of Gravity|pie chart]].** Because gait integrates every system, the elderly gait disorder is usually multifactorial; the reasoning goal is the *reversible* dominant slice worth intervening on — the discipline that restrains reflexive spine surgery.
- **[[Base Rate|Base rate]] set against the atypical.** Common causes (cervical myelopathy, neuropathy, parkinsonism) are named first and then displaced by the 20-year course, the dysarthria, and the sensory pattern — "common things common," but not here.
- **[[Shower Sign|Shower sign]] / [[Romberg Sign|Romberg]] as a sensory-ataxia probe.** Worse-with-eyes-closed localizes to proprioceptive (large-fiber/[[Dorsal Column|dorsal-column]]) pathways — a bedside discriminator between sensory and cerebellar ataxia.
- **Cerebellar exam schema and the *missing* sign.** [[Nystagmus|Nystagmus]] (central/direction-changing), [[Scanning Speech|scanning dysarthria]], [[Dysmetria|dysmetria]], [[Dysdiadochokinesia|dysdiadochokinesia]], and **[[Intention Tremor|intention tremor]]** (vs a precise action tremor) define the exam; the **absent nystagmus** is treated as a clue (form-specific sparing), not a refutation.
- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring]] and "explaining away."** All three admit being drawn to the cerebellum and *tossing away* the weakness, sensory loss, and absent nystagmus — Berkowitz names this a **dangerous exercise** and keeps a superimposed treatable disease on the table, the metacognitive counterweight.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Test interpretation]] and genetic reasoning.** **[[Whole Exome Sequencing|Whole-exome misses trinucleotide repeats]]** (send a targeted panel); low-titer **[[Anti-GAD Ataxia|anti-GAD]]** false positives; imaging expectations (sagittal-midline vermian atrophy; incidental white matter); and **[[Anticipation (Genetics)|anticipation]]** (a five-year-old affected) plus a non-skipping AD pedigree pointing to a repeat-expansion **[[Spinocerebellar Ataxia|SCA]]** — resolved as **[[Spinocerebellar Ataxia Type 7|SCA7]]** with its repeat-length-dependent retinal phenotype.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Humility]].** Retrospective clarity ("easy to see it was cerebellar and hereditary") against prospective difficulty ("presented five years in, everyone would have missed it") — the honest coda on SCA heterogeneity.

## Teaching Discussion

- Decode "clumsiness" before generating a differential: it may mean weakness, sensory loss/impaired proprioception, incoordination (cerebellar or sensory ataxia), or apraxia; targeted history (weak vs numb vs uncoordinated), distribution, and time course sort these.
- Treat the elderly gait disorder as multifactorial and look for the reversible dominant slice: gait needs strength, sensation, proprioception, coordination, vestibular and visual input, and higher-order programming, so weigh whether cervical/lumbar intervention addresses enough of the pie to justify its risk.
- Let time course reroute a symmetric cerebellar syndrome: decades favor degenerative/genetic disease, whereas a months-long course elevates paraneoplastic and autoimmune (anti-Yo/Ri/Ma, gluten, anti-GAD) and nutritional/toxic (B12, vitamin E, nitrous oxide) causes.
- Use the shower sign and Romberg to separate sensory from cerebellar ataxia: markedly worse with eyes closed indicates proprioceptive (dorsal-column/large-fiber) loss, since cerebellar ataxia worsens far less with eye closure.
- Anchor the cerebellar exam on nystagmus (central/direction-changing), scanning dysarthria, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, and intention tremor — and interpret missing signs (e.g., absent nystagmus) as possible form-specific sparing rather than exclusion, while explicitly resisting the urge to explain away every misfit.
- Recognize that genetic ataxias can present late (variable penetrance; sometimes unmasked by a medication), read the pedigree for inheritance and anticipation, and remember the phenotypic overlap heuristic: ataxia + neuropathy suggests recessive ataxias, ataxia + spasticity suggests dominant ataxias.
- Choose genetic tests deliberately: whole-exome sequencing is efficient for many hereditary neurologic diseases but misses trinucleotide-repeat expansions, so send a targeted repeat/ataxia panel for suspected SCAs — and recall SCA7's signature retinal degeneration, whose severity (and the ocular findings/nystagmus) track with CAG repeat length.
