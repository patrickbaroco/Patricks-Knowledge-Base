## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 74-year-old man with vascular risk factors and compensated cirrhosis presents with three days of sudden-onset, focal left groin pain reproducible on palpation over the femoral triangle, with robust distal pulses. CT shows fat stranding around the common femoral artery and vein, prompting concern for vasculitis versus a perivascular infectious/inflammatory process; markedly elevated inflammatory markers and a PET-avid mass encasing the femoral artery push the workup toward presumed infection. Blood cultures remain negative for four days despite empiric antibiotics, the leukocytosis normalizes quickly, and — most strikingly — the mass is no longer visible on repeat imaging days later. Random biopsies eventually show fat necrosis with reactive inflammation, confirming the process but leaving its underlying trigger explicitly, deliberately unresolved.

## Pearls

- Groin pain workup begins by localizing: is this local pathology within the palpated area, or pain referred from elsewhere? The groin uniquely receives referred pain from three separate compartments — the retroperitoneum (classically kidney stones), the leg/thigh, and the GU system (the testes) — so referred sources should be considered even when a patient points confidently to a specific spot.
- Reproducible focal tenderness on palpation meaningfully raises the probability of local (versus referred) pathology, and in the groin should raise concern for an incarcerated hernia as a no-miss diagnosis; critically, the absence of a palpable bulge does not exclude this — a less prominent bulge is, if anything, associated with higher incarceration risk.
- Watching a patient walk is a fast, low-cost way to interrogate hip or pelvic bony pathology (e.g., avascular necrosis of the femoral head) as a cause of inguinal pain: marked pain with ambulation raises concern for a hip-joint process, though pelvic pathology can sometimes be present without a dramatic pain-with-walking correlation.
- IV contrast increases diagnostic yield for abdominal/pelvic pathology by roughly 30-40% over non-contrast imaging; given the now well-established low absolute risk of contrast-induced nephropathy, the more useful clinical question is "do I actually need the answer this scan would give me," not "is the creatinine too high to give contrast."
- Fat stranding on CT surrounding a blood vessel, without contralateral symmetry to suggest an incidental finding, should prompt a structured question: is this a problem of the vessel itself (vasculitis), or a problem of the tissue adjacent to the vessel that is secondarily irritating it (infection, malignancy, or another inflammatory process)? Reframing "perivascular" as distinct from "vascular" changes the initial differential.
- Vasculitis is classically a systemic, often symmetric process; a strikingly unilateral or anatomically isolated vascular-appearing inflammatory process should lower the probability of primary vasculitis and raise the probability of an adjacent, localized process — one host explicitly described defaulting to "vasculitis, infection, then autoimmune vasculitis" as a hardwired heuristic earned from prior humbling misses on similar-looking cases.
- Spontaneous endovascular infection (a mycotic/infected arterial focus) without a preceding trauma or injection history should prompt consideration of Staph aureus, the Streptococcus milleri group, and — particularly in diabetic patients — invasive Klebsiella, favoring broad empiric coverage (vancomycin plus a gram-negative agent) pending cultures and source control.
- A rapidly enlarging, intensely PET-avid mass that essentially disappears within days is faster than blood, infection, or most malignant processes would plausibly resolve, and should raise suspicion for a self-limited, autoinfarcting process — fat necrosis is a classic example, as are entities like keratoacanthoma (a skin tumor known to necrose and regress its own blood supply).
- Culture-negative "infection" with rapid clinical and leukocytosis improvement despite an initially convincing radiographic and inflammatory-marker picture should prompt reconsideration of whether the process is infectious at all; alternatives include transient/cleared bacteremia, a culture-negative organism (e.g., Bartonella), or a non-infectious vascular or inflammatory process such as an intramural hematoma or focal fat necrosis.
- Re-imaging a mass that has clinically "gone quiet" is an underused but highly informative diagnostic move: the finding that a process has disappeared can itself be the most discriminating piece of data in the case, narrowing the differential to entities capable of that specific tempo of resolution.
- Even biopsy-proven pathology can leave the underlying trigger unresolved — mature adipose tissue with fat necrosis, reactive fibrovascular proliferation, and mixed inflammation confirms fat necrosis as the process but does not, by itself, explain why it occurred (severe local atherosclerosis, an unrecognized vascular event, or another cause) — and it can be appropriate to leave a case explicitly open rather than force a premature unifying explanation.

## Problem Representation

A man with vascular risk factors and compensated cirrhosis presenting with acute-onset, focal, reproducible left groin pain and mild tachycardia, found on imaging to have fat stranding and later a PET-avid mass encasing the femoral artery — initially concerning for vasculitis, then presumed infection — that resolved spontaneously and was ultimately characterized on biopsy as fat necrosis of unresolved underlying etiology.

## Case Narrative

Reza presented a case of his own involving a 74-year-old man with hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and well-compensated cirrhosis (attributed to alcohol and hepatitis B), who presented with sudden-onset, severe left groin pain three days prior, unresponsive to Tylenol and NSAIDs. On exam there was no palpable mass, but the patient reliably localized and reproduced his pain to a spot corresponding to the inguinal region overlying the femoral artery. His heart rate was 102, temperature 97.1°F, and he was normotensive.

Rabih organized his initial approach around localization: groin pain should first be sorted into local pathology (something within the palpated region) versus pain referred from one of three compartments — the retroperitoneum (classically kidney stones), the leg or thigh, or the GU system (the testes). The patient's ability to reliably reproduce his pain on palpation shifted the probability strongly toward a local process. Within local causes, Rabih flagged an incarcerated hernia as the key no-miss diagnosis, explicitly noting that the absence of a palpable bulge does not exclude this — a less prominent bulge, if anything, carries a higher risk of incarceration. He suggested a simple next step (can the patient walk without marked pain, to help exonerate hip or pelvic bony pathology like avascular necrosis) before proceeding toward imaging, anticipating the need to scan for a possible inguinal hernia.

Reza reported robust, easily palpable dorsalis pedis pulses, then shared initial labs — white count 14,000, hemoglobin 13.5, platelets 134,000, creatinine 1.8 (near his baseline of 1.6-1.7), normal sodium — before turning to a contrast-enhanced CT abdomen and pelvis. Before revealing the findings, he asked Rabih to address the common reluctance to use IV contrast in patients with borderline renal function. Rabih responded that contrast increases diagnostic yield for abdominal/pelvic pathology by roughly 30-40% over non-contrast imaging, and that this benefit substantially outweighs the now well-recognized low absolute risk of contrast-induced nephropathy; his practical framework is to first clarify precisely what clinical question needs answering, and only pursue contrast if the answer genuinely requires it (for example, a patient with hip pain and an inability to bear weight might only need a non-contrast CT or plain film of the bony pelvis).

The CT showed moderate fat stranding surrounding the left common femoral artery and vein, with an indistinct venous margin. Rabih reasoned through this novel (to him) finding using analogical reasoning: fat stranding around a vessel, without contralateral symmetry to suggest an incidental process, likely reflects either a disease process located between the artery and vein, or true vessel-wall inflammation. Inflammation of perivascular fat raises concern for vasculitis (typically framed as autoimmune) but should also prompt consideration of infectious or malignant vascular inflammation. He prioritized asking about a foreign body, injection, or external irritation at that site, wondered about the route of the patient's hepatitis B acquisition as a possible clue toward injection drug use, and recommended blood cultures, a careful cardiac exam and history to exclude an endovascular source like endocarditis, assessment for trauma, and early vascular surgery involvement given the limits of managing this picture without subspecialty input.

Reza confirmed no trauma or injection drug use history. He then described extensive arterial plaque burden seen on his own review of the CT, prompting a vascular surgery consult; a DVT ultrasound was negative, and ESR (>100) and CRP were markedly elevated. Vascular surgery recommended a PET-CT for better characterization, which showed an intensely hypermetabolic, roughly 3.8cm mass encasing the left femoral artery, radiographically consistent with an abscess.

Rabih connected the case to a similar prior presentation from his own training — a patient with a femoral-popliteal bypass graft infected with Klebsiella — and reasoned that the momentum of the case (a mass encasing, rather than diffusely inflaming, the vessel; a distribution inconsistent with a systemic vasculitic pattern) favored infection over vasculitis. He anticipated the patient would likely need surgical source control for both treatment and diagnostic clarity, and in the meantime recommended a practical checklist: confirm blood cultures are pending, exclude endocarditis and other potential sources of a spontaneous endovascular infection (he noted the rest of the abdomen had already been imaged without evidence of a liver, kidney, or skin abscess, and the patient had no pulmonary symptoms to suggest a lung source), and cover empirically for Staph aureus, Streptococcus milleri group, and — given the patient's diabetes — invasive Klebsiella, favoring vancomycin plus broad gram-negative coverage.

Reza then asked Rabih directly why he had not seriously entertained autoimmune vasculitis, despite Reza himself having initially written an extensive differential centered on medium-to-large-vessel vasculitis. Rabih attributed this to the acute (rather than subacute) tempo of onset, and to the fact that most vasculitides are systemic diseases, whereas this process appeared anatomically isolated to the femoral artery; he also candidly attributed part of his reasoning to a hardwired heuristic earned from having been "humbled" on prior cases — defaulting to "vasculitis, infection, then autoimmune vasculitis" rather than leading with autoimmune disease. Reza added a detail from a separate conversation with a rheumatologist, who had made the same point independently: vasculitis is usually a symmetric process, so an asymmetric, unilateral presentation should prioritize an adjacent (perivascular) pathology over a primary vessel-wall problem.

The patient was started on vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam, with ID consulted and a plan to obtain an image-guided biopsy via interventional radiology. Before the biopsy results were available, Reza reported an unexpected course: blood cultures remained negative through four days, the white count normalized by day two, and the patient's pain improved with pain management alone. Rabih reasoned through the implications: if this had truly been an endovascular process that eroded through the femoral artery to seed the surrounding tissue, sustained bacteremia would be expected, and negative cultures raised the possibility of transient/cleared bacteremia or a culture-negative organism such as Bartonella — though he found neither hypothesis fully satisfying. He introduced an alternative frame, drawing on a prior case (recalled from either the Patreon feed or RLR) involving an aortic intramural hematoma or penetrating aortic ulcer: given the patient's vascular risk factors, could this instead represent blood, or an atypical inflammatory process around the vessel, rather than infection? He characterized this as not yet actionable — antibiotics needed to continue pending more data — but flagged that culture negativity plus rapid improvement of an apparently severe infection was reason to doubt the infectious hypothesis.

When IR attempted the image-guided biopsy, they could no longer locate the mass on repeat imaging — it was no longer visible. They proceeded with random biopsies of the region where the mass had previously been seen on PET-CT; results, ultimately confirmed at an outside facility given initial pathologist uncertainty, showed mature adipose tissue with fat necrosis, reactive fibrovascular proliferation, and mixed acute and chronic inflammation, with pathology unable to definitively determine the clinical significance of the findings.

Rabih reasoned that the initial CT finding — fat stranding specifically between the artery and vein — combined with the mass's rapid disappearance, reframed the question from "is this a blood vessel problem" to "is this actually a problem of the connective tissue adjacent to the vessel." He noted that blood does not resolve that quickly, infection does not typically resolve that quickly, and a PET-avid mass gone within days is most consistent with fat necrosis — he could not think of another entity fitting that specific tempo, though he allowed there could be others. He concluded the patient likely did not need continued antibiotics and, absent other red flags, was likely to do well clinically, while noting ongoing vascular surveillance would be reasonable given the uncertainty about whether an underlying vascular event had occurred.

Reza summarized his own diagnostic journey through the case — from an initial focus on medium-to-large-vessel vasculitis, to a working diagnosis of perivascular infection, to a final landing point of fat necrosis — and raised the open question of what caused the fat necrosis in the first place. He described asking an AI assistant whether severe atherosclerosis could itself cause perivascular inflammation and fat necrosis (it suggested this was possible, among several other candidate explanations) and then posing the same question to a vascular surgeon colleague, who reported never having encountered such a phenomenon in 20 years of practice despite reviewing striking imaging of a heavily calcified femoral artery. Rabih recalled a separate case from his own experience — a rapidly enlarging nasal mass that had entirely resolved by the time of a scheduled dermatology biopsy, ultimately attributed to keratoacanthoma, a skin tumor known to necrose and regress its own blood supply — as an analogous example of the same broader phenomenon: a lesion's disappearance over time can itself be the most powerful diagnostic clue. The episode closed without resolving the underlying trigger for this patient's fat necrosis, explicitly framed as an open question for further reflection rather than a solved case.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| Reproducible focal tenderness over the femoral triangle | Increases probability of local (rather than referred) pathology; raises concern for incarcerated hernia |
| No palpable groin mass or bulge | Does not exclude hernia — a smaller/less-appreciable bulge is associated with higher incarceration risk |
| Robust, easily palpable dorsalis pedis pulses | Argues against acute limb-threatening macrovascular occlusion |
| CT: fat stranding around the common femoral artery/vein, indistinct venous margin | Raises concern for either primary vasculitis or a perivascular inflammatory/infectious process |
| Markedly elevated ESR (>100) and CRP | Supports an active inflammatory process; nonspecific for underlying cause |
| PET-avid, 3.8cm hypermetabolic mass encasing the femoral artery | Raises specific concern for an abscess/mycotic process encasing the vessel |
| Unilateral distribution without systemic/symmetric involvement | Lowers probability of primary vasculitis; favors an adjacent, localized process |
| Blood cultures negative x4 days despite the presumed severe endovascular infection | Lowers probability of true bacteremic endovascular infection; raises consideration of a non-infectious process |
| Rapid clinical improvement (leukocytosis normalized by day 2, pain improved) | Argues against ongoing severe infection; favors a self-limited process |
| Repeat imaging: mass no longer identifiable within days | Strongly favors a rapidly autoinfarcting/self-resolving process (e.g., fat necrosis) over infection or malignancy |
| Core biopsy: mature adipose tissue with fat necrosis, reactive fibrovascular proliferation, mixed inflammation | Confirms fat necrosis as the process; does not establish the inciting cause |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Perivascular fat necrosis mimicking vasculitis/infection illness script: fat stranding and a PET-avid mass encasing a peripheral artery, initially concerning for vasculitis or mycotic infection, that resolves within days on repeat imaging and shows fat necrosis with reactive inflammation on biopsy — the tempo of resolution, faster than blood, infection, or malignancy would plausibly allow, is the key discriminating feature.

The case is a clean example of resisting anatomic anchoring: framing a perivascular finding as a "vessel problem" (vasculitis) rather than a "problem next to the vessel" (infection, fat necrosis, or another adjacent process) required deliberately separating the vessel from its surrounding tissue as distinct diagnostic targets. It is also an explicit example of experience-calibrated heuristic ordering — one host's default sequence of "vasculitis, infection, then autoimmune vasculitis" was described as earned through prior misses rather than derived purely from textbook epidemiology, and was reinforced independently by an outside rheumatologist's point about vasculitis's typical symmetry.

## Teaching Discussion

The central teaching point is the reframing of a perivascular finding from "vessel pathology" to "pathology adjacent to the vessel" — fat stranding around an artery does not automatically implicate the artery itself, and deliberately asking whether the abnormality lies in the vessel wall or the surrounding tissue changes the differential meaningfully, here ultimately pointing away from vasculitis and toward a localized process in the perivascular fat.

A second teaching point is the diagnostic value of re-imaging a process that has gone clinically quiet: the discovery that a PET-avid, 3.8cm mass was no longer visible within days was, in this case, more diagnostically powerful than any single lab value, since it narrowed the differential to entities capable of resolving on that specific rapid tempo — with fat necrosis and keratoacanthoma offered as two anatomically distinct examples of the same broader "self-necrosing lesion" phenomenon.

A third teaching point is procedural: the episode models comfort with an explicitly unresolved ending. Biopsy confirmed fat necrosis as the process but did not explain why it occurred, and both hosts treated this as an appropriate stopping point for direct patient care (further workup was not judged necessary once fat necrosis was confirmed and the patient was improving) while leaving the underlying trigger — possibly severe atherosclerosis, possibly an unrecognized vascular event — as an open question for further reflection rather than forcing a premature unifying diagnosis.
