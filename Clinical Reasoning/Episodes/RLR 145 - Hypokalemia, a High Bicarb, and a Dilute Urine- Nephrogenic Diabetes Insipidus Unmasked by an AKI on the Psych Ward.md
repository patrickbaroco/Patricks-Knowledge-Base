## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Opens with a quick teaching "tidbit" on arteriovenous malformations before the main case: a psychiatry-service diabetes-management consult that unravels into a nephrology puzzle. A patient on glargine, metformin, empagliflozin, and lisinopril develops an acute kidney injury with a paradoxical combination of low potassium, high bicarbonate, and a strikingly dilute urine. The reasoning moves from acute-kidney-injury basics through a careful metabolic-alkalosis-versus-respiratory-acidosis-compensation workup, a detour into apparent mineralocorticoid excess and the diagnostic power of urine chloride, and finally lands on the real answer: a dilute urine in a volume-depleted, AKI patient is diabetes insipidus, not psychogenic polydipsia — and the patient's own AKI has already performed the water deprivation test "for free." The case closes on a conservative, watch-and-reassess plan that resolves without dramatic intervention, framed by a broader discussion of when aggressive workup is warranted versus when restraint is the better form of clinical judgment.

## Pearls

- Arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) lack a capillary bed — blood flows directly from artery to venule — making them structurally fragile and prone to bleeding.
- Aging is the primary risk factor for AVMs: reduced intestinal perfusion over time triggers angiogenesis, but the new vessels created are flawed and don't perfuse tissue effectively.
- Aortic stenosis and acquired von Willebrand syndrome (Heyde syndrome) don't cause AVMs to form; they share aging as a common risk factor, and the coexisting coagulopathy simply makes any AVMs that are present more likely to bleed clinically.
- In a patient with new AKI, glucosuria-driven osmotic diuresis (worsened by an SGLT2 inhibitor) plus an ACE inhibitor on board is a very plausible, easy-to-address explanation for volume depletion and kidney injury.
- An elevated bicarbonate in the setting of AKI is a red flag precisely because it's the opposite of the expected pattern (impaired acid excretion should lower bicarb) — before interpreting it, determine whether the bicarb is the primary abnormality (metabolic alkalosis) or is compensating for a chronic respiratory acidosis, by comparing bicarb and pCO2 together and trending a VBG.
- Hypokalemia is usually due to loss, not intake — GI losses are rarely silent, so unexplained hypokalemia without GI symptoms should raise suspicion for renal potassium loss, most often mediated by aldosterone (physiologic or pathologic).
- Low serum potassium plus elevated bicarbonate is a classic pairing for a high-aldosterone-tone metabolic alkalosis, but the pairing alone doesn't confirm the mechanism — it only earns the hypothesis further testing.
- Take a patient's trajectory before ordering new tests: pulling prior bicarb, pH, PCO2, smoking status, and BMI (as proxies for COPD or obesity hypoventilation) can clarify whether you're dealing with primary metabolic alkalosis or chronic respiratory acidosis before a single new lab is drawn.
- A suppressed renin and undetectable aldosterone do not rule out a mineralocorticoid-mediated process — they can instead point toward apparent mineralocorticoid excess (AME), where a different molecule (genetic enzyme defects, licorice, steroids, or certain antifungals like posaconazole) activates the mineralocorticoid receptor without aldosterone itself being elevated.
- A high serum bicarbonate necessarily implies a low serum chloride — the two move in opposite directions — so a hypochloremic patient should mount a low urine chloride if the kidney is conserving chloride appropriately.
- Urine chloride is the key discriminator in metabolic alkalosis: a low urine chloride suggests a physiologically "purposeful" reason for bicarb retention (such as vomiting), while a high urine chloride in a hypochloremic patient suggests pathologic renal chloride wasting — from diuretics, Bartter/Gitelman syndrome, or pathologic mineralocorticoid excess.
- A dilute urine (low specific gravity) in a volume-depleted, AKI patient is a paradox that points straight to diabetes insipidus — the kidney should be concentrating urine in the face of dehydration, and its failure to do so is the finding itself.
- Absence of glucosuria on urinalysis argues against osmotic diuresis (diabetes mellitus) as the driver of a dilute polyuric picture, leaving diabetes insipidus (central or nephrogenic) as the more likely explanation.
- Central diabetes insipidus is generally more common than nephrogenic DI, but lithium (common on a psychiatry service) and hypokalemia (which impairs aquaporin-2 channel function) are both classic, synergistic causes of nephrogenic DI and should be specifically considered in this context.
- A patient who is already volume-depleted and has developed AKI has effectively already failed a water deprivation test — there is no need to formally withhold fluids to distinguish diabetes insipidus from psychogenic polydipsia, since true psychogenic polydipsia cannot coexist with an ongoing AKI from dehydration.
- Mislabeling diabetes insipidus as psychogenic polydipsia and restricting fluids is dangerous — a missed vasopressin dose in a patient with known central DI can send serum sodium from 140 to 170 overnight, causing catastrophic seizures.
- Metformin no longer needs to be reflexively held for imaging contrast exposure in a patient without AKI; continuing it when appropriate reduces the need for inpatient sliding-scale insulin.
- If the body isn't "complaining" — the patient is asymptomatic — either nothing serious is wrong, or something is wrong but mild enough to likely self-correct; over-medicalizing an asymptomatic abnormality can do more harm than the abnormality itself.
- Documenting a contingency plan in a note (an "if this doesn't improve, my next step is X") primes the right differential for whoever picks the case up later, even when the initial workup is deliberately conservative.
- The right posture for ambiguous, non-emergent findings: overthink the possibilities, undertest in the moment, and stay ready to pivot quickly the moment new data arrives.

## Problem Representation

A patient on a psychiatry service, being managed with glargine, metformin, empagliflozin, and lisinopril for diabetes and hypertension, develops an acute kidney injury (creatinine 1.40 from a baseline of 1.1) accompanied by hypokalemia (potassium 3.1), an unexpectedly elevated bicarbonate (30), and — once further tested — a strikingly dilute urine (specific gravity 1.005) without glucosuria, ultimately traced to a hypokalemia- and possible lithium-driven nephrogenic diabetes insipidus superimposed on volume depletion.

## Case Narrative

### Quick Tidbit: Arteriovenous Malformations

Before the main case, Reza shared a short teaching segment — a new format the pair is trialing — on arteriovenous malformations, prompted by a recent patient with a hemoglobin of 6.6 who was found to have AVMs on colonoscopy. Working through the physiology with the help of an AI assistant, he laid out the normal circulatory path (artery to capillary bed to venule to vein) and how an AVM short-circuits it, with blood flowing directly from artery to venule with no intervening capillary bed — a structural fragility that makes these vessels prone to bleeding. The primary risk factor is aging: as intestinal perfusion declines over time, the bowel responds with angiogenesis, but the new vessels it builds are flawed and don't actually perfuse tissue well, becoming AVMs. He used the segment to correct a common misconception: aortic stenosis and acquired von Willebrand syndrome (the pairing known as Heyde syndrome) don't cause AVMs to form. Rather, the same aging process that predisposes to AVMs also predisposes to aortic stenosis, and the coexisting acquired coagulopathy from shear-driven von Willebrand factor degradation simply makes bleeding from AVMs that are already present more likely and more severe.

### Main Case: The Psychiatry Consult That Wasn't About Diabetes

Reza presented as the medicine consultant, called by the psychiatry service for help managing a patient's diabetes, with glucose readings running in the 200s–300s. The patient's outpatient regimen was 20 units of glargine at night with no nutritional insulin, metformin 1 gram twice daily, and empagliflozin; for hypertension, the patient was on lisinopril alone. On chasing down the chemistry panel, though, Reza found something more interesting than a glucose problem: sodium 135, potassium 3.1, bicarbonate 30, and a creatinine of 1.40 — up from a baseline of 1.1 six months earlier, confirming an acute kidney injury.

Robbie's first move was to explain the AKI mechanistically: glucosuria (worsened by the SGLT2 inhibitor) drags water out with it into the urine, and if the patient isn't compensating fully by drinking more, that alone can drive volume depletion and kidney injury — compounded by the effect of the ACE inhibitor. He proposed holding the offending medications and hydrating as an easy first step. But the elevated bicarbonate stopped him: AKI usually impairs acid excretion and lowers bicarbonate, so a high bicarbonate in this setting needed an explanation of its own. The critical first branch point, he explained, is whether the bicarb elevation is the primary problem (a metabolic alkalosis) or is compensating for a chronic respiratory acidosis reflected in an elevated pCO2 — which would mean the bicarb wasn't a problem to fix at all, but a protective response. He asked for the trend of the bicarb, a prior pH, and the patient's smoking status and BMI as proxies for COPD or obesity hypoventilation syndrome.

Turning to the potassium of 3.1, Robbie reasoned that hypokalemia is usually from loss rather than reduced intake, and that GI losses without symptoms are unusual — pointing instead toward renal loss, most often mediated by aldosterone, whether physiologic (as with diuretic use) or pathologic. The combination of hypokalemia and an elevated bicarbonate fit a high-aldosterone-tone metabolic alkalosis, though he flagged that the hypokalemia was mild enough that it could also be an unrelated, transient finding layered on top of a primarily respiratory picture.

Reza then supplied prior data: blood pressure the day before was 159/90, and two prior venous blood gases sent conflicting signals — a more recent sample (about two weeks prior) showed a mildly alkalotic pH with a bicarbonate in the high 20s to low 30s and a normal pCO2, while a sample from roughly a year earlier showed a near-normal pH with a markedly elevated pCO2 of 60, suggesting a prominent chronic respiratory acidosis. Robbie acknowledged the tension directly: one data point argued for pure metabolic alkalosis, the other for chronic respiratory acidosis with metabolic compensation — and rather than force a conclusion, the right move was to get a fresh gas today.

Reza admitted he'd been tempted by an elegant unifying hypothesis — hypertension, hypokalemia, and an elevated bicarbonate together suggesting hyperaldosteronism — and had already sent renin and aldosterone levels: both came back low, renin essentially undetectable and aldosterone undetectable. Robbie used this as a teaching moment about the danger of over-interpreting a single lab pairing. A reflexive read of undetectable renin and aldosterone is "no hyperaldosteronism, case closed" — but the more careful conclusion is that the aldosterone axis itself is off, which raises a second possibility: apparent mineralocorticoid excess, where some other molecule (genetic enzyme defects, licorice, steroids, or certain antifungals such as posaconazole) is activating the mineralocorticoid receptor directly without aldosterone being elevated at all. He argued the only way to make real progress was to go back to fundamentals: confirm whether this truly was a metabolic alkalosis (rather than respiratory compensation) using a fresh VBG, and clarify the mechanism of the hypokalemia — simple hypomagnesemia versus a distinct renal-wasting process — using a urine chloride.

He explained the urine chloride logic carefully: a high serum bicarbonate necessarily means a low serum chloride, so a properly functioning kidney facing hypochloremia should reabsorb chloride aggressively and produce a low urine chloride. If a hypochloremic patient instead has a high urine chloride, that signals either pathologic mineralocorticoid activity or ongoing renal chloride losses from diuretics or, rarely, congenital tubulopathies like Bartter or Gitelman syndrome. A low urine chloride, by contrast, points toward a physiologically "purposeful" reason for the bicarb retention, such as vomiting — even in the absence of reported vomiting or diarrhea.

The freshest VBG (from about two weeks prior) showed a pH of 7.43, a pCO2 of 42, and a bicarbonate in the high 20s to 30 — a metabolic alkalosis, and likely even more pronounced than that once accounting for venous blood typically running more acidic than arterial. Given the elevated bicarb, low potassium, and this alkalotic gas, Robbie's working impression was a metabolic alkalosis with a high aldosterone-like tone, and he wanted to know whether there was a plausible driver — the patient wasn't vomiting and wasn't on a diuretic, which made the picture more intriguing. Reza then revealed the urine chloride: very low. That finding argued against pathologic aldosterone-mediated or diuretic-driven chloride wasting, pointing instead toward a physiologically appropriate, if unexplained, reason for chloride and bicarb handling.

The next data point reframed the whole case. A urinalysis — sent by the psychiatry team for reasons that weren't entirely clear — showed a specific gravity of 1.005 (markedly dilute), with no glucose, white cells, red cells, or protein. Robbie's reaction was that this finding couldn't be dismissed as incidental: a low specific gravity is often benign in a well-hydrated person, but this patient was dehydrated, with an active AKI. A dehydrated patient with dilute urine is a paradox with only one real explanation — the kidney is failing to concentrate urine despite a strong physiologic signal to do so. That is the definition of diabetes insipidus, and the absence of glucosuria on the same urinalysis argued against diabetes mellitus (osmotic diuresis) as the cause of the polyuric picture. Central DI is generally more common than nephrogenic DI, but this patient had two specific reasons to favor a nephrogenic process: being on a psychiatry service raised the possibility of lithium, a classic cause of nephrogenic DI (often layered on top of some degree of intrinsic kidney disease), and separately, hypokalemia itself is known to impair the function of the aquaporin-2 channel, which alone can produce a urinary concentrating defect.

Reza raised the obvious clinical trap: a dilute urine on a psychiatry patient could easily be mislabeled as psychogenic polydipsia and "treated" with fluid restriction — a genuinely dangerous move, since he recalled a separate real case where a patient with known central DI missed a vasopressin dose and their sodium rocketed from 140 to 170 overnight, causing catastrophic ICU seizures. Robbie's answer was elegant: this patient's own AKI had already performed the differentiating test. The formal way to distinguish DI from psychogenic polydipsia is a water deprivation test — literally withholding water to see whether the patient can concentrate their urine. This patient was already volume-depleted, with an AKI to prove it, and their urine was still dilute — the water deprivation test had already happened, for free, and psychogenic polydipsia could be excluded outright, since a person in a state of water intoxication cannot simultaneously be dehydrated with an AKI.

Reza used the moment to add a related teaching point about metformin: the older reflex of holding it broadly for any AKI or contrast exposure is outdated — if a patient doesn't have significant kidney injury, metformin can typically be continued even around contrast administration, and doing so reduces the need for inpatient sliding-scale insulin. In this patient, though, both lisinopril and metformin were held given the confirmed AKI. With renin and aldosterone already drawn and unhelpful, the plan was conservative: repeat the urinalysis, repeat the basic metabolic panel, monitor ins and outs closely, and — critically — do not restrict fluids. On repeat testing, the creatinine had fallen to 1.16 (near the patient's baseline), the potassium had normalized to 4.0, and the specific gravity had improved to 1.016; metformin was restarted, and the patient did well without any specific intervention beyond withdrawal of the offending medications and supportive monitoring.

Reza closed by reflecting on how much reasoning the case had activated — hyperaldosteronism, apparent mineralocorticoid excess, urine chloride physiology, central versus nephrogenic diabetes insipidus — for a problem that ultimately resolved with nothing more than repeat labs and observation. Robbie offered a broader philosophy in response: if a patient's body isn't signaling distress, either nothing is meaningfully wrong or the problem is mild enough to self-correct, and over-medicalizing asymptomatic findings causes real harm. He contrasted this with a recent "Friday virtual morning report" case where an aggressive workup had appropriately caught a life-threatening thrombotic disorder — the point being that the right amount of workup depends on how sick the patient actually is, not on how interesting the differential looks on paper. Reza matched this with a story from his own training at Johns Hopkins: a patient with C. diff, an AKI, and proteinuria in the setting of diabetes, whose elevated inflammatory markers he chose not to chase immediately, but for whom he explicitly documented a contingency plan — if the kidney function didn't improve, the next step was an ANCA vasculitis workup. Two weeks later, the chief resident confirmed the patient did in fact have GPA or MPA, validating the value of "priming" a differential in the chart even while deliberately not acting on it yet. The episode's closing formulation for this style of reasoning: overthink the possibilities, undertest in the moment, and stay ready to pivot the instant new data arrives.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

This case is a study in resisting premature closure on an attractive unifying diagnosis. Hypertension, hypokalemia, and an elevated bicarbonate form a classic illness script for hyperaldosteronism, and both hosts flag how tempting — and how risky — it is to lock onto that script before confirming its individual components (is this truly a metabolic alkalosis? is the hypokalemia even renal in origin?). The undetectable renin and aldosterone could easily be misread as ruling out the entire mineralocorticoid axis, when in fact they open a second, less familiar illness script (apparent mineralocorticoid excess) that a less careful reasoner might miss entirely. The case's real turn — from an endocrine puzzle to diabetes insipidus — hinges on refusing to treat an incidental-seeming lab (urine specific gravity) as noise, and on the elegant use of the patient's own physiologic state (AKI equals ongoing water deprivation) as a natural experiment that made further testing unnecessary. The closing discussion is an explicit meditation on calibrating workup intensity to how symptomatic and how sick the patient actually is, paired with the "priming the differential without acting on it" habit as a middle path between reflexive extensive testing and premature closure.

## Teaching Discussion

The episode's central teaching arc is less about a single diagnosis and more about a chain of sequential branch points, each of which could have been closed prematurely: is the elevated bicarb primary or compensatory; is the hypokalemia significant or incidental; does a suppressed aldosterone rule out a mineralocorticoid process; and finally, does a dilute urine in a psychiatric patient mean psychogenic polydipsia or diabetes insipidus. At each step, the hosts model resisting the most convenient inference in favor of the one supported by mechanism. The urine chloride discussion is presented as an underused but powerful discriminator in metabolic alkalosis, explicitly flagged by both hosts as underrepresented in typical case discussions because metabolic alkalosis itself is less commonly discussed than metabolic acidosis. The diabetes insipidus reasoning is built around a genuinely novel bedside insight — that an already-dehydrated, AKI patient has involuntarily completed their own water deprivation test — which lets the team exclude psychogenic polydipsia without ordering anything further. The closing reflection functions as a companion piece to the show's frequent emphasis on diagnostic humility, but flips it: here the humility is about not chasing every abnormal value to its root cause when the patient's clinical trajectory is reassuring, while still documenting a contingency plan (Reza's Hopkins ANCA vasculitis story) in case the trajectory changes.
