## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A recorded-live unknown: a 63-year-old woman, a devoted knitter followed for three years in a county-hospital clinic for well-controlled diabetes and hypertension, whose decade of [[Pruritus|itching]] has been quietly copied forward, unevaluated, from note to note. When her frustration finally lifts the itch to the top of the problem list, Rabih works it up: itchy everywhere, worse at night, nothing helps, and — the pivotal negative — no rash, on history or exam. Reza approaches pruritus by [[Base Rate|base rate]] first (xerosis, atopic dermatitis, eczema, scabies), then treats the 10-year tempo and the absence of any primary skin lesion as red flags pointing *internal*. Borrowing the Curbsiders' internal-versus-external rash frame by [[Analogical Reasoning|analogy]] and recalling [[Moses Murdoch Pruritus Schema|Moses Murdoch's pruritus schema]], he runs an organ approach (hepatobiliary, renal, thyroid) beside a category approach (malignancy — an *indolent* [[Indolent Lymphoma|lymphoma]] given the tempo; infection; autoimmune). Labs settle it toward the liver: normal CBC, renal, and thyroid, but a cholestatic/mixed pattern with an alkaline phosphatase of 350, AST 75, bilirubin 1.5, and a high GGT. A pristine [[Right Upper Quadrant Ultrasound|ultrasound]], negative hepatitis serologies, a clean [[MRCP]], and an unremarkable med reconciliation strip away extrahepatic obstruction and drugs, leaving [[Intrahepatic Cholestasis|intrahepatic cholestasis]]. Reza — prompted by a chief-resident memory — names it: [[Primary Biliary Cholangitis|primary biliary cholangitis]], confirmed by a very positive [[Antimitochondrial Antibody|AMA]] (with a high total IgM as a faster clue) and a positive ANA. She responds dramatically to [[Ursodeoxycholic Acid|ursodiol]]. The gut-punch coda: her alkaline phosphatase had been elevated and ignored for five years, and one test would have ended years of misery — a [[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive autopsy]] on schema use, diagnostic inertia, and the advocacy, gender, and immigration threads that let a morbid diagnosis hide in plain sight.

## Pearls

- **Work [[Pruritus|pruritus]] by [[Base Rate|base rate]] first, spend cognitive energy proportionally, and let red flags earn the deeper dive.** Start with the common causes (xerosis, atopic dermatitis, eczema, [[Scabies|scabies]]); a long duration and *failure of prior therapy* are the signals that license moving to sinister etiologies.
- **The most useful finding in itch is the *absence* of a primary rash — and the trap is the secondary rash the patient makes by scratching.** Excoriations are a consequence, not a clue; itch without a primary lesion points *internal* and reframes the whole workup.
- **[[Analogical Reasoning|Reason by analogy]] when a complaint is unfamiliar: internal-versus-external, borrowed from rash, ports cleanly onto pruritus.** A frame built for one problem can scaffold a problem you rarely see, so long as you name that you are borrowing it.
- **Itch localizes to organs and to categories — run both lists.** Organ-specific: hepatobiliary ([[Cholestatic Pruritus|bile-salt deposition]]), renal ([[Uremic Pruritus|uremia]]), thyroid. Category: malignancy (an *[[Indolent Lymphoma|indolent]]* lymphoma if the itch is chronic), infection ([[Secondary Syphilis|syphilis]] — but expect a lesion), autoimmune.
- **A cholestatic/mixed pattern with a predominant [[Alkaline Phosphatase|alkaline phosphatase]] in a woman with chronic pruritus is [[Primary Biliary Cholangitis|PBC]] until proven otherwise — and one antibody makes the diagnosis.** Send the [[Antimitochondrial Antibody|AMA]] (and ANA) *before* reaching for a liver biopsy; a high total IgM is a faster corroborating clue.
- **A single unaddressed number can carry a morbid diagnosis for years.** A years-elevated alkaline phosphatase that is copied forward rather than evaluated is [[Diagnostic Momentum|diagnostic inertia]] made concrete — the fix is not more memory but a [[Schema-Based Reasoning|schema]] in front of you at the moment of framing.

## Problem Representation

A 63-year-old woman with well-controlled type 2 diabetes and hypertension, followed longitudinally in a county-hospital primary-care clinic, presents with roughly a decade of generalized pruritus that is worse at night, refractory to every therapy tried, and — importantly — unaccompanied by any primary rash on history or focused examination; laboratory evaluation shows a normal CBC, renal panel, and thyroid studies but a cholestatic-predominant/mixed liver chemistry pattern (alkaline phosphatase ~350, AST ~75, total bilirubin 1.5, albumin 3, INR 1.3, elevated GGT), with a normal right-upper-quadrant ultrasound, negative viral hepatitis serologies, a normal MRCP, and no offending medications — ultimately diagnosed as primary biliary cholangitis on a strongly positive antimitochondrial antibody (with a markedly elevated total IgM and positive ANA), with a dramatic biochemical and symptomatic response to ursodeoxycholic acid.

## Case Narrative

The episode is recorded live for the VMR community, with twenty-one participants Reza can turn to for help. After Rabih shares a personal grief — a tragedy in his hometown in Lebanon — and Reza a lighthearted story about a buzzing sound in his apartment, Rabih introduces "Clinic Robbie," his outpatient self, and the observation that most of ambulatory medicine is knowing your patient. He has cared for this patient for three years: a 63-year-old woman with diabetes and hypertension, both well controlled, and a gifted knitter whose handiwork he has collected. She has also been itchy for as long as she can remember — a decade — and it has never changed. For a long time the itch was mentioned in passing and, appropriately or not, deprioritized behind the diabetes and hypertension, copied forward from a prior provider's note with no evaluation beyond "continue topical therapy."

Reza frames the problem as a chronic, decade-long disorder with a chief complaint of [[Pruritus|pruritus]] — a notoriously slippery complaint. He begins with the discipline of [[Base Rate|base rate]]: list the common causes and spend cognitive energy in proportion to prevalence, digging deeper only when there is a reason. The common causes he names are xerosis (dry skin), atopic dermatitis, and eczema; the most important first move is to *look at the skin* for a primary cutaneous clue — while remembering that a patient who itches will scratch, so the secondary excoriations are a consequence, not the primary etiology. He flags the 10-year duration as a red flag that makes the common (and presumably already-treated) causes less likely.

Rabih sharpens the history: the itch is everywhere, worse at night, refractory to everything handed to her, without associated symptoms, and — on both history and a focused exam — with *no rash*. Reza reads the absence of any primary lesion as evidence of an *internal* cause and reaches for structure. He recalls team member [[Moses Murdoch Pruritus Schema|Moses Murdoch's pruritus schema]] and, explicitly, borrows the Curbsiders' internal-versus-external frame from *rash* and applies it to itch — naming the move as [[Analogical Reasoning|analogical reasoning]] for a complaint he rarely works. He adds [[Scabies|scabies]] to the common list, then splits the sinister differential two ways. By organ: the hepatobiliary system (obstruction → [[Cholestatic Pruritus|bile-salt deposition]] irritating sensory nerves), renal insufficiency ([[Uremic Pruritus|uremic pruritus]] with a rising BUN), and thyroid disease. By category: malignancy — specifically lymphoma, and if a lymphoma has smoldered for 10 years it must be an [[Indolent Lymphoma|indolent]] one (with the caveat that indolent lymphomas can transform to aggressive forms like diffuse large B-cell lymphoma); infection such as [[Secondary Syphilis|secondary syphilis]], though he would expect a cutaneous lesion; and autoimmunity, from which few itch-linked diagnoses come readily to mind. Absent liver, kidney, or thyroid disease, his lead would be an indolent malignancy.

Rabih returns the labs: CBC, renal panel, and thyroid studies all normal — but the liver chemistries are abnormal, with an AST of 75, an alkaline phosphatase of 350, a total bilirubin of 1.5, an albumin of 3, an INR of 1.3, and an elevated GGT (confirming the alkaline phosphatase is of hepatic origin). Before the diagnosis is revealed, the two detour into a teaching aside on bilirubin — a callback to the prior episode where Reza had teased Rabih for fractionating a nearly normal value. Reza offers a "case within a case": a 25-year-old woman with right-upper-quadrant pain, a bilirubin of 1.5 with a direct of only 0.3 (indirect-predominant), normal transaminases, a leukocytosis, a positive Murphy sign, and a normal ultrasound and CT. Rabih reasons that indirect hyperbilirubinemia narrows to occult [[Hemolysis|hemolysis]] versus [[Gilbert Syndrome|Gilbert syndrome]] and bets Gilbert; Reza reveals the true diagnosis was pelvic inflammatory disease (perihepatitis), a lesson he traces back to RLR 005 — the point being that fractionating the bilirubin genuinely directs the workup.

Back in the case, Reza categorizes the liver pattern as hepatocellular, cholestatic, or infiltrative and calls this a mixed pattern; given the predominance of the alkaline phosphatase he bets on [[Intrahepatic Cholestasis|intrahepatic]] over extrahepatic cholestasis, without fully excluding the latter. He weighs "true-true-and-related" against "true-true-and-unrelated" and, because the itch and the liver both point the same way, treats them as linked while acknowledging that common abnormal-LFT drivers (hepatitis C, alcohol, MASH — metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis) are prevalent and could be incidental. With pruritus now signaling the hepatobiliary tree, his lead hypotheses become an infiltrative/granulomatous process (sarcoid, lymphoma) or occult extrahepatic obstruction; he is less worried about infection given the absent leukocytosis, fever, and weight loss, and notes that a 10-year course excludes pancreatic adenocarcinoma as an obstructing cause. He would start with a [[Right Upper Quadrant Ultrasound|right-upper-quadrant ultrasound]], escalate to [[MRCP]], and reserve liver biopsy for a granulomatous hunt.

The data narrow relentlessly. The ultrasound is pristine — clean biliary tree and gallbladder — and hepatitis serologies are negative; the absence of ductal dilation further argues against extrahepatic obstruction. Reza reframes, and pointedly *drops the pruritus* to work the liver on its own terms: intrahepatic cholestasis with a predominant alkaline phosphatase, most concerning for an infiltrative process, a medication, or an ingested herb. The MRCP is normal and the medication reconciliation reveals no offending agent.

The answer arrives as a memory: a chief resident at the same county hospital who once asked Reza about a decade of pruritus, and the case that taught him [[Primary Biliary Cholangitis|primary biliary cholangitis]] (renamed from "cirrhosis," since not every PBC progresses to cirrhosis) as a cause of intense itch. In a woman with intrahepatic cholestasis, this must be considered before a biopsy. He sends the ANA and the [[Antimitochondrial Antibody|antimitochondrial antibody]]. The AMA returns strongly positive, the ANA positive, and a total IgM — which Rabih notes returns faster and served as an early clue — markedly elevated. The diagnosis is primary biliary cholangitis. She is started on [[Ursodeoxycholic Acid|ursodiol]], whose response tends to be binary, and hers is dramatic: her alkaline phosphatase falls, her pruritus improves markedly, and she knits more than ever.

Rabih closes on the case's weight. Reviewing her chart, he finds an alkaline phosphatase that had been elevated and untouched for five years — a morbid diagnosis that needed a single test hiding behind a copied-forward problem — and speaks candidly about his own two years of missing it, and about the advocacy, gender, and immigration-status threads that make the delay both understandable and sad. Reza performs a [[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive autopsy]] on his own reasoning: why, seeing a cholestatic pattern and pruritus, did PBC not surface instantly? Because he rarely thinks about PBC in day-to-day practice — which is exactly the argument for a [[Schema-Based Reasoning|schema]]. Both a pruritus schema and an intrahepatic-cholestasis schema would have listed PBC; the job is to frame the patient and then fall back on the checklist, ideally with the schema literally in front of you.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Diagnostic Momentum|Diagnostic momentum / inertia]] via the copy-forward note.** The single most consequential error is structural: a pruritus problem carried forward across providers and years with "continue topical therapy," never re-derived. The abnormal alkaline phosphatase sat unaddressed for five years. The lesson both hosts draw is that inherited, copied-forward problems deserve fresh evaluation, not perpetuation.
- **[[Base Rate|Base-rate discipline]], used well and then updated.** Reza deliberately spends cognitive energy in proportion to prevalence — common causes first — but treats duration and treatment-failure as legitimate triggers to escalate. This is base-rate reasoning as a *starting* posture, not a stopping rule.
- **[[Analogical Reasoning|Analogical reasoning]] for an unfamiliar complaint.** Facing a chief complaint he rarely works, Reza ports the internal-versus-external rash frame onto pruritus and names the borrowing explicitly. Analogy scaffolds reasoning about the unfamiliar — its safety lies in flagging that a frame is being transplanted.
- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|Schema over recall]].** The cognitive autopsy's core finding is that PBC did not surface reflexively because it is rare in day-to-day practice — a recall failure, not a reasoning failure. The remedy is a schema (pruritus; intrahepatic cholestasis) consulted at the moment of framing, so the answer does not depend on what happens to be top-of-mind.
- **True-true-and-related vs unrelated, held open.** Reza commits to the linked interpretation (itch and cholestasis as one process) while explicitly preserving the possibility that a prevalent LFT abnormality (hep C, alcohol, MASH) could be incidental — resisting the pull to force a single unifying story prematurely.
- **[[Cognitive Autopsy|Cognitive autopsy]] as the closing discipline.** Rather than celebrate the answer, Reza dissects the *delay to* the answer, and Rabih names the non-cognitive contributors — advocacy, gender, immigration status — that shape which patients get their abnormal labs pursued. The autopsy treats the miss, not just the hit, as the teaching material.

## Teaching Discussion

- Approach pruritus like any complaint: enumerate the common causes first (xerosis, atopic dermatitis, eczema, scabies) and let the base rate direct your effort, escalating to sinister etiologies only when a red flag — here, a decade of refractory itch — earns it.
- The decisive dermatologic question in itch is whether a *primary* rash exists; if it does not, the cause is likely internal, and the excoriations from scratching are a secondary change to be discounted rather than chased.
- When a complaint is unfamiliar, borrow a frame by analogy and say so. Internal-versus-external, built for rash, structures pruritus cleanly — and naming the transplant keeps you honest about its limits.
- Run pruritus against both an organ list (hepatobiliary bile-salt deposition, uremia, thyroid) and a category list (malignancy — indolent lymphoma if chronic, with the caveat it can transform; infection like syphilis, expecting a lesion; autoimmune), so a rare cause is not missed for lack of a place to put it.
- Categorize abnormal liver chemistries as hepatocellular, cholestatic, or infiltrative; an alkaline-phosphatase-predominant mixed pattern with a corroborating GGT points intrahepatic, and the absence of ductal dilation on imaging argues against extrahepatic obstruction.
- In a woman with chronic pruritus and intrahepatic cholestasis, put PBC at the top and send the AMA (and ANA) before a liver biopsy; a markedly elevated total IgM is a faster corroborating clue, and the biochemical/symptomatic response to ursodiol — though binary — can itself confirm the diagnosis.
- Fractionating even a mildly elevated bilirubin can redirect the workup: indirect-predominant hyperbilirubinemia narrows to occult hemolysis versus Gilbert syndrome, a lesson worth the extra order (callback to RLR 005 and the bilirubin debate of RLR 020).
- The durable safeguard against missing a rare-but-morbid diagnosis is not memorization but a schema consulted at the point of framing — and a willingness to re-evaluate inherited, copied-forward problems rather than carry them forward untouched. The non-cognitive drivers of delay (advocacy, gender, immigration status) belong in the autopsy too.
