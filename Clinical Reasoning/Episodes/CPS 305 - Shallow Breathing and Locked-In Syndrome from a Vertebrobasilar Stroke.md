> [!summary] Summary
> A 64-year-old woman with no significant past medical history develops sudden right-sided neck pain while gardening, followed by a biphasic neurologic catastrophe: a transient episode of global weakness and mutism that resolves to a residual facial droop, then — hours later — a second, sustained episode of shallow breathing, left gaze preference, and quadriplegia with a decreasing level of responsiveness. Sequential cranial-nerve examination localizes the lesion caudal to the pons: intact pupils and eye movements, intact face, but **absent cough and gag reflexes** (CN IX/X, nucleus ambiguus) and flaccid quadriplegia with sensation preserved only to light touch — a **locked-in-like state**. Imaging reveals bilateral cerebellar infarcts plus a cervicomedullary/upper cervical cord infarct, attributed to an occluded right vertebral artery with a presumed embolic contribution, since a single unilateral vertebral occlusion cannot by itself explain bilateral findings. A full stroke-cause workup — echocardiogram, cardiac monitoring, hypercoagulable panel, vascular risk factors — is entirely unremarkable, and the case is ultimately classified as an **embolic stroke of undetermined source (ESUS)**. After roughly two months of ventilatory support, the patient makes a remarkable recovery, regaining full motor function and independent breathing.

## Pearls

- An ill-fitting symptom (episodic shortness of breath in a neurology clinic) can be made tractable by **"rounding to the nearest thing"** — translating it into a recognizable neurologic category (here, paroxysmal neurologic events: TIA, seizure, migraine) rather than getting stuck on the symptom's non-neurologic face value.
- **Conjugate gaze deviation localizes**, but the rule flips depending on level: a destructive/ablative **hemispheric** lesion (e.g., MCA stroke) sends the eyes *toward* the lesion (and away from the hemiparetic side), while an irritative **seizure focus** sends the eyes *away* from the focus; a **pontine** lesion reverses the hemispheric rule and sends the eyes *away* from the lesion, toward the hemiparetic side, because the paramedian pontine reticular formation (PPRF) drives gaze to the *same* side it sits on.
- **Basilar artery thrombosis can mimic seizure** — patients can develop stimulus-triggered posturing and opisthotonus that looks like status epilepticus. Anchoring on a seizure diagnosis in a patient with no seizure history and an acute presentation risks missing a treatable vascular catastrophe.
- **Absent cough and gag reflexes localize precisely to the medulla** (nucleus ambiguus, CN IX/X) — a simple bedside test that, combined with intact cranial nerves above it, can localize a brainstem lesion to a specific level before imaging is even available.
- The **anterior spinal artery** arises from the vertebral arteries and supplies the bilateral medial medulla and spinal cord — so unilateral vertebral artery disease can still produce bilateral corticospinal and respiratory (phrenic/anterior horn) findings.
- Even with a full modern stroke-cause workup, a specific etiology is often never found — **embolic stroke of undetermined source (ESUS)** is a real, common endpoint, and recent trial data has not shown a clear benefit of anticoagulation over aspirin in these patients.

## Problem Representation

A 64-year-old woman with no significant past medical history presents with sudden right-sided neck pain, nausea, and diaphoresis while gardening, followed by a transient episode of inability to speak or move that resolves to a residual left facial droop, then — after an interval of apparent recovery — a second, sustained episode of shallow breathing, left gaze preference, quadriplegia, and decreased responsiveness, ultimately localizing to a locked-in-like state from bilateral posterior-circulation and cervicomedullary/upper cervical cord infarction due to right vertebral artery occlusion of undetermined cause.

## Case Narrative

Vanessa presented the case to Maria and Sridhara, with Aaron Berkowitz moderating. The chief concern was deliberately disorienting for a *neurology* morning report: a 64-year-old woman with no significant medical history and a complaint of **shallow breathing for a few minutes' duration**. Berkowitz immediately flagged the oddity — "just to confirm it's neurology morning report, right?" — modeling the reflex of first excluding cardiopulmonary causes before entering a neurologic differential, since by definition this patient had already been triaged away from other specialties.

Maria's first pass reasoning split shortness of breath into two neurologic buckets: peripheral neuromuscular weakness (myopathy, neuromuscular junction disease, polyneuropathy) causing respiratory muscle fatigue, versus a central/encephalopathic process with an altered breathing pattern. Working through the neuromuscular buckets in more detail — muscle (e.g., muscular dystrophies), neuromuscular junction (myasthenia gravis, Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome, botulism), and motor nerve (Guillain-Barré syndrome, CIDP) — Berkowitz added texture from clinical experience: Lambert-Eaton and botulism are both exceedingly rare and, when they do present, are rarely isolated respiratory complaints without other prominent findings (proximal weakness and autonomic dysfunction for Lambert-Eaton; descending paralysis with prominent bulbar and often pupillary involvement for botulism, distinguishing it from Guillain-Barré). Myasthenia gravis, the most common of the three, typically presents with ocular findings (ptosis, diplopia) rather than isolated dyspnea. Guillain-Barré and ALS can both cause respiratory failure from muscle weakness, but usually only after other regions are already involved — isolated respiratory failure as a presenting symptom would be atypical for any of these.

The key pivot came when Vanessa clarified that the shallow breathing was **episodic and acute**, not chronic or subacute. Sridhara raised **epilepsy** as a cause of paroxysmal breathing disturbances (patients hyperventilating or feeling unable to catch their breath during seizures), and Berkowitz generalized this into an explicit reasoning heuristic he called **"rounding to the nearest thing"**: when a symptom itself is hard to reason about directly (episodic shortness of breath), translate it into the nearest *recognizable* neurologic category — here, episodic neurologic symptoms generally — and ask whether that category's usual causes (TIA, seizure, migraine) fit. Migraine was quickly dismissed as a poor fit; TIA was noted as vanishingly rare under modern strict definitions; but seizure survived, since seizures "can do anything," including triggering autonomic phenomena (tachycardia, bradycardia, diaphoresis, gastric sensations) via the insular cortex's cardiac representation (the "cardunculus," a concept Berkowitz attributed to his mentor **Marty Samuels**) or, as Sridhara added, insular seizures specifically causing a sensation of laryngeal constriction that patients describe as an inability to breathe.

Vanessa then delivered the rest of the history, and it reframed the entire case. Seven hours before consultation, the patient had been gardening when she developed sudden, moderate-intensity, right-sided neck pain associated with nausea, diaphoresis, and (per the transcript) a sensation prompting her to contact a telemedicine service, which referred her to a hospital. En route she did not lose consciousness by report, but was observed sitting with her head back, unable to speak or move her extremities, with cold extremities. She was brought in emergently; by the time she reached the emergency department this had spontaneously resolved, leaving only a residual left facial droop. She was admitted to the neuro ICU for observation — and then, a few hours later, developed a second episode: shallow breathing, left gaze preference, inability to move any extremity, and decreasing responsiveness.

Maria worked through this systematically. The right-sided neck pain raised immediate concern for **cervical arterial dissection** (carotid or vertebral), especially given the absence of any reported trauma. The transient, near-total loss of function followed by resolution suggested an ischemic (TIA-like) rather than fixed structural process. The left gaze preference was used as a discriminator between stroke and seizure: an **irritative lesion (seizure)** pushes gaze *away* from the seizure focus, while an **ablative lesion (stroke)** allows the unopposed contralateral hemisphere to push gaze *toward* the lesion. Critically, the **shallow breathing** raised concern that the process localized to the brainstem, where autonomic, cardiovascular, and respiratory control converge — a potentially life-threatening localization regardless of the underlying mechanism, stroke or seizure.

Berkowitz used this moment to lay out a broader framework for sudden neurologic presentations: sudden neurology is generally **stroke, seizure, trauma, or a toxic-metabolic process** (with the caveat that toxic-metabolic processes rarely produce such focal deficits). No trauma was reported. Neck pain strongly favored a vascular explanation (dissection) over seizure. He then asked directly whether the brainstem itself can generate a seizure; Maria correctly intuited "no" — seizures arise from cortical gray matter, and the brainstem and cerebellum, lacking the relevant cortical architecture, are not seizure generators, though they can certainly be involved in *strokes* that produce dramatic, seizure-mimicking phenomena.

Sridhara then introduced the specific, high-stakes possibility: **vertebral artery dissection with basilar artery thrombosis**. He noted that the vertebral arteries give rise to the **anterior spinal artery**, so a vertebrobasilar process can infarct both the brainstem and the cervical spinal cord, producing bilateral weakness and phrenic-nerve-mediated respiratory compromise from anterior horn cell involvement. He also flagged a crucial anchoring-bias trap: basilar artery occlusion can produce **stimulus-triggered posturing and opisthotonus** that mimics status epilepticus, and clinicians without a prior seizure history who see this pattern should not anchor prematurely on epilepsy. On gaze deviation specifically, Sridhara added the brainstem-level exception to Maria's hemispheric rule: gaze deviation *toward* the side of weakness suggests seizure at the hemispheric level, but can also occur with a **pontine stroke**, where the rule inverts relative to hemispheric strokes.

Berkowitz then walked through the anatomy of horizontal gaze in more depth. At the hemispheric level, the frontal eye field (FEF) sends the eyes to the *contralateral* field during normal activation; a destructive left MCA stroke silences the left FEF, so the unopposed right FEF wins and the eyes deviate to the left — *toward* the lesion, *away* from the resulting right-sided hemiparesis. A left-hemisphere seizure hyperactivates the left FEF, pushing the eyes to the right — *away* from the focus. At the brainstem level, the horizontal gaze centers (the paramedian pontine reticular formation, or PPRF) work in the opposite direction: the left PPRF sends the eyes to the *left* (ipsilateral, not contralateral). A left pontine lesion — which also produces right-sided weakness, since it is above the corticospinal tract decussation — therefore causes gaze deviation to the *right*, because the left PPRF is knocked out and cannot send the eyes left. Berkowitz added a further wrinkle from the literature: **thalamic hemorrhage** can produce "wrong-way eyes" — the opposite of the expected hemispheric pattern — a phenomenon originally described by **Miller Fisher**, though Berkowitz noted that a mentor who trained directly with Fisher corrected his own prior teaching that this represented brainstem-consistent deviation; the mechanism remains poorly understood, and neither Berkowitz nor Sridhara had personally observed a case. He also closed the loop on the post-ictal state: after a left-hemisphere seizure with right-sided gaze deviation, the post-ictal (Todd's) phase can produce a *left*-sided gaze deviation that mimics a new stroke — a reminder that an unwitnessed seizure with a post-ictal exam is always on the differential for an apparent new stroke.

Returning to the case, Berkowitz described the broader basilar-thrombosis illness script: patients can present with syncope-like loss of consciousness from ischemia to the reticular activating system or bilateral thalami, seizure-like posturing at onset, acute agitation or delirium, and — in the most severe, fortunately uncommon cases — **locked-in syndrome**.

Vanessa then gave the exam. Vital signs were markedly abnormal: heart rate 68, respiratory rate 38, blood pressure 76/49, oxygen saturation 80%, temperature 97.9°F. General exam was unremarkable (equal chest expansion, bilateral breath sounds, midline trachea, soft abdomen, palpable pulses, no hematoma). The neurologic exam was the crux of the case: the patient was **alert**, able to nod yes/no appropriately and mouth words, following simple commands from the neck up; pupils were 3mm, equal, round, and reactive; extraocular movements were full; there was no facial asymmetry — but she had **absent gag and absent cough reflexes**, and all four extremities were **0/5** with sensation preserved only to light touch. She was intubated.

Maria immediately used the normal blood pressure to argue against a Cushing-response/impending-herniation picture, and, seeing complete paralysis below the neck with all cranial nerves intact through the face, reasoned toward the basilar artery — she explicitly recognized this did not fit the usual asymmetric, face-involving pattern of an MCA or ACA stroke, nor could bilateral hemispheric involvement explain a patient who remained alert (bihemispheric strokes causing this degree of deficit would not spare consciousness). The preserved eye movements with absent speech led her to name the pattern: **locked-in syndrome**.

Berkowitz walked the group down the brainstem level by level using the exam alone: intact pupils and extraocular movements placed the midbrain (and higher) as intact; a normal face meant the pons (CN VII nucleus/fascicle) was also functioning; the **absent cough and gag reflexes** — CN IX and X, mediated by the nucleus ambiguus — localized specifically to the **medulla**; and the complete flaccid quadriplegia below that placed the causative lesion at or below the medulla. He noted that had the cough/gag reflex not been checked, one might have mistaken pure acute quadriplegia with neck pain for a high cervical spinal catastrophe (disc herniation, vertebral collapse, or — in an extreme historical example he recalled — traumatic atlanto-axial dislocation), but the bulbar findings confirmed the process extended into the brainstem itself, favoring a vertebrobasilar (vertebral-artery-territory) stroke or, as Sridhara reiterated, an anterior spinal artery infarct at the cervicomedullary junction.

CT head was negative for hemorrhage. CTA of the brain and neck showed an **age-indeterminate occlusion of the distal V3/V4 segment of the right vertebral artery**, with an otherwise unremarkable CTA. Given she was within the treatment window, she received IV thrombolysis (tenecteplase), though with an NIH Stroke Scale of only 2 at that specific moment, no additional endovascular intervention was pursued at that time. She was admitted to the neuro ICU for observation.

MRI brain showed acute infarctions in the posterior circulation involving the cervicomedullary junction and upper spinal cord. MRI cervical spine showed abnormal edema in the inferior right cerebellar hemisphere, the right ventral cervicomedullary junction, and the ventral upper cervical spinal cord extending to the mid-body of C2. Maria correctly attributed the ventral medullary/upper cord findings to the **anterior spinal artery**, and the cerebellar findings to **PICA**, both of which arise from the vertebral arteries — but she flagged the puzzle at the heart of the case: the imaging showed **bilateral** cerebellar involvement, which a single occluded right vertebral artery could not straightforwardly explain, since there is no retrograde pathway for a unilateral vertebral clot to reach the contralateral PICA territory.

Sridhara offered two possible explanations for bilateral PICA infarcts from unilateral vertebral disease: (1) some patients have an anatomic variant in which both PICAs arise from the same vertebral artery, or (2) a cardioembolic source could shower emboli into both posterior circulations independently. He also described a distinct mechanism — unilateral PICA infarct with swelling compressing the contralateral PICA territory via fourth-ventricle obstruction — but noted this typically evolves over time with rising intracranial pressure rather than being present at initial presentation, making it a less likely explanation here. Berkowitz agreed the bilaterality raised the pretest probability of a more proximal, likely cardiac, embolic source rather than the vertebral occlusion being fully causative — while noting that incidental, non-causative vertebral artery disease is not uncommon in stroke patients generally.

The group outlined the standard stroke-cause workup for a patient without traditional cerebrovascular risk factors: transthoracic echocardiogram, prolonged cardiac (Holter or implantable) monitoring for atrial fibrillation, and — particularly relevant in a "medically young" 64-year-old with no risk factors — an extended hypercoagulable workup (antiphospholipid antibodies, and, in the presence of a PFO, thrombophilia testing that otherwise mainly causes venous rather than arterial disease). Sridhara named TTE and AFib monitoring as his own first steps.

Vanessa revealed the final diagnosis: **acute ischemic stroke involving the right cerebellum, right thalamus, left cerebellum, medulla, and anterior spinal cord, secondary to a right vertebral artery thrombus, with a likely spinal shock syndrome.** No dissection was identified on imaging. The extended workup — TTE, cardiac monitoring, hypercoagulable panel, A1c, blood pressure, and cholesterol — was entirely unremarkable. Berkowitz named this endpoint explicitly: **embolic stroke of undetermined source (ESUS)**, and used it to discuss the modern DOAC-versus-aspirin trials in ESUS patients, which — despite the presumption that an occult atrial fibrillation or other embolic source must be present — have repeatedly failed to show a benefit of anticoagulation over aspirin, raising open questions about whether these patients truly have an undetected embolic mechanism, or whether even the very-low-burden atrial fibrillation sometimes eventually detected months later is clinically causal.

Vanessa closed with the follow-up: the patient required ventilatory support for approximately two months, but was ultimately weaned off, regained her motor drive fully, and at follow-up had full, symmetric movement and no breathing problems — a remarkable recovery from a presentation that, at its nadir, looked like a devastating, potentially permanent locked-in state.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

**Basilar artery (vertebrobasilar) thrombosis.** Can present with syncope-like loss of consciousness (ischemia to the reticular activating system or bilateral thalami), stimulus-triggered posturing/opisthotonus that mimics seizure, acute agitation or delirium, and — in the most severe cases — locked-in syndrome. Course can be biphasic or stuttering as partial recanalization and re-occlusion occur, as in this patient's initial transient episode followed by a sustained second event.

**Locked-in syndrome.** Preserved consciousness and (classically) vertical eye movements and blinking, with global paralysis of the limbs and lower cranial nerves below the level of the lesion. Must be actively distinguished from disorders of consciousness (coma, unresponsive wakefulness) because the patient is fully aware — a distinction this case made explicitly via intact command-following and eye movements despite complete quadriplegia and bulbar paralysis.

**Vertebral artery / PICA / anterior spinal artery anatomy.** The vertebral arteries give rise to both the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA — dorsolateral medulla and inferior cerebellum) and the anterior spinal artery (bilateral medial medulla and spinal cord). This dual supply explains how a single-vessel (unilateral vertebral) process can, in principle, cause a mix of unilateral (PICA) and bilateral (anterior spinal artery) findings — though true bilateral PICA involvement, as seen here, is unusual from unilateral vertebral disease alone and should raise suspicion for either a vascular variant (shared PICA origin) or a proximal embolic source.

**Anchoring bias — basilar thrombosis mistaken for seizure.** Stimulus-triggered posturing and opisthotonus from a basilar occlusion can closely mimic status epilepticus. A clinician who anchors on a seizure diagnosis in this setting — especially in a patient without a prior seizure history and an acute, focal-feeling presentation — risks delaying recognition of a time-critical vascular emergency.

**Reasoning tool — "rounding to the nearest thing."** When a presenting symptom does not map cleanly onto a familiar framework (episodic shortness of breath, in a neurology context), translate it into the nearest recognizable category the framework *does* cover (episodic neurologic symptoms generally) and reason from there (TIA vs. seizure vs. migraine), rather than getting stuck reasoning about the literal symptom in isolation.

**Diagnostic humility — embolic stroke of undetermined source.** Even with a comprehensive modern stroke-cause workup (echocardiography, prolonged rhythm monitoring, hypercoagulable and antiphospholipid testing, vascular risk factor assessment), no cause was identified. ESUS is explicitly framed not as a failure of the workup but as a recognized, common endpoint — and recent trial evidence has not shown a benefit of anticoagulation over aspirin in these patients, underscoring that neither the diagnosis nor the optimal treatment is always resolvable, even after due diligence.

## Teaching Discussion

This episode is built around **sequential, exam-driven localization of the brainstem and cervical spinal cord** — perhaps the purest demonstration in this series of using cranial nerve findings alone, from rostral to caudal, to localize a lesion before imaging confirms it: intact pupils and extraocular movements (midbrain), an intact face (pons), then the pivotal finding of **absent cough and gag reflexes** localizing to the medulla (nucleus ambiguus, CN IX/X), with quadriplegia below establishing the caudal extent. This is the same "neighborhood"-style localization logic used elsewhere in the series (e.g., the CN VI/CN VII neighborhood sign in [[CPS 199 - Diplopia and an Isolated Microvascular Sixth Nerve Palsy|CPS 199]]), applied here to bulbar reflexes rather than ocular motor findings.

The **horizontal gaze pathway** teaching in this episode is unusually thorough and worth treating as a standalone reference: hemispheric frontal eye fields drive gaze *contralaterally* when active, so a destructive hemispheric lesion (e.g., MCA stroke) causes gaze deviation *toward* the lesion, while a seizure focus causes deviation *away* from it. The brainstem's paramedian pontine reticular formation (PPRF) reverses this logic, driving gaze *ipsilaterally*, so a pontine lesion causes gaze deviation *away* from the lesion (toward the hemiparetic side) — the opposite pattern from a hemispheric stroke despite both causing contralateral weakness. Thalamic hemorrhage can produce "wrong-way eyes" that violate even this brainstem rule, a phenomenon first described by Miller Fisher and still not mechanistically well understood. Post-ictal (Todd's) gaze deviation can mimic a new contralateral stroke, rounding out the differential for any apparent acute gaze deviation.

The episode also reinforces a recurring theme across this series' vascular cases: **bilateral or otherwise anatomically incongruent infarcts should prompt a search for a proximal (often cardioembolic) source** rather than being explained away by a single, incidentally-discovered vessel occlusion — the same reasoning pattern (don't let one abnormal finding "explain" the whole picture if the anatomy doesn't actually fit) that appears in [[CPS 199 - Diplopia and an Isolated Microvascular Sixth Nerve Palsy|CPS 199]]'s use of Hickam's Dictum, though here applied to vascular anatomy rather than to two separate diagnoses.
