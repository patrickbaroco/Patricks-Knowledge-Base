## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A standard-format **Neurology VMR**, presented by Doug Pett with discussants Hannah Roberts and Dhruv "Drew" Srinivasachar, taught by **Aaron Berkowitz**. A 38-year-old man from Guatemala with three weeks of progressively worsening, now constant headache and two weeks of progressive binocular blurry vision — worse with Valsalva and when lying flat — is found to have bilateral papilledema with sluggish pupils, bilateral CN VI palsies, and left ptosis. CT shows obstructive-appearing ventriculomegaly with uncal herniation compressing the midbrain; further imaging confirms **communicating hydrocephalus**, and CSF shows a lymphocytic-and-eosinophilic pleocytosis. MRI reveals septated, grape-cluster cystic lesions in the basal cisterns with leptomeningeal enhancement — the "racemose" form of **neurocysticercosis**, confirmed by CSF and serum antibody testing and metagenomic sequencing. Treatment with antihelminthics, high-dose steroids, and CSF diversion (EVD, then VP shunt) resolves the papilledema and restores his vision.

## Pearls

- **A headache severe or persistent enough to prompt an ED visit is itself a red flag, even if described as "mild."** Framing a headache as low-grade because of its intensity can obscure the more important question of whether it's new, progressive, or associated with other red-flag features — "mild" is not the same as "benign."
- **The blurry-vision differential splits first into acuity loss versus diplopia**, and the test is simple: cover one eye. Diplopia resolves with one eye closed; true visual acuity loss (a problem with the eye, optic nerve, or visual pathway itself) does not — this patient's vision stayed blurry with either eye covered, pointing away from an isolated ocular-motor problem and toward a bilateral process affecting vision itself.
- **The cavernous sinus (OTOM CAT: CN III, IV, V1, V2, VI, and the carotid/sympathetics) cannot explain isolated visual acuity loss**, because CN II (the optic nerve) does not traverse it — a useful negative check whenever cavernous sinus syndrome is on the differential for a patient with more than just ocular-motor findings.
- **Headache worse with Valsalva, coughing, straining, or lying flat — "brain orthopnea" — signals elevated intracranial pressure**, the same mechanistic principle taught in earlier episodes, here from obstructed CSF outflow rather than a mass lesion.
- **Communicating hydrocephalus is distinguished from non-communicating (obstructive) hydrocephalus by whether CSF can still reach and exit the fourth ventricle**: an enlarged fourth ventricle with a patent aqueduct and transependymal flow implies the obstruction is downstream, at the level of CSF resorption (arachnoid granulations) rather than within the ventricular system itself.
- **CSF eosinophilia is a specific and often-overlooked clue**, narrowing the differential toward parasitic infection, fungal infection (especially *Coccidioides*), or a hypereosinophilic process — a finding easy to miss if the differential cell count isn't scrutinized beyond the total white count.
- **Neurocysticercosis is transmitted fecal-orally by ingesting *Taenia solium* eggs shed in an infected person's stool — not by eating undercooked pork.** Eating undercooked pork causes taeniasis (an intestinal tapeworm from ingesting larval cysts in meat), a different and far more benign condition; cysticercosis requires ingesting eggs, typically through contaminated food, water, or poor hand hygiene, meaning even a vegetarian in an endemic area is at risk.

## Problem Representation

A 38-year-old man from Guatemala (in the US for 10 years) with hypertension and chronic neck pain presents with three weeks of progressively worsening, now constant, dull-to-pulsating bilateral frontal headache — worse with Valsalva, coughing, and lying flat — and two weeks of progressive, binocular blurry vision, following an ED visit two months earlier for a milder headache that resolved with symptomatic treatment. Exam shows hypertension, bilateral 4+ papilledema with exudates and microhemorrhages, sluggish 5mm pupils bilaterally, bilateral CN VI palsies, and left ptosis, without other focal deficits. CT shows ventriculomegaly with transependymal edema and uncal herniation compressing the midbrain; further evaluation confirms communicating hydrocephalus from a basal meningeal process, and CSF shows a lymphocytic-and-eosinophilic pleocytosis with low protein and normal glucose. MRI shows septated, non-enhancing-to-mildly-enhancing cystic lesions in the basal cisterns with leptomeningeal enhancement, and CSF/serum antibody testing plus metagenomic sequencing confirm racemose neurocysticercosis, treated with antihelminthics, high-dose steroids, and CSF diversion.

## Case Narrative

This episode returns to the standard **Neurology VMR** format — introduced with the same [[Neurophobia]]-focused framing as [[CPS 146 - Right-Sided Weakness and Numbness and Medial Medullary Syndrome from a Vertebral Artery Dissection|CPS 146]] — presented by Doug Pett with discussants Hannah Roberts and Dhruv "Drew" Srinivasachar, taught by Aaron Berkowitz.

**Chief concern: headache and blurry vision.** Before the history, the discussion frames headache using the primary-versus-secondary split: primary headaches (migraine, tension, cluster) have no identifiable structural cause, while secondary headaches result from an underlying process — infection, hemorrhage, mass, elevated or reduced intracranial pressure — and warrant urgent evaluation. The discussants raise red-flag features (systemic symptoms, neurologic signs, onset patterns, older age, pattern change, positional or Valsalva-related triggers, prior similar headache history) as the practical screen for which headaches need workup, and Berkowitz works through the same substance without formally endorsing the mnemonic, emphasizing instead that a headache severe enough to prompt an ED visit is itself notable regardless of how "mild" it's described — an early instance of guarding against **[[Framing Bias|framing bias]]**, since labeling a headache as mild can anchor a clinician away from taking it seriously. For blurry vision, he establishes the same first branch point used for diplopia in other episodes but applied to vision loss broadly: is this acuity loss (a problem with the eye, optic nerve, or visual pathway) or diplopia (an ocular-motor problem)? Covering one eye is the test — diplopia resolves, acuity loss does not.

**History: a red flag in retrospect.** The patient is a 38-year-old man from Guatemala, in the US for 10 years, with hypertension on metoprolol, chronic neck pain, and two years of alcohol sobriety after heavy prior use, unemployed and previously working in a restaurant, with no tobacco or drug use and no family history of headache. Two months earlier he'd been seen in the ED for a milder, intermittent headache with nausea and vomiting, treated with IV NSAIDs and fluids, and discharged after a reportedly normal screening neurologic exam without further workup — a visit Berkowitz flags in retrospect as the missed opportunity: an ED visit for headache is itself a red flag worth a more structured evaluation, independent of how the pain is characterized. He now returns with three weeks of progressively worsening, now constant, dull-to-pulsating bilateral forehead headache, and two weeks of progressive blurry vision — initially just difficulty reading, now frank blurry vision in both eyes that does not resolve with either eye closed. The headache is worse with Valsalva, coughing, straining, and lying flat (though it doesn't wake him from sleep) — the same **"brain orthopnea"** pattern taught in earlier episodes, here explicitly tied to elevated intracranial pressure. He denies diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, weakness, and vertigo, and is afebrile with stable weight and no systemic infectious symptoms, arguing at this stage against an acute infectious or inflammatory process and toward a more indolent one.

**Exam: papilledema and a compressive-appearing cranial neuropathy pattern.** Vitals show hypertension (156/96) with a normal heart rate, he's afebrile, and BMI is 36; the neck is supple with a negative jolt test (arguing against acute meningitis). Visual acuity is markedly reduced (20/70 right, 20/200 left), and fundoscopy shows 4+ papilledema bilaterally with exudates and microhemorrhages — bilateral optic nerve swelling from elevated intracranial pressure, explaining the acuity loss without a need to invoke diplopia. Pupils are 5mm and sluggish bilaterally, there's bilateral limited abduction (bilateral CN VI palsies), and left ptosis; the remainder of the cranial nerve, motor, sensory, reflex, coordination, and gait exam is normal. Berkowitz first checks whether a single cavernous sinus lesion could explain everything via the OTOM CAT framework (CN III, IV, V1, V2, VI, plus the carotid and sympathetic fibers), but rules this out as a complete explanation because CN II — responsible for the visual acuity loss — never passes through the cavernous sinus; whatever is happening here must be affecting the optic nerves as well, favoring a more diffuse process over an isolated cavernous sinus lesion. The bilateral, symmetric papilledema plus bilateral CN VI palsies (CN VI's long subarachnoid course over the clivus makes it a classic false-localizing sign of elevated intracranial pressure alone) together point strongly toward a diffuse process raising intracranial pressure, with the sluggish pupils and left ptosis suggesting early midbrain compression from herniation.

**Imaging: obstructive-appearing ventriculomegaly, then a second look.** Non-contrast CT shows dilation of both lateral ventricles and the temporal horns, transependymal edema (CSF forced across the ependymal lining under pressure), and uncal herniation compressing the midbrain — explaining the sluggish pupils and ptosis on exam. Initially read as suggesting an obstructive process, the scan is re-examined after further imaging prompts a second look, revealing a subtle abnormality in the basal cisterns displacing the midbrain laterally — missed on first pass, a reminder Berkowitz makes explicit about revisiting prior imaging once new information reframes what to look for. Further imaging detail — an enlarged fourth ventricle with a patent cerebral aqueduct and ongoing transependymal flow — confirms **acute communicating hydrocephalus**: the obstruction is not within the ventricular system but downstream, at the level of CSF resorption or outflow, implicating a basal meningeal process rather than a discrete mass blocking the aqueduct.

**Differential for communicating hydrocephalus from a meningeal process.** Berkowitz builds the differential around the pachymeninges (dura) versus leptomeninges (arachnoid and pia) distinction, framing communicating hydrocephalus with basal cistern involvement as pointing to a subacute-to-chronic basal meningitis. Infectious causes include fungal disease (especially *Cryptococcus*, including *C. gattii* in immunocompetent hosts), tuberculosis, and parasitic infection; non-infectious, inflammatory causes include pachymeningitis from rheumatoid arthritis, sarcoidosis, IgG4-related disease, Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada syndrome, and Behçet's disease — an explicit broadening of "meningitis" beyond infection to include inflammatory disease of the meninges. An external ventricular drain (EVD) is placed both to relieve pressure and to sample CSF: opening pressure is markedly elevated (25 cm H2O), CSF protein is low (22), glucose is normal relative to a paired serum glucose of 100 (CSF 55), red cells are minimal (3), and white cells are elevated at 33, with a differential of 68% lymphocytes and a notable **5% eosinophils**. The eosinophilia specifically raises parasitic infection, fungal infection (again *Coccidioides*), and hypereosinophilic syndromes; bacterial cultures are negative, arguing against a typical bacterial meningitis. Berkowitz briefly considers the rare, rapidly fatal free-living amebic meningoencephalitides (*Naegleria*, *Balamuthia*, *Acanthamoeba*) and *Angiostrongylus* (rat lungworm), but sets them aside given this patient's comparatively indolent, weeks-long course rather than the fulminant presentation those organisms typically produce.

**MRI: racemose neurocysticercosis.** MRI shows septated, grape-cluster-like cystic lesions in the basal cisterns with mild enhancement, plus a peripherally enhancing lesion near the right gyrus rectus and optic nerve with associated leptomeningeal enhancement. Berkowitz identifies this as a later-stage — colloidal, granular, or nodular — presentation of **racemose neurocysticercosis**, the subarachnoid form of the disease in which cysts proliferate in clusters within the CSF spaces rather than forming the single, well-demarcated parenchymal cyst with a visible scolex that produces the more commonly taught "slam dunk" vesicular-stage imaging appearance.

**Confirmatory workup and the transmission teaching point.** Testing returns negative for cryptococcal antigen, *Coccidioides* serology and antigen, other fungal markers, QuantiFERON-TB, and CSF ACE (arguing against sarcoidosis), while CSF cysticercosis antibody and serum cysticercosis IgG are both positive, and metagenomic next-generation sequencing of the CSF — the same agnostic, hypothesis-free sequencing approach pioneered by Michael Wilson's group and published in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, referenced previously for its diagnostic yield in unexplained meningoencephalitis — detects *Taenia solium*. Berkowitz uses the result to correct a common misconception: cysticercosis is acquired by ingesting *T. solium* eggs shed in the stool of a person carrying the adult tapeworm, transmitted via contaminated food, water, or poor hand hygiene (including person-to-person spread or self-inoculation), not by eating undercooked pork — eating larval cysts in undercooked pork causes taeniasis, an intestinal tapeworm infection that is a different and far less consequential condition. Guatemala, where the patient emigrated from, is an endemic region for the disease.

**Treatment and outcome.** Management combines antihelminthic therapy (dual agents, per standard neurocysticercosis regimens) with high-dose corticosteroids, the steroids intended to blunt the inflammatory response provoked as the antihelminthics kill the organisms — a reaction that can transiently worsen edema and mass effect if unopposed. The acute hydrocephalus is managed with the EVD already in place, and because racemose (subarachnoid) cysts require a longer course of antiparasitic therapy than parenchymal cysts, a permanent VP shunt is eventually placed rather than relying on temporary drainage. At follow-up, the patient is headache-free, his papilledema has markedly improved, and his vision has recovered to 20/30 bilaterally.

**Closing teaching points.** Maria's closing synthesis reiterates the primary-versus-secondary headache framework and red-flag screening; the cavernous sinus cranial nerve list (III, IV, V1, V2, VI) and why it cannot explain isolated CN II findings; the Cushing reflex (hypertension, bradycardia, and irregular respirations as a triad of critically elevated intracranial pressure); the mechanism of papilledema; the **[[Localization × Time Course|E=mc² time-course framework]]**'s subacute bucket for this presentation; **idiopathic intracranial hypertension (pseudotumor cerebri)** as an illness script to distinguish from this case — including the classic associations with obesity, young women, and exposures like hypervitaminosis A, tetracyclines, and retinoids; venous sinus thrombosis as a commonly missed cause of elevated intracranial pressure that should be actively excluded; the communicating-versus-non-communicating hydrocephalus distinction; a broadened definition of meningitis spanning both infectious and inflammatory causes; the diagnostic value of scrutinizing CSF eosinophils for fungal, parasitic, and neurosarcoid disease; and, finally, the corrected neurocysticercosis transmission mechanism tied back to the patient's history in an endemic country.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **A "mild" headache that prompts an ED visit is a red flag, not reassurance.** Naming this explicitly is a direct guard against **[[Framing Bias|framing bias]]** — the intensity descriptor a patient or prior clinician attaches to a headache shouldn't substitute for a structured red-flag assessment.
- **"Predict before you look," reused again.** As in the prior five episodes, Berkowitz reasons toward the CT and MRI findings — obstructive-appearing ventriculomegaly, then a basal cisternal process — before they're revealed, and revisits the initial CT once new information reframes what to look for, an explicit habit of returning to prior data rather than anchoring on the first read.
- **A negative finding actively used to rule out, not just deprioritize.** CN II's absence from the cavernous sinus is used as a hard anatomic argument against a unifying single cavernous sinus lesion, the same disciplined use of anatomy to exclude, rather than merely disfavor, a tempting unifying diagnosis seen throughout this vault.
- **Scrutinizing the CSF differential, not just the total count.** The 5% eosinophils would be easy to overlook within an otherwise unremarkable lymphocyte-predominant pleocytosis, but is treated as a specific, high-value data point that meaningfully narrows the differential — a reminder that aggregate lab values can hide their most informative component.
- **A geographic and epidemiologic detail held until it's earned, not front-loaded.** The patient's origin in an endemic country is present from the outset but isn't allowed to shortcut the workup — the full differential (fungal, tubercular, inflammatory, and parasitic) is built and systematically narrowed before the diagnosis is confirmed serologically and genomically.
- **Correcting a common misconception explicitly, not just stating the correct mechanism.** Berkowitz doesn't just teach how neurocysticercosis is transmitted — he names and corrects the specific wrong belief (undercooked pork) that most learners hold, a teaching pattern aimed at overwriting an entrenched illness script rather than simply adding a new one.

## Teaching Discussion

- Use the primary-versus-secondary headache framework and a structured red-flag screen for every headache presentation, and treat an ED visit for headache as itself notable regardless of how the pain is characterized.
- Split blurry vision first into acuity loss versus diplopia by covering one eye — diplopia resolves, true acuity loss does not — before building a localization differential.
- Remember the cavernous sinus cranial nerve list (III, IV, V1, V2, VI, plus carotid/sympathetic fibers) via OTOM CAT, and that CN II never traverses it — isolated visual acuity loss argues against an isolated cavernous sinus lesion.
- Recognize "brain orthopnea" — headache worse with Valsalva, coughing, straining, or lying flat — as a marker of elevated intracranial pressure.
- Distinguish communicating from non-communicating hydrocephalus by whether the fourth ventricle is enlarged with a patent aqueduct and transependymal flow (communicating, obstruction downstream at CSF resorption) versus a discrete obstructing lesion within the ventricular system (non-communicating).
- Broaden the differential for basal/communicating-hydrocephalus-associated meningitis beyond infection to include inflammatory pachymeningitis (rheumatoid arthritis, sarcoidosis, IgG4-related disease, Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada, Behçet's), and distinguish pachymeningeal (dural) from leptomeningeal (arachnoid/pial) processes.
- Scrutinize the CSF differential for eosinophils specifically — a clue toward parasitic infection, fungal infection (especially *Coccidioides*), or hypereosinophilic syndromes that's easy to miss within an otherwise unremarkable cell count.
- Recognize racemose (subarachnoid, grape-cluster) neurocysticercosis as a later-stage imaging pattern distinct from the classic single vesicular cyst with a visible scolex, and know it requires longer antiparasitic therapy and often permanent CSF diversion.
- Correct the common misconception that neurocysticercosis comes from eating undercooked pork — cysticercosis is fecal-orally transmitted by ingesting *T. solium* eggs, while eating larval cysts in pork causes the separate, more benign condition of taeniasis.
