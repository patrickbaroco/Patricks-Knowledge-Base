## Pearls

- Before pursuing a double-vision workup, distinguish true diplopia (double vision from ocular misalignment) from blurred vision (a refractive/optical problem) — the fastest bedside discriminator is asking the patient to alternately cover each eye and report what happens to the image.
- If diplopia resolves when either eye is covered, that is binocular diplopia, implicating a problem of ocular alignment somewhere along the pathway of cranial nerves III/IV/VI, brainstem nuclei, the neuromuscular junction, or the extraocular muscles themselves.
- If diplopia persists in each eye individually even with the other eye covered (bilateral monocular diplopia), this is rare and effectively excludes an ocular-alignment problem — a single, well-aligned eye should see one image — redirecting localization toward the visual sensory pathway itself, or toward a psychogenic process.
- Lesions along the optic pathway proximal to the cortex (optic nerve, chiasm, radiations) classically cause partial visual field loss (e.g., bitemporal hemianopia, a missing quadrant), not true doubling of an image — a key discriminator once ocular misalignment has been excluded, pointing localization toward the occipital visual cortex when frank diplopia (rather than field loss) is the complaint.
- For a suspected deep foot infection, plain X-ray sensitivity is reported as over 90% — comparable to CT — because the foot has comparatively little soft tissue, unlike the thigh or proximal leg, where deep infection can hide far more easily and cross-sectional imaging becomes necessary.
- Bounding, easily palpable pulses meaningfully lower the probability that macrovascular ischemia explains an acute foot presentation, but do not exclude ischemia altogether — isolated microvascular disease can still coexist with entirely normal palpable pulses.
- Patients with severe peripheral neuropathy can still experience severe pain from an acute foot injury despite blunted baseline sensation, and should be treated aggressively with titrated IV opioids regardless of the underlying neuropathy — the same neuropathy that blunts pain perception also predisposes to unnoticed repetitive trauma and secondary infection.
- When a strongly suspected inflammatory or infectious process returns with a uniformly unremarkable inflammatory panel (normal WBC and differential, low ESR/CRP, normal renal function across multiple concordant markers), resist anchoring on immune incompetence as the default explanation and seriously reconsider whether the process is inflammatory at all.
- Gravity-dependent color change is a powerful, low-cost bedside test: skin color that reverses completely with limb elevation and returns with dependency — without any corresponding change in pain — points toward blood pooled within (rather than leaked out of) blood vessels; true inflammatory erythema, driven by extravasated fluid and neutrophils in the interstitium, does not resolve with elevation.
- Acute Charcot (neuro)arthropathy is pathophysiologically the same process as complex regional pain syndrome — the distinguishing feature is that Charcot occurs specifically in a patient with pre-existing peripheral neuropathy (roughly 70% diabetic, but any neuropathy can be causative) after minor trauma, producing autonomic dysregulation-driven pathologic vasodilation without vascular leak, which is why its erythema is fully reversible with elevation.
- Peripheral nerves are trophic to bone, not only to muscle; loss of autonomic/neuronal input leaves bone vulnerable to fracture, poor healing, and dislocation from even minor trauma — a mechanism worth remembering any time a neuropathic patient's imaging looks dramatically worse than their reported injury.
- Acute Charcot arthropathy can be radiographically silent early in its course — a normal X-ray days into symptoms does not exclude it — and MRI can detect bone marrow edema before X-ray becomes abnormal; earlier diagnosis matters because no treatment reverses the process itself, only strict non-weight-bearing prevents further fracture and dislocation.

## Case Narrative

Rabih opened by telling Reza he had two remarkable cases that arrived within an hour of each other, and that he intended to present them differently than usual: he would take Reza and the audience to the "tension point" of the first case and stop there, inviting reflection before resolving it in a future episode, then move on to the second case in full.

**Case 1.** A woman presented at 10:45 a.m. reporting, "I'm seeing double." Reza's first move was to confirm this was genuinely diplopia rather than blurred vision, since the two have entirely different workups — blurred vision starts with a refractive/optical evaluation, while diplopia implies a misalignment of one or both eyes. The standard test is to have the patient cover one eye, then the other, and see whether the doubling resolves; if it does with either eye covered, that confirms binocular diplopia (ocular misalignment). Rabih relayed exactly this exam: he held up a finger, and the patient reported seeing two clear copies of it, one directly in line and one offset. When he asked her to cover her right eye, the doubling persisted; when she covered her left eye instead, the doubling still persisted.

Reza recognized immediately how unusual this was. If doubling persists in each eye individually, each eye — now perfectly aligned to the target with no competing eye to conflict with — should logically see a single image, yet doesn't. This effectively takes the entire ocular motor pathway out of consideration: an intact cortex, brainstem nuclei, cranial nerves III/IV/VI, neuromuscular junction, and extraocular muscles are all implicated in aligning the eyes, and none of them can explain doubling that persists with each eye tested in isolation. Reza reasoned that the pathology must instead sit somewhere along the visual sensory pathway — retina, optic nerve, optic chiasm, lateral geniculate nucleus, the optic radiations, or the visual cortex — but noted that lesions at most of those sites classically produce partial visual field loss (a blind spot, a hemianopia, a missing quadrant), not true doubling of an image. The one location he could reconcile with frank diplopia in a well-aligned eye was the occipital visual cortex, alongside the alternative possibility of a psychogenic process. He recommended a thorough ophthalmologic exam and an MRI focused on the visual cortex.

Rabih confirmed this was exactly the right direction, but stopped there deliberately, telling Reza and the audience to sit with the tension point and do some reading before the case's resolution in a future episode — explicitly signaling that "round two" of this case (its second half) would come later. *(Update: this portion of the case is resolved in RLR 113 — a right occipital stroke, ultimately traced to a left atrial myxoma — rather than in the immediately following episode, RLR 112, which covers unrelated cases.)*

**Case 2.** Roughly thirty minutes after handing that first patient off for consultant guidance, Rabih was approached in person by the VA's podiatry team — a group he described as unusually accessible, with an office one door down from the emergency department — who told him they had a patient, Mr. B, who likely needed to go to the OR for a severe foot infection, and asked Rabih to obtain labs and an X-ray while they got set up.

The patient arrived in a motorized scooter, visibly tremulous with pain, and reported that he had stubbed his foot a week earlier, after which the nail of his second toe fell off; since then, the foot had become excruciatingly painful, swollen, and red. Vital signs were normal. On exam, Rabih specifically checked pulses — a habit he attributed directly to his recent experience with a prior isolated-microvascular-ischemia case — and found them bounding, essentially ruling out gross macrovascular compromise. The foot was diffusely swollen and red from just distal to the ankle, without any skin ulceration, though the patient had chronic toenail fungal changes.

Reza, working from this description alone, reasoned that a breach in the skin's barrier function (the stubbed, denuded toe) created an entry point for bacterial infection, and that the redness and swelling were consistent with this. He recommended imaging to exclude a fracture, a low threshold for contrast-enhanced CT regardless of renal function given his concern for a deep, rapidly progressing infection, empiric antibiotics covering Staph and Strep (plus Pseudomonas if the patient were diabetic), and basic inflammatory labs.

Rabih then filled in background: the patient had severe, longstanding (roughly ten-year), non-progressive, idiopathic peripheral neuropathy, suspected to be hereditary (possibly Charcot-Marie-Tooth), without access to specialty care given where he lived; he was not diabetic. Rabih also relayed a teaching point from podiatry: because the foot has comparatively little soft tissue compared with the thigh or proximal leg, plain X-ray sensitivity for invasive foot infection (including necrotizing fasciitis) is quoted above 90%, making it nearly as useful as CT specifically in this anatomic location; podiatry felt ischemia was not a major concern given the bounding pulses and recommended basic labs and an X-ray ahead of a planned operative debridement.

On the subject of pain control, Reza recommended treating the severe pain aggressively with titrated IV opioids (starting around 0.2-0.4mg IV hydromorphone, adjusted by body habitus), drawing a parallel to a young man with Charcot-Marie-Tooth-related neuropathy he had encountered in residency who developed recurrent toe infections and eventual osteomyelitis from unnoticed repetitive trauma — and to his experience managing severe sickle cell pain crises with patient-controlled analgesia in Baltimore. Rabih had already given the department's standard 0.5mg IV hydromorphone order and empiric vancomycin plus meropenem, acknowledging in retrospect that he probably should have added specific Pseudomonas coverage.

Reza was then asked to predict the patient's labs before seeing them. Given the patient's age (75) and the apparent severity of the presentation, he predicted a white count above 15,000 (possibly 20,000) with a neutrophil-predominant differential, an ESR above 100, an elevated CRP, and likely acute kidney injury from the severe pain and inflammatory state.

The actual results were the opposite of every prediction: ESR 3, CRP low, white count 5 with a normal differential, normal platelets, and normal renal function — essentially no biochemical evidence of inflammation whatsoever. Reza, comparing the moment to resetting after a bad golf shot, discarded his prior framing and reasoned through the new data fresh. He considered two possibilities: either the patient simply could not mount an inflammatory response (judged unlikely, given no history of immunocompromise and the striking concordance of multiple independent normal markers), or the process was not actually inflammatory at all. He did not stop antibiotics outright, but reframed the working problem list away from "cellulitis, possibly complicated" toward "red, swollen foot in a patient with peripheral neuropathy," widening the differential to non-inflammatory causes: he specifically recalled a residency case of a patient with recurrent presumed cellulitis, treated through multiple oral antibiotic courses, who was ultimately found on MRI to have Charcot arthropathy from accumulated diabetic microfractures. He proposed a fracture workup (CT, with MRI potentially superior for detecting the bone marrow changes of early Charcot), noted that a unilateral, foot-confined swelling made DVT very unlikely, and even briefly entertained (and largely dismissed) angioedema given the atypical, gravity-independent-seeming location.

At this point Rabih introduced the case's pivotal data point: a photograph of the foot hanging in a gravity-dependent position, deeply red, followed four minutes later by a second photograph of the same foot elevated, now completely white with no residual redness — while the patient's excruciating pain was unchanged in both positions. Reza reasoned through the physiology live: gravity assists arterial inflow to a dependent limb while opposing venous return, so color that reverses fully with elevation suggested some form of venous congestion resolving as blood was assisted back out of the limb — though he explicitly contrasted this with a prior patient of his own in Baltimore whose limb had shown the opposite pattern (worsening pallor and pain with elevation, consistent with severe arterial disease), underscoring that the direction of the gravity-dependent change is the key diagnostic signal, and candidly noted his uncertainty about which mechanism fit this case.

Rabih then revealed the imaging: on X-ray, every joint of the midfoot — the entire Lisfranc joint complex, from the first through the fifth tarsometatarsal joints — was dislocated, a finding wildly disproportionate to the reported trigger of stubbing a toe. This confirmed acute Charcot (neuro)arthropathy.

Rabih explained the underlying physiology in detail. Acute Charcot arthropathy is mechanistically identical to complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) — both involve pathologic vasodilation and bone changes following trauma — but Charcot specifically describes this process occurring in a patient with pre-existing peripheral neuropathy (roughly 70% of cases diabetic, though any neuropathy can be causative), whereas CRPS classically occurs without underlying neuropathy. The mechanism is autonomic dysregulation causing pathologic capillary vasodilation without vascular leak: blood remains contained within dilated vessels rather than extravasating into the interstitium, which is precisely why elevating the limb can make the redness vanish almost immediately — unlike true inflammatory erythema (as in cellulitis), which is fixed in place by neutrophils and fluid already present in the tissue and does not resolve with position change. He extended the teaching to bone physiology broadly: peripheral nerves are trophic not only to muscle but to bone, and loss of neuronal/autonomic input leaves bone fragile, poorly able to heal, and prone to fracture and dislocation from minimal trauma — a phenomenon he described as a "neurological catastrophe" or "stroke" of the foot. He noted that ideally Charcot is diagnosed even earlier via MRI, which can detect bone marrow edema before X-ray changes appear; this patient's X-ray at an outside hospital, obtained three days into his symptoms, had initially been normal. Rabih credited his own recognition of the pattern partly to reflecting extensively on a recent personal miss involving ischemia, which had made him newly attentive to foot pathology broadly, and credited a podiatry resident who taught him much of the underlying physiology informally in the ER hallway. Management for acute Charcot is entirely supportive: there is no treatment that reverses the process itself, so the focus is strict non-weight-bearing (a specialized boot, continued reliance on the patient's motorized scooter) to prevent further fracture and dislocation while the acute phase runs its course.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

**Case 1: Diplopia (unresolved — Part 1)**

| Finding | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| Patient reports seeing double, not blurred, when fixating on a target | Favors true diplopia (ocular misalignment) over a refractive/blurred-vision problem |
| Diplopia persists when the right eye alone is covered | Argues against a purely binocular-alignment problem localized to that eye |
| Diplopia persists when the left eye alone is covered | Confirms bilateral monocular diplopia — excludes ocular misalignment as the mechanism entirely |
| Diplopia present in each eye individually | Redirects localization to the visual sensory pathway; true doubling (not field loss) favors the occipital visual cortex, or a psychogenic process |

| Finding | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| Minor toe trauma, denuded nail, non-diabetic severe peripheral neuropathy | Raises concern for infection through a skin breach; neuropathy raises baseline suspicion for occult foot pathology |
| Bounding, easily palpable pulses | Lowers probability of significant macrovascular ischemia (does not exclude microvascular disease) |
| Normal WBC/differential, ESR 3, low CRP, normal renal function | Strongly argues against an active inflammatory/infectious process despite the alarming exam |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Acute Charcot arthropathy illness script: a patient with pre-existing peripheral neuropathy (diabetic or otherwise) develops gravity-dependent erythema and swelling after minor, sometimes unnoticed, trauma, with a normal or unremarkable inflammatory panel, and X-ray (or earlier MRI) showing microfractures or dislocation grossly disproportionate to the inciting injury — mechanistically identical to complex regional pain syndrome but distinguished by the presence of underlying neuropathy.

The episode is an explicit, narrated example of anti-anchoring discipline: Reza's "wipe the slate clean" framing (borrowed from golf) describes deliberately discarding a confidently predicted hypothesis once new data flatly contradicts it, rather than trying to rationalize the prediction. Rabih's early consideration of Charcot is also an explicit example of productive availability bias — a recent personal miss involving ischemia primed him to scrutinize this foot more carefully than he otherwise might have, illustrating how recency can sharpen rather than distort pattern recognition when consciously acknowledged.

## Teaching Discussion

The central teaching point of Case 2 is the physiologic distinction between color change from vasodilation-with-pooling (fully reversible with limb position) and color change from true inflammatory extravasation (fixed regardless of position) — the same gravity-dependent logic Reza had previously applied to a case of severe arterial ischemia (where elevation worsened pallor and pain) is redeployed here in the opposite direction, underscoring that the *direction* of a gravity-dependent color change, not just its presence, is the discriminating signal.

A second teaching point is the extension of neuropathy's consequences beyond muscle to bone: nerves are trophic to bone, and their loss leaves bone fragile and prone to catastrophic fracture/dislocation from trivial trauma, reframing Charcot arthropathy as a direct downstream consequence of denervation rather than a purely mechanical or inflammatory process.

A third, structural teaching point is the episode's deliberate cliffhanger format for Case 1: Rabih explicitly walks the audience to a specific anatomical "tension point" (bilateral monocular diplopia excluding ocular misalignment) and stops, modeling how to reason confidently up to the edge of one's current knowledge before resolving the case in a subsequent episode. **This case is resolved in RLR 113** (a right occipital stroke from a left atrial myxoma) rather than in RLR 112, consistent with how the RLR 054/RLR 056 cliffhanger pair was previously linked.
