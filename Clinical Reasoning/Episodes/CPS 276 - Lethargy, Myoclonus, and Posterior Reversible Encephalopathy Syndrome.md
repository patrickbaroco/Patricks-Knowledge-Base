The exam is non-focal (an **[[Encephalopathy|encephalopathy]]**), but the **blood pressure is 211/114** with hypoxia and flash pulmonary edema — and that number is the case. The discussant **Kiara** flags the hypertensive emergency and a possible [[Cushing Reflex|Cushing]]/herniation picture; **Maria** names **[[Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome|PRES]]**, the radiologic syndrome of failed cerebral autoregulation seen with hypertension, chemotherapy, and eclampsia. ## Pearls

- **Characterize the movement before you localize it.** In [[Movement Disorder Approach|movement disorders]], the key first step is watching the movement (especially while the patient is distracted) and naming it — hyperkinetic (tremor, chorea, dystonia, **[[Myoclonus|myoclonus]]**, tic) vs hypokinetic (parkinsonism) — because each movement carries its own differential. Myoclonus is a jerk so fast you can't imitate it, moving a muscle across a joint, often irregular; distinguish it from **[[Clonus|clonus]]** (rhythmic, from severe hyperreflexia), **[[Fasciculation|fasciculations]]** (fibers twitch without moving the joint — LMN/ALS), and — crucially here — **[[Myoclonic Seizure|myoclonic seizure]]**.
- **[[Myoclonus|Myoclonus]] has a four-column differential, and in the hospital it's usually toxic-metabolic.** *Primary neurologic* (juvenile myoclonic and other epilepsies, Lafora, dystonia-myoclonus, [[Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease|CJD]] and other dementias); *structural* (cortical/subcortical/brainstem/spinal generators); *systemic* (post-hypoxic/post-arrest, **renal failure**, other metabolic); *drugs* (opioids — paradoxically — and SSRIs). Physiologic hypnic jerks (hypnagogic/hypnopompic) are the everyday version.
- **In a cancer patient, "the first ten things on the differential are the cancer."** Work the **[[Cancer and the Nervous System|three mechanisms]]** — direct/metastatic, treatment-related, and [[Paraneoplastic Neurologic Syndrome|paraneoplastic]] — plus immunosuppression, and search diligently before blaming anything else. Here **[[Oxaliplatin Neuropathy|oxaliplatin]]** causes a cold-triggered sensory neuropathy (not myoclonus or lethargy), and [[Opsoclonus-Myoclonus Syndrome|opsoclonus-myoclonus]] (neuroblastoma in children; anti-Ri, breast/thymoma in adults) doesn't fit colon cancer — yet the answer was still treatment-related.
- **Don't skim the vitals — sometimes the vital sign *is* the diagnosis.** A non-focal [[Encephalopathy|encephalopathy]] with a blood pressure of **211/114** points at **[[Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome|PRES]]** — the radiologic syndrome of failed cerebral autoregulation and vasogenic edema seen with hypertensive emergency, eclampsia, calcineurin inhibitors, and (combination) chemotherapy. It presents with headache, altered mental status, seizures, and cortical visual symptoms; the name is a triple misnomer (not always *posterior*, *reversible*, or an *encephalopathy*), it overlaps **[[Reversible Cerebral Vasoconstriction Syndrome|RCVS]]**, and treatment is to lower the blood pressure (and magnesium in eclampsia).
- **PRES is a radiologic diagnosis — read the MRI carefully.** Classic imaging is posterior-predominant white-matter (FLAIR/T2) vasogenic edema in parieto-occipital/watershed regions, but it can extend anteriorly, into deep gray, brainstem, and even cord. It is usually reversible, but severe cases develop hemorrhage or infarction with **diffusion restriction** (as in this atypical case) and won't reverse. Beware "imaging-negative PRES" — if the MRI is normal, call it hypertensive encephalopathy and treat identically rather than forcing the label.
- **In altered mental status, a lumbar puncture is usually a rule-*out*, not a rule-in.** When the story sounds like a UTI or another systemic driver, the LP rarely surprises you with meningitis — image first when ICP is a concern, and reserve a lower threshold for the immunocompromised (who may not mount fever or classic imaging) or when there is truly no alternative source. "If you've discussed the LP this much, just do it" — but expect to confirm your gut, not overturn it.

## Problem Representation

A 39-year-old man with essential hypertension, type 2 diabetes, stage 2 chronic kidney disease, and newly diagnosed metastatic colorectal cancer presents two days after his first dose of FOLFOX with progressive fatigue evolving into disorientation, lethargy, and bilateral upper-extremity myoclonic jerking, without fever or focal complaints. He is somnolent and non-focal on exam (a global encephalopathy) but markedly hypertensive at 211/114 with hypoxia, increased work of breathing, and fine bibasilar crackles. Labs show acute-on-chronic kidney injury (creatinine 3.7 from a baseline of 2.1), hyperuricemia and hyperphosphatemia consistent with tumor lysis, and a bland CSF; brain MRI shows a diffuse, diffusion-positive cerebral pattern read as atypical posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome, and EEG shows rhythmic hemispheric delta with subclinical seizure activity. The unifying diagnosis is chemotherapy- and hypertension-precipitated PRES with myoclonic seizures, accompanied by tumor lysis syndrome and flash pulmonary edema requiring temporary renal replacement therapy, treated with valproate and intravenous antihypertensives.

## Case Narrative

A monthly CP Solvers Neurology VMR with **Aaron Berkowitz** teaching, **Yasmin** presenting, **Kiara** and **Maria** discussing, and **Laura** on teaching points. The chief concern: **lethargy and myoclonic jerking**.

**Characterizing the movement.** Berkowitz reframes the usual localization reflex: in **[[Movement Disorder Approach|movement disorders]]**, the first task is not *where* but *what* — watch the movement (ideally while the patient is distracted by giving history) and classify it as **hyperkinetic** (tremor, chorea, dystonia, [[Myoclonus|myoclonus]], tic, automatisms) or **hypokinetic** (parkinsonism). He has Maria define **myoclonus** — a jerk so rapid you couldn't imitate it, moving a muscle across a joint, often irregular — and carefully separates it from its mimics: **[[Clonus|clonus]]** (rhythmic, from severe hyperreflexia on ankle dorsiflexion), **[[Fasciculation|fasciculations]]** (single fascicles twitching without moving the joint, as in LMN disease/ALS or the benign post-texting thumb twitch), and polyminimyoclonus (which can masquerade as tremor). Each movement carries its own **four-column differential** — primary neurologic, structural, systemic/toxic-metabolic, and drug/toxin — and for myoclonus he loads them: **primary** (juvenile myoclonic and other myoclonic epilepsies, Lafora body, dystonia-myoclonus, [[Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease|CJD]] and other dementias); **structural** (cortical/subcortical/brainstem/spinal generators); **systemic** (post-arrest/hypoxic-ischemic, **renal failure**, toxic-metabolic — the most common in hospital); and **drugs** (opioids, SSRIs). He splits the **lethargy** the same way — sleepiness (narcolepsy; the physiologic hypnic jerks of dozing) versus a depressed level of consciousness — and offers the "thinking-fast" pairing of altered mental status *plus* myoclonus: [[Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease|CJD]], reassuringly rare, alongside the far more likely renal/hepatic/toxic causes.

**The cancer lens.** Yasmin reveals the pivotal history: newly diagnosed **metastatic colorectal (cecal) cancer** with liver metastases, and a **first dose of FOLFOX two days earlier**, after which fatigue progressed to disorientation and bilateral upper-limb myoclonic jerking. Past history adds essential hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and **stage 2 CKD (baseline creatinine 2.1)**; medications include hydralazine, carvedilol, and amlodipine. Maria and Berkowitz apply the **[[Cancer and the Nervous System|cancer-and-the-nervous-system]] rule** — the mentor's line that "the first ten things on the differential *are* the cancer" — across three mechanisms: **direct/metastatic** (hard to site a colon met that produces myoclonus + lethargy), **treatment-related** (FOLFOX = 5-FU + **oxaliplatin** + leucovorin; [[Oxaliplatin Neuropathy|oxaliplatin]] causes a cold-triggered sensory neuropathy, not this), and **[[Paraneoplastic Neurologic Syndrome|paraneoplastic]]** ([[Opsoclonus-Myoclonus Syndrome|opsoclonus-myoclonus]] — neuroblastoma in children, anti-Ri with breast/thymoma in adults — but colon cancer is rarely paraneoplastic), plus the **immunosuppression** that reopens infection. Kiara adds the patient's own **CKD**, raising uremic myoclonus if the creatinine has risen. Nothing yet unifies it.

**The vital sign that is the case.** The exam is the reveal by omission: **BP 211/114**, SpO2 87% on room air (up to 95% on 3 L), RR 28, increased work of breathing, fine bibasilar crackles (flash pulmonary edema), and a somnolent, **non-focal** patient — pupils equal and reactive, moving all limbs, disoriented to time — i.e., a global **[[Encephalopathy|encephalopathy]]**. Berkowitz teaches how to examine the non-participatory patient (blink-to-threat for fields, symmetry of spontaneous movement and grimace, withdrawal to noxious stimulus) to hunt focality, finds none, and then zeroes in on the **blood pressure**. Kiara reads the hypertension + respiratory distress as possible raised ICP with a **[[Cushing Reflex|Cushing response]]**/herniation — a sharp thought Berkowitz honors but tempers (reactive pupils, no bradycardia, still-preserved speech argue against full herniation). He asks the room for the diagnosis the blood pressure suggests, and **Maria names [[Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome|PRES]]**.

**PRES, taught.** Berkowitz and Maria build it out: **posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome** (formerly RPLS) is a **radiologic** diagnosis of **failed cerebral autoregulation** with vasogenic edema, arising in **hypertensive emergency** (primary or secondary), **eclampsia**, **calcineurin inhibitors** (tacrolimus), and **(combination) chemotherapy**. It presents with headache, altered mental status, seizures, and cortical visual symptoms (including cortical blindness), overlaps **[[Reversible Cerebral Vasoconstriction Syndrome|RCVS]]**, and its name is a triple misnomer — not always *posterior* (can extend anteriorly along watershed territories, into deep gray, brainstem, even cord), not always *reversible* (severe cases hemorrhage or infarct, with diffusion restriction), and not always an *encephalopathy* (can be seizure alone). He flags the "imaging-negative PRES" debate — a normal MRI in hypertensive encephalopathy is better called just that, treated identically. He keeps two cancer-hypercoagulable alternatives on the board — **[[Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis|venous sinus thrombosis]]** (mucinous adenocarcinoma) and **[[Marantic Endocarditis|marantic endocarditis]]** ([[Trousseau Syndrome|Trousseau]]) with showered emboli — but lands on PRES.

**The LP digression and the data.** Asked whether to tap, both discussants (and Berkowitz) would **image first** (MRI, or CT if urgent) given possible raised ICP and low infectious suspicion, deferring LP. Berkowitz uses it to teach that in AMS an LP is **usually a rule-out, not a rule-in** — polling residents (Robby, Jack: "never" found unexpected meningitis when the gut said UTI), with the exception of the immunocompromised or a case with no alternative source. Labs show the storm: **AKI** (creatinine 3.7 from 2.1), **hyperuricemia 13.8 and hyperphosphatemia 6.3** (**[[Tumor Lysis Syndrome|tumor lysis syndrome]]**), a mild leukocytosis and anemia, normal ammonia and lactate, negative infectious studies, and a **bland CSF** (glucose 104, normal cells/protein). The **MRI** shows **diffuse bilateral cerebral hemispheric diffusion signal abnormality** read as an atypical PRES pattern.

**Resolution.** The final diagnosis is **PRES with [[Myoclonic Seizure|myoclonic seizures]]**, precipitated by the combination of **FOLFOX (all three agents neurotoxic), hypertensive emergency, and CKD** — with concurrent **tumor lysis syndrome** and **flash pulmonary edema** requiring temporary **CRRT**. EEG clarified the movement: **rhythmic hemispheric delta activity prolonged during somnolence**, so the "myoclonus" was actually **myoclonic/subtle seizures**, not the movement disorder — the exact ambiguity Berkowitz had flagged at the outset. He was treated with **valproate** and **IV antihypertensives** with improvement, and the case honored the rule that a neurologic problem in a cancer patient traced back to the cancer's treatment.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **The [[Movement Disorder Approach|movement-disorder script]] — characterize before localizing.** Naming the movement (myoclonus) and separating it from clonus, fasciculation, and seizure sets the differential — and the twist is that the "myoclonus" was ultimately **[[Myoclonic Seizure|seizure]]**, exactly the ambiguity Berkowitz named up front.
- **The [[Cancer and the Nervous System|cancer-and-the-nervous-system]] schema.** "The first ten things are the cancer" forced a diligent walk through direct, treatment-related, and [[Paraneoplastic Neurologic Syndrome|paraneoplastic]] mechanisms — the same completeness heuristic that anchored [[CPS 269 - Headache and Leptomeningeal Lymphomatosis|CPS 269]] — and the answer (treatment-related PRES) honored the rule.
- **Don't skip the vitals — data-gathering as diagnosis.** The pivotal datum was a **blood pressure**, not a neurologic sign; the non-focal exam was uninformative, and reading the vital signs is what selected PRES. A reminder that the "exam" includes the numbers at the top of the chart.
- **[[Unifying Diagnosis|Unifying]] a systemic storm.** PRES, AKI, tumor lysis, and flash pulmonary edema are not four coincidences but one post-chemotherapy cascade — the hypertension/CKD/chemo triad tying the encephalopathy to the renal and pulmonary failure.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Test interpretation]] as the crux.** PRES is a *radiologic* diagnosis (with the "imaging-negative" caveat), diffusion restriction flags the atypical/irreversible variant, and the EEG reclassified the movement — each test weighted for what it can and cannot establish.
- **[[Base Rate|Base rates]] and the LP.** Berkowitz's teaching that an LP in AMS is usually a rule-out rather than a rule-in (poll of residents: "never" surprised) calibrates the pretest probability of meningitis against the salience of "must not miss it," with the immunocompromised exception preserved.

## Teaching Discussion

- Approach abnormal movements by characterizing the movement first (hyperkinetic vs hypokinetic; myoclonus vs clonus vs fasciculation vs seizure) before localizing, because each movement carries its own four-column differential (primary neurologic, structural, systemic/toxic-metabolic, drug/toxin) — and remember that in the hospital, myoclonus is most often toxic-metabolic.
- In any cancer patient with a neurologic symptom, treat the cancer as the leading suspect through three mechanisms (direct/metastatic, treatment-related, paraneoplastic) plus immunosuppression, and search diligently before attributing the problem elsewhere; recognize oxaliplatin's cold-triggered sensory neuropathy and the opsoclonus-myoclonus paraneoplastic syndrome, neither of which fit here.
- Read the vital signs as part of the neurologic assessment: a non-focal encephalopathy with severe hypertension should raise PRES, the radiologic syndrome of failed cerebral autoregulation seen with hypertensive emergency, eclampsia, calcineurin inhibitors, and combination chemotherapy, presenting with headache, altered mental status, seizures, and cortical visual symptoms.
- Diagnose PRES on imaging (posterior-predominant white-matter vasogenic edema that can extend anteriorly, into deep gray, brainstem, or cord), expect it to be usually but not always reversible (diffusion restriction and hemorrhage mark severe, irreversible cases), and call a normal MRI in hypertensive encephalopathy exactly that rather than "imaging-negative PRES" — treating both by lowering the blood pressure (and magnesium in eclampsia).
- Distinguish myoclonus the movement disorder from myoclonic or subtle seizures with EEG when the picture is ambiguous, since bilateral jerking in an encephalopathic patient with rhythmic epileptiform activity is seizure, not a movement disorder, and is treated with antiseizure medication.
- Interpret the lumbar puncture in altered mental status as usually a rule-out rather than a rule-in: image first when intracranial pressure is a concern and infectious suspicion is low, keep a lower threshold in the immunocompromised (who may not mount fever or classic imaging) or when no alternative source exists, and expect to confirm rather than overturn your clinical gut.
- Recognize the systemic cascade of new chemotherapy — tumor lysis syndrome (hyperuricemia, hyperphosphatemia), acute kidney injury, and flash pulmonary edema — as co-drivers that can precipitate and worsen PRES, sometimes requiring renal replacement therapy.
