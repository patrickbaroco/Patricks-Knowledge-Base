## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 70-year-old man with well-controlled Parkinson's disease presents with a week of worsening, exertional fatigue and an exam that looks more Parkinsonian than his baseline. New severe pancytopenia with a mildly prolonged INR redirects the case toward the bone marrow; teardrop cells, no circulating blasts, and a markedly elevated LDH and uric acid point toward a high-turnover marrow-infiltrating process. A dry tap on bone marrow biopsy and progressively worsening cytopenias and coagulopathy converge on the one leukemia classically associated with DIC — acute promyelocytic leukemia — confirmed by classic marrow findings and treated with ATRA and arsenic trioxide.

## Pearls

- For fatigue as the presenting complaint, first ask whether it is exertional or constant: exertional fatigue should be worked up like dyspnea (heart, lungs, red blood cells), while constant, non-exertional fatigue opens a much broader list (depression, hypothyroidism, and more).
- A known Parkinson's disease patient who looks more Parkinsonian than their documented baseline — per a reliable historian like a spouse — should raise concern for an acute systemic illness exacerbating the motor exam, not be dismissed as "that's just his Parkinson's"; Parkinsonism is a mimicked category, and true idiopathic Parkinson's disease doesn't fluctuate acutely on its own.
- Approach pancytopenia by counting the affected cell lines: one cytopenia favors a peripheral process (the "Vegas bet"); two (anemia plus thrombocytopenia) still favor the periphery — especially MAHA — but should leave the marrow open; three cytopenias shift the center of gravity to the bone marrow, with a short list of peripheral exceptions (some tick-borne illnesses, CLL, autoimmune destruction as in lupus).
- Before transfusing a cytopenic patient, send your diagnostic studies first — the peripheral smear and other first-tier labs should be drawn off the patient's own blood, not the unit about to be transfused.
- LDH and uric acid elevated together, without evidence of hemolysis, tissue infarction, or classic tumor lysis, point toward high cell turnover from an underlying hematologic malignancy — a powerful and easily underused pairing of clues.
- Roughly 20% of leukemias are "aleukemic" (no circulating blasts seen on the peripheral smear), and leukemia can present as pancytopenia rather than leukocytosis, since not every clonal cell escapes the marrow into the periphery — a clean smear or a low white count should not be used to argue against leukemia.

## Problem Representation

A 70-year-old man with well-controlled Parkinson's disease and a remote history of treated nasal squamous cell carcinoma presents with one week of progressive, exertional fatigue and a gait and posture more Parkinsonian than his documented baseline; new severe pancytopenia (hemoglobin 6.3, white count 1.9, platelets 35) with a mildly prolonged INR and PTT, an elevated LDH and uric acid, and teardrop cells without circulating blasts on smear redirect the workup to the bone marrow, where a dry aspirate and progressively worsening cytopenias and coagulopathy ultimately confirm acute promyelocytic leukemia.

## Case Narrative

A 70-year-old man and his wife travel roughly four hours to the ED because his condition has been "worsening" every day for about a week. Before more detail is available, the discussant reframes fatigue — often dismissed as nonspecific — as a symptom that can be systematically approached once it is the presenting complaint: the single most important branch point is whether the fatigue is exertional (in which case it should be treated like dyspnea, rooting the workup in the heart, lungs, and red blood cells) or constant regardless of activity (opening a much broader differential including depression and hypothyroidism). Given that the patient had been an active gardener until a week ago, exertional fatigue is anticipated as the more likely pattern.

Vital signs are normal. Direct questioning confirms the fatigue is present even at rest but distinctly worse with exertion, and the patient adds that his knees buckle and he feels like he might fall when walking. Prompted by this, the discussant gets him up and observes a shuffling gait, stooped posture, and bradykinesia — a Parkinsonian picture. His wife confirms his only relevant history is well-managed Parkinson's disease on carbidopa-levodopa, plus a nasal squamous cell carcinoma treated with chemotherapy and radiation a decade earlier; she also notes that while his gait looks like his usual Parkinsonian baseline, he appears far more fatigued than normal. Given his overall unwell appearance, he is moved to an acute-care bed for basic labs.

The discussant reasons that this new information sharpens rather than resolves the fatigue workup: feeling unwell at rest, worsening with exertion, still raises concern for an underlying cardiopulmonary or hematologic process. On the Parkinson's disease history, the discussant is explicit that idiopathic Parkinson's disease is a slowly evolving neurodegenerative process that shouldn't itself explain an acute delta from a functional, gardening baseline to today's exam — raising the alternative that whatever new process is driving this presentation is transiently exacerbating his underlying Parkinsonism, and flagging that Parkinsonism itself is a mimicked category worth interrogating (how was the original diagnosis made, how long has he had it) rather than accepted uncritically. The initial plan: assess oxygenation with ambulation, a chest X-ray, hemoglobin, and a comprehensive metabolic panel.

Results: EKG normal sinus rhythm; chest X-ray clear; COVID, RSV, and influenza negative; liver enzymes and bilirubin normal; INR 1.3 and PTT 40 (both mildly abnormal); renal function normal; platelets 35,000; hemoglobin 6.3; white count 1.9 — severe pancytopenia, against a fully normal CBC obtained just six months earlier at a routine visit.

The discussant reads this as confirming that whatever is driving his motor exacerbation is also causing pancytopenia, and reframes the approach to pancytopenia around counting the number of affected cell lines: with all three lines down, the center of gravity shifts decisively to the bone marrow, with a short list of peripheral exceptions (certain tick-borne illnesses causing destruction of all three lines, CLL, and autoimmune processes like lupus) — all felt unlikely here given the white count and lack of supporting features, though tick exposure is flagged as worth asking about given his gardening. Marrow-based pancytopenia is organized into three buckets: something suppressing the marrow (viral cytokines, alcohol or other toxins), the marrow lacking synthetic building blocks (B12 or copper deficiency, low testosterone or thyroid hormone in men), or something infiltrating the marrow (granulomas, cancer). First-tier testing is proposed: a peripheral smear (looking specifically for schistocytes, blasts, and teardrop cells), a reticulocyte count, B12, MMA, and homocysteine, a TSH, and HIV/hepatitis C serologies — sent deliberately before transfusing, since the patient (hemoglobin under 7, significantly symptomatic) needs blood, but the diagnostic specimens must be drawn from the patient, not the transfused unit.

Results: B12 and homocysteine normal (MMA never resulted, sent too late to return); LDH approximately 450; uric acid 8–9; HIV, hepatitis C, and hepatitis B negative; the tick panel not entirely reported but nothing else notable; the peripheral smear shows teardrop cells and no blasts; the reticulocyte count is inappropriately low.

The discussant reads the teardrop cells as the marrow "crying" from an infiltrating process, and treats the combined LDH and uric acid elevation — in a patient with normal renal function (so an elevated potassium wouldn't be expected even with high turnover) — as a strong signal of high cell turnover consistent with an underlying leukemia, explicitly drawing a contrast with a recent prior RLR case where uric acid alone was elevated from a chronic, subacute tumor-lysis-type process in a myeloproliferative neoplasm. Other causes of an elevated LDH are worked through and set aside: hemolysis (haptoglobin sent given how subtle hemolysis can be, though bilirubin and AST are unremarkable), tissue infarction (no evidence of pulmonary, cardiac, or renal infarction), and large-burden metastatic solid disease (felt less likely than a "liquid" cancer given how confined the process seems to the marrow). The leading concern becomes acute leukemia, with explicit attention to screening the smear for Auer rods (a marker of a curable-if-caught-early AML subtype) given the patient's age places him at elevated AML risk. A bone marrow biopsy is requested.

The bone marrow aspirate is dry despite repeated attempts, prompting transfer to a neighboring hospital capable of IR-guided biopsy. While awaiting that result, the patient's cytopenias and coagulopathy worsen substantially: white count falls below 1, hemoglobin dips to 5 despite four to five units of transfused blood, platelets fall to 15, and his coagulopathy progresses (INR to roughly 2, PTT to 45–46).

The discussant integrates the dry tap (raising concern for myelofibrosis, a fibrotic myeloproliferative neoplasm that can make aspiration difficult, though also compatible with a densely hypercellular, leukemic marrow) with the new coagulopathy: an elevated INR alongside pancytopenia and suspected leukemia points to the specific overlap of DIC and leukemia — acute promyelocytic leukemia (APML). He explains that leukemia can present as pancytopenia rather than the classically taught leukocytosis, since not all malignant clonal cells escape the marrow (some undergo apoptosis in place), and that roughly 20% of leukemias are "aleukemic" with no blasts visible on a peripheral smear. He voices one hesitation explicitly: APML's typical epidemiology peaks between ages 20 and 60, and this patient is 70 — but weighed against the coagulopathy, the difficulty of aspiration, the high LDH, and a fully normal CBC just six months earlier (arguing against the more indolent, chronic tempo of myelofibrosis), his leading concern remains acute leukemia, specifically APML, and he states that with a real suspicion of APML, empiric all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) should be given regardless of confirmatory testing, given its role in inducing differentiation of the malignant clone and the comparatively low harm of treating presumptively.

The diagnosis is ultimately confirmed by classic bone marrow biopsy findings and the associated translocation, and the patient is started on ATRA and arsenic trioxide. In discussion afterward, the presenting discussant reflects that he had initially overlooked the mildly elevated INR (1.3) as a meaningful clue during the ED visit — in retrospect a powerful early signal — and that ATRA was not started empirically in the ED because the coagulopathy at that point felt too mild, and no confirmatory finding (no Auer rods, no bone marrow result yet) was available to justify it; both discussants acknowledge this is a genuinely difficult real-time threshold question, one that experience with many such cases helps calibrate.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The presenting discussant explicitly performs a cognitive autopsy after the diagnosis is confirmed: he had noted the mildly elevated INR (1.3) at the initial ED visit but did not weight it as meaningfully abnormal or leukemia-suggestive in the moment, only recognizing in retrospect how powerful a clue it was once DIC and APML became the leading concern — a candid example of a genuinely useful finding being present all along but under-weighted until the rest of the picture forced a re-read.

The discussant is transparent about a base-rate tension he holds throughout: APML's classic epidemiology peaks between ages 20 and 60, and this patient is 70, which tempers his confidence — but he explicitly reasons past that friction using the specific configuration of findings (coagulopathy, dry tap, high LDH, an acute deviation from a normal CBC six months prior) rather than either discounting the diagnosis on age alone or ignoring the epidemiological mismatch.

The discussant also names a specific illness script he is actively drawing on — a prior case of a 20-year-old in Chicago with pancytopenia, DIC, and APML — as the reason he reflexively raises APML this quickly; both discussants reflect afterward on how directly personal case experience shapes real-time pattern recognition, and on how difficult it is to calibrate the exact threshold for empiric treatment (ATRA) before a confirmatory bone marrow result, acknowledging that this kind of judgment is built through repeated exposure to similar borderline cases rather than derived from a fixed rule.

## Teaching Discussion

Fatigue, though often labeled nonspecific, can be approached systematically once it is the presenting complaint: exertional fatigue should be treated as a dyspnea-equivalent, rooting the workup in cardiac, pulmonary, and hematologic causes, while constant, non-exertional fatigue opens a broader differential (depression, hypothyroidism, and other systemic causes).

Pancytopenia is best organized by counting affected cell lines: one cytopenia favors a peripheral process; two (anemia and thrombocytopenia) still lean peripheral (e.g., MAHA) while allowing for a marrow process; three cytopenias shift the primary suspicion to the bone marrow itself, with tick-borne illness, CLL, and autoimmune destruction (e.g., lupus) as recognized peripheral exceptions.

Marrow-based pancytopenia divides into three mechanisms: marrow suppression (viral cytokine effect, toxins such as alcohol), a lack of synthetic building blocks (B12 or copper deficiency, low testosterone or thyroid hormone in men), or marrow infiltration (granulomatous disease, cancer) — teardrop cells on smear are a classic signal of an infiltrated, "crying" marrow.

First-tier pancytopenia workup should include a peripheral smear (schistocytes, blasts, teardrop cells), a reticulocyte count, B12/MMA/homocysteine, TSH, and viral serologies (HIV, hepatitis B/C) — drawn before transfusion so the diagnostic picture reflects the patient's own blood rather than the transfused product.

LDH and uric acid, elevated together without evidence of hemolysis, tissue infarction, or classic acute tumor lysis, point toward high cell turnover from a hematologic malignancy; this pairing is a distinct and complementary clue to teardrop cells and a dry marrow tap.

A dry bone marrow aspirate is a recognized clue to myelofibrosis, but can equally reflect a densely hypercellular marrow packed with leukemic blasts — clinical tempo (an acute deviation from a normal CBC months earlier, versus a more gradually evolving cytopenia) helps distinguish the two.

Leukemia does not require an elevated white count to be present: some clonal cells never escape the marrow (undergoing apoptosis in place), producing pancytopenia rather than leukocytosis, and roughly 20% of leukemias are "aleukemic," with no blasts visible on the peripheral smear.

Acute promyelocytic leukemia is the leukemia most classically associated with DIC; when APML is genuinely suspected, all-trans retinoic acid is often started empirically given the relatively low harm of early treatment weighed against the high mortality of untreated APML-associated coagulopathy — though ATRA is not without real risk (retinoic acid syndrome, skin reactions, hepatotoxicity), and the threshold for empiric treatment before confirmatory testing is a genuine judgment call that experienced clinicians calibrate case by case.
