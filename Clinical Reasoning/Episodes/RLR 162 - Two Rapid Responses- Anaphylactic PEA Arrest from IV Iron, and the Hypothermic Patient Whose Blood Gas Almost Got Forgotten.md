## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Reza, narrating from his role as a hospitalist carrying the code pager, presents Robbie with two real rapid responses and asks him to reason through each as pragmatically as if he'd just walked into the room. The first is a man in his late 50s with swollen lips, pooling secretions, and rapidly falling oxygen saturation who arrests with a pulseless narrow-complex rhythm — ultimately anaphylaxis to an IV iron infusion (ferric carboxymaltose) given for iron-deficiency anemia during a heart-failure-with-preserved-ejection-fraction workup, successfully resuscitated after roughly twenty minutes of well-run CPR. The second is a 94-year-old man with advanced dementia and multiple indwelling devices (G-tube, suprapubic catheter, tunneled dialysis catheter, tracheostomy collar) found unresponsive and hypothermic — reflexively worked up as infection, empirically escalated to meropenem on a resident's suggestion, and ultimately explained by a pseudomonal pneumonia causing severe hypercapnic respiratory failure. The second case includes an explicitly self-deprecating aside: despite having taught the hypercapnia/blood-gas lesson to Reza just one episode earlier, Robbie initially forgets to reflexively order a repeat gas himself — a live demonstration of how even core reflexes require constant reinforcement.

## Pearls

- Simultaneous ventilation impairment (e.g., lip/airway swelling, pooled secretions) and oxygenation impairment (falling SpO2) that seem to belong to two separate problems should raise anaphylaxis as a unifying explanation — it is one of the few processes that causes both upper airway and lower airway/alveolar compromise at once.
- Profound hypoxemia can blunt the body's normal drive to appear distressed or air-hungry; a patient who looks eerily calm or "staring into the distance" while critically hypoxemic may not display the alarm you'd expect.
- Rapid response teams are typically smaller and lack a crash cart and dedicated airway team, unlike a code blue/code 99, which recruits ICU, airway, and pharmacy support — the decision of which to call should hinge largely on the airway exam and overall trajectory, not on a fear of over-calling.
- If you're on the fence about escalating to a code blue, escalate — and if you're still unsure, ask a colleague in the room rather than shouldering the decision alone; for any high-stakes activation (code blue, CTPE), a healthy false-alarm rate is a feature, not a failure.
- Running a code: confirm code status first; prioritize high-quality CPR (backboard, a rotating line of compressors, real-time feedback on rate/depth) and rapid rhythm/pulse checks to identify a shockable rhythm; assign dedicated roles (airway team, IV/IO access, medication and timekeeping, a "code whisperer"); and set an explicit tone for closed-loop communication and room control from the first sentence.
- Pulseless electrical activity in anaphylaxis is typically driven by a combination of profound hypoxemia and severe vasoplegia (pathologic vasodilation) — knowing this shapes both the resuscitation approach and the expected post-ROSC hemodynamics (vasopressor-refractory hypotension).
- A single rare adverse drug reaction (anaphylaxis to IV iron occurs in roughly 1 in 100,000 infusions) should not create durable avoidance bias against prescribing that medication in the future — an appropriately rare, logical baseline risk shouldn't be inflated by one vivid personal experience.
- Hypothermia without environmental exposure is infection until proven otherwise in the vast majority of cases, and hypothermic septic patients are often sicker — with worse outcomes — than febrile ones, warranting a low threshold for empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics while the workup proceeds.
- In vitro antibiotic susceptibility testing (e.g., piperacillin-tazobactam) does not always predict in vivo clinical efficacy, particularly with organisms like ESBL-producing Enterobacterales — trust the clinical/epidemiologic picture (recent hospitalization, prior resistant organisms) when escalating empiric coverage, even against reassuring susceptibility data.
- In a patient with altered mental status, a blood gas for PCO2 can be just as important — arguably more so in some practices — than a fingerstick glucose; hypercapnic encephalopathy is easy to miss when attention is anchored on infection or hypoglycemia alone.
- Elevated CO2 is directly vasodilatory (systemically and cerebrally) and blunts vasopressor responsiveness in acidotic patients — correcting the acidosis itself, not just adding pressors, can be what resolves refractory hypotension.

## Problem Representation

**Case 1:** A man in his late 50s with HFpEF undergoing transthyretin amyloidosis workup develops lip swelling, pooling oral secretions, altered responsiveness, and rapidly falling oxygen saturation (70% → 60% → 56%) with normal heart rate and blood pressure, minutes after starting an IV ferric carboxymaltose infusion for iron-deficiency anemia (ferritin saturation 11%), progressing to a pulseless narrow-complex (PEA) arrest.

**Case 2:** A 94-year-old man with advanced dementia, full code status, and multiple indwelling devices (G-tube, suprapubic catheter, tunneled hemodialysis catheter, tracheostomy collar on room air) is found acutely unresponsive from his baseline (normally command-following, eyes open) with normal oxygen saturation and blood pressure but confirmed hypothermia (92°F versus a baseline of 96°F).

## Case Narrative

**Case 1.** Reza sets the scene as a real rapid response he ran, framed explicitly as a pragmatic exercise: what would you actually do, right now, walking into this room? A man in his late 50s is staring off into the distance, not communicating, with visibly swollen lips and pooling secretions. Robbie's first instinct is that this looks like acute upper airway obstruction, but he flags a disconnect — most people with an evolving airway obstruction are visibly alarmed, not eerily calm, so he deliberately slows down rather than committing to a single problem. When Reza reveals the oxygen saturation is falling rapidly (70% → 60% → 56%) despite normal vitals otherwise and a preserved pulse, Robbie names the situation as combined oxygenation failure (localizing to the alveolar-capillary membrane) and ventilation failure (the lip swelling and secretion pooling suggesting impaired air movement) — a mixed respiratory failure that is especially dangerous because the two problems require different, sometimes conflicting, interventions. Supplemental oxygen addresses the oxygenation half; addressing the ventilation half is trickier, since positive pressure ventilation in a possible obstructive process risks worsening secretions or precipitating aspiration. He frames the immediate priority as airway status, requiring a careful intraoral exam, and works through ABC as CAB (circulation first, since it's reassuring here, then airway, then breathing).

Reza asks about the threshold for escalating from a rapid response to a full code blue. Robbie distinguishes the two: a rapid response is a small team (roughly four people) without a crash cart or dedicated airway team, appropriate when a straightforward intervention (like supplemental oxygen for atelectasis or reversing opioid-induced hypoventilation) might resolve things; a code blue recruits the ICU team, an airway team, and a pharmacist. Given this patient's trajectory — 10 liters of oxygen not budging the falling saturation — Robbie says his hand would already be on the code blue button. His broader advice for trainees hesitant to escalate: high-stakes interventions should tolerate a high rate of "false alarms" by design, and you're rarely making the decision alone — asking a colleague in the room whether to escalate is always reasonable.

Reza confirms he did activate a code 99 after non-rebreather failed to help, and shares a practical habit: as soon as a patient is desaturating this severely, have someone keep a finger on the pulse and the bed positioned to start compressions immediately, because the pulse may be lost at any moment — which is exactly what happened, within about 30 seconds of activation. He then supplies the backstory: the patient has HFpEF and is being worked up for transthyretin amyloidosis, with a ferritin saturation of 11% prompting a ferric carboxymaltose (IV iron) infusion. Within a few cc's of the infusion, he developed pruritus, prompting the nurse to stop it — and Reza arrived to find the iron still hanging, the lip swelling and pruritus in progress, and then a lost pulse.

Robbie reasons this now clearly points to anaphylaxis from IV iron, a described phenomenon, but emphasizes that at the moment of a lost pulse, diagnosis takes a back seat to code fundamentals: confirm code status, then become this patient's heart with high-quality CPR (backboard in place, a rotating line of compressors, real-time feedback on depth and rate), attach the monitor as quickly as possible to check for a shockable rhythm, and in parallel assign dedicated roles — two people for the airway (an intubator and respiratory therapy), someone for IV/IO access, a "code whisperer" advising the leader, and people dedicated to medications and timekeeping. He stresses that a code leader should set an explicit tone immediately — naming themselves as leader, demanding closed-loop communication and quiet in the room — to prevent the chaos that often creeps into codes with too many well-meaning people.

Reza narrates what actually happened: IM epinephrine was given as soon as the iron and lip swelling were recognized, IV access was suboptimal so two IOs were placed, a full team assembled (residents, an ICU attending whispering guidance, a pharmacist, a timekeeper), and after roughly two minutes without a pulse, the rhythm on the pads showed a narrow-complex PEA. Chest compressions continued, with IV/IO epinephrine, methylprednisolone, and diphenhydramine given, for roughly twenty minutes with intermittent return and loss of pulses before the patient was intubated (at the anesthesiologist's prompt) and ultimately achieved sustained ROSC. He was found trying to pull out his endotracheal tube later that night, was extubated within 24 hours, had his IOs removed the next day, and was transferred out of the ICU — a highly successful outcome for what began as a witnessed, immediately treated arrest in a relatively young patient. Reza closes with an explicit teaching point: because anaphylaxis to ferric carboxymaltose is rare (roughly 1 in 100,000 infusions), providers — including the one who ordered it — shouldn't develop a durable, illogical aversion to prescribing IV iron going forward; a single rare adverse event shouldn't retroactively change otherwise sound practice, an idea he compares to how clinicians should reason about other rare, severe drug reactions.

Robbie closes the case with two unifying physiologic points: the PEA itself likely reflected a combination of profound hypoxemia and severe anaphylaxis-driven vasoplegia (pathologic vasodilation), and the case is an elegant illustration of how anaphylaxis uniquely causes simultaneous upper airway (lip/oropharyngeal) swelling and lower airway/alveolar compromise — explaining why the initial ventilation-versus-oxygenation "disconnect" Robbie flagged at the outset wasn't really a disconnect at all once the unifying diagnosis was in view.

**Case 2.** The second rapid response arrives with a crisp one-liner from the resident: a 94-year-old man, full code, with advanced dementia, in and out of the ICU previously, now with a G-tube, a tracheostomy collar on room air, and a suprapubic catheter, found unresponsive from a baseline in which he typically follows commands and has his eyes open. Vitals are entirely normal except for a temperature of 92°F, confirmed on repeat measurement, against a documented baseline around 96°F.

Robbie notes the vitals buy a little more time than Case 1, but the mental status change still raises aspiration risk and demands urgency. His leading clue is the hypothermia itself: absent environmental exposure, roughly 90% of hypothermia in a hospitalized patient is infectious. He proposes an EKG, fingerstick glucose, and basic labs, and says he wouldn't fault empiric antibiotics being started while the workup proceeds given how sick the patient appears. Reza confirms the point-of-care glucose reflex (credited to mentor Lawrence Tierney as a standard part of any encephalopathy workup) and reveals an additional device: a tunneled hemodialysis catheter, with dialysis three times weekly. Asked for a fuller workup, Robbie adds warming measures, a CBC, blood cultures (with time-to-positivity comparison between the catheter and a peripheral site, given bacteremia risk from the tunneled line), renal panel, LFTs, a chest X-ray, a bladder scan with urine studies from the suprapubic catheter, a head CT given the degree of altered mental status, and — invoking the hospital's own hypothermia schema — a TSH, while acknowledging medications should be reviewed as a contributor to impaired thermoregulation. He self-deprecatingly notes that after years of hypothermia nearly always being infection, he might personally be "dulled" to some of these less common considerations, joking that an enthusiastic trainee is more likely to remember them than he is.

Reza describes how the actual team unfolded in real time: recognizing the constellation of a suprapubic catheter, a tunneled line, and a tracheostomy as multiple infection risk factors, they started empiric vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam, until a resident suggested switching to meropenem given the patient's history of ESBL — a call Reza praised in the moment. Labs returned without leukocytosis, a stable renal panel appropriate for a dialysis patient, an unremarkable bladder scan, and a head CT that was obtained as planned; the patient was triaged to a step-down level of care. TSH and cortisol both returned normal, appropriately lowering less common endocrine causes of hypothermia.

The turn in the case is a pointed, self-referential moment: Reza reveals there was one additional lab sent as part of the hospital's standard "rainbow labs" panel that explained the acute presentation, and neither he nor Robbie had specifically ordered it — a repeat blood gas. Robbie, initially unable to recall it, is reminded that this is precisely the lesson he'd taught Reza the week before (RLR 161): even in an altered, hypothermic patient, a blood gas for PCO2 deserves the same reflexive priority as a fingerstick glucose. Robbie frames his own lapse as an illustration of how even well-practiced reflexes erode without deliberate reinforcement — attributing part of the miss to working with a highly capable team whose ER nurses obtain point-of-care gases automatically, without anyone needing to consciously order them, and reflecting that "inconsequential mistakes," made and immediately corrected, are one of the most durable ways to relearn a lesson.

The gas findings: this patient's baseline VBG runs around pH 7.3 with a PCO2 in the mid-50s; his ABG now showed a pH of about 7.1, a PCO2 of 120, and a PaO2 of 54 — severe acute-on-chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure with superimposed hypoxemia. He was placed on the ventilator through his tracheostomy collar and woke almost immediately once his CO2 was corrected. A respiratory culture subsequently grew Pseudomonas, explaining the presentation as pneumonia-driven hypoventilation causing hypercapnia, which in turn caused both the hypoactive mental status and — via CO2's vasodilatory effects — a component of the hypothermia and a transient episode of acidosis-driven, vasopressor-refractory hypotension that resolved once the pH normalized. Robbie closes by naming the clinical pearl explicitly: CO2 is a potent vasodilator (both systemically, producing flushed/red extremities, and cerebrally, where blood flow tracks directly with CO2), and severe acidosis blunts vasopressor efficacy — meaning correcting the underlying acid-base derangement can be as important as escalating pressor support.

| Response to epinephrine, steroids, antihistamines, and sustained CPR with eventual ROSC | Supports anaphylaxis as the cause and confirms a reversible process |

**Case 2**

| Finding | Effect on differential |
|---|---|
| Acute change from baseline mental status in a patient with multiple indwelling devices | Raises infection as the leading cause, given the number of potential portals of entry |
| Confirmed hypothermia (92°F vs. baseline 96°F) without environmental exposure | Strongly raises infection (~90% of such presentations); lowers primary environmental/exposure-related hypothermia |

| Respiratory culture grows Pseudomonas | Localizes the infectious source to pneumonia, explaining the hypoventilation/hypercapnia cascade |
| Transient vasopressor-refractory hypotension that resolves with pH normalization | Confirms severe acidemia (via CO2-mediated vasodilation) as the driver of hemodynamic instability, rather than a primary cardiac or septic-shock process |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Case 1 is a compact lesson in resisting premature diagnostic closure: Robbie deliberately holds off naming "airway obstruction" the moment he sees swollen lips, because the patient's paradoxically calm affect doesn't fit that script — a disconnect that turns out to be explained, rather than resolved, once anaphylaxis unifies both the ventilation and oxygenation problems. Case 2 illustrates outcome-independent reflex maintenance: the hosts explicitly frame Robbie's own lapse (forgetting to reflexively order a repeat gas, despite having taught that exact lesson the week before) not as a failure but as proof that reflexes decay without repetition — and that working with a highly capable team can paradoxically erode an individual clinician's own vigilance, since the system compensates silently. Both cases also reinforce a recurring RLR theme: don't let a single rare, vivid experience (an anaphylactic reaction; a forgotten reflex) distort future practice disproportionately to its actual base rate or significance.

## Teaching Discussion

The IV iron anaphylaxis case offers a compact, transferable code-leadership framework: confirm code status, prioritize CPR quality and rhythm/pulse checks over diagnostic reasoning in the first moments, assign explicit roles, and set the tone for closed-loop communication from the first sentence. Clinically, it also supplies a durable unifying illness script — simultaneous upper and lower airway compromise, which individually point to different physiologic problems (ventilation vs. oxygenation), should raise anaphylaxis as the process capable of causing both at once. The rapid-response-versus-code-blue distinction, and the explicit permission to over-call rather than under-call, is broadly transferable advice for trainees anxious about escalation decisions.

The hypothermia case reinforces a well-established RLR principle (hypothermia is infection until proven otherwise) while adding two more specific, high-yield teachings: the limits of in vitro susceptibility testing in guiding real-world antibiotic choice for resistant organisms like ESBL Enterobacterales, and the CO2-mediated vasodilation mechanism that can produce vasopressor-refractory hypotension in severe acidemia — treatable by correcting the underlying gas exchange problem rather than escalating pressors alone. Most memorably, the case becomes a live demonstration of the show's broader philosophy about learning: the hosts explicitly frame a small, immediately corrected lapse (forgetting to reflexively order a gas) as valuable precisely because it is inconsequential, arguing that repeated exposure to "boring" bread-and-butter presentations is what builds the pattern recognition needed to catch the rare deviation — a direct connective thread to the following episode's ketoacidosis case, where the same philosophy resurfaces explicitly.
