## Pearls

- **Translate any bleeding complaint into [[Blood Per Perineum|"blood per perineum"]] before you build a differential.** [[Gross Hematuria|Frank hematuria]] is really *red substance from the perineum*: confirm it is blood and confirm it comes from the urinary tract (rather than gynecologic or GI sources) before committing. A cohesive chronology and the marriage of flank pain to the bleeding both argue the history is reliable and the source is urinary.
- **In gross hematuria, chase the complication before the cause — the immediate threat is [[Clot Retention|urinary retention]].** Whatever the etiology, macroscopic bleeding commonly obstructs; assess for retention (and get a post-void residual even when asymptomatic) before working up why the patient is bleeding.
- **Macroscopic and microscopic hematuria share a differential but invert its priorities.** The list of causes is the same; the *amount* of blood re-weights it. [[Microscopic versus Macroscopic Hematuria|Macroscopic hematuria]] carries roughly a 40% chance of malignancy and makes [[Glomerulonephritis|glomerulonephritis]] unlikely (IgA nephropathy is the classic exception), whereas microscopic hematuria is <5% cancer and keeps glomerular disease firmly in play. More blood means more cancer and less glomerulonephritis.
- **Work hematuria [[Anatomical Approach|anatomically]] up the tract, and let volume and flank pain localize.** Bladder (malignancy, stones), prostate (prostatitis, Foley trauma), ureter (lumen/stone, wall/fibrosis, external compression — usually only *microscopic*), and kidney (a mass or cyst, or a vascular lesion — arterial embolism, glomerular, or renal vein thrombosis). Macroscopic bleeding prioritizes the parenchymal causes that bleed briskly, and flank pain pushes the problem into the upper tract.
- **Young [[Chronic Kidney Disease|CKD]] is never diabetes or hypertension — reason about it with an [[Analogic Reasoning|intrarenal-AKI schema]], where the interstitium holds the money.** Like any chronic organ disease (Reza's dementia analogy), chronic kidney disease has no pre- or post-renal category. Glomerular (FSGS, IgA), tubular (recurrent ATN, myeloma), and vascular causes matter, but interstitial disease dominates non-diabetic/non-hypertensive CKD. The two causes with a special propensity to bleed are [[Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease|ADPKD]] (cyst rupture) and [[Sickle Cell Nephropathy|sickle cell nephropathy]] (papillary necrosis) — and long-standing ESRD itself yields [[Acquired Cystic Kidney Disease|acquired cysts]] (small kidneys with cysts, versus large in ADPKD) that can bleed.
- **A supratherapeutic INR can bleed *without* a culprit lesion — but always interrogate the two morbid causes, [[Liver Disease|liver disease]] and [[Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation|DIC]].** The [[Superwarfarin Coagulopathy|synthetic-cannabinoid/superwarfarin outbreak]] proved that profound anticoagulation alone can cause spontaneous urinary bleeding with no anatomic lesion. Still, an elevated INR earns a structured review — warfarin dosing, interacting drugs, antibiotics (gut vitamin-K flora), and diet — plus a look at the liver and a DIC screen before it is dismissed as the whole story.

## Problem Representation

A young man with congenital end-stage renal disease (two atrophic kidneys, on peritoneal dialysis since late adolescence, ~1 cup of urine daily) and a congenital aortic valvulopathy status-post mechanical valve replacement, anticoagulated on warfarin (goal INR 2.5–3.5), presents with one week of gross hematuria and paroxysmal flank pain radiating to the groin — once voiding a cup of frank blood. He is found to have a supratherapeutic INR of 5.6 (though he reports his INR habitually oscillates between 2 and 6, self-managed with diet), a urinalysis confirming gross blood, normal liver enzymes, platelets, and fibrinogen with no schistocytes, and an isolated LDH of 550. A non-contrast (stone-protocol) CT shows bilateral renal atrophy with multiple punctate hyperdensities (cortical calcifications versus non-obstructing stones) and no ureteral/bladder stone or hydronephrosis; after persistent bleeding, a contrast-enhanced CT reveals abnormal enhancement in the medial right kidney with enhancing soft tissue extending into an irregularly thickened right renal pelvis, raising concern for an infiltrating neoplasm versus inflammation. A thorough urologic evaluation with cystoscopy, ureteroscopy, retrograde pyelogram, ureteral stenting, brushings, and bladder biopsy is negative for malignancy; the hematuria resolves as the INR corrects, leaving the diagnosis unresolved and dependent on time and serial imaging.

## Case Narrative

The episode opens on banter — a "Reza loves Robbie" palindrome, Rabih ("Robbie Baby Blue Geha") back after two weeks on service, and an announcement that Charmeen is now running operations for the Clinical Problem Solvers. Rabih then introduces a new format inspired by **Jack Penner**: [[Diagnostic Authenticity|authentic diagnosis]]. Rather than assume the discussant would have obtained each datum coming their way, Reza will tell Rabih *nothing he does not explicitly ask for* — "if I don't think of it and don't ask for it, then it never happened" — to replicate real front-line reasoning where the clinician must actively pull every piece of data.

The chief complaint flashes up: **Frank hematuria**. Rabih immediately reframes. [[Gross Hematuria|Frank hematuria]] translates to *macroscopic* hematuria, but more fundamentally he takes a step back and calls any such complaint [[Blood Per Perineum|blood per perineum]] — even that overstates it; it is really *red substance* from the perineum, leaving a two-by-two of *where is it from* and *is it blood*. He has been humbled by red diapers assumed to be GI bleeding that turned out urinary, and by [[Blood Per Os|blood from the mouth]] that could be nasal, airway, or GI. Patients localize their own bleeding well unless there is dementia or encephalopathy.

He then lays out the principle that will organize the case: when the kidneys, ureters, bladder, or prostate bleed, the *list* of causes is unchanged, but the *amount* of bleeding re-prioritizes it. We study [[Microscopic versus Macroscopic Hematuria|microscopic and macroscopic hematuria]] as different problems not because the differential differs but because the weighting does. Microscopic hematuria keeps [[Glomerulonephritis|glomerulonephritis]] in play; macroscopic hematuria makes glomerular bleeding unusual (IgA nephropathy the classic exception) and instead carries a ~40% chance of cancer, versus <5% for microscopic. More blood, more cancer, less glomerulonephritis. But his very first concern in a patient presenting *for care* with gross hematuria is not the cause at all — it is the complication, [[Clot Retention|urinary retention]], which he would assess for (post-void residual) even absent symptoms.

Asking his own leading questions, Rabih establishes a young man, one week of symptoms. Reza gives only what is requested: a week ago flank pain, tolerable, then frank blood on voiding — once a full cup of blood — with paroxysmal flank pain radiating into the groin prompting the visit. And a key background fact surfaces (heard, or noticed as dialysis access): [[End-Stage Renal Disease|end-stage renal disease]] on peritoneal dialysis, making only a cup of urine a day. The cohesive chronology (against encephalopathy) and the flank pain married to the bleeding convince Rabih this is genuinely urinary macroscopic hematuria; the low baseline urine output, however, blinds him to obstruction, which he sets aside to study separately.

Rabih ascends the tract [[Anatomical Approach|anatomically]]. **Lower tract:** bladder (malignancy or stones) and prostate (prostatitis or Foley trauma). **Ureter:** a luminal stone (but stones rarely cause *macroscopic* bleeding), wall disease like fibrosis, or external compression from a retroperitoneal tumor — all usually microscopic. **Kidney:** either a visible mass (cancer or cyst) or a vascular problem — arterial (embolism), glomerular (capillary), or venous ([[Renal Vein Thrombosis|renal vein thrombosis]]). Because macroscopic hematuria prioritizes the parenchymal causes that bleed heavily and the flank pain points upward, he localizes to the upper tract/kidney.

He then turns to the CKD, refusing to let it stay in the background. In all comers, US end-stage renal disease is diabetes and hypertension until proven otherwise — and this young patient proves it otherwise. He reasons by [[Analogic Reasoning|analogy]]: treat [[Chronic Kidney Disease|CKD]] like [[Acute Kidney Injury|intrarenal AKI]] with no pre- or post-renal category, because by the time disease is chronic it lives in the organ itself (as with any chronic organ failure, or dementia). Glomerular causes beyond diabetes/hypertension are FSGS and IgA nephropathy; tubular causes are recurrent ATN and [[Multiple Myeloma|multiple myeloma]]; vascular causes like renal artery stenosis are rare; but the *money* in non-diabetic, non-hypertensive CKD is the interstitium — NSAIDs, heavy metals, autoimmune interstitial disease (lupus, sarcoid, IgG4), infections (TB, schistosomiasis), and genetic disease (ADPKD, sickle cell, Fabry). Two of these bleed like no other: [[Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease|ADPKD]] (ruptured cysts) and [[Sickle Cell Nephropathy|sickle cell nephropathy]] (papillary necrosis).

Reza then feeds the rest of the past history unprompted, judging it too helpful to withhold: the patient was born with two atrophic kidneys, reached dialysis around age 17–18, and has a congenital aortic valvulopathy replaced early with a **mechanical valve**, for which he takes [[Warfarin|warfarin]] with a goal INR of 2.5–3.5. Rabih flags two new dimensions. First, long-standing ESRD produces [[Acquired Cystic Kidney Disease|acquired cysts]] — distinguished from ADPKD by kidney size (small with cysts here, versus large) — that can bleed independent of the original cause. Second, the word *warfarin* raises a competing hypothesis entirely: a coagulopathy making vulnerable kidneys bleed, or diffuse bleeding not localized to the kidney at all. He summarizes — foreground: a week of macroscopic hematuria with flank pain; background: ESRD, aortopathy, warfarin — and asks for the two data he most wants: the **UA** (to verify the bleeding) and the **INR**.

A teaching exchange follows: does a very high INR itself explain bleeding, or must there be a culprit lesion? Rabih's answer has changed over his career. He was taught there is always a bleeding lesion, but the [[Superwarfarin Coagulopathy|synthetic-marijuana outbreak]] laced with a long-half-life brodifacoum-like compound produced INRs of 9–10 for months and spontaneous urinary bleeding with *no* demonstrable anatomic lesion, resolving when the coagulopathy resolved. So: not necessarily.

Reza gives the data. INR **5.6**; UA confirms gross blood (the patient literally showed a specimen of red urine). Rabih now sees an INR probably sufficient to trigger bleeding, spontaneous or not, and frames a fork: fix the INR and watch, or use the INR as a *lucky* prompt that might catch a malignancy earlier — helped by the fact that the most morbid actionable cause, cancer, is less likely in someone young. He would still pursue a hematuria workup (cystoscopy for bladder causes, CT urogram for ureter and parenchyma), but first interrogates *why* the INR is elevated: the INR reflects factors 7, 10, 5, and 2, and can rise from reduced production or increased consumption. The common driver in a warfarin patient is a dose change — more warfarin taken or given, an interacting co-prescription, antibiotics consuming gut vitamin-K flora, or dietary vitamin-K change. But he will not stop at the simple explanations: the two morbid hypotheses to always ask when an INR climbs are [[Liver Disease|liver disease]] and [[Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation|DIC]]. He asks for liver enzymes, platelets, and a smear.

The "warfarin review of systems" returns reassuring: liver enzymes normal, DIC screen (platelets, fibrinogen) normal, no schistocytes. The patient reports this INR is *usual* for him — he swings between 2 and 6 and modifies his diet at home to bring it down — with no diarrhea, antibiotics, or dose change. Reza notes that even among adherent warfarin patients only ~75% sit in-window, and riffs that DOACs ("quit calling them NOACs — they're old now") have revolutionized this. With the INR reframed as possibly *noise*, Rabih returns to the anatomic visualization — prior similar episodes (none), bleeding elsewhere (none), and imaging.

The team obtained a stone-protocol **non-contrast CT** for the paroxysmal, groin-radiating flank pain. It showed bilateral renal atrophy with multiple punctate hyperdensities (cortical calcifications or non-obstructing stones), no ureteral or bladder stone, and no hydroureteronephrosis. Rabih reads this as reassuring against renal cell or transitional cell carcinoma and bladder malignancy — many hematuria patients never get a clear diagnosis, and the job is to exclude embolic/vascular disease, cancer, and glomerulonephritis. He would be left unsure what triggered the bleed: perhaps a stone plus the supratherapeutic INR. The admitting resident (Reza) had in fact written a unifying note — stones, supratherapeutic INR, paroxysmal groin-radiating flank pain — but with a crucial hedge: *if symptoms don't resolve with supportive management, step back and broaden the differential*, letting the treatment itself become a diagnostic test.

That hedge proves prescient. The patient continues to bleed. He transfers to a second attending who, Reza emphasizes, *brilliantly stepped back* rather than let [[Diagnostic Momentum|diagnostic momentum]] carry her, asked why this was still happening, consulted urology, and obtained a **contrast-enhanced CT**. It showed abnormal enhancement in the medial aspect of the atrophic right kidney with enhancing soft tissue extending into the renal pelvis, irregular thickening of portions of the right renal pelvis — "the possibility of an infiltrating mass/neoplasm must be considered, along with inflammatory etiologies given the patient's age" — plus diffuse bladder wall thickening thought possibly to reflect an under-descended bladder.

Rabih reflects on the kidney as a malignancy site: unlike brain, lung, liver, and adrenal, the kidney (like the spleen) is a rare site of *metastatic* disease, so most renal tumors are primary. If this lesion is cancer, [[Renal Cell Carcinoma|renal cell carcinoma]] leads, though the pelvic location raises [[Transitional Cell Carcinoma|transitional cell carcinoma]]; either would be unusual this young absent a syndrome linking the aortopathy and kidney disease. He notes benign possibilities too — an [[Angiomyolipoma|angiomyolipoma]] tied to certain vasculopathies. Given a ~40% cancer base rate in macroscopic hematuria *plus* an enhancing lesion, concern is high — but not all masses are cancer (infiltrative disease, histiocytic disorders, sarcoidosis, infection remain possible). Because radiologists reliably call RCC and imaging can be diagnostic enough to go straight to nephrectomy rather than biopsy — and because newer gadolinium agents allow contrast MRI even in ESRD — his next move is a conversation with radiology about whether this is RCC, needs biopsy/MRI, or warrants urology for nephrectomy.

Reza smiles because two threads were still dangling. The labs also showed an **LDH of 550** with no schistocytes — which he had listed as a *separate problem to return to* and then, tellingly, forgot to integrate. And he recalls a pearl from Dr. Jeffrey Collis (SF VA): a stone can radiate to the groin, but so can **renal cell carcinoma** — it belongs on the differential for flank pain radiating to the groin. His real-time [[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive autopsy]]: he never resolved the LDH, and he failed to consider a **congenital syndrome** that might raise this young patient's baseline cancer vulnerability — the background that would make "cancer in a young person" less improbable.

Urology performed cystoscopy, ureteroscopy, and a retrograde pyelogram: the proximal right ureter was mildly constricted with distal dilation; they placed a stent, took brush samples, sampled a couple of erythematous bladder areas, and sent cytology on everything — all negative. They started continuous bladder irrigation for a possible obstructing clot, then re-entered, removed the stent, evacuated clots, and resampled — again no malignancy. Their conclusion: no evidence of urinary tract cancer. As the INR came down, the bleeding resolved, and the patient was discharged with the diagnosis unresolved.

Both hosts close on uncertainty. We live in limbo far more than in certainty, Rabih says; [[Time as a Diagnostic Test|time]] is a key test, and a concerning radiographic lesion that resolves after correcting an alternative hypothesis (the INR) and extensive negative sampling calls for serial imaging of that site rather than false closure. Reza's summary metaphor: the practice of medicine is not a straight walk but bouncing between walls — right leg out, left leg out — moving forward while never quite settling. They land somewhere reassuring for the patient without settling it at all. Reza gives a closing shout-out to NEJM Case 34-2020, a 74-year-old man with CKD whose 23-year story again turns on time.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Blood Per Perineum|Source-and-substance reframing]].** Before any differential, Rabih refuses to accept "hematuria" at face value, translating it to *red substance per perineum* and demanding verification of both that it is blood and that it is urinary — a guard against the diaper/oropharyngeal misattributions that have burned him.
- **[[Anatomical Approach|Anatomical reasoning]] with volume as the weighting function.** He enumerates the tract layer by layer (bladder, prostate, ureter's lumen/wall/external compression, kidney's mass/cyst/vascular) and lets the *amount* of blood, not the raw list, set priorities — macroscopic volume promoting parenchymal causes and demoting the typically microscopic ureteral ones.
- **[[Base Rate|Base-rate discipline]].** The ~40% malignancy rate of macroscopic hematuria anchors concern for cancer, but is explicitly tempered by the patient's youth; when an enhancing lesion appears, the base rate and the imaging compound rather than substitute.
- **[[Analogic Reasoning|Analogic reasoning]] for CKD.** Reza's dementia analogy — chronic organ disease has no pre-/post-category because the disease now lives *in* the organ — lets him repurpose the intrarenal-AKI schema for CKD, then filter it to the two causes (ADPKD, sickle cell) with a propensity to bleed.
- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring]] on a unifying story, and its correction.** The admitting team anchored on a tidy stone-plus-supratherapeutic-INR narrative; the second attending's willingness to step back when supportive care failed is the explicit antidote.
- **[[Diagnostic Momentum|Diagnostic momentum]] resisted.** Reza praises the colleague who "did not let diagnostic momentum take her," re-asked *why is this happening*, and converted a non-contrast into a contrast study — reframing continued treatment as an ongoing diagnostic test.
- **[[Treatment as a Diagnostic Test|Treatment (and imaging) as soft tests]].** The admitting note pre-committed to broadening the differential if supportive management failed, treating the therapeutic response itself as data; likewise a non-contrast "stone" CT is understood as an incomplete test that can falsely reassure.
- **[[Cognitive Autopsy|Cognitive autopsy]] in real time.** Reza narrates his own two misses — never integrating the isolated LDH of 550, and never entertaining a congenital cancer-predisposition syndrome that would make malignancy in a young patient less improbable — modeling reflection without defensiveness.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Diagnostic humility]] as the closing stance.** Both hosts explicitly settle on *unsettled*: living in limbo, using time and serial imaging rather than forcing closure on a lesion that resolved alongside INR correction and exhaustive negative sampling.

## Teaching Discussion

Any bleeding complaint deserves reframing before a differential is built. "Hematuria," "hematochezia," and vaginal bleeding all reduce to *red substance from the perineum*, and the clinician's first task is to confirm the substance is blood and to localize its source, because patients (barring encephalopathy) localize their own bleeding well but observers frequently misattribute it. Once macroscopic urinary bleeding is confirmed, the immediate priority is the *complication* — clot-mediated urinary retention — not the cause; a post-void residual is warranted even without obstructive symptoms.

Microscopic and macroscopic hematuria are the same differential under different weights. The volume of blood, not the list of causes, is what changes: macroscopic hematuria carries roughly a 40% malignancy risk and makes glomerulonephritis unlikely (IgA nephropathy excepted), while microscopic hematuria is under 5% cancer and keeps glomerular disease in play. Working the tract anatomically — bladder, prostate, ureter (lumen, wall, external compression), and kidney (mass/cyst versus arterial, glomerular, or venous vascular lesions) — and then letting the amount of blood and the presence of flank pain localize the process keeps the reasoning disciplined; flank pain in particular pushes toward the upper tract and the parenchyma, where renal cell carcinoma can masquerade as a stone radiating to the groin.

Chronic kidney disease in a young patient is never diabetic or hypertensive nephropathy and is best approached with the intrarenal-AKI schema, since chronic disease resides within the organ and has no pre- or post-renal category. Glomerular, tubular, and vascular causes each contribute, but the interstitium dominates the non-diabetic/non-hypertensive differential; among all of these, ADPKD (cyst rupture) and sickle cell nephropathy (papillary necrosis) bleed most readily, and long-standing end-stage kidneys additionally acquire cysts — distinguished from ADPKD by small rather than large kidney size — that can themselves bleed.

An elevated INR must be interrogated rather than dismissed or over-trusted. It reflects the combined activity of factors 7, 10, 5, and 2, and can rise from reduced production or increased consumption; in a warfarin patient the usual driver is a change in dose, an interacting drug, antibiotics depleting gut vitamin-K flora, or dietary vitamin K, but the two morbid causes — liver disease and DIC — should always be excluded. Critically, profound anticoagulation can produce spontaneous bleeding without any anatomic lesion, as the superwarfarin/synthetic-cannabinoid outbreak demonstrated, so the presence of a supratherapeutic INR neither requires nor excludes a structural culprit. When bleeding persists despite supportive management and a reassuring initial (non-contrast) study, the correct move is to treat the failed response as a diagnostic test, resist diagnostic momentum, and broaden the workup — while accepting that many hematuria evaluations end without a definitive diagnosis and are best resolved by time and serial imaging.

## Handbook Opportunities

- A **gross hematuria** work-up card — reframe to blood per perineum, exclude the complication (clot retention/PVR) first, then the anatomical tract differential weighted by macroscopic volume, and the cystoscopy + CT urogram pathway.
- A **microscopic vs macroscopic hematuria** card — shared differential, the malignancy and glomerulonephritis probability shifts, and when each prioritization applies.
- A **CKD in the young patient** card — the intrarenal-AKI schema, the interstitial-disease emphasis, and the bleeding causes (ADPKD, sickle cell, acquired cystic disease of ESRD).
- An **approach to the elevated INR** card — factor 7/10/5/2 physiology, production vs consumption, the warfarin-specific drivers (dose, drug interactions, antibiotics, diet), and the two morbid causes (liver disease, DIC), plus the superwarfarin lesson that anticoagulation can bleed without a lesion.
- A **diagnostic momentum / treatment-as-a-test** card — the second-attending move: re-ask the question when supportive care fails, upgrade the study (non-contrast → contrast), and use serial imaging when the diagnosis stays open.
