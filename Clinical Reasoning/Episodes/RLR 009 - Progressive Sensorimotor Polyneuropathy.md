## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 69-year-old man carries five-to-six years of ascending numbness, tingling, imbalance, and falls that began in his fingers and toes and was labeled first [[Carpal Tunnel Syndrome|carpal tunnel]] and then [[Radiation-Induced Neuropathy|radiation-induced neuropathy]] from 1995 Hodgkin treatment — a diagnosis that traveled with him across many neurologists, rheumatologists, and oncologists without ever being re-examined. Reza and Rabih rebuild the case from scratch using [[Neurologic Equation|localization × time course]]: a chronic, symmetric, [[Length-Dependent Polyneuropathy|length-dependent]] sensorimotor [[Polyneuropathy|polyneuropathy]] with profound distal atrophy, areflexia, and a foot drop, now creeping into bulbar territory (hoarseness, [[Dysphagia|dysphagia]], 35-lb weight loss). They run a disciplined internist workup — [[SPEP|SPEP]], B1/B12, heavy metals, [[Sjögren Syndrome|Sjögren]] — and, when the [[EMG and Nerve Conduction Study|EMG]] returns *axonal* (not demyelinating), correctly abandon [[CIDP|CIDP]] and the demyelinating paraprotein neuropathies ([[POEMS Syndrome|POEMS]], IgM), converging on a [[Paraneoplastic Neuropathy|paraneoplastic]] or occult hematologic cause. The actual answer sat just outside their schema: [[Hereditary ATTR Amyloidosis|hereditary transthyretin (ATTR) amyloidosis]], confirmed by genetic testing *after* a falsely negative [[Fat Pad Biopsy|fat pad biopsy]]. Reza goes on record that the miss was a knowledge gap — hereditary ATTR was simply not on his [[Polyneuropathy|polyneuropathy]] schema — and that the heart (an unexplained MI without risk factors) was the clue amyloid always leaves behind.

## Pearls

- **In any neuropathy, localize with [[Neurologic Equation|localization × time course]] before reaching for a cause.** Aaron Berkowitz's equation forces two questions first — *where* is the lesion and *how fast* did it evolve — and here the answers (peripheral, symmetric, chronic-progressive) collapse the differential to [[Polyneuropathy|polyneuropathy]], excluding [[Mononeuropathy Multiplex|mononeuropathy multiplex]] and the acute polyneuropathies ([[Guillain-Barré Syndrome|GBS]], porphyria, heavy metals) in one move.
- **Profound distal muscle atrophy with areflexia is a lower-motor-neuron, peripheral-nerve signature — and it can make reflexes uninterpretable.** With no muscle bulk left to answer the [[Reflex Arc|reflex arc]], you cannot use hypo- vs hyper-reflexia to sort [[Upper vs Lower Motor Neuron|upper from lower motor neuron]]; the atrophy itself, plus preserved bowel/bladder, is what localizes to peripheral nerve rather than cord.
- **When a neuropathy climbs into bulbar territory, escalate — the syndrome now threatens the airway.** Hoarseness (a [[Recurrent Laryngeal Nerve|recurrent laryngeal nerve]] clue), [[Dysphagia|dysphagia]], and weight loss mean the disease has reached the head; before blaming the nerve, ask whether a single [[Mediastinal Mass|mediastinal lesion]] could compress esophagus and bronchus while a paraneoplastic process attacks the nerve.
- **The [[EMG and Nerve Conduction Study|EMG]] is the MRI of the peripheral nerve, and axonal vs demyelinating is the highest-yield fork.** An *axonal* result here kills [[CIDP|CIDP]] and the demyelinating paraprotein neuropathies ([[POEMS Syndrome|POEMS]], IgM-related), and it fits [[Hereditary ATTR Amyloidosis|hereditary ATTR]] — the diagnosis is being narrowed by physiology, not just serology.
- **A negative [[Fat Pad Biopsy|fat pad biopsy]] does not exclude amyloidosis when pretest probability is high.** Sensitivity runs ~90%, so a negative test in a wasted patient with little subcutaneous fat should prompt the next test, not closure; here [[TTR Genetic Testing|TTR genetic testing]] sealed the diagnosis after the fat pad was negative.
- **Amyloid loves the heart — an MI with no risk factors is a clue, not a coincidence.** A never-smoker, non-diabetic, normolipidemic man with a first MI at 60 and infiltrative-looking cardiac disease should raise [[Cardiac Amyloidosis|cardiac amyloid]]; testing has risen because [[Hereditary ATTR Amyloidosis|ATTR]] is now treatable, and a "worse-than-typical, faster-than-typical" neuropathy is the phenotype that should trigger the test.

## Problem Representation

A 69-year-old man with treated Hodgkin lymphoma (neck ± chest radiation, 1995), premature coronary disease without atherosclerotic risk factors (first MI at 60), atrial fibrillation/flutter with a pacemaker, a chronic cough, and a 35-lb weight loss presents with five-to-six years of chronic, progressive, symmetric, length-dependent numbness and tingling ascending from fingers and toes, now with severe distal-predominant muscle atrophy and weakness, glove-and-stocking sensory loss to mid-calf and mid-forearm, impaired vibration/proprioception, diffuse areflexia, a left foot drop, and emerging bulbar signs (hoarseness, dysphagia); EMG shows a severe length-dependent sensorimotor axonal polyradiculoneuropathy, and after a negative fat pad biopsy, genetic testing confirms hereditary transthyretin (ATTR) amyloidosis.

## Case Narrative

Lisa Sanders introduces the case with Margaret Means, a PGY-4 pediatric neurology resident who met the patient in the Penn neuromuscular clinic. The patient is a 69-year-old man who, beginning in 2016, noticed tingling and numbness in his fingers and tripping over his feet. An initial neurologist diagnosed [[Carpal Tunnel Syndrome|carpal tunnel syndrome]] and prescribed wrist splints (no benefit) plus balance physical therapy, then reframed the picture as a neuropathy attributed to the radiation he received for [[Hodgkin Lymphoma|Hodgkin lymphoma]] in 1995. Over the next five-to-six years the neuropathy progressed until he could no longer button a shirt, open a door, or grip a jar; he switched to slip-on shoes and gave up golf after a club flew out of his hands mid-swing. He saw many neurologists, several rheumatologists, and a couple of oncologists across South Carolina; one rheumatologist tested for every rheumatologic disease she could name, gave two weeks of high-dose steroids without benefit, and referred him onward. He ultimately reached Penn, where a neurologist saw him by telehealth and then in person before sending him to the neuromuscular clinic.

Reza opens with the [[Neurologic Equation|neurologic equation]] he credits to Aaron Berkowitz: a diagnosis in neurology equals **[[Localization|localization]] × time course**. The time course is chronic and progressive over years. Converting "numbness and tingling" to paresthesia and a [[Peripheral Neuropathy|peripheral neuropathy]], he sorts peripheral neuropathy into three buckets — [[Mononeuropathy|mononeuropathy]] (e.g., carpal tunnel, excluded by the multifocal, progressive course), [[Mononeuropathy Multiplex|mononeuropathy multiplex]] (the vasculitic wrist-drop/foot-drop pattern), and [[Polyneuropathy|polyneuropathy]] — and lands on a symmetric polyneuropathy. He notes the useful corollary that *acute* polyneuropathies are few ([[Guillain-Barré Syndrome|GBS]], heavy-metal toxicity, acute intermittent porphyria), so a chronic course broadens rather than narrows the list; he keeps the central nervous system on the table pending imaging and asks whether there are motor and autonomic features beyond the sensory ones.

Rabih layers on the pertinent negatives and the falls. The absence of urinary and bowel symptoms argues against an atypical [[Myelopathy|myelopathy]], and intact mentation and movement argue against a brain process. The falls, he reasons, hint at either subtle weakness or advanced large-fiber sensory involvement — a [[Sensory Ataxia|sensory ataxia]] from loss of proprioception and vibration, the afferent limb of the [[Reflex Arc|reflex arc]], which would produce depressed reflexes despite preserved strength. He frames two parallel pathways: confirm peripheral localization and characterize it by [[EMG and Nerve Conduction Study|EMG]] (axonal vs demyelinating), and exclude a central mimic by MRI if the exam demands it.

More history arrives. The past medical history includes a first MI in 2012 and a second with stents in 2017, atrial fibrillation/flutter with a pacemaker (2017), a persistent pleural effusion after a bad fall with rib fractures, a chronic cough attributed to asthma but unresponsive to bronchodilators, and the 1995 Hodgkin radiation. Medications are only apixaban, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and vitamin D. Review of systems reveals a 35-lb weight loss (175 → 140, BMI ~19), the chronic cough, progressive hoarseness, and dysphagia with choking. Rabih pulls the cough, [[Dysphonia|dysphonia]], and [[Dysphagia|dysphagia]] toward the neurologic syndrome (microaspiration, diaphragmatic weakness, oropharyngeal dysphagia) but cautions against over-reading: a [[Mediastinal Mass|mediastinal lesion]] compressing esophagus and bronchus — recurrent Hodgkin, or a thymoma — could produce the same triad and even drive a [[Paraneoplastic Neuropathy|paraneoplastic]] process. Reza treats the neuropathy as the case's center of gravity (the most debilitating problem), insists the pulmonary and bulbar findings must be *explained by or clues to* the final diagnosis rather than dismissed, and reads the weight loss as a possible marker of an inflammatory (infection/cancer/autoimmune) syndrome — while conceding the dysphagia alone could account for it.

Margaret delivers the exam. The patient is thin with a hoarse voice and occasional cough; mental status and all cranial nerves are intact except the hoarseness, with no ptosis or ophthalmoplegia. There is striking distal-predominant muscle atrophy (intrinsic hand muscles, forearms, distal legs to the calves) without fasciculations, distal-worse-than-proximal weakness (ankle dorsiflexors and toe extensors the weakest, barely flickering; 3/5 in the hands; 4/5 at the hips), glove-and-stocking loss of light touch and pinprick to mid-calf and mid-forearm bilaterally, reduced vibration and proprioception, and near-complete **areflexia** (only a trace left brachioradialis). Gait shows a left foot drop with a slapping quality but no dysmetria or ataxic instability. Margaret also flags the striking absence of cardiac risk factors — never smoked, not diabetic, no hyperlipidemia, first MI at 60 — inviting the question of whether the heart belongs to the same disease.

Reza reframes with the exam in hand. The reflexes cannot arbitrate [[Upper vs Lower Motor Neuron|upper vs lower motor neuron]] because there is no muscle left to complete the arc; but the profound atrophy proves lower-motor-neuron/peripheral involvement, and the intact bowel/bladder argues against cord. He works the foot drop ("toes to the nose") down its localization chain — L5 → sciatic → peroneal — and, finding it non-anatomic and multifocal over five years, pushes into rarer causes ([[Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis|ALS]], vasculitis), neither fitting. Rabih takes the hoarseness as evidence the syndrome has reached bulbar territory, raising the airway stakes, and offers the generalist's toolbox for a befuddling neuropathy: heavy-metal and nutritional (B1, B12) exposures, scrutiny of any chemotherapy, and the "two S's" — an [[SPEP|SPEP]] for cryptic hematologic neuropathies and [[Sjögren Syndrome|Sjögren]] (often seronegative when it presents as neuropathy, so ask about sicca symptoms). Lisa corrects one assumption: the patient received radiation only, never chemotherapy.

The data close the loop. The patient had already had multiple normal-workup EMGs; the repeat shows a **severe length-dependent sensorimotor axonal polyradiculoneuropathy**. Rabih seizes on *axonal*: it argues against [[CIDP|CIDP]]/AIDP and against the demyelinating paraprotein neuropathies ([[POEMS Syndrome|POEMS]], IgM-related), and it reduces the pretest for a late-presenting [[Charcot-Marie-Tooth Disease|Charcot-Marie-Tooth]]. Drawing on Berkowitz's approach to non-compressive radiculopathy, he flags infections (herpesviruses, Lyme), autoimmune causes (sarcoid, Sjögren), and — most worrying — a paraneoplastic cause, proposing a sicca history/lip biopsy, a chest film for lymphadenopathy, and a search for an occult tumor. Reza shares Berkowitz's polyneuropathy schema on screen and reasons down it to a neoplastic-associated or rare-hereditary cause, noting the SPEP was negative and the steroid non-response lowers the odds of an immune-mediated process. The team's plan was a repeat SPEP (still negative), an in-office [[Fat Pad Biopsy|fat pad biopsy]] (negative), then a PET-CT and possible nerve biopsy — but they also sent **[[TTR Genetic Testing|TTR genetic testing]]**, which returned **positive for hereditary transthyretin (ATTR) amyloidosis**.

Margaret closes the reasoning: the fat pad was negative partly because the patient had almost no subcutaneous fat left, and the genetics sealed it. The family history — a brother with "multiple myeloma" (possibly misread amyloid) and parents who died of MIs and heart failure — fits an autosomal-dominant, cardiotropic disease. She underscores the practical lesson: testing for [[Hereditary ATTR Amyloidosis|ATTR]] has increased because effective treatments now exist, and a neuropathy that is more severe or more rapidly progressive than typical should trigger the test.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Data point | Hypothesis raised/adjusted | Direction of update |
|---|---|---|
| Chronic progressive, symmetric, ascending paresthesias | Polyneuropathy over mononeuropathy/mononeuropathy multiplex | Strongly raised; frames the case |
| Multifocal + progressive course | Carpal tunnel / single mononeuropathy | Excluded |
| Years-long time course | Acute polyneuropathies (GBS, porphyria, heavy metals) | Excluded; chronic list broadened |
| Intact bowel/bladder, normal mentation | Myelopathy / central process | Lowered |
| Falls, reduced vibration/proprioception | Large-fiber sensory ataxia | Raised |
| Profound distal atrophy, areflexia | Lower-motor-neuron / peripheral-nerve localization | Confirmed peripheral; reflexes uninterpretable |
| MI at 60 with no atherosclerotic risk factors | Infiltrative/systemic disease involving the heart | Raised (clue, per Margaret) |
| Hoarseness, dysphagia, weight loss | Bulbar spread vs mediastinal compression/paraneoplastic | Both raised; escalates urgency |
| Steroid non-response | Immune-mediated (CIDP, vasculitis, Sjögren) | Lowered |
| Negative SPEP (×2) | Paraprotein-associated neuropathy | Lowered |
| **Axonal** EMG (not demyelinating) | CIDP/AIDP, POEMS, IgM neuropathy | Strongly lowered |
| Axonal + polyradicular pattern | Paraneoplastic, hereditary, infiltrative | Raised |
| Negative fat pad biopsy (little subcutaneous fat) | Amyloidosis | *Not* excluded — low sensitivity in context |
| Positive TTR genetic test | Hereditary ATTR amyloidosis | Confirmed |
| Family history (AD pattern, cardiac deaths, "myeloma" brother) | Hereditary ATTR amyloidosis | Retrospectively supportive |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Diagnostic Momentum]] and [[Anchoring Bias|anchoring]] on radiation-induced neuropathy.** The 1995 Hodgkin radiation supplied a tidy, unfalsifiable label — [[Radiation-Induced Neuropathy|radiation-induced neuropathy]] — that every subsequent clinician inherited and none re-opened, letting a length-dependent sensorimotor process ride for five-to-six years. Lisa notes the literature on radiation neuropathy is thin and doesn't fit this phenotype; the anchor persisted on plausibility, not evidence. The earlier [[Carpal Tunnel Syndrome|carpal tunnel]] label is the same error in miniature — the common diagnosis for hand paresthesia, retained past its expiration.
- **[[Search Satisficing]] — calling off the hunt at the first fitting cause.** Reza's explicit self-audit is that the miss was not faulty reasoning but a **[[Illness Script|schema gap]]**: hereditary ATTR was absent from his polyneuropathy list, so the search satisficed at paraneoplastic/hereditary-unspecified. He goes "on record" that he knew AL amyloid attacked nerve but not that hereditary ATTR did the same — a knowledge boundary, not a cognitive slip.
- **Resisting [[Premature Closure]] after a negative test.** The [[Fat Pad Biopsy|fat pad biopsy]] returned negative, yet the team sent [[TTR Genetic Testing|genetic testing]] anyway because pretest probability stayed high and the negative was explained by cachexia. Lisa's point that fat pad sensitivity is ~90% — good, but not good enough to override a strong prior — is the anti-premature-closure lesson of the episode.
- **[[Bayesian Reasoning|Bayesian]] use of the EMG as a physiologic discriminator.** *Axonal vs demyelinating* did more diagnostic work than any serology: the axonal result subtracted CIDP, POEMS, and IgM neuropathy in one stroke and re-weighted toward infiltrative/hereditary/paraneoplastic causes — physiology narrowing the differential ahead of pathology.
- **[[Diseases Travel Together|Diseases travel together]] — reading the heart, cough, and voice as one disease.** Reza's rule that the final diagnosis must *explain or be clued by* the extra-neural findings is what keeps the risk-factor-free MI, the chronic cough, the hoarseness, and the weight loss in play; amyloid's cardiotropism ultimately ties the heart to the nerve. The competing frame — a single [[Mediastinal Mass|mediastinal lesion]] linking dysphagia, dysphonia, and cough — is the disciplined alternative he refuses to discard prematurely.
- **[[Cognitive Autopsy|Cognitive autopsy]].** The episode ends with an explicit retrospective: the heart was the clue (Margaret's assist), the phenotype was "worse and faster than typical," and the fix is to add hereditary ATTR to the [[Polyneuropathy|polyneuropathy]] schema so the next such patient is tested up front.

## Teaching Discussion

- Berkowitz's [[Neurologic Equation|neurologic equation]] (localization × time course) is the entry move for any neuropathy: settle *where* and *how fast* before *why*. Here it yields a chronic, symmetric, length-dependent sensorimotor polyneuropathy and excludes both mononeuropathy multiplex and the short list of acute polyneuropathies.
- Reflexes lose their discriminating power when muscle bulk is gone. Severe distal atrophy means the [[Reflex Arc|reflex arc]] has no effector, so areflexia cannot be read as evidence for a particular level; the atrophy itself localizes to lower motor neuron/peripheral nerve, and preserved bowel/bladder argues against myelopathy.
- Bulbar creep (hoarseness via the [[Recurrent Laryngeal Nerve|recurrent laryngeal nerve]], dysphagia) marks a severe, ascending syndrome and raises the risk of aspiration and cardiopulmonary collapse — a reason to move faster. Always ask whether a single [[Mediastinal Mass|mediastinal]] lesion could explain the bulbar triad structurally before attributing it all to nerve.
- The generalist's befuddled-neuropathy toolbox: nutritional (B1, B12), toxic (heavy metals, neurotoxic chemotherapy such as cisplatin — though rarely this severe), and the "two S's," [[SPEP|SPEP]] and [[Sjögren Syndrome|Sjögren]] (frequently seronegative as a neuropathy, so ask about sicca symptoms and consider lip biopsy). Refer for [[EMG and Nerve Conduction Study|EMG]] — the MRI of the peripheral nerve.
- Axonal vs demyelinating is the single most useful EMG fork. Demyelinating points toward CIDP/AIDP and the paraprotein neuropathies ([[POEMS Syndrome|POEMS]], IgM); axonal points away from them and toward toxic, hereditary, paraneoplastic, and infiltrative ([[Amyloidosis|amyloid]]) causes.
- [[Hereditary ATTR Amyloidosis|Hereditary ATTR amyloidosis]] is an autosomal-dominant, adult-onset, length-dependent axonal polyneuropathy with prominent autonomic and **cardiac** involvement; it can masquerade as CIDP or an idiopathic neuropathy and is under-diagnosed. A fat pad aspirate (~90% sensitive) can miss it, especially in cachexia — genetic testing is decisive. Because disease-modifying therapy now exists, a neuropathy that is more severe or more rapidly progressive than expected warrants testing.
- Read comorbidities as possible parts of the syndrome: a premature MI without atherosclerotic risk factors should raise infiltrative [[Cardiac Amyloidosis|cardiac disease]]; a "multiple myeloma" in the family may be misremembered amyloidosis, and an autosomal-dominant pattern with cardiac deaths supports a hereditary amyloid.

## Handbook Opportunities

- A **hereditary ATTR amyloidosis card** — when to suspect (severe/rapid axonal neuropathy, autonomic and cardiac involvement, family history), how to test (genetics decisive; fat pad can miss), and why it matters now (treatment exists).
- An **approach-to-polyneuropathy card** — localization × time course, the three buckets, and the axonal-vs-demyelinating fork with a cause list under each arm.
- A **red-flags-for-cardiac-amyloid entry** — unexplained MI/HF without risk factors, low-voltage ECG with LVH, conduction disease and pacemaker, plus neuropathy overlap.
- A **fat pad / tissue biopsy sensitivity card** — test characteristics for amyloid and the cachexia false-negative caveat.
- A **bulbar-red-flags-in-neuropathy note** — hoarseness/dysphagia as markers of severe ascending disease and airway risk, with the mediastinal-compression alternative.
