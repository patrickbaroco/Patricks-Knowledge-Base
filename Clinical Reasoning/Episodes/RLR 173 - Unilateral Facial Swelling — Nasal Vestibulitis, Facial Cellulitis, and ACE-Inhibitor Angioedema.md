## Pearls

- **Facial (any) swelling is edema — fluid that has left the vessels — so ask what the fluid is: water, pus, or blood.** The **[[Blood Pus Water|"blood/pus/water"]]** schema (the same one used for splenomegaly and pulmonary consolidation) maps swelling to **[[Angioedema|water/angioedema]]**, **[[Facial Cellulitis|pus/WBC/infection]]**, or **[[Facial Trauma|blood/RBC/trauma]]** — and for the lip, add **[[AL Amyloidosis|AL amyloid]]** — turning an unfamiliar chief concern into a short, mechanistic differential.
- **Face infections enter through a structural vulnerability, and there is a hierarchy: teeth, then salivary glands, then the nose.** When the teeth are clean, express the **[[Sialadenitis|salivary (parotid/submandibular) ducts]]** and, crucially, inspect the **[[Nasal Vestibulitis|nasal vestibule]]** — the way an infected leg reveals a tiny skin break, the face reveals its **[[Structural Vulnerability|portal of entry]]** (Reza's **[[Analogical Reasoning|parotid-duct-as-cystic-duct]]** analogy: obstruction → abrupt swelling → sialadenitis).
- **Nasal vestibulitis is a Staph/MRSA infection of the nasal vestibule — keep it on the facial-swelling list even when the patient doesn't mention the nose.** We all carry **[[Staphylococcus aureus|S. aureus/MRSA]]** in the nares; **[[Nasal Vestibulitis|vestibulitis]]** is predisposed by **nasal trauma/picking**, can spread across the face, and — via the **[[Danger Triangle|danger triangle]]** — intracranially, so a purulent nostril (found here only by luck) is the tell.
- **Localize the *direction* of the infection: inside-out versus outside-in.** An **[[Inside-Out vs Outside-In|inside-out]]** process rises from a deep/mucosal nidus (vestibulitis, sinus, salivary — or even a dorsal-root-ganglion **[[Herpes Zoster|zoster]]**, which can present *sine herpete*), while an **outside-in** process starts at a skin portal (**[[Eczema|eczema]] at the ear**, a bite) and threatens to go deep — and **[[Erysipelas|erysipelas]]** (sharply demarcated, raised) is always worth considering.
- **The face is precious territory — keep a low threshold for imaging.** Because the eye and brain are next door (like the tight closed space of hand **[[Flexor Tenosynovitis|flexor tenosynovitis]]**), extensive facial swelling warrants defining its full extent (CT for deep-space infection), and a sharply **dermatomal** distribution should prompt an eye exam for **[[Herpes Zoster Ophthalmicus|zoster ophthalmicus]]** and attention to immune status.
- **ACE-inhibitor angioedema is bradykinin-mediated, not histaminergic — it does not respond to steroids, antihistamines, or epinephrine.** Management of **[[ACE Inhibitor Angioedema|ACEi angioedema]]** is to **stop the drug** and **protect the airway** (watch for **[[Laryngeal Edema|laryngeal edema]]**), reserving **[[Bradykinin Angioedema|bradykinin-targeted agents]]** (icatibant, C1-INH) for severe/airway disease; it can occur **remote** from initiation, **ARB cross-reactivity** is small, and **trauma may unmask** it — and beware the **[[Therapeutic Momentum|momentum]]** (patient/nurse expectation) that drives reflexive, useless anaphylaxis treatment.

## Problem Representation

Three patients present on the same day, each with abrupt-onset unilateral facial swelling, illustrating a single framework (swelling as edema of water, pus, or blood, and facial infection entering through a structural vulnerability). Patient 1 has one day of atraumatic left-face pain and swelling over the nasolabial fold and jaw that mimics an odontogenic infection but has normal teeth and salivary glands; a chance drop of purulent nasal discharge reveals MRSA nasal vestibulitis with facial spread, managed (against advice for admission/imaging) with oral doxycycline and return precautions. Patient 2 has abrupt left-face pain, swelling, and redness with a fever of 38.5°C, a leukocytosis of 14,000, and a CT showing facial cellulitis without deep-space infection, traced to a portal of entry in eczematous, excoriated skin at the top of the ear (exacerbated by glasses/beard-trapped moisture), treated with vancomycin plus oral cephalexin and doxycycline. Patient 3 has abrupt painless left-lip swelling on an ACE inhibitor — isolated bradykinin-mediated angioedema without laryngeal, cutaneous, or gastrointestinal involvement (a blood speck from a self-inflicted post-swelling bite) — which does not respond to the steroids, antihistamine, and epinephrine administered under therapeutic momentum, resolving with observation and ACE-inhibitor discontinuation. The unifying teaching is a mechanistic edema schema, the structural-vulnerability model of facial infection, the inside-out versus outside-in localization, and the imperative to treat the face cautiously.

## Case Narrative

*A three-case "spot the swelling" episode: Rabih presents three same-day patients with abrupt unilateral facial swelling; Reza builds a shared framework and reasons through each.*

**The framework.** Reza reframes an uncommon chief concern (he sees true unilateral facial swelling ~once a year) mechanistically: swelling is **[[Facial Swelling|edema]]** — fluid leaving the vessels into the interstitium — so, as with a pulmonary consolidation, ask *what* the fluid is: **[[Blood Pus Water|water, WBC, or RBC]]** (angioedema, infection, trauma). Each maps to a category — **[[Angioedema|angioedema]]** (water; note the face is *anti-gravity*, an odd place for bland edema), **[[Facial Cellulitis|infection]]** (pus; skin/soft tissue, dental, sinus, salivary), or **[[Facial Trauma|trauma]]** (blood) — and his first branch is **traumatic vs atraumatic**.

**Case 1 — looks odontogenic, is nasal vestibulitis.** A man has one day of **atraumatic** left-face pain and swelling over the **nasolabial fold and jaw**, tender, mildly red. Reza's reflex — and Rabih's — is an **[[Odontogenic Infection|odontogenic]]** source (jaw involvement, redness), but the **teeth are clean**. Reza deploys **[[Analogical Reasoning|analogical reasoning]]**: an abrupt swelling near the parotid could be **[[Sialadenitis|sialadenitis/parotitis]]** — the parotid duct behaving like the cystic duct (obstruction → swelling) — so he'd **express the salivary ducts** and, failing that, **CT the face** to map the surrounding structures. The submandibular and parotid glands are also normal; the diagnosis comes by **luck** — a drop of **pus from the left nostril** during the exam, revealing extensive **[[Nasal Vestibulitis|nasal vestibulitis]]** with facial spread, **MRSA** on swab. Rabih's lesson: after **teeth**, the next facial **[[Structural Vulnerability|portals of vulnerability]]** are the **salivary glands** and the **nose** (we all carry **[[Staphylococcus aureus|Staph/MRSA]]** in the nares; picking/trauma is the nidus). The patient declined labs/CT/IV, chose **oral doxycycline** with return precautions.

**Case 2 — facial cellulitis from an eczema portal.** Two hours later, a man presents with abrupt left-face pain, swelling, and **redness**, **fever 38.5°C**, mild tachycardia. Reza escalates **infection** (pus/WBC) and asks the key question — is the portal **on the surface or inside**? He notes the near-**dermatomal** look raises **[[Herpes Zoster|herpes zoster]]** (including **[[Herpes Zoster Ophthalmicus|zoster sine herpete]]** — no vesicles), keeps **[[Erysipelas|erysipelas]]** (sharply demarcated, raised — not quite this) on the list, and — given no exposures — briefly considers contact dermatitis (water). He'd get a **CBC**, keep a **low threshold for CT**, evaluate the **eyes** if dermatomal, and check **immune status**. Workup: **WBC 14,000**, CT showing **[[Facial Cellulitis|facial cellulitis]]** without deep-space infection; treated with a dose of **vancomycin** plus outpatient **cephalexin + doxycycline**. The portal, found only on **zooming in**, is **[[Eczema|eczema]] at the top of the ear** — excoriated from scratching, with **glasses/beard-trapped moisture** — a **[[Structural Vulnerability|skin breakdown]]** serving as the nidus (Rabih wondering about an additional structural immunodeficiency). Reza crystallizes the contrast: **[[Inside-Out vs Outside-In|Case 1 started internal and rose to the surface; Case 2 started external and threatens to go deep.]]**

**Case 3 — ACE-inhibitor angioedema.** A man has **abrupt painless left-lip swelling**. Reza reflexively thinks **[[Angioedema|angioedema]]**, but pauses at a **speck of blood** on the lip — could this be a bite/trauma with secondary oral-flora infection? The sequence clarifies: the lip **ballooned first**, and he **nicked it afterward** while inspecting it. On an **ACE inhibitor**, this is **[[ACE Inhibitor Angioedema|ACEi-induced bradykinin angioedema]]**. Reza teaches the mechanism: **[[Bradykinin Angioedema|bradykinin-mediated]]** (ACE normally degrades bradykinin; ARBs, blocking the receptor, don't raise it, so **ARB cross-reactivity is small**), it can occur **remote** from initiation, and management is largely **supportive** — **stop the ACE**, watch for **[[Laryngeal Edema|laryngeal edema]]/airway** compromise, and reserve **bradykinin-targeted therapy** for severe disease; refer to allergy if future ACE/ARB is needed. He confirms **no laryngeal, cutaneous, or GI involvement**. Rabih then makes the episode's honest confession: despite knowing the patient would **not benefit** from **[[Therapeutic Momentum|steroids, antihistamines, or epinephrine]]** (this is not a mast-cell/anaphylactoid reaction), the **momentum** — patient expectation, nurse expectation, and his own end-of-day fatigue — led him to give **solumedrol, fexofenadine, and even epinephrine**; the patient was observed two hours and went home well. Reza empathizes (harmless, and defensible as "it *might* progress"), notes the RLR **[[RLR 035 - Airway Obstruction and ACE-Inhibitor Angioedema|prior ACE-angioedema episode]]** and that **trauma may unmask** ACE angioedema, and closes on the humility theme — how much of medicine we "know" but don't (the anti-NMDA-receptor-encephalitis-once-called-psychosis parallel).

**Takeaways.** Reza's synthesis: run facial (and lip) swelling through **[[Blood Pus Water|blood/pus/water]]** (plus amyloid for the lip); always consider **infection** and its **[[Inside-Out vs Outside-In|direction]]** (inside-out vs outside-in); and treat the **[[Face is Precious Territory|face like a hand with flexor tenosynovitis]]** — a high-stakes, closed, precious territory (eye, brain) where a **low threshold for imaging** is wisdom, not excess.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Blood Pus Water|Blood/pus/water]] edema schema ([[Schema-Based Reasoning|schema-based reasoning]]).** Swelling → what fluid left the vessels (angioedema/infection/trauma; + amyloid for the lip) — the same schema as splenomegaly and consolidation, applied to the face.
- **[[Structural Vulnerability|Structural vulnerability]] and its hierarchy ([[Anatomical Approach|anatomical approach]]).** Facial infection enters through a portal — **teeth > [[Sialadenitis|salivary glands]] > [[Nasal Vestibulitis|nose]]** (or a **skin** break) — so a clean mouth mandates examining the ducts and the nasal vestibule.
- **[[Analogical Reasoning|Analogical reasoning]].** Parotid-duct-as-cystic-duct (obstruction → sialadenitis), the leg's tiny skin break as the model for a facial portal, and the hand's **[[Flexor Tenosynovitis|flexor tenosynovitis]]** as the model for facial-space stakes.
- **[[Inside-Out vs Outside-In|Direction of spread]].** Inside-out (vestibulitis/sinus/salivary/**[[Herpes Zoster|DRG zoster]]**) vs outside-in (**[[Eczema|eczema]]**/bite) — a clean way to localize and to gauge what's threatened.
- **[[Face is Precious Territory|Precious territory]] ([[Base Rate|base rate]] + caution).** Proximity to eye and brain (the **[[Danger Triangle|danger triangle]]**) justifies a low imaging threshold even when the base rate is benign.
- **[[Bradykinin Angioedema|Mechanism dictates therapy]] ([[Test Interpretation|test/therapy interpretation]]).** **[[ACE Inhibitor Angioedema|ACEi angioedema]]** is bradykinin-, not histamine-, mediated, so steroids/antihistamines/epi don't work; recognizing the mechanism is the whole management decision.
- **[[Therapeutic Momentum|Therapeutic momentum]] and [[Diagnostic Humility|humility]].** The honest admission that expectation, culture, and fatigue drove non-indicated treatment — and the closing reflection on how much of medicine is still unknown — models candid self-awareness.

## Teaching Discussion

- Approach any facial or lip swelling as edema and ask what fluid has left the vessels — water (angioedema), pus or white cells (infection), or blood (trauma), adding amyloid for isolated lip swelling — using the same blood/pus/water schema applied to splenomegaly and pulmonary consolidation.
- Localize facial infection to a structural portal of entry in a hierarchy — teeth, then salivary glands, then the nose — so that a clean mouth prompts expressing the salivary ducts and inspecting the nasal vestibule, since patients may not volunteer nasal symptoms.
- Recognize nasal vestibulitis as a Staph/MRSA infection of the nasal vestibule, predisposed by nasal trauma or picking, capable of spreading across the face and intracranially through the danger triangle.
- Distinguish the direction of a facial process — inside-out (a deep or mucosal nidus surfacing, including dorsal-root-ganglion zoster that can appear without vesicles) versus outside-in (a skin portal such as eczema or a bite heading deeper) — and keep erysipelas and contact dermatitis on the differential.
- Treat the face as precious territory given its proximity to the eye and brain, maintaining a low threshold for CT to define the extent of extensive swelling, evaluating the eye when a dermatomal zoster is possible, and considering immune status.
- Manage ACE-inhibitor angioedema as a bradykinin-mediated process that does not respond to steroids, antihistamines, or epinephrine — stopping the drug, protecting the airway, and reserving icatibant or C1-inhibitor for severe or airway-threatening disease — while recognizing it can occur remote from initiation, that ARB cross-reactivity is small, and that trauma may unmask it.
- Guard against therapeutic momentum: patient and staff expectations (and clinician fatigue) can drive reflexive, non-indicated anaphylaxis treatment, so name the true mechanism and, when giving in, do so knowingly rather than mistaking the diagnosis.
