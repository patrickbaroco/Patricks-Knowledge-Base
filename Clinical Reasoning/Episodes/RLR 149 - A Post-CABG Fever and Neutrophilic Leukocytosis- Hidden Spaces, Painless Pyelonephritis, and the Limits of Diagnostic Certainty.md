## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A man in his 70s, three weeks after CABG and now recovering in acute rehab, develops fever and neutrophilic leukocytosis with no localizing symptoms. Reza narrates the case nearly in real time as it unfolds over several days, working through Robbie's "hidden spaces" framework (occult disease in the mediastinum and pelvis), a reflex not to accept reassurance from a "clean" exam alone, and an eventual pragmatic treatment of Pseudomonas found on urine culture — all without ever reaching full diagnostic certainty. The episode closes on a philosophical note: a diagnosis is a useful label, not the actual goal; the actual goal is shepherding the patient safely through the illness.

## Pearls

- A fever alone in an otherwise well, communicative, non-immunosuppressed patient is common and not intrinsically alarming — but the hospital/post-surgical context raises the pretest probability of a real bacterial process enough to change the calculus.
- Neutrophil-predominant leukocytosis is classically taught as favoring bacterial over viral infection, but it can occur in some viral infections too — it narrows the differential, it doesn't settle it.
- "Hidden spaces" framework: certain anatomic compartments (the mediastinum, the pelvis) can harbor substantial occult disease — 10-15 cm in the mediastinum, up to ~20 cm in the pelvis — before becoming clinically overt. This is why thymomas and ovarian cancers often present very late.
- Post-sternotomy mediastinitis classically presents in a 2-4 week post-operative window and can be deceptively subtle — mild chest pain, low-grade fever, a benign-looking incision — analogous to the reassuring surface exam of early necrotizing fasciitis masking serious underlying pathology.
- "Cost-conscious, astute clinician" principle: pursue diagnostic hypotheses in an order that matches how sick or stable the patient currently is. An unstable patient goes straight to definitive imaging; a stable patient can tolerate a more sequential workup.
- Mnemonic caveat: "no pyuria, no bacteriuria, no symptoms = no UTI" is a useful screening heuristic, but it has recognized exceptions — TB, severe immunosuppression, and painless bilateral pyelonephritis can all present atypically.
- Painless bilateral pyelonephritis and prostatitis are both diagnoses that can present with minimal or no urinary symptoms, particularly in diabetic patients.
- Chronic, incidental pyuria and glucosuria are common in older adults and diabetics and can be a diagnostic "fake out" — present without representing the actual driver of a current fever.
- Radiographic pneumonia resolves more slowly than clinical symptoms do; an old, stable infiltrate on chest x-ray from weeks prior is not diagnostically useful for a new presentation.
- A trending white count matters more than a single value or the degree of fever — a rising WBC over serial measurements is a stronger signal to act on than an isolated temperature reading.
- Anaerobic coverage is not standard for post-sternotomy mediastinitis, unlike descending or oropharyngeal-source mediastinitis, which do require anaerobic activity.
- IV contrast nephropathy is far less supported by the literature than commonly assumed in clinical practice — a useful reminder not to let contrast-avoidance reflexively delay indicated imaging.
- Whether to treat empirically or wait for culture data before imaging often reflects practice-style variation (CT access speed, day of week, surgical input, patient preference) rather than a difference in medical knowledge — "showing your work" matters more than being retrospectively proven right.
- Culture-negative bacteremia is real — even Staph aureus bacteremia has a meaningful false-negative rate on blood cultures — so a negative blood culture does not fully exclude bacteremia.
- Patients can and do spontaneously clear bacterial infections, including UTIs, without antibiotics — a reminder that clinical improvement after starting antibiotics doesn't prove the antibiotics were necessary.
- A diagnosis is a useful predictive and communication tool, not an end in itself; the clinician's core job is to shepherd the patient to safety, which does not always require diagnostic certainty.

## Problem Representation

A man in his 70s, three weeks status post coronary artery bypass grafting and currently in inpatient acute rehabilitation, develops an acute fever (102°F) with neutrophil-predominant leukocytosis (WBC 15,000) but is entirely asymptomatic and non-toxic-appearing on exam, with a chronically tender but non-worsening sternal incision, prompting a stepwise reasoning process through post-sternotomy mediastinitis, painless pyelonephritis/prostatitis, and asymptomatic bacteriuria as competing explanations, ultimately trending toward a markedly higher leukocytosis (peak near 30,000) without a single unifying diagnosis, treated pragmatically with empiric antibiotics and eventually narrowed to a completed course for Pseudomonas isolated on urine culture despite negative imaging for mediastinitis and negative blood cultures.

## Case Narrative

Reza, currently rounding on the medicine consult service, is asked to see a man in his 70s who is three weeks removed from coronary artery bypass grafting and is now recovering in an inpatient acute rehabilitation bed (a distinct status from a general inpatient hospital bed). The night before the consult, the patient spiked a fever to 102°F with a heart rate of 96 and normal blood pressure; his white count came back at 15,000. The PM&R resident covering him — praised by Reza for the thoroughness of the workup — had already sent a respiratory viral swab (negative for COVID, flu, and RSV), a urinalysis and urine culture, a chest x-ray, and additional basic labs before Reza arrived. On evaluation, the patient is entirely asymptomatic: no chest pain, no back pain, no diarrhea, no cough, no urinary symptoms, and no cognitive or psychiatric barrier that would make him an unreliable historian.

Robbie's opening framing, working through the case with Reza in real time, starts by asking whether this is genuinely an emergency at all. A single 102°F fever in an otherwise well, conversant patient is common and not automatically alarming — but the hospital setting raises the baseline pretest probability of something real, and the neutrophil-predominant pattern of the leukocytosis nudges the differential toward bacterial causes, while acknowledging some viral infections can produce neutrophilia too.

When Reza specifically asks how the CABG history should shape his thinking, Robbie broadens the frame beyond the heart itself: any invasive surgery breaches the mediastinal fibrous-tissue barrier, and any left-behind prosthetic material adds generic post-surgical infection risk. From there he introduces what he calls the "hidden spaces" concept — certain anatomic compartments, particularly the mediastinum and the pelvis, can conceal substantial disease (10-15 cm in the mediastinum, and, referencing a prior case Maddie had presented, up to roughly 20 cm in the pelvis) before it becomes clinically apparent, which is part of why thymomas and ovarian cancers are so often diagnosed late. Applied here, this raises post-sternotomy mediastinitis as a real possibility — a process that can present deceptively subtly, sometimes with nothing more than mild chest discomfort and low-grade fever.

Reza needles Robbie for not leading with the CABG history immediately. Robbie explains the sequencing was deliberate: a cost-conscious, astute clinician pursues hypotheses in an order calibrated to how sick or stable the patient currently is, rather than jumping straight to the most invasive workup for a stable patient. Reza draws a parallel to a story about Gurpreet Dhaliwal defending an omitted differential item to a skeptical audience member at a conference, jokingly casting himself as having just played the "angry doctor" role toward Robbie.

Further data arrive. The sternal incision is clean-looking but chronically tender in a way that has not worsened. The urinalysis shows many white cells, glucosuria, and positive nitrites. A new piece of history surfaces: the patient is status post pancreatectomy and manages his glucose with an insulin pump, previously well-controlled, but his glucose has climbed to roughly 400 with no recent pump changes. Blood and urine cultures are pending. The chest x-ray shows a left lower lobe opacity that was already present weeks earlier during a prior pneumonia — Robbie flags that radiographic resolution lags clinical resolution, so this finding isn't useful for interpreting the current fever. The patient's creatinine (1.4-1.5) is at his chronic baseline.

Robbie reframes the central decision as "how sure do we need to be," weighing the high-stakes 90-day post-surgical window against the patient's current clinical stability. He treats the pyuria and glucosuria as a possible diagnostic "fake out" — chronic, incidental findings common in older diabetic adults — while also raising painless bilateral pyelonephritis and prostatitis as diagnoses, both previously discussed on the show, that can present with little or no urinary symptomatology in diabetic patients. He proposes a plan that moves toward CT chest/abdomen/pelvis if the patient doesn't improve, and explicitly frames the antibiotics-versus-imaging choice as reflecting practice-style variation — CT access speed, day of the week, surgical input, patient preference — rather than a gap in medical knowledge. He suggests simply asking the patient whether he'd prefer to watch and wait or move straight to imaging.

On empiric antibiotics specifically, Robbie notes the patient doesn't clearly meet standard triggers (sepsis, neutropenic fever, decompensated cirrhosis), so he leans toward pursuing diagnostic clarity first, while granting that empiric UTI treatment would also be a defensible choice. Pressed on whether the absence of urinary symptoms changes his thinking, Robbie clarifies that his "no pyuria, no bacteriuria, no symptoms — no UTI" mnemonic doesn't mean the absence of symptoms rules out a UTI; TB, severe immunosuppression, and painless bilateral pyelonephritis are recognized exceptions. What's actually pulling him toward imaging, he clarifies, isn't the absence of urinary symptoms — it's the plausibility of mediastinitis as an alternative source. Asked what he'd do if there were no CABG history at all, Robbie says he'd still lean toward imaging, citing how low-friction CT access is in his own ED practice ("a CT scan is like an X-ray and a UA" there).

Reza, working the case in real time with his intern Colin, initially holds off on both antibiotics and imaging — blood cultures are still within their first 24 hours, the patient is stable, and Reza wants to avoid unnecessary contrast exposure (though he notes he personally doesn't believe the literature supports IV contrast nephropathy as a major concern). He consults a de-identified summary of the case with GPT-5.0 about the timing of post-CABG mediastinitis; the model highlights the classic 2-4 week post-operative window (the patient is exactly at week three), draws an analogy to type 2 necrotizing fasciitis (a deceptively reassuring surface exam overlying serious underlying pathology), and cites a mortality rate around 25% for mediastinitis requiring urgent surgical intervention. This prompts Reza to reverse course and start empiric vancomycin plus cefepime rather than wait for culture results. Factoring in the patient's own preference to avoid another facility transfer, and his confidence in how closely PM&R is monitoring him, Reza decides to keep the patient on the rehab service rather than transfer to general medicine.

The next day, the patient is still febrile (controlled to about 100°F with Tylenol), his white count has risen to 20,000, and he is found biking in the rehab gym — still entirely asymptomatic. Intern Colin raises the question of broadening to meropenem given the positive nitrites (possible ESBL E. coli) and whether to scan now. Robbie, hearing about this in the discussion, offers a parallel from his own teaching with his intern Kat, working through a PE pretest-probability threshold exercise where she ultimately accepted roughly a 2% pretest probability — a 1-in-50 scan-to-true-PE ratio — as reasonable grounds to scan (that particular patient turned out to be PE-negative). He uses this to argue the operative question for this case is not "if" to scan but "when," since the estimated probability of either mediastinitis or pyelonephritis already exceeds a reasonable action threshold, and no single blood culture result, positive or negative, would eliminate the need for imaging either way. On Colin's specific ESBL concern, Robbie lays out three equally plausible explanations for the lack of 24-hour improvement — it's simply too soon, there's a source-control failure, or the organism is resistant — with no principled way yet to favor one. His own personal inclination, he says, would be to go "all hands on deck," broadening to meropenem and imaging immediately — explicitly framed as a genuine, unresolved difference in approach from Reza's more conservative path, with both hosts agreeing that showing the reasoning matters more than being proven right in retrospect.

Reza explains what specifically concerned him enough to act: not the fever, but the trending white count. He recalls a formative case from residency — a patient with back pain and a rising white count whose outside MRI had read as negative, whom Reza nearly discharged before re-imaging revealed an epidural abscess — a case that taught him "the white count doesn't lie." His actual decision here: he does not broaden to meropenem, reasoning that anaerobic coverage isn't standard for post-sternotomy mediastinitis (unlike descending or oropharyngeal-source mediastinitis), and continues vancomycin and cefepime while watching closely — but he does decide to image now, explicitly self-critiquing his earlier dismissal of the painless-pyelonephritis possibility as premature. He proactively alerts cardiothoracic surgery through PM&R resident Andrew, citing his own recent lesson from "the urology case in the Fournier's" (RLR 146) as the reason he isn't willing to let a reassuring subspecialist opinion alone stop him from imaging. He praises Andrew's foresight in care coordination — planning to go directly from rehab to the surgical ICU if mediastinitis is confirmed, avoiding an unnecessary intermediate transfer — and asks Andrew to update him personally, staying engaged with the case through the evening.

That evening, after delays with IV placement and imaging, Reza pushes for an expedited radiology read and, with self-deprecating humor about "channeling his inner Robbie," attempts to interpret the CT himself — admitting he hadn't even reviewed the urinary tract images yet, again citing his earlier deprioritization of the pyelonephritis hypothesis. He gets a teleradiologist, Dr. Song, on the phone for a live read: no evidence of mediastinitis, an incidental small air bubble in the bladder attributed to the Foley catheter, a pristine bladder wall, no perinephric stranding, and no radiographic evidence of a urinary tract infection. Reza relays the negative findings to Andrew, continues the current antibiotics, and stays on as medicine consult.

Over the following days, the white count rises further to 18,000, then falls to 13,000. Blood cultures (one set drawn) remain negative. The urine culture eventually grows Pseudomonas.

Asked for his final take, Robbie says he's genuinely surprised such a dense neutrophilic leukocytosis lacks a single unifying explanation. His pragmatic recommendation is to treat the Pseudomonas found on culture — completing a seven-day antipseudomonal course, ideally narrowed to an oral fluoroquinolone — while continuing close monitoring. He reframes the clinician's actual job as shepherding the patient to safety rather than achieving diagnostic certainty, noting that "my most common diagnosis is I don't know, but you're going to get better." He raises culture-negative bacteremia as a real possibility, noting even Staph aureus has a meaningful false-negative rate on blood cultures. When Reza presses him on whether he's specifically calling this a UTI, Robbie declines to commit to a confident label, saying he can't find a single disease that perfectly matches every detail of the case. Reza, noting the entirely negative imaging, floats simply stopping antibiotics now and favoring "asymptomatic bacteriuria" as the more defensible label over "UTI." Robbie points out that stopping now is logically equivalent to concluding the antibiotics were never needed in the first place, explains his own decision to start them was driven mainly by the depth of the neutrophilia (which he recalls peaking near 30,000), and — citing a prior RLR reference to a "randomized controlled trial" of spontaneous UTI resolution — acknowledges patients absolutely can clear bacterial infections without treatment. He concludes there's no way to know definitively which explanation is correct, and reframes the continue-versus-stop decision as mattering far less than maintaining a strong relationship and continuity of care with the patient: "if you have a good relationship with him, flip a coin... it doesn't matter as much as the relationship."

In the actual real-world course, the weekend covering provider transitioned the patient to oral levofloxacin to complete a seven-day course under a UTI-framed diagnosis. Reza says he personally wouldn't have confidently labeled this a UTI, but is comfortable with the decision to complete the course given the post-CABG risk context. Robbie closes with a tongue-in-cheek joke imagining the two of them alternately countermanding each other's orders on a shared hypothetical patient ("medicine is a scam, maybe"), followed by sincere closing reflections from both hosts.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Data Point | Effect on Differential |
|---|---|
| Fever + neutrophilic leukocytosis, but patient entirely asymptomatic and hospitalized post-surgically | Raises overall suspicion for bacterial infection above baseline; narrows toward hospital/surgical-associated sources despite reassuring exam |
| Recent CABG (~3 weeks prior), sternal incision chronically tender | Raises pretest probability of post-sternotomy mediastinitis into the classic 2-4 week window |
| Chest x-ray with old, unchanged LLL opacity | Non-contributory; radiographic pneumonia resolution lags clinical resolution |
| Urinalysis with pyuria, glucosuria, positive nitrites; poorly controlled glucose without pump changes | Raises suspicion for urinary tract source (painless pyelonephritis) but also raises "fake out" possibility of chronic incidental pyuria |
| GPT-5.0 consult citing mediastinitis mortality (~25%) and classic timing window | Prompts empiric antibiotic initiation despite stable vitals and pending cultures |
| Rising trend in WBC (15,000 → 20,000) despite clinically stable, asymptomatic patient | Identified as the single most concerning data point (more than the fever itself); triggers imaging despite reassuring exam |
| CT with live teleradiology read: no mediastinitis, pristine bladder wall, no perinephric stranding | Substantially lowers probability of both leading hypotheses (mediastinitis, pyelonephritis) without eliminating either entirely |
| Urine culture positive for Pseudomonas; blood cultures negative | Provides a plausible but not fully unifying explanation; culture-negative bacteremia remains a live possibility |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Post-sternotomy mediastinitis illness script**: occurs in a classic 2-4 week post-CABG window; can present with only mild chest pain, low-grade fever, and a deceptively benign-appearing incision — analogous to the reassuring exam that can mask early necrotizing fasciitis.
- **Painless bilateral pyelonephritis illness script**: occurs particularly in diabetic patients; urinary symptoms may be absent or minimal despite a genuine renal infection, alongside TB and severe immunosuppression as other causes of "silent" infection.
- **Anchoring/premature closure bias**: Reza's initial deprioritization of the pyelonephritis hypothesis (not reviewing the urinary tract images until later) illustrates how an early working hypothesis can narrow subsequent attention, even for an experienced clinician who later self-corrects.
- **Reassurance bias / relinquishing clinical judgment**: Reza's explicit invocation of the RLR 146 Fournier's/urology case as his stated reason for not letting a negative or reassuring subspecialty impression alone stop him from pursuing imaging — a recurring theme across this stretch of episodes.
- **Formative-memory-driven pattern recognition**: Reza's decision to prioritize the trending white count over the fever itself is explicitly traced to a specific residency-era near-miss (an epidural abscess nearly discharged on a reassuring outside MRI) — case-based recall functioning as the trigger for continued vigilance.
- **Availability/threshold reasoning made explicit**: Robbie's parallel PE pretest-probability teaching example with his intern Kat is used to argue that the actionable question is "when to scan," not "if to scan," once an estimated probability crosses a reasonable threshold — a Bayesian action-threshold framework applied conversationally rather than with an explicit calculator.

## Teaching Discussion

Robbie's central teaching point is that the diagnostic process is inseparable from a resource-and-risk-calibrated decision about how much certainty is actually needed before acting — the "cost-conscious, astute clinician" framing he uses to justify sequencing hypotheses (medications and simple explanations first, invasive workup only as stability allows) rather than reflexively jumping to the most comprehensive test available. His "hidden spaces" concept (mediastinum and pelvis as anatomic reservoirs for occult, late-presenting disease) is introduced here conversationally, with an explicit plan to formalize it as a schema on rlrcpsolvers.com.

A second major thread is the tension between empiric treatment and diagnostic patience. Robbie and Reza openly diverge on how aggressively to broaden antibiotics and how quickly to image once the white count starts rising further — and rather than resolve this disagreement, they use it to model that reasonable, well-justified clinicians can land in different places, and that "showing your work" (being able to articulate why a decision was made) matters more than being retrospectively proven correct.

The episode's most explicit metacognitive teaching comes in the closing exchange: Robbie reframes "diagnosis" itself as a useful but constructed label rather than an objectively fixed category — his point being that the clinician's actual obligation is to help the patient get better and stay safe, not to achieve a satisfying diagnostic label for its own sake. Reza complicates a recurring RLR refrain (that how a patient looks trumps other data) by noting that in this case, despite the patient looking excellent throughout — literally biking in the rehab gym — the trending white count was the signal that justified continued vigilance, an explicit exception worth naming rather than a contradiction to dismiss.
