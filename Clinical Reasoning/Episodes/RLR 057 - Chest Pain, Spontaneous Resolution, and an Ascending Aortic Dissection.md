> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A deliberately **rapid-fire** episode (Rabih's ED shift starts in seven minutes) on a **morning-report chest-pain** case whose lesson is bought at a price. A **48-year-old man with hypertension** wakes at **exactly 1 a.m. with severe chest pain** — he thinks it started in his **back** and moved to the chest — no radiation, no dyspnea, no nausea. In the ED: **BP 190/100 falling to 162/85, equal in both arms, no murmur**; **high-sensitivity troponin negative ×3**; EKG with **T-wave inversions inferiorly (II/III/aVF) and laterally (V5/V6)**; **normal CBC, chem-7, and chest x-ray**. **Nitroglycerin does nothing; acetaminophen and the pain fully resolves.** Reassured by the negative troponins, non-diagnostic EKG, and **spontaneous resolution**, the ED, the night admitter, and then the day team each decline further imaging. Rabih builds the reasoning: **the words "chest pain" end the history** — you get an **EKG and CXR** regardless, and the history's real value is **risk factors for the killers** (ACS, PE, aortic dissection). He teaches that **spontaneous resolution argues *against* PE and dissection** (large vessels don't fix themselves) and *for* **ACS** (small-vessel disease resolves via collaterals, autolysis, rest) — but flags the crucial caveat that the umbrella diagnosis is **[[acute aortic syndrome]]**, not just dissection: a **[[penetrating aortic ulcer]]** or **[[intramural hematoma]]** can cause **transient** pain that recurs. He dismantles two false-reassurances — **intact exercise capacity doesn't exclude CAD** (non-flow-limiting "[[vulnerable plaque|vulnerable]]" plaques are the ones that rupture) and **normal pulses/CXR barely lower the odds of dissection** (asymmetric BP ~10–15%, mediastinal widening only ~80%). His rule: **"you cannot diagnose unstable angina without ruling out PE and dissection,"** and **if you're thinking this hard about a scan, you've already earned it** — get a **CT (dissection protocol) or use D-dimer** to help. Twelve hours later the pain returns identically → **CT confirms an ascending (Type A) aortic dissection**; he is started on **esmolol** (lower BP *and* contractility to cut aortic **shear stress**) and **transferred for surgery**. The teaching spine: **acute aortic syndrome ≠ aortic dissection**; **the deadly diagnosis you must exclude before "unstable angina"**; **anti-impulse therapy before any vasodilator** (and never anticoagulate a dissection mistaken for ACS); and a **test-threshold philosophy** — *"how many scans to catch one dissection? A thousand — worth it."*

**Pearls**

- **"Chest pain" ends the history — then you hunt risk factors.** For any chest pain you will get an **EKG (within minutes) and a CXR** regardless of the story, because those tests outperform the narrative. The most valuable history is then **risk factors for the killers** — **CAD**, **PE**, and **aortic dissection**.
- **Non-diagnostic EKG changes may be chronic — but assume the heart "feels heat."** T-wave inversions can reflect **hypertensive cardiomyopathy** rather than an acute event; you can't prove they're acute, so you must ask *where the strain is coming from* — **ACS, PE, or dissection**.
- **Spontaneous resolution argues against PE and dissection, for ACS.** **Large-vessel** catastrophes rarely "fix themselves"; **ACS** can resolve via **collateralization, spontaneous thrombolysis, and reduced demand at rest**. *But* — the umbrella is **[[acute aortic syndrome]]**: a **[[penetrating aortic ulcer]]** or **[[intramural hematoma]]** can give **transient** pain that recurs, so resolution lowers but does not eliminate aortic risk.
- **Intact exercise capacity does NOT exclude CAD/ACS.** Exercise tolerance is a proxy for **flow-limiting** plaque. But a **small, non-flow-limiting [[vulnerable plaque]]** — invisible to exertional symptoms — is precisely the kind that **ruptures** and causes ACS. Reduced tolerance → suspect CAD; normal tolerance → *either* no CAD *or* a rupture-prone non-obstructive plaque.
- **You cannot diagnose unstable angina without excluding PE and aortic dissection.** With **chest pain + EKG changes + negative troponin**, "unstable angina" is the reflex label — but it is unprovable (and dangerous) until the **great-vessel** killers are off the table.
- **Normal pulses and a normal CXR barely lower the odds of dissection.** **Asymmetric BP** appears in only ~**10–15%** (and in older patients often reflects subclavian stenosis), **mediastinal widening** in only ~**80%**, focal neuro deficits in ~**20%**. Their **negative predictive value is poor** — a benign exam does not rule out dissection.
- **If you're thinking this hard about scanning, you've already earned the scan.** Like the **LP-for-meningitis** or **TB rule-out** heuristic: the effortful "should I or shouldn't I?" is itself the indication. Use a **CT dissection protocol** (also catches significant PE) — or a **D-dimer**, which has good NPV for low-to-intermediate-probability PE *and* acute aortic syndrome.
- **Anti-impulse therapy first; never thin the blood of a dissection.** Reduce aortic **shear stress (dP/dt)** — both **blood pressure** and **left-ventricular contractility** — with a **short-acting β-blocker (esmolol)** *before* any vasodilator (hydralazine alone would raise reflex tachycardia and shear). And the catastrophe to avoid: **aspirin + heparin** given to a "NSTE-ACS" that is actually a **dissection**.
- **Have a test-threshold philosophy for the can't-miss diagnosis.** *"How many CTs to catch one dissection? A thousand."* Cast a **wide net**; be a **sensitive radar**. Operationalize it: **"am I worried or not?"** (a gestalt most clinicians agree on) → if worried, **you need an answer within an hour** → if the EKG and troponin don't give it, the remaining test is a **contrast CT**.

**Problem Representation**
A 48-year-old hypertensive man with abrupt nocturnal chest pain (radiating from the back), non-diagnostic ischemic EKG changes, negative high-sensitivity troponins, and spontaneous resolution — initially framed as possible unstable angina and observed — who re-presented within 12 hours and was found to have an ascending (Type A) aortic dissection, an acute aortic syndrome whose transient first episode was the missed warning.

## Case Narrative

Fresh from **SGIM in Denver**, the hosts run this one **"rapid-diagnosis style"** — Rabih has **seven minutes** until his ED shift. Reza presents a **morning-report** case: a **48-year-old man with hypertension** who **woke at exactly 1 a.m. with severe chest pain**, which he suspects began in his **back** before settling in his chest — **no radiation, no dyspnea, no nausea/vomiting**, on antihypertensives only. In the ED his **systolic pressure was 190/100** (falling to **162/85** on recheck), **equal in both arms**, with **no murmur**. **High-sensitivity troponins were negative three times**; the **EKG showed T-wave inversions inferiorly (II/III/aVF) and laterally (V5/V6)**; **CBC, chem-7, and chest x-ray were normal**. **Nitroglycerin had no effect**, but **acetaminophen was given and the chest pain resolved completely** within an hour. Reassured by the **negative troponins, non-diagnostic EKG, and spontaneous resolution**, the ED team pursued nothing further; the **night admitter** and then the **day team** likewise saw a comfortable, pain-free patient and deferred.

Rabih's first principle: **"the moment someone says chest pain, the conversation ends in my mind"** — because you will get **higher-performance data (EKG, CXR)** than the narrative can provide. After the words "chest pain," the history's job is to surface **risk factors for the scary causes** — **CAD, PE, aortic dissection**. Here, "chest pain" was immediately followed by **hypertension**, a risk factor for **both CAD and dissection**. The **EKG** shows **non-ST-elevation changes**, but he cautions they **may be chronic** (**hypertensive cardiomyopathy**) — unprovable either way — so one must **assume the heart is "feeling heat"** and ask its source: **ACS, PE, or dissection**.

Then he integrates the two reassuring data points. **Negative troponins** and — more powerfully — **spontaneous resolution of pain**. His key teaching: **spontaneous resolution markedly lowers PE and dissection** (these are **large-vessel** diseases that "have very low probability of fixing themselves") while **sparing ACS**, because ACS is **small-vessel** disease that can improve via **collateralization, spontaneous thrombolysis, and rest reducing myocardial demand**. So the live differential becomes **unstable angina vs hypertensive heart disease vs a benign cause in a high-risk host**. The next discriminator he reaches for is **exercise capacity before onset** — but immediately warns of its trap: a patient with **intact exertional capacity is not cleared of CAD**, because **non-flow-limiting plaques** (too small to limit flow or provoke exertional symptoms) are **more prone to rupture** and cause ACS. Reduced tolerance → think CAD; normal tolerance → *either* no CAD *or* a **[[vulnerable plaque|vulnerable non-obstructive plaque]]**.

Reza raises the **day team's actual decision**: with **back pain, a precise onset time**, but a now-**asymptomatic** patient, **normal CXR, and equal arm pressures**, they **declined the CT for dissection**. Rabih is candid — he is **"algorithmic"** and would have **scanned early** (a **dissection-protocol CT**, which also captures significant PE) to **"put it to bed"** before spontaneous resolution muddied the picture — and states the rule: **"you cannot diagnose unstable angina without ruling out PE and aortic dissection."** He then dismantles the exam's reassurance: **asymmetric BP is present in only ~10–15%** (and in older patients often just reflects **subclavian stenosis**), **mediastinal widening in only ~80%**, **focal neuro deficits ~20%** — so a **normal exam and CXR have poor negative predictive value**. Crucially, he reframes the whole category: the umbrella is **[[acute aortic syndrome]] (AAS)** — **dissection + [[penetrating aortic ulcer]] + [[intramural hematoma]]** — and while a *progressive dissection* resolving is hard to imagine, a **penetrating ulcer or intramural hematoma** can cause **transient, back-radiating, sleep-waking pain that resolves and then dissects hours to days later**. He would **use a D-dimer** (good NPV for low-to-intermediate-probability PE and AAS) to sharpen the decision — and invokes the **meningitis-LP / TB-rule-out** heuristic: **"if you're sitting there thinking this hard about excluding a dissection, you've thought too much — just address it."**

The **outcome** vindicates the worry: **within 12 hours the identical pain recurs**, a **CT aortic protocol** is finally done, and it shows an **ascending (Type A) aortic dissection**. Rabih walks the **aortopathy workup** — distinguish **dissection on a chronically diseased aorta vs a normal one** by CT; in a patient **under 50**, investigate a **bicuspid aortic valve with aortopathy** and **heritable connective-tissue disease** (**Marfan**, **Ehlers-Danlos — especially vascular type**, with its operative risks and family-screening implications). **Inflammatory aortitis** (Takayasu, GCA) tends to cause **aneurysm/rupture, not dissection**; and **hypertension**, though a sufficient risk factor, usually strikes older patients — so his best guess for this 48-year-old is **hypertension plus a subclinical connective-tissue disease**. **Management**: immediate **anti-impulse therapy with esmolol** to cut **aortic shear stress** — lowering both **blood pressure** and the **projectile force (dP/dt)** of LV ejection — *before* any vasodilator, then **surgery** (transfer from the VA to UCSF for repair). Reza underscores the stakes: had this been mislabeled **NSTE-ACS** and treated with **aspirin + heparin (± hydralazine)**, anticoagulating and raising shear on a dissection would have been **catastrophic**.

They close on **test-threshold philosophy**. Reza confesses the humbling pattern ("*every time I thought it was a dissection or PE, it wasn't; when I didn't, it was*") and highlights the **exact-time-of-onset** as a clue to an abrupt vascular event. Rabih offers his operating system: decide **"am I worried or not?"** (a gestalt clinicians reliably share); if **worried, demand an answer within an hour**; if the **EKG is non-diagnostic and the troponin negative**, the remaining test is a **contrast CT** — and he is willing to **"waste" ~95 scans to catch ~5 PEs/dissections**, because the miss is **death**. A **positive troponin with EKG changes in a stable patient**, by contrast, is **ACS until proven otherwise** (a troponin-leaking PE or dissection would not be subtle). And he names the reassuring counter-cues that let him **worry less** — the fourth normal ED visit, the patient **eating a sandwich**, the patient who can **focus on a sore toe** while describing chest pain.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The episode is a lesson in **not letting reassuring data close a lethal differential**. Three "benign" signals — **negative troponins, a non-diagnostic EKG, and spontaneous resolution** — sequentially disarmed three teams, and each was **individually reasonable**. Rabih's antidote is a **category correction**: reframing "aortic dissection" as **acute aortic syndrome** restores the possibility of a **transient** first hit (penetrating ulcer / intramural hematoma), so **resolution stops being exculpatory**. He also disarms two classic **false-negative heuristics** — **intact exercise capacity** (which conflates *flow-limiting* with *any* CAD and ignores the rupture-prone **vulnerable plaque**) and **normal pulses/CXR** (whose poor NPV is quantified: asymmetric BP ~10–15%, widening ~80%). The connective principle is **sequence and threshold discipline**: **"unstable angina" is a diagnosis of exclusion** that requires PE and AAS to be addressed first, and the **effortful deliberation about scanning is itself the indication** (the meningitis-LP analogy). Finally, Rabih models a **calibrated gestalt** — the binary **"worried / not worried,"** the **one-hour answer rule**, and the honest accounting of a **low hit rate (~5%) that is still worth it** because the downside is death — the mature version of **anti-anchoring**: a rigid workup that *forces* the great-vessel killers onto the table so a comfortable "rule-out" cannot quietly become a fatal miss.

## Teaching Discussion

**Framing the acute chest-pain patient.** The story is low-yield relative to **immediate objective tests**: get an **EKG within ~10 minutes** and a **CXR**, then mine the history for **risk factors** for **ACS, PE, and aortic dissection**. Interpret **non-ST-elevation EKG changes** cautiously — they may be **chronic** (LVH/strain from hypertension) — and treat them as a prompt to ask *which* of the three killers is at play rather than as proof of ischemia.

**What spontaneous resolution does — and doesn't — mean.** Relief of pain is **strong evidence against ongoing large-vessel catastrophe** (a progressing dissection or a large PE) and **compatible with ACS**, which resolves through **collateral flow, endogenous fibrinolysis, and demand reduction at rest**. The trap is equating "aortic dissection" with the whole aortic category: **[[acute aortic syndrome]]** also includes **[[penetrating aortic ulcer]]** and **[[intramural hematoma]]**, which can produce **transient** pain and then **evolve into dissection or rupture** — so resolution **lowers but does not eliminate** aortic risk.

**CAD is not ACS, and exercise capacity is an imperfect proxy.** Exertional tolerance reflects **flow-limiting** stenosis. Acute coronary syndromes, however, are often driven by **non-obstructive, lipid-rich [[vulnerable plaque|vulnerable plaques]]** that **rupture** — precisely the lesions that do **not** limit flow or exertion. Therefore **normal exercise capacity cannot exclude ACS**, and the presence of a can't-miss vascular differential should not be relaxed by a reassuring functional history.

**Excluding the great-vessel killers before "unstable angina."** With **chest pain + ischemic EKG changes + negative troponin**, the working label is **unstable angina** — but it is **unprovable and unsafe** until **PE and aortic dissection** are excluded, because their treatments **diverge catastrophically** (anticoagulation/antiplatelet therapy for ACS would be **lethal** in dissection). This sequencing — *rule out the aorta and the pulmonary arteries first* — is the episode's central safety rule.

**The poor negative predictive value of the "classic" dissection findings.** Textbook signs are **insensitive**: **pulse/BP differential** in only ~**10–15%** (and frequently confounded by **subclavian stenosis** in older adults), **mediastinal widening** in ~**80%** (so a **normal CXR** misses ~1 in 5), and **focal neurologic deficits** in ~**20%**. A **normal exam and film do not rule out dissection.** Structured tools help: the **Aortic Dissection Detection Risk Score (ADD-RS)**, often combined with **D-dimer** (high sensitivity/NPV for acute aortic syndrome at low-to-intermediate pretest probability), can safely lower the threshold to **CT angiography**, the definitive test.

**Classifying and working up the dissection.** **Stanford Type A** (ascending) is a **surgical emergency**; **Type B** (descending) is usually managed medically unless complicated. On CT, distinguish **dissection of a chronically diseased aorta vs a structurally normal one**. In patients **<50–60**, pursue **bicuspid aortic valve with aortopathy** and **heritable thoracic aortic disease** — **Marfan**, **Loeys-Dietz**, and **vascular Ehlers-Danlos** (which carries major **operative and family-screening** implications). **Inflammatory aortitis** (Takayasu, giant cell) more often causes **aneurysm/rupture than dissection**. **Hypertension** is a sufficient risk factor but skews older, so a young dissector without another cause likely has a **subclinical connective-tissue predisposition**.

**Anti-impulse therapy and the shear-stress principle.** The immediate goal is to reduce **aortic wall shear stress (dP/dt)** — a product of **blood pressure** *and* the **velocity of LV ejection**. A **short-acting β-blocker (esmolol)** lowers **both** heart rate/contractility and pressure and is **first-line before any vasodilator**; giving a vasodilator (e.g., **hydralazine**) *without* β-blockade provokes **reflex tachycardia** that **increases** shear and can extend the dissection. Target roughly **HR <60** and **SBP 100–120**, then **emergent surgery** for Type A (transfer if on-site cardiac surgery is unavailable). **Do not anticoagulate.**

**A test-threshold operating system for can't-miss disease.** For a life-or-death, treatable diagnosis, the acceptable **number-needed-to-scan** is high. Operationally: **(1)** form the binary **"worried / not worried"** gestalt; **(2)** if worried, commit to **an answer within an hour**; **(3)** if the EKG is non-diagnostic and the troponin negative, proceed to **contrast CT**. Refine the gestalt over time using **reassuring counter-cues** (a repeatedly negative frequent-flyer, a patient eating comfortably, a patient who can attend to a minor complaint) and **red flags** (abrupt onset, exact time of onset, pain that wakes from sleep). A **positive troponin with EKG changes in a hemodynamically stable patient** is **ACS until proven otherwise**, since a troponin-leaking PE or dissection would be clinically overt.
