## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> An 82-year-old man who hasn't seen a doctor in six decades presents with three months of [[Illness Tempo|subacute, then rapidly progressive]] weakness, numbness, and paresthesias of both arms and legs, back pain, weight loss to cachexia, and — the datum that won't fit — **numbness of the jaw and around the mouth**, plus new urinary retention. With Rabih out, Aaron Berkowitz runs a live [[Neuroanatomical Localization|localization]] under deliberately imperfect exam data (Reza's exam, the neurologist's exam, and the patient's report all disagree). He works the [[Anatomical Approach|spine-not-spinal-cord]] discipline: sensorimotor symptoms below the neck put us in the [[Spine vs Peripheral Nerve|spine (cord or nerve roots) or the peripheral nerves]] — not muscle or NMJ. The [[Length-Dependent vs Non-Length-Dependent Neuropathy|hands and feet starting together]] is *non-length-dependent* (unusual — points away from the common axonal neuropathies), and the [[Urinary Retention|urinary retention]] pulls him back "into the cord." The face forces a [[Hickam's Dictum|second process]]: the [[Trigeminal Nerve|trigeminal]] lives at brainstem/meninges/skull-base, not the cord. He applies his signature [[Leave-One-Out Reasoning|take-one-symptom-out]] heuristic and flags a [[When to Worry|when-to-worry]] gestalt — rapid tempo + weight loss + a chest-x-ray lung nodule — that makes cancer and a [[Paraneoplastic Neurologic Syndrome|paraneoplastic]] process loom. Imaging resolves it as **two** things: compressive [[Cauda Equina Syndrome|cauda equina]] from severe L4–L5 stenosis, *and* diffuse [[Leptomeningeal Carcinomatosis|leptomeningeal enhancement]] over brain, cord, and roots. A [[Froin Syndrome|Froin]]-pattern CSF (very high protein, cytology negative — [[Test Interpretation|not sensitive]]) and a spiculated left-upper-lobe mass with PEs seal it: **invasive lung adenocarcinoma (MET exon 14 skipping) with leptomeningeal metastases** — the [[Leptomeningeal Carcinomatosis|leptomeningeal]] process being the one entity that ties the face, arms, legs, and roots together.

## Pearls

- **Localize to the "spine," not the "spinal cord" — so you never forget the roots.** A symmetric sensorimotor process below the neck is either in the spinal *column* (cord **or** nerve roots / [[Cauda Equina Syndrome|cauda equina]]) or in the peripheral nerves; sensory-and-motor involvement excludes muscle and NMJ. Keeping the [[Cauda Equina Syndrome|cauda equina]] explicitly in view is what later lets the leptomeningeal root disease make sense. ([[Neuroanatomical Localization|localization]])
- **Non-length-dependent = a clue, because most neuropathies are length-dependent.** Common axonal neuropathies (diabetes, B12) hit the *longest* nerves first — feet, then creeping to the ankles/shins before the fingers. Hands and feet declaring *together* breaks that rule and points to a demyelinating neuropathy ([[CIDP|CIDP]]/[[Guillain-Barré Syndrome|GBS]]) **or** a cord/root process that doesn't obey the length principle. ([[Length-Dependent vs Non-Length-Dependent Neuropathy|length principle]])
- **When a symptom won't fit, use the [[Leave-One-Out Reasoning|leave-one-out]] test.** "If I take one finding out, do the rest add up?" If the bowel/bladder is real → cord/roots, and neuropathy can't do that; if the face is real → not the cord alone. Naming which datum you're provisionally discarding — and why — keeps a messy picture from collapsing into a bad unifier. ([[Diagnostic Humility|humility about the data]])
- **Facial numbness localizes to brainstem, meninges, skull base, or face — and a [[Numb Chin Syndrome|numb chin]] is ominous.** The [[Trigeminal Nerve|trigeminal nerve]] rarely fails in isolation from the tiny brainstem; a lone division points peripherally. [[Numb Chin Syndrome|Numb chin syndrome]] (mental-nerve infiltration) sounds benign but is often metastatic (jaw mets, myeloma). Perioral paresthesias also come from **hyperventilation → alkalosis → albumin binds calcium → functional [[Hypocalcemia|hypocalcemia]]** (a prior-episode callback). ([[Anatomical Approach|anatomical approach]])
- **Your localization is only as good as your exam — and exams disagree.** Reza's exam, the neurologist's, and the patient's report conflicted (one reflex vs all reflexes; reported facial numbness vs a normal facial exam). The disciplined move is to localize *anyway*, name the uncertainty, and [[Return to the Bedside|go back to re-examine]] (sit him up for reflexes, seek a sensory level on the back). ([[Diagnostic Humility|diagnostic humility]])
- **Read the [[Illness Tempo|tempo]] as the "[[When to Worry|when to worry]]" signal.** Numbness, tingling, and back pain are mundane; a rapidly progressive subacute course with cachexia and a lung nodule is not. Tempo plus weight loss plus a mass reframes a common complaint into cancer with metastatic or [[Paraneoplastic Neurologic Syndrome|paraneoplastic]] neurologic spread. ([[Illness Tempo|tempo]])
- **[[Pachymeningeal vs Leptomeningeal Enhancement|Pachy- vs leptomeningeal enhancement]] have different differentials (the orange-peel model).** Dura = the peel (pachy: low-pressure/CSF leak if smooth; dural mets, neurosarcoid, rheumatoid/vasculitic pachymeningitis if lumpy). Arachnoid+pia = the wispy layer and the segments (lepto: infectious/inflammatory meningitis, neurosarcoid, and **carcinomatous meningitis / [[Leptomeningeal Carcinomatosis|leptomeningeal metastases]]**). Nerve roots are wrapped in leptomeninges — so root enhancement is a leptomeningeal sign. ([[Test Interpretation|neuroimaging interpretation]])

## Problem Representation

An 82-year-old man with no medical care in sixty years and chronic low back pain presents with three months of acute-on-chronic severe back pain followed within days by numbness and tingling that began in the fingers *and* toes together and ascended to involve both legs, the groin/perianal area, both arms to the elbows, and — strikingly — the jaw and perioral region, with progressive proximal and distal limb weakness, profound weight loss to cachexia, multiple falls, and new urinary retention (a liter drained by Foley). Examinations conflict (patchy weakness and diminished light touch on the internist's exam versus a normal sensory and reflex exam by neurology; downgoing toes, no Hoffman, no clonus), and vibration/proprioception were untested. Hemoglobin is 12.7 with an otherwise normal metabolic panel, and chest x-ray shows emphysema plus a 2.5 × 5 cm left suprahilar nodular density. The syndrome localizes to the spinal canal and nerve roots with a superimposed diffuse process reaching the cranial nerves, and neuroimaging reveals compressive cauda equina from severe L4–L5 stenosis *together with* diffuse leptomeningeal enhancement over brain, cord, and roots; a spiculated left-upper-lobe mass with pulmonary emboli, a Froin-pattern CSF (protein 311, 11 WBC monocyte/lymphocyte-predominant, cytology negative), and a subsequent lung biopsy establish invasive pulmonary adenocarcinoma (MET exon 14 skipping mutation) with leptomeningeal carcinomatosis.

## Case Narrative

Reza presents the case in two aliquots with Rabih absent, and Aaron Berkowitz narrates his reasoning in real time. An 82-year-old man with no medical contact in six decades develops severe acute-on-chronic low back pain, and three days later numbness and tingling in the fingers and toes that over months ascends to both legs, the groin and perianal region, both arms, and the jaw/perioral area, with worsening weakness, cachectic weight loss ("he can see his ribs"), falls, urinary retention, and difficulty reaching the toilet for bowel movements. Crucially, Reza narrates two *different* exams — his own (4/5 left hip flexion and biceps, weak grip, marked thigh/arm atrophy, patchy diminished light touch, no elicitable patellar reflex, no face numbness on exam) and the neurologist's (no sensory deficit, normal reflexes, normal sphincter tone, no saddle anesthesia) — deliberately preserving the discordance that clinicians actually face. Labs are near-normal (hemoglobin 12.7); chest x-ray shows emphysema and a left suprahilar nodule.

Aaron begins by clarifying, then localizes aloud. He asks the two exam questions that matter: was **vibration/proprioception** tested (no — a potentially important gap), and was there a **Babinski** (downgoing, with negative Hoffman and no clonus). He establishes that hands and feet were affected *together* from the start and that the course is subacute over months with a steeper decline in the last month. His reasoning oscillates: mundane lumbar degenerative disease can't explain arm involvement (and "if you have degenerative disease downstairs you probably have it upstairs" — cervical stenosis can decompensate subacutely), and the face can't come from the cervical cord because facial sensation is trigeminal, tied to the brainstem well above the cervical spine. Setting the common back pain aside, he frames a symmetric sensorimotor process below the neck as living in the **spine (cord or roots) or the peripheral nerves** — not muscle, not NMJ.

Two features tug against each other. The **urinary retention**, if real, pulls "back into the cord," which is why he probes reflexes and Babinski (a cord process running for months should be hyperreflexic — and in an 82-year-old, even *present* ankle reflexes might count as relative hyperreflexia given expected age-related neuropathy). Against that, the **non-length-dependent** pattern (hands and feet together) is atypical for the common length-dependent axonal neuropathies (diabetes, B12) and would instead suggest a demyelinating neuropathy ([[CIDP|CIDP]], the subacute cousin of [[Guillain-Barré Syndrome|Guillain-Barré]]) — but those shouldn't cause bowel/bladder. He deploys his [[Leave-One-Out Reasoning|leave-one-out]] heuristic explicitly: if the bowel/bladder is real we're in the cord and neuropathy is out; if the face is real we're not in the cord alone. He also flags the **B12 / subacute combined degeneration** possibility (but that shouldn't cause bowel/bladder or face) and a not-to-miss "gourmet" diagnosis in an older man with a subacute cord syndrome — a **[[Dural AV Fistula|dural arteriovenous fistula]]** (venous congestion mimicking transverse myelitis, worsening with steroids/LP, needing spinal angiography). Zooming out, the [[When to Worry|when-to-worry]] gestalt — rapid tempo, cachexia, a lung lesion — makes **cancer** and a **[[Paraneoplastic Neurologic Syndrome|paraneoplastic]]** syndrome (anti-Hu with small-cell; paraneoplastic myeloneuropathy) loom large. He commits, tentatively, to the cord while conceding the bowel/bladder could be a red herring, and would start with **cervical MRI** (the one scan if forced to choose) and consider LP, planning to re-examine at the bedside for a sensory level.

Between aliquots, Reza pushes on the **face**: as an internist he thinks perioral numbness → hypocalcemia (or hyperventilation-induced functional hypocalcemia) and **[[Numb Chin Syndrome|numb chin syndrome]]** → mental-nerve malignant infiltration. Aaron builds the trigeminal localization map — **brainstem, meninges, skull base, or the facial compartment (orbit/jaw)** — noting a solitary trigeminal division in isolation is implausible from the tiny brainstem and points peripherally; a unilateral V2/V3 numbness in a cancer patient raises **leptomeningeal metastases** coating cranial nerves, or **skull-base/bony mets** (he even floats occult prostate cancer explaining the retention, back pain, dural/bony spread). Reza supplies the physiology callback: hyperventilation → alkalosis → albumin binds free calcium → **functional [[Hypocalcemia|hypocalcemia]]** and perioral numbness.

The second aliquot delivers imaging. Lumbar MRI: multilevel compression deformities (acute, subacute, and chronic), grade-1 anterolisthesis at L4–L5, and **severe canal stenosis with mass effect on the cauda equina** — a compressive [[Cauda Equina Syndrome|cauda equina syndrome]] that explains the legs and bowel/bladder. But there is also **abnormal leptomeningeal enhancement** of the distal cord and cauda equina roots, and MRI of brain/C-spine/T-spine shows **diffuse leptomeningeal enhancement everywhere**. CT chest: a **spiculated left-upper-lobe mass** plus **pulmonary emboli**. LP: 11 WBC (monocyte/lymphocyte-predominant), 0 RBC ("champagne tap"), glucose 42, **protein markedly elevated at 311**, cytology negative for malignancy. Aaron dissects it: the compression explains the cauda equina piece; the **leptomeninges** (arachnoid + pia, the "wispy" layers, versus the dural "peel" — the orange-peel model) enhancing over cord, roots, and brain explain the diffuse limb symptoms and the face, since nerve roots are sheathed in leptomeninges and the trigeminal differential included the meninges. He distinguishes **pachymeningeal** (dural: low-pressure/CSF leak, venous sinus thrombosis, dural mets, neurosarcoid, rheumatoid/vasculitic pachymeningitis) from **leptomeningeal** (infectious/inflammatory meningitis, neurosarcoid, and **carcinomatous meningitis / [[Leptomeningeal Carcinomatosis|leptomeningeal metastases]]**) enhancement, and reconciles the reflexes: below L1 the canal holds roots (lower motor neurons), so **absent reflexes** are expected, and Reza's two patellar reflexes (L4) may have sneaked out above the L5–S1 compression.

On management and interpretation: neurosurgery deems it non-acute and non-surgical (too many damaged vertebrae, stage-IV disease). The CSF is explained by **spinal block** — the [[Froin Syndrome|Froin syndrome]] of very high protein with non-circulating CSF below a compression — and negative cytology reflects the **low sensitivity** of CSF cytology for leptomeningeal cancer (needing up to three large-volume taps for high yield), not absence of disease. A CT-guided lung biopsy shows **invasive adenocarcinoma with a MET exon 14 skipping mutation** (prompting targeted therapy); PET lights up only the lung mass (leptomeningeal disease and the fractures are PET-negative — the leptomeninges too thin to be avid), which does not dissuade oncology from diagnosing leptomeningeal carcinomatosis. Aaron reflects with his [[Cognitive Autopsy|autopsy]]: he nearly over-invoked a rare paraneoplastic syndrome instead of asking "how would one process get *everywhere*?" — the answer being leptomeningeal spread. Reza closes on a communication teaching point, praising his resident for delivering devastating news with silence, presence, and a hand held — "tossing the ball underhand and waiting for the patient to throw it back."

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Neuroanatomical Localization|Localization as the master move]] — "spine, not spinal cord."** Aaron deliberately says *spine* to keep the [[Cauda Equina Syndrome|cauda equina]] roots in play alongside the cord; the discipline pays off when the leptomeningeal root disease, not a cord lesion, proves central. Sensory-and-motor excludes muscle/NMJ from the start.
- **[[Length-Dependent vs Non-Length-Dependent Neuropathy|The length principle as a discriminator]].** Recognizing that hands-and-feet-together is *non-length-dependent* — atypical for the common neuropathies — is what forces the differential toward demyelinating disease or a central (cord/root) process.
- **[[Leave-One-Out Reasoning|Leave-one-out reasoning] under noisy data.** With three disagreeing exams, Aaron repeatedly tests which single finding, if discarded, makes the rest cohere — and names his provisional discard rather than forcing a false unifier. A model of [[Diagnostic Humility|reasoning transparently under uncertainty]].
- **[[Hickam's Dictum|Two processes, not one]].** The final answer is genuinely dual — compressive cauda equina *and* diffuse leptomeningeal carcinomatosis — yet leptomeningeal spread is the near-unifier that reaches the face, arms, legs, and roots. The tension between a single elegant explanation and real-world coexisting pathology is the case's spine.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Reading enhancement, CSF, and PET correctly]].** Pachy- vs leptomeningeal enhancement (orange-peel model) carries different differentials; a very high CSF protein with negative cytology is [[Froin Syndrome|Froin]] plus an *insensitive* test, not a negative result; PET-negativity of thin leptomeninges doesn't exclude disease. Each is an interpretation, not a raw fact.
- **[[Illness Tempo|Tempo and the "when to worry" gestalt]].** Mundane symptoms (numbness, tingling, back pain) become alarming through pace + cachexia + a mass; the tempo is the trigger to escalate.
- **[[Cognitive Autopsy|Autopsy]]: resisting the seductive rare diagnosis.** Aaron flags his pull toward a rare [[Paraneoplastic Neurologic Syndrome|paraneoplastic]] syndrome and re-grounds in the base-rate question — "how would one thing get everywhere?" — landing on leptomeningeal metastases.

## Teaching Discussion

- Localize to the spine rather than the spinal cord so the nerve roots and cauda equina stay on your map; a symmetric sensorimotor syndrome below the neck lives in the spinal column (cord or roots) or peripheral nerves, and sensory-plus-motor involvement excludes muscle and neuromuscular junction.
- Use the length principle: most neuropathies are length-dependent (feet before hands), so hands and feet declaring together is non-length-dependent and shifts the differential to demyelinating neuropathy (CIDP/GBS) or a cord/root process.
- When a finding won't fit, apply leave-one-out reasoning — provisionally discard one symptom, see whether the rest cohere, and name what you set aside and why; bowel/bladder retention pulls toward cord/roots, and true facial numbness pulls above the cervical cord.
- Localize facial numbness along the trigeminal nerve (brainstem, meninges, skull base, or face); an isolated division points peripherally, numb chin syndrome suggests malignant mental-nerve infiltration, and perioral paresthesias can arise from hyperventilation-induced alkalosis and functional hypocalcemia.
- Trust — but reconcile — the exam: reflexes reflect the lesion's level (below L1 you strike roots, so expect absent reflexes and lower-motor-neuron signs), and when examiners disagree, re-examine at the bedside (sit the patient up, seek a sensory level on the back).
- Distinguish pachymeningeal (dural) from leptomeningeal (arachnoid/pia) enhancement, which carry different differentials; leptomeningeal enhancement over brain, cord, and roots in a cancer patient is carcinomatous meningitis until proven otherwise.
- Interpret confirmatory tests by their limits: a high CSF protein below a block is Froin syndrome, CSF cytology is insensitive for leptomeningeal cancer (up to three large-volume taps for high yield), and thin leptomeningeal disease can be PET- and even MRI-negative.
- Keep the cancer-and-neurology schema: a neurologic problem in a cancer patient is unrelated (diagnosis of exclusion), direct compression/metastasis, treatment-related, or paraneoplastic — and ask "how could one process reach all these sites?" before reaching for a rare paraneoplastic antibody.
- Read tempo as a red flag: rapidly progressive subacute neurologic decline with weight loss and a lung mass is a "when to worry" pattern that reframes common complaints toward malignancy.
- Deliver bad news with silence and presence: state it plainly, then wait — "toss the ball underhand and let the patient throw it back."
