> [!note] A guest-presented **ICU** case (Brandon Oto, critical care PA). Its explicit thesis: *the diagnosis is not the point — the process is*, and **many ICU patients pass through without a diagnosis** because supportive/empiric care crowds it out. The case ends **partly unsolved** (the patient died), which is the teaching.

## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 69-year-old man (diabetes, HTN, mild COPD, chronic trach for **vocal cord paralysis**, chronic back pain) is **found down** after weeks of worsening back pain and **progressive leg weakness** (to bedbound) plus a week of dyspnea (treated as CAP with antibiotics/steroids), now acutely encephalopathic. The ICU picture is a flood of data — **hypotension (60s/40s), hypothermia (33 °C), WBC 20, AKI (Cr 5.6), DKA, CRP >300, urinary retention (1.5 L), LLL opacity, hyporeflexia, absent plantars**. The reasoners model **ICU signal-vs-noise**: pick the **[[Center of Gravity|most severely abnormal datum as the core of the apple]]** and explain the rest through it. Hypotension + hypothermia → **[[Sepsis|sepsis]]** foremost (with [[Myxedema Coma|myxedema]] and [[Adrenal Insufficiency|adrenal insufficiency]] screened and negative), while the **encephalopathy + hyporeflexia + urinary retention + gait loss** force the **[[Neuroanatomical Localization|neuraxis]]** (cord/cauda, not brain, since the patient *reported* the gait decline). MRI shows only a **chronic, stable** intramedullary fat lesion plus nonspecific T2 cord signals; a difficult LP is a bloody, **[[Traumatic LP|RBC-corrected]]** non-pleocytosis. Then the real problem: **fever on ~37 of 50 days** despite treated infections — a **[[Fever of Unknown Origin|FUO]]** where the fever/illness **disconnect** points to drugs, autoimmune disease, or malignancy, and where **[[Fever vs Hyperthermia|fever vs hyperthermia]]** and drug fever get scrutinized. An exhaustive workup lands on a **[[Monoclonal Gammopathy|biclonal gammopathy]]** (IgG-λ + IgA heavy chain) with an inflammatory **[[Paraprotein-Associated Neuropathy|sural-nerve neuropathy]]** — a **[[Monoclonal Gammopathy of Clinical Significance|monoclonal gammopathy of clinical significance]]** on the [[POEMS Syndrome|POEMS]] spectrum — and, posthumously, an elevated **[[FGFR3 Antibody Neuropathy|FGFR3 antibody]]**. The fevers stay unexplained: some answers, not all.

## Pearls

- **In the data-rich ICU, the problem is [[Center of Gravity|signal vs noise]] — anchor on the most severely abnormal datum and explain the rest through it.** With a flood of abnormalities, pick the "core of the apple" and ask *which findings must I explain by the end?*, rather than chasing every value at once. ([[RLR 072 - Refractory Fever and Biclonal Gammopathy of Clinical Significance|RLR 072]])
- **Hypotension + hypothermia = [[Sepsis|sepsis]] until proven otherwise — but screen the endocrine mimics.** At 60/40 the pulse pressure is uninformative; touch the patient (cold/clammy → cardiogenic/hypovolemic, though end-stage sepsis is cold too). Hypothermia's low-hanging fruit is **[[Myxedema Coma|myxedema coma]]** (hypothermia, hyporeflexia, edema, obtundation → TSH) and **[[Adrenal Insufficiency|adrenal insufficiency]]** (endocrinopathies travel in pairs; check eosinophils; have a low threshold for stress-dose steroids). ([[RLR 072 - Refractory Fever and Biclonal Gammopathy of Clinical Significance|RLR 072]])
- **Encephalopathy + hyporeflexia + urinary retention + a *reported* gait decline localizes to the cord/cauda, not the brain.** A patient who can *relay* his progressive gait problem makes a brain cause less likely; hyporeflexia (LMN) with retention points to the **[[Cauda Equina Syndrome|cord/cauda]]** (or a [[Guillain-Barré Syndrome|GBS/Bickerstaff]] variant — though GBS doesn't typically cause retention). Don't image only the lumbosacral spine: a **[[Intramedullary Spinal Cord Lesion|cord lesion at any level above]]** can cause leg weakness and retention. ([[RLR 072 - Refractory Fever and Biclonal Gammopathy of Clinical Significance|RLR 072]])
- **Correct the [[Traumatic LP|CSF cell count]] for blood before calling a pleocytosis.** A very bloody tap (48,000 RBC) with ~50 nucleated cells is a *traumatic tap* — roughly 1 WBC per 1,000 RBC predicts ~48 — so it's "a champagne tap without being a champagne tap," not real inflammation. ([[RLR 072 - Refractory Fever and Biclonal Gammopathy of Clinical Significance|RLR 072]])
- **A fever that persists without clinical deterioration is a *disconnect* worth chasing.** Fever exists to alarm about impending serious infection; when it drones on while the patient stays status quo, think — **in the hospital, drugs first** ([[Drug Fever|drug fever]]), then the outpatient **[[Fever of Unknown Origin|FUO]]** triad (occult infection, autoimmune disease, malignancy), and only after an exhaustive search, **[[Central Fever|neurogenic/central fever]]**. ([[RLR 072 - Refractory Fever and Biclonal Gammopathy of Clinical Significance|RLR 072]])
- **Separate [[Fever vs Hyperthermia|fever from hyperthermia]] by the response to antipyretics.** True fever (a set-point elevation from cytokines/prostaglandins — infection, autoimmune, malignancy) responds to NSAIDs/acetaminophen; **hyperthermia** (heat production/dissipation failure, e.g., drug-induced) does not. ([[RLR 072 - Refractory Fever and Biclonal Gammopathy of Clinical Significance|RLR 072]])
- **Read a paraprotein for *type* and *company*, not just presence — and be wary in an inflamed patient.** Inflammation makes plasma cells proliferate *polyclonally* (a broad hump), so a **discrete [[Monoclonal Gammopathy|M-spike]]** in a febrile patient is odd; a **non-IgG** spike (here IgA) argues against simple [[MGUS|MGUS]], paraproteins can arise from lymphocytes (≈30% of CLL) as well as plasma cells, and a paraprotein *plus a neuropathy* raises **[[AL Amyloidosis|AL amyloid]]** and **[[POEMS Syndrome|POEMS]]** — the [[Monoclonal Gammopathy of Clinical Significance|"clinical significance"]] being harm from the protein's *structure*, not its quantity (cutaneous, neurologic, renal, hematologic — e.g. **[[Scleromyxedema|scleromyxedema]]**, **[[VEXAS Syndrome|VEXAS]]**). ([[RLR 072 - Refractory Fever and Biclonal Gammopathy of Clinical Significance|RLR 072]])
- **It is acceptable — and honest — to leave a case partly unsolved, and to put it on the record.** Some presentations sit at the edge of medical knowledge (VEXAS was named only ~2021); documenting the mystery lets a future reader recognize it, and the **primary team** is the one obligated to try. ([[RLR 072 - Refractory Fever and Biclonal Gammopathy of Clinical Significance|RLR 072]])

## Problem Representation

A 69-year-old man with diabetes, hypertension, mild COPD, a chronic tracheostomy for vocal cord paralysis, and chronic degenerative back pain is found down after three to four weeks of worsening back pain and progressive lower-extremity weakness (to bedbound) and one week of dyspnea (treated empirically for pneumonia), now acutely encephalopathic. He is hypotensive, hypothermic (33 °C), leukocytotic (WBC 20), in DKA with a severe AKI (Cr 5.6 from 0.6), hypocalcemic, with CRP >300, a left-lower-lobe opacity, and 1.5 L of retained urine; the neurologic exam shows a non-interactive encephalopathy with lower-extremity-predominant weakness, hyporeflexia, and absent plantar responses. Spine and brain MRI reveal only a chronic stable intramedullary fat-containing lesion with nonspecific T2 cord signals and chronic stenosis; a traumatic LP shows no true pleocytosis after RBC correction. Over a 50-day course he is febrile on ~37 days despite treated pseudomonas pneumonias and a sacral wound, with an exhaustive negative FUO workup except a new biclonal gammopathy (serum IgG-lambda and IgA heavy chain, urine IgG-lambda), an inflammatory sural-nerve neuropathy without amyloid, and a posthumously elevated FGFR3 antibody — best characterized as a monoclonal (biclonal) gammopathy of clinical significance with an associated neuropathy on the POEMS spectrum, with the fevers and encephalopathy left unexplained.

## Case Narrative

Brandon Oto, a critical-care PA, presents an ICU case explicitly as an exercise in **process over diagnosis**, framing the ICU's challenge as **data overload** (the intelligence-agency problem: too many streams, not too few) and the field's tendency to treat supportively without ever naming the disease. A **69-year-old man** — diabetes, HTN, mild COPD, a **chronic trach placed for vocal cord paralysis** (not respiratory failure), chronic back pain — is **found down** after weeks of worsening back pain and **progressive leg weakness** (walker → bedbound) plus a week of new dyspnea (treated outpatient as CAP/COPD with cefuroxime and prednisone), now acutely lethargic and non-verbal.

Rabih opens with **three parallel trains**: the *background* (the unusual trach for vocal-cord paralysis — hold that thread, as bilateral vocal cord palsy suggests local neck disease and may tie to a neurologic syndrome), and the *foreground* (encephalopathy, dyspnea, gait failure). AMS is most urgent; he screens hypoglycemia and — given a trach and possible neuromuscular weakness — **hypercarbia**, framing the key question as *is the neurologic process in the brain, or in the diaphragm (hypercarbia)?*

The ED data flood in: **hypotension 60s/40s, hypothermia 93 °F (33 °C)**, mild hypoxemia, a normal PCO₂, **WBC 20**, **AKI (Cr 5.6 from 0.6)**, **hyperglycemia 400–500 with ketosis (DKA)**, **Ca 6.9**, **CRP >300**, normal LFTs, a normal head CT, a **left-lower-lobe opacity**, and **1.5 L of retained urine** (new). He's intubated via the trach, started on vanc/pip-tazo, pressors, insulin, and fluids. The ICU exam: eyes open but not tracking, pupils reactive, follows no commands, withdraws arms (less in legs) to pain, **hyporeflexic** with **absent plantar responses**, mild diffuse edema, a few superficial **bullous lesions** on the thigh, and pale fingertips.

Reza models the **[[Center of Gravity|data-prioritization]] move**: with overwhelming data, anchor on the **most severely abnormal** finding — here the **hypotension** ("without pressure you die") — and explain the rest through it. He runs the **[[Shock|hypotension schema]]** (hypovolemic/obstructive/cardiogenic/distributive), notes the pulse pressure is useless at 60/40, and that **hypotension + hypothermia makes [[Sepsis|sepsis]] the greatest concern** (with aspiration risk from dysphagia/trach → severe pneumonia, MRSA/pseudomonas). He then works the **hypothermia** differential — **[[Myxedema Coma|myxedema coma]]** (hypothermia + hyporeflexia + edema + obtundation → TSH) and **[[Adrenal Insufficiency|adrenal insufficiency]]** (endocrinopathies travel in pairs; check eosinophils; low threshold for stress-dose steroids) — while noting the hyperglycemia/ketosis argues *against* hypocortisolism, and that stress hyperglycemia can shock the pancreas into ketosis. And he flags the **encephalopathy + hyporeflexia + back pain + gait failure** as possibly a **cord** process (shock cord, myelopathy) or a **[[Guillain-Barré Syndrome|GBS/Bickerstaff]]** variant, admitting he lacks a unifying formulation and would "chase every thread."

Rabih reframes the infection question: there are **too many signals** (lung; urinary retention as UTI/BPH), the patient may be **too young (60s)** to be this sick from simple pneumonia/UTI, and the **subacute tail** argues for pausing. He runs the infection checklist (bacteremia, liver, **neuraxis** — a *diffuse* meningitis/encephalitis, not focal) and highlights the **urinary retention** as alarming for a **[[Cauda Equina Syndrome|cord/cauda]]** problem. His practical playbook: antibiotics, hydrate, cross-sectional CT — but the real questions are **MRI of the neuraxis and an LP**, with concrete tips (hold prophylactic anticoagulation if tapping; **MRI before LP**; confirm no pacemaker), and the localization pearl that a *reported* gait decline makes a **brain** cause less likely than **cord/cauda/GBS**.

Endocrine screens (eosinophils, **TSH, cortisol**) come back **normal**, closing the myxedema/adrenal doors. A non-contrast **MRI** (AKI) shows an **intramedullary T12 fat-containing lesion** (dermoid/lipoma) that is **chronic and stable over ~10 years** — *not* the cause — plus chronic cauda root compression and **two nonspecific short-segment T2 hyperintensities** (T6 and T12/L1). Reza teaches the **[[Intramedullary Spinal Cord Lesion|cord-lesion]] lesson** — leg weakness + retention can localize to *any* level, so don't image only the lumbosacral spine — and works the **T2-cord-signal** differential (non-traumatic: inflammation [infection, cancer, sarcoid] vs demyelination [MS, NMOSD, ADEM]). A later **gadolinium** MRI of the whole neuraxis and brain is **normal** (no demyelination, no cord enhancement), and a technically brutal **LP** (IR, 1 cc) shows **48,000 RBC with ~50 nucleated cells** — which Reza corrects for blood (~1 WBC/1,000 RBC ≈ 48) to reveal **no true pleocytosis** ("a champagne tap without being a champagne tap").

The case then becomes a **50-day course** with **fever on ~37 of 50 days** (101–103 °F, often >20 h/day) despite recurrent **pseudomonas pneumonias** and a debrided **sacral wound** — the fevers outlasting the infections. Rabih names the **disconnect**: a fever that persists *without* clinical deterioration loses its alarm meaning, so in the hospital scrutinize **[[Drug Fever|drugs]] first**, then the **[[Fever of Unknown Origin|FUO]]** triad (occult infection, autoimmune, malignancy), and only after an exhaustive search, **[[Central Fever|neurogenic fever]]**; reach for cheap tests (**LDH, ferritin**) and lean on pharmacy/ID. Reza adds the **[[Fever vs Hyperthermia|fever-vs-hyperthermia]]** discriminator (response to antipyretics: true fever falls, hyperthermia doesn't) and validates the RBC-corrected CSF read.

The exhaustive workup: LDH/ferritin indeterminate (ferritin ~900); EEG generalized slowing; a broad **autoimmune panel** (ANA, dsDNA, ANCA, HLA-B27, anti-NMDA, anti-CCP, anti-Smith, SSA/SSB) negative; **RF mildly elevated (16)**; tick panel/HIV/beta-D-glucan/galactomannan/C. diff/O&P negative; a **HepB core IgM** positive but surface-Ag/PCR negative (false positive); a **temporal artery biopsy** normal; CT sinuses with bland mucosal thickening; a **CTA** revealing a **PE** (anticoagulated → an obturator hematoma → held); and finally **SPEP/immunofixation** showing **two discrete spikes** — a serum **IgG-lambda** and an **IgA heavy chain** (no clear light chain) — with a **urine monoclonal IgG-lambda**, elevated but **ratio-normal free light chains**, and a **normal IFE three years prior** (so new).

Reza and Rabih interpret the **[[Monoclonal Gammopathy|paraprotein]]**: SPEP distinguishes a broad **polyclonal** hump (inflammation) from a discrete **M-spike** (one clone), and it is **odd for an inflamed, febrile patient to have a monoclonal protein** (inflammation → polyclonal). **[[Multiple Myeloma|Myeloma]] essentially never causes fever**; is this **[[MGUS|MGUS]]** (IgG, <1.5 g/dL, no end-organ CRAB) — argued against by the **IgA** component and the difficulty of assessing end-organ damage in a critically ill patient? Rabih broadens to a **[[Monoclonal Gammopathy of Clinical Significance|monoclonal gammopathy of clinical significance]]**, where the protein is harmful by **structure, not burden**, across cutaneous/neurologic/renal/hematologic categories — naming the rare, *hectic* paraprotein syndromes **[[Scleromyxedema|scleromyxedema]]** and **[[VEXAS Syndrome|VEXAS]]** (check the MCV; discovered ~2021) — and notes paraproteins can arise from **lymphocytes** too (≈30% of CLL). The paraprotein-plus-neuropathy pairing raises **[[AL Amyloidosis|AL amyloid]]** and **[[POEMS Syndrome|POEMS]]**.

The dedicated workup: a **bone marrow** with **5–10% plasma cells** (slight lambda) — a plasma-cell neoplasm, *not* myeloma; a **sural-nerve biopsy** with **myelin and axon loss**, **Congo-red pink staining but no apple-green birefringence** (not amyloid), and **CD68 macrophages/CD3 T cells** (inflammatory) — raising **POEMS**; low testosterone (soft), a **VEGF** initially normal then mildly elevated (129; ULN 86), normal PTH, no organomegaly, no sclerotic lesions. Tumor board: **not quite POEMS**, but a **biclonal gammopathy of clinical significance** with the neuropathy as the significance. **IVIG** (2 days) had no impact; before rituximab/steroids, the patient was made **comfort measures** and died.

**Posthumously**, an NMO IgG was negative, but a sensory-motor antibody panel returned an elevated **[[FGFR3 Antibody Neuropathy|FGFR3 antibody]] (8,000; ULN 3,000)**. Brandon shares what he later learned: FGFR3-antibody neuropathy is a **Protean** entity (subacute/chronic, demyelinating/axonal, sensory/motor, length-dependent or not), tested only at WashU, with a level that doesn't track disease, typically presenting with **paresthesias, gait issues, and autonomic/neurogenic-bladder features** (no fever described), associated with autoimmune conditions and some cancers — and possibly linked to the **t(4;14)** translocation (FGFR3 dysregulation) seen in some myeloma/MGUS. He also contrasts **IgM-MGUS neuropathy** (better-defined anti-MAG/anti-ganglioside antibodies) with the murkier **non-IgM** case here.

Both close on the episode's thesis: **we don't answer many cases**, and that's honest — some sit at the **edge of knowledge**, and putting them **on the record** lets a future reader recognize the pattern. Brandon's parting challenge: the failure is usually not the difficulty but that **we don't try hard enough**, and the **primary team** — whoever holds the patient — is the one obligated to try.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Center of Gravity|Prioritization under data overload]].** The signature ICU move: choose the most severely abnormal datum as the core and explain outward, and enumerate *which findings must be explained* — a defense against drowning in numbers.
- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|Stacked schemas]].** Hypotension, hypothermia, encephalopathy, T2-cord-signal, FUO, and paraprotein schemas each convert a chaotic picture into a few discriminable branches — and screening the endocrine mimics of hypothermia early prevents fixation on sepsis alone.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Interpreting, not tallying]].** RBC-correcting the CSF, reading SPEP type/company, the fever-vs-hyperthermia antipyretic test, ferritin/VEGF nuance, and a false-positive HepB core IgM — every result is weighed for meaning.
- **[[Neuroanatomical Localization|Localization discipline]].** A *reported* gait decline down-weights the brain; retention + hyporeflexia up-weight cord/cauda; and "don't image only the lumbosacral spine" keeps the whole cord in view.
- **[[Base Rate|Base rate on the paraprotein]].** A monoclonal spike is common with age (MGUS) but *odd* in an inflamed patient, and a non-IgG component and an associated neuropathy shift it from incidental to clinically significant — resisting both over- and under-reading.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Tolerating an unsolved case]].** The explicit acceptance that not every case is answerable, that some sit at the edge of knowledge, and that documenting the mystery is itself valuable — with the primary team owning the effort.

## Teaching Discussion

- In the data-rich ICU, treat diagnosis as signal-versus-noise: anchor on the most severely abnormal finding, explain the rest through it, and list the findings you must account for.
- Read hypotension plus hypothermia as sepsis until proven otherwise, but screen myxedema coma and adrenal insufficiency (TSH, cortisol, eosinophils) and keep a low threshold for stress-dose steroids.
- Localize encephalopathy with hyporeflexia and urinary retention to the cord/cauda (a reported gait decline argues against a brain cause), and image the whole cord rather than only the lumbosacral spine.
- Correct the CSF cell count for a bloody tap (~1 WBC per 1,000 RBC) before diagnosing a pleocytosis.
- Chase a fever that persists without clinical deterioration as a disconnect: in-hospital, suspect drug fever first, then the FUO triad, then neurogenic fever, and distinguish fever from hyperthermia by the response to antipyretics.
- Interpret a paraprotein by type and associated features: a discrete or non-IgG spike in a febrile patient is odd, paraproteins can arise from lymphocytes, and a paraprotein plus neuropathy raises AL amyloid, POEMS, and the broader monoclonal-gammopathy-of-clinical-significance syndromes (scleromyxedema, VEXAS).
- Accept that some cases remain unsolved at the edge of current knowledge; document them for future recognition, and recognize that the primary team is obligated to make the diagnostic effort.
