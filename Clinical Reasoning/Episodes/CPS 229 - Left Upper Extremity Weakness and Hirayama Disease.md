## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A Neurology VMR built around a **41-year-old man** with **roughly one year of progressive, now-static left ring/small-finger and grip weakness** — first noticed shifting gears on a motorcycle and buttoning a shirt — accompanied, from about six months in, by twitching that spread to all four limbs, superimposed on prominent, oddly distributed hand wasting. Discussants build the **[[Anatomical Approach|full motor pathway]]** from cortex to muscle, initially pattern-match the medial two-digit weakness to an **ulnar neuropathy** (both a discussant and Berkowitz momentarily "hear" tingling that was never actually reported — a self-caught **[[Anchoring Bias|anchoring bias]]**), then widen out toward **[[Motor Neuron Disease|ALS/motor neuron disease]]** once the twitching emerges, using Berkowitz's own referral-bias anecdotes about how a clinician's recent case mix inflates their "worry meter" for a diagnosis. Exam undercuts the tidy nerve story — wasting spans **both median- and ulnar-innervated intrinsic hand muscles**, weakness is **distal greater than proximal**, reflexes are **mildly diminished rather than brisk**, and **no fasciculations are observed** despite the reported history — and undercuts ALS too, since a truly static one-limb course over a year without bulbar, sensory, or other-limb involvement fits poorly. A standard cervical MRI is unrevealing, but a **dynamic flexion MRI** — obtained specifically because the presenter's attending recognized the striking oblique hand-wasting pattern — shows loss of the posterior CSF space and cord compression on neck flexion that resolves in neutral position, confirming **[[Hirayama Disease|Hirayama disease]]** (monomelic amyotrophy / cervical flexion myelopathy), a rare, usually self-limited anterior-horn-cell disease easily mistaken for a nerve, root, or motor-neuron-disease process on standard imaging and exam alone.

## Pearls

- **A same-sided pattern that spans more than one nerve's territory is a localization clue, not a coincidence.** Wasting confined to ulnar-innervated muscles points to the ulnar nerve; wasting in **both** median- (thenar) and ulnar- (hypothenar/interossei) innervated muscles means the process must be more proximal — a root, multiple roots, the plexus, or the anterior horn cells themselves.
- **"Radial nerve is the BEST"** — Brachioradialis, Extensors (wrist/finger/thumb), Supinator, Triceps — and for the hand, remember **median = thumb side, ulnar = pinky side** (with the flexor digitorum profundus breaking the rule: median for digits 1–2, ulnar for 3–4).
- **A weak, wasted limb can have inappropriately "normal" or preserved reflexes** — the presence (rather than absence) of reflexes in a severely atrophied limb should itself raise suspicion for a combined upper-plus-lower-motor-neuron process like **[[Motor Neuron Disease|ALS]]**, the same way a "normal" hormone level can be inappropriately normal in the wrong clinical context.
- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring on the expected symptom, not just the expected diagnosis.]]** Two separate discussants both "recalled" hearing that the patient had tingling — because an ulnar-looking hand pattern primes the expectation of a sensory complaint — when in fact the presenter had said only twitching, never tingling. Catching this in real time is as important as catching diagnostic anchoring.
- **Standard-position cervical MRI can be entirely normal in [[Hirayama Disease|Hirayama disease]].** The pathology (excessive anterior cord displacement/compression against the vertebral bodies on flexion, with engorgement of the posterior epidural venous plexus) is only visible on a **dynamic flexion MRI** — order it specifically when the exam pattern (young adult, unilateral or asymmetric distal arm wasting, static after progression) suggests the diagnosis.
- **EMG is an active, iterative extension of the physical exam, not a passive recording like an EKG or EEG.** The electromyographer tests one muscle, updates their hypothesis, and chooses the next muscle based on that result — narrowing a mononeuropathy vs. polyradiculopathy vs. plexopathy vs. anterior-horn-cell process the same way a clinician narrows a differential at the bedside.

## Problem Representation

A 41-year-old man presents with roughly one year of progressive, now-static left-hand and forearm weakness and wasting — beginning with the ring and little finger, causing difficulty shifting motorcycle gears and buttoning a shirt, and progressing to difficulty lifting objects before plateauing — accompanied by oddly distributed intrinsic hand wasting extending toward the forearm and, from about six months after onset, twitching that spread to involve all four limbs (though no fasciculations were witnessed on formal exam). There is no sensory disturbance, no cranial nerve or bulbar involvement, no cerebellar signs, no bowel/bladder or autonomic dysfunction, and no systemic symptoms (fever, weight loss, rash, arthralgias). A local physician had raised possible motor neuron disease, but this was never formally evaluated. Exam shows a normal general and systemic exam (no rashes, nerve thickening, connective tissue stigmata, or Horner syndrome), normal cranial nerves, small-muscle wasting and hypotonia of the left hand spanning both median- and ulnar-innervated intrinsic muscles, mildly diminished (1+) reflexes throughout including the affected limb, normal sensation and cerebellar exam, and a distal-greater-than-proximal left-arm weakness gradient (wrist 3/5; elbow flexion/extension 4–5/5) without weakness elsewhere. CPK is mildly elevated (504); routine labs, viral serology, and a standard-position cervical MRI are unremarkable. A dynamic cervical flexion MRI shows loss of the posterior CSF space with cord compression around C6–C7 on neck flexion — normal in the neutral position — confirming Hirayama disease (monomelic amyotrophy / cervical flexion myelopathy) as a static, anterior-horn-cell process mimicking both a peripheral mononeuropathy and early motor neuron disease.

## Case Narrative

Vijay presents a 41-year-old man with left upper-limb weakness. Berkowitz asks Valeria for her first thoughts, and she gives a comprehensive framework: localize along the motor pathway from cortex through corona radiata, internal capsule, brainstem, decussation, spinal cord, anterior horn cell, root, plexus, nerve, neuromuscular junction, and muscle, while separately tracking tempo — sudden onset favoring vascular causes, more gradual onset opening infectious, inflammatory, and neoplastic causes — and flagging that extrinsic compression (for example, from a structure at the lung apex) could also affect the motor pathway peripherally. Berkowitz builds on this, walking through the **[[Anatomical Approach|full motor pathway]]** in detail: a cortical lesion could produce isolated arm weakness given the hand's disproportionate representation in the motor **homunculus** (perhaps with subtle, unnoticed facial involvement); internal capsule lesions isolated to the arm are possible but would need to be very small given how tightly packed the face/arm/leg fibers are there; brainstem is similarly unlikely to cause pure arm involvement without other findings; and the spinal cord or peripheral nervous system (plexus, root, or nerve) remain live possibilities, with the pattern and distribution of weakness and sensory loss needed to localize further. He distinguishes sudden brain lesions (favoring stroke) from the acute-to-chronic tempo spectrum relevant to infectious, inflammatory, and neoplastic causes elsewhere along the pathway.

Berkowitz asks John what he wants to know before hearing the history. John wants to confirm this is *true* weakness rather than incoordination or **[[Illness Tempo|asthenia]]** misperceived as weakness, and wants associated symptoms — sensory, systemic, or elsewhere along the neuraxis — to sharpen the localization. Berkowitz reinforces this: a patient's subjective "weakness" might really be sensory loss impairing grip, ataxia/clumsiness, or generalized asthenia (fatigue from illness, anemia, etc.) — which would be atypical if this focal — and stresses asking about pain, sensory change (positive symptoms like tingling/neuropathic pain vs. negative symptoms like numbness), systemic features, and, critically, whether there is *any* weakness elsewhere, since patients (especially older ones) may dismiss early proximal-leg weakness as just "getting older."

Vijay reports the HPI: the patient first noticed weakness of the ring and little finger of the left hand while shifting gears on his motorcycle, then difficulty buttoning and unbuttoning his shirt, progressing to difficulty lifting objects off the ground. This progressed over about one year and then became static. There is associated thinning of the hand, with a striking, oblique wasting pattern extending from the medial hand up into the forearm. Six months after the weakness began, twitching appeared in the same region and later spread to all four limbs, with no clear aggravating or relieving factors and no temperature dependence. There is no sensory disturbance, no cerebellar symptoms, no cranial nerve symptoms, no weakness in any other limb, no bowel/bladder or autonomic involvement, and no fever, weight loss, rash, or joint pain. Past medical history is unremarkable and the patient is on no medications; notably, a local doctor had previously raised the possibility of motor neuron disease but the patient was never formally worked up until presenting here.

John, hearing this, initially thinks of a low **ulnar mononeuropathy** given the ring/little-finger weakness and hypothenar-pattern atrophy, but is uncertain how to fit the reported twitching in all four limbs with a single mononeuropathy — raising either multiple neuropathies or, more likely, **ALS/motor neuron disease** — while noting ALS doesn't typically cause sensory change, which fits the case so far. He also flags that "twitching" needs clarification on exam (fasciculation vs. tremor) and wonders whether MS is plausible, though the patient's age (41) is a bit old for a first MS presentation. Berkowitz then catches something: both he and John recall hearing that the patient had *tingling* — but Vijay clarifies there was never any tingling, only twitching. Berkowitz names this explicitly as **[[Anchoring Bias|anchoring bias]]**: because the medial-two-finger pattern primed the expectation of an ulnar sensory complaint, both of them "heard" a symptom that was never actually reported.

Berkowitz uses this as a launchpad to teach upper-extremity nerve anatomy: the five major arm nerves are musculocutaneous (biceps — "making a muscle" mnemonic), axillary (deltoid/teres minor — both very rarely injured in isolation given how short and proximal they are off the plexus), radial ("radial nerve is the **BEST**": Brachioradialis, wrist/finger/thumb Extensors, Supinator, Triceps), median, and ulnar. For the hand, median generally governs the thumb (lateral) side and ulnar the pinky (medial) side, with the flexor digitorum profundus breaking the rule (median for digits 1–2, ulnar for 3–4) and the interossei/finger abductors and adductors all ulnar. Because most median-innervated forearm muscles are long flexors while median's only intrinsic hand muscle is the abductor pollicis brevis (carpal-tunnel territory), ulnar neuropathy tends to be more functionally disabling. The ulnar nerve is palpable at the elbow (the "funny bone"), and its sensory territory is distal to the wrist only — useful for distinguishing it from a C8 root lesion, whose sensory territory extends proximal to the wrist. Berkowitz then asks John directly: given that the reported tingling wasn't real, what does the *absence* of sensory symptoms alongside these motor features suggest? John answers ALS/motor neuron disease, reasoning that fasciculations (the "twitching") are a classic ALS feature.

Berkowitz elaborates on motor neuron disease broadly — ALS is the most common variant (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis; "Lou Gehrig's disease"), with recognized subtypes including a bibrachial variant (both arms first), a bulbar variant, **[[Motor Neuron Disease|progressive lateral sclerosis]]** (pure upper motor neuron), **progressive muscular atrophy** (pure lower motor neuron), and pediatric **spinal muscular atrophies** (now treated with intrathecal antisense oligonucleotides). He shares an anecdote about a patient with an apparently simple perineal-nerve foot drop (from sleeping outdoors) that an EMG unexpectedly revealed to be ALS with fasciculations across the entire chest — illustrating how ALS can present very focally and progressively in one limb before spreading, and how a specialist who has recently seen several such cases develops an elevated "worry meter" (a **[[Base Rate|base-rate]]**/referral-bias effect) that a generalist might not share. He also flags **Hirayama disease** as a rare motor-neuron-disease mimic to keep in mind for this kind of presentation, and notes the "roundsmanship" **anchoring** lesson: being told a prior clinician's suspected diagnosis (here, motor neuron disease) should not make the group less likely to consider it, even though morning-report culture sometimes creates that instinct.

Asked what would help distinguish this from motor neuron disease, Valeria wants to confirm the exact pattern of weakness (isolated to one nerve/root territory or broader), whether reflexes are affected (expecting them preserved if this is purely one nerve or root), and sensory findings. Berkowitz recommends Michael Charnas's detailed video exam of the upper extremity (naming muscle, nerve, and root for each) as a teaching resource, and reiterates that even just knowing the five major nerves lets a clinician determine whether a pattern is mono-, poly-, or non-neuropathic. Asked what to look for specifically in ALS, Valeria (drawing on a prior VMR) correctly predicts a **mixed upper-and-lower-motor-neuron picture within the same limb** — atrophy, fasciculations, *and* brisk reflexes together, or absent reflexes with a Babinski — one of the few conditions to produce that combination. Berkowitz confirms this and lists other UMN+LMN mimickers: B12 deficiency (myelopathy plus neuropathy, though usually with sensory findings this patient lacks), cervical spondylotic myelopathy with a coexisting diabetic neuropathy (the most common real-world combination), and rare adrenomyeloneuropathy.

Vijay presents the exam: normal vitals; no rashes, nerve thickening, connective tissue stigmata, facial dysmorphism, or Horner syndrome; normal cranial nerves including jaw jerk; small-muscle hand wasting with hypotonia spanning **both median- and ulnar-innervated territory**; reflexes mildly diminished (1+) throughout, including biceps, triceps, supinator, knee, and ankle; normal sensation and cerebellar exam; and left-arm weakness graded 3/5 at the wrist versus 4–5/5 at the elbow (distal greater than proximal), with no weakness elsewhere. No fasciculations were observed during the roughly 90-minute exam despite the reported twitching history. Berkowitz highlights the reflex finding: rather than being brisk (as classic ALS teaching predicts), reflexes here are mildly *diminished* — though he notes reflexes could also be "inappropriately" preserved (not fully absent) in a limb this weak and wasted, the way a hormone level can be inappropriately normal in the wrong clinical context, which would still keep ALS on the table. Combined with no fasciculations anywhere and a truly static one-year course, though, ALS becomes somewhat less likely.

Berkowitz points out the pattern goes beyond a pure ulnar distribution — the thenar eminence (median-innervated abductor pollicis brevis) is also wasted — meaning the process cannot be confined to the ulnar nerve alone, and must involve either multiple nerves, a root or multiple roots (C7 on the thumb side, C8 on the pinky side), or a more proximal anterior-horn-cell process. He asks why Vijay specifically reported *no* Horner syndrome; Valeria correctly reasons this was to assess for a lower brachial plexus process (which can compress the nearby **oculosympathetic pathway**, running from hypothalamus through the dorsolateral brainstem down to C8–T1 and back up along the carotid to the eye, producing ptosis, miosis, and anhidrosis) — its absence makes a lower-plexus or lung-apex lesion somewhat less likely, though not impossible. Vijay then describes the striking oblique wasting pattern extending from the hand into the forearm, sparing the brachioradialis.

Berkowitz reiterates that reflexes being present (rather than absent) in such a wasted, weak limb could still be "inappropriately" preserved evidence for ALS, but that the absence of fasciculations anywhere and the one-year static course argue the other way. Asked what tests would help, John suggests EMG (to characterize the pattern of muscle involvement) and an MRI, which Berkowitz confirms should be of the cervical spine (or brachial plexus, if the radiology team can protocol it). Berkowitz frames EMG as an *active*, iterative test — unlike a passively recorded EKG or EEG, the electromyographer tests a muscle, updates their working hypothesis based on the result (is this median, or median-plus-C6/C7?), and chooses the next muscle to test accordingly — effectively extending the physical exam into muscles that are hard to assess directly. He also raises other motor-neuron-disease mimics worth knowing: HTLV-1 myelopathy (from a prior patient case) and, rarely, HIV, and observes that Vijay's mention of no skin findings was likely meant to screen for leprosy (endemic in India, causing thickened, palpable nerves near the skin, especially cold areas like the nose) or vasculitic mononeuritis multiplex, both of which the exam argues against.

Vijay reveals the diagnosis-defining data: CPK was mildly elevated (504); other labs, viral serology, and a standard-position cervical MRI were unremarkable (mild multilevel disc bulges only). But because the wasting pattern was so distinctive, his attending obtained a **dynamic flexion cervical MRI**, which showed loss of the posterior CSF space and cord compression around C6–C7 specifically when the neck was flexed — normal in the neutral position. Berkowitz recognizes this as classic for **Hirayama disease** (juvenile/young-adult monomelic amyotrophy), describing his own single prior exposure (a former piano student of his old piano teacher) and noting the mechanism is thought to involve an abnormally mobile posterior dura/ligamentous structure that compresses the anterior horn cells of the cord on flexion — meaning a standard, neutral-position MRI is very often falsely normal, and the diagnosis requires deliberately imaging in flexion. He notes the condition is generally sporadic, without a well-established genetic basis.

Vijay shares a teaching pearl from his attending: because the wasting doesn't fit any single peripheral nerve (radial would need wrist *and* finger drop together; ulnar would need to spare the index/middle fingers, which it doesn't here) and doesn't fit a myotome-based motor-neuron-disease pattern either, the localization has to be *within* a segment — a sub-localization pattern that pointed toward Hirayama disease specifically. He notes his center has seen three to four cases; one patient with continued progression underwent surgical dural decompression and improved, though most patients (diagnosed only after the limb is already wasted, as here) plateau and are managed conservatively, sometimes with a cervical collar, since the benefit of surgery at that stage is uncertain. Berkowitz closes by reflecting on how rare and easy to overlook this diagnosis is, thanking Vijay for a beautifully presented case with well-placed exam details (the absent Horner sign, absent skin findings) that quietly steered the group's localization throughout. Gabby closes with a structured recap reinforcing the key teaching points: localization-before-tempo as the opening move, distinguishing true weakness from asthenia, attending to positive/negative sensory symptoms and systemic features, the four-tier tempo framework (hyperacute/acute/subacute/chronic), the radial "BEST" mnemonic and five-nerve framework, and the differential for combined upper-and-lower-motor-neuron presentations (ALS, B12 deficiency, Hirayama disease, HIV, HTLV-1).

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring on an expected symptom, not just an expected diagnosis.]]** Two discussants independently "heard" tingling that the presenter never reported, because the ulnar-looking hand pattern primed the expectation of a sensory complaint — a reminder that anchoring can distort what a listener *perceives*, not only what they conclude.
- **[[Anchoring Bias|"Roundsmanship" / referral anchoring.]]** Berkowitz names the instinct to discount a diagnosis simply because a prior clinician already suggested it ("would he make it that easy for us?") as its own bias, and warns against uncritically accepting *or* uncritically dismissing another clinician's suspicion.
- **[[Base Rate|The "worry meter" effect of recent case exposure.]]** Berkowitz's anecdote about an EMG specialist whose recent run of ALS-in-disguise patients kept their index of suspicion elevated illustrates how a clinician's personal case mix — not just true population base rates — calibrates diagnostic vigilance.
- **[[Illness Script|Testing the exam against a composite illness script.]]** The group explicitly checks this patient against the ALS script (atrophy + fasciculations + brisk reflexes together in one limb) and finds a mismatch (diminished reflexes, no observed fasciculations), using the mismatch itself as diagnostic information rather than forcing the case to fit.
- **[[Test Interpretation|EMG and dynamic imaging as active, hypothesis-driven tests.]]** EMG is framed as an iterative extension of the physical exam, and the decision to obtain a *dynamic flexion* MRI (rather than trusting a normal standard-position study) reflects the same principle: order the test that actually interrogates the leading hypothesis.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Sitting with "this isn't making sense."]]** Berkowitz explicitly pauses mid-case to acknowledge the pattern doesn't fit any single peripheral nerve, root, or the classic ALS script — modeling tolerance for a genuinely unresolved localization before more data (the flexion MRI) arrives.

## Teaching Discussion

- Localize weakness along the full motor pathway (cortex → corona radiata → internal capsule → brainstem → decussation → cord → anterior horn cell → root → plexus → nerve → NMJ → muscle) before weighting tempo, and remember the hand's outsized cortical representation can make even a small cortical lesion present as isolated arm/hand weakness.
- Know the five major arm nerves and their core actions: musculocutaneous (biceps), axillary (deltoid/teres minor, rarely injured alone), radial ("BEST" — brachioradialis, extensors, supinator, triceps), median (thumb-side hand, long forearm flexors), and ulnar (pinky-side hand, interossei/hand intrinsics) — enough to determine whether a pattern fits one nerve, multiple nerves, one root, multiple roots, or a plexus/proximal process.
- Wasting or weakness that spans more than one nerve's territory (e.g., both median- and ulnar-innervated hand muscles) rules out a simple mononeuropathy and should prompt consideration of a root, plexus, or anterior-horn-cell process.
- Distinguish true weakness from asthenia (a global sense of tiredness/illness, atypical when this focal) and from sensory loss or ataxia misperceived as weakness, by directly asking about positive/negative sensory symptoms, pain, and systemic features.
- Recognize the ALS/motor neuron disease illness script as classically requiring BOTH upper motor neuron (brisk reflexes, Babinski) and lower motor neuron (atrophy, fasciculations) signs, often in the *same* limb — and know the variant spectrum (bibrachial, bulbar, progressive lateral sclerosis, progressive muscular atrophy, spinal muscular atrophy) and mimics (B12 deficiency, cervical spondylotic myelopathy plus neuropathy, HIV, HTLV-1, adrenomyeloneuropathy, Hirayama disease).
- Suspect Hirayama disease (monomelic amyotrophy/cervical flexion myelopathy) in a young adult with static, unilateral or asymmetric, distally-predominant arm/hand wasting after a period of progression — and know that standard-position cervical MRI is frequently normal, requiring a dedicated dynamic flexion MRI to demonstrate cord compression.
- Use EMG as an active, iterative test that narrows mononeuropathy vs. polyradiculopathy vs. plexopathy vs. anterior-horn-cell disease, the same way sequential bedside findings narrow a differential.
- Use absence of Horner syndrome to argue (not prove) against a lower brachial plexus or lung-apex process, and absence of skin/nerve findings to argue against leprosy or vasculitic mononeuritis multiplex in the right epidemiologic context.
