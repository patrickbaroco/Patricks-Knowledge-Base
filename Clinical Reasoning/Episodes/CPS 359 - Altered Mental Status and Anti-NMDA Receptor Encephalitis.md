## Pearls

- **A young person with new psychiatric symptoms, no psychiatric history, and a viral prodrome is [[Anti-NMDA Receptor Encephalitis|anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis]] until proven otherwise.** First described by Josep Dalmau (~2007), it classically evolves through a systemic prodrome → a psychiatric phase (often with amnesia, misdiagnosed as first-break schizophrenia) → a movement disorder (orofacial dyskinesia) → seizures and [[Dysautonomia|dysautonomia]]; treatment walks the patient back through the stages. Roughly **50% have an ovarian teratoma**, so hunt for one.
- **[[Altered Mental Status|Altered mental status]] = [[Anatomical Approach|localization]] × [[Illness Tempo|time course]], first gated medical vs neurologic vs psychiatric.** AMS localizes to brain; the signal-to-noise for a *neurologic* cause among hospital consults is low (most are toxic-metabolic), so the discipline is to not miss the rare, treatable neurologic and psychiatric diagnoses. Sort the neurologic causes by tempo: sudden (seizure/post-ictal, hemorrhage, select strokes), acute (meningitis/encephalitis), subacute (immune-mediated, mass), chronic (degenerative).
- **A few ischemic strokes cause *pure* altered mental status — know them.** Bilateral thalamic infarcts (artery of Percheron), a basilar/brainstem stroke hitting the reticular activating system, bilateral ACA infarcts (personality change), a PCA infarct presenting as amnesia, a shower of emboli disrupting networks diffusely, and a right inferior-division MCA infarct presenting as acute agitated confusion. Otherwise, an ischemic stroke usually brings a focal deficit, and depressed consciousness favors hemorrhage (immediate mass effect).
- **When "altered mental status" is really a focal cognitive deficit, the case changes.** Delirium means attention is the primary modality affected; but a consult for "confusion" sometimes turns out to be aphasia, amnesia, or apraxia — a focal process masquerading as global encephalopathy. Name which it is before committing to a differential.
- **Send the NMDA antibody from CSF, not serum, and don't wait on it to act.** Serum NMDA antibodies are non-specific — series exist of serum-positive patients without the disease, treated inappropriately — so **CSF is the specific test**; it also takes weeks (reference labs run immunofluorescence on brain tissue). Meanwhile, a bland viral-pattern CSF, a normal early MRI, and negative HSV/VZV PCR let you peel off antibiotics, and a persistently sick, undiagnosed patient can reasonably get empiric high-dose steroids while the antibody is pending.
- **Cell-surface vs intracellular antibodies is the organizing distinction.** [[Cell-Surface vs Intracellular Antibodies|Cell-surface antibodies]] (NMDA, LGI1) are directly pathogenic, often **non-paraneoplastic**, and very treatable — remove the antibody (plasma exchange, rituximab) and patients recover. Intracellular/onconeural antibodies (anti-Hu, anti-Yo) are almost always **paraneoplastic** and portend poor recovery because neuronal death has already occurred.

## Problem Representation

A previously healthy 30-year-old woman, one week out from an upper respiratory infection, presents over two days with acute confusion, agitation, a delusional fear of dying, echolalia, and apparent auditory hallucinations — an abrupt neuropsychiatric syndrome with no prior psychiatric history. After antipsychotics and a benzodiazepine she develops whole-body rigidity, low-grade fever, and tachycardia, prompting neurology consultation; exam shows catatonia with waxy repositioning, orofacial dyskinesia, impaired swallowing, and near-mutism with preserved wakefulness. Work-up shows a mildly elevated CRP and CPK, a normal admission toxicology screen, a normal CT, diffuse EEG slowing, and a bland viral-pattern CSF (10 lymphocytes, protein 105, normal glucose, negative HSV/VZV/enterovirus PCR), with an initially normal MRI and a negative pelvic ultrasound. The evolving diagnosis is an immune-mediated limbic encephalitis — most likely anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis — confirmed by a second MRI showing left hippocampal signal and a CSF NMDA antibody returning positive at twenty days, in a patient who deteriorated into dysautonomia and respiratory failure before recovering with plasma exchange.

## Case Narrative

Another entry in the CP Solvers campaign to **[[Neurophobia|end neurophobia]]**, with neurologist **Aaron Berkowitz** teaching, an aspiring neurologist from Lima — Valeria — presenting, and A. Chan reasoning alongside. The chief concern is deliberately bare: **altered mental status**.

**Framing AMS before the history.** Berkowitz builds the approach with A. The broad buckets are **medical, neurologic, and psychiatric**, and he stresses the real-world challenge: neurologists are consulted for [[Altered Mental Status|AMS]] so often, and the cause is *so* frequently toxic-metabolic, that the **signal-to-noise ratio** for finding a genuine neurologic diagnosis is low — the constant worry is missing the rare one. Localization is easy here (AMS → brain/brainstem, not cord, root, nerve, junction, or muscle), so the work is **[[Illness Tempo|time course]]**, the other half of his fundamental equation. He and A walk the neurologic causes by tempo. *Sudden:* [[Seizure|seizure]] or post-ictal state (most seizures are unwitnessed — the patient is simply "found down"), and hemorrhagic more than ischemic stroke (hemorrhage causes immediate mass effect; ischemia usually brings a focal deficit). Berkowitz enumerates the **ischemic strokes that cause pure AMS** — bilateral thalami (artery of Percheron), a brainstem/basilar stroke striking the [[Reticular Activating System|reticular activating system]], bilateral ACA infarcts (personality change from inferior frontal involvement with motor sparing by collaterals), a PCA infarct presenting as amnesia, a **shower of emboli** after cardiac surgery, and a right inferior-division MCA infarct presenting as **acute agitated confusion**. *Acute-not-sudden:* meningitis/encephalitis over hours-to-days. *Subacute:* limbic/immune-mediated conditions and expanding or multifocal mass lesions. *Chronic:* neurodegenerative disease. He adds a crucial discipline — decide whether "AMS" is a **global encephalopathy** (attention primarily affected = delirium) or a **focal cognitive deficit** (aphasia, amnesia, apraxia) masquerading as confusion.

**HPI.** The case is a **30-year-old woman**, previously healthy, brought in by her partner for two days of acute confusion, agitation, and a **fear of dying**, with **echolalia** (repeating what others say and do) and episodes of **whispering and talking while alone**. A week earlier, on a surf trip to northern Peru, she had an **upper respiratory infection**. She denies seizures, headaches, fever, weakness, or abnormal movements. Berkowitz reframes the syndrome as it is: anxiety, delusion, agitation, and hallucination — a **psychiatric presentation** in a young person with a **viral prodrome** and no prior psychiatric history. He names the two syndromes that must not be missed here: **[[Anti-NMDA Receptor Encephalitis|anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis]]** (a post-viral-feeling prodrome → psychiatric phase, classically misdiagnosed as first-break schizophrenia) and, as A offers, **[[ADEM|acute disseminated encephalomyelitis]]** (a post-infectious CNS "cousin of Guillain-Barré," commoner in children). He recounts a prior NMDA case referred *by a psychiatrist* whose clinical reasoning — a first-break patient oscillating between withdrawn and psychotic, not responding to anti-dopaminergics as expected — earned the neurology consult that found an ovarian teratoma and a full recovery.

**The antipsychotic reaction.** Psychiatry, consulted first, started **haloperidol, lorazepam, and olanzapine**, and the agitation eased — but **twelve hours later** she developed whole-body **stiffness, tachycardia, and mild fever**, and neurology was called for non-response. Her history is otherwise thin (oral contraceptives for abnormal uterine bleeding; social alcohol; marijuana once or twice weekly). Berkowitz reads the reaction carefully: haloperidol and olanzapine are anti-dopaminergic, so an **acute dystonic reaction** or a neuroleptic hypersensitivity is plausible, and **[[Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome|neuroleptic malignant syndrome]]** (rigidity, high fever, dysautonomia) belongs on the list. But such a strong reaction in a 30-year-old suggests **something already wrong with the nervous system** — and NMDA encephalitis itself produces dysautonomia (labile heart rate and blood pressure, hypoventilation). He primes the exam: look for **meningismus**, but above all for a **movement disorder** that would betray neurology hiding under the psychiatry.

**Exam.** Temperature 38.5 °C, HR 105, RR 14, BP 110/75, SpO2 98% on room air. She is **stiff and catatonic** in an uncomfortable, forced posture — arms held up, mouth forced open (an orofacial/dystonic quality) — with impaired swallowing and food refusal, diffuse stiffness that eases (incompletely) with benzodiazepines, and normal reflexes. Critically, when the medication effect wanes she is **not spastic or rigid but catatonic**: the limbs can be passively repositioned and then hold their new position (**waxy flexibility**). She is awake but only groans, with no comprehensible speech — now the fourth day of illness. Berkowitz notes ideas flying by in the chat (**tetanus**, **rabies**) and honors them, but reasons them out: true muscle stiffness (tetanus) does not fit a passively-movable catatonic patient, and rabies would not produce this catatonia. He names his own **[[Anchoring Bias|anchor]]** aloud — a young, healthy woman with a viral prodrome, a psychiatric presentation, and now extrapyramidal features keeps pulling him to NMDA encephalitis — while acknowledging the antipsychotics confound the movement findings.

**Testing and empiric treatment.** Basic labs and a **toxicology screen** (negative on admission for barbiturates, TCAs, benzodiazepines, amphetamines, cocaine) begin the medical rule-out; CRP is elevated at 50 and CPK mildly up at 300. When she became febrile, the team started **empiric meningitis/encephalitis therapy** — what Berkowitz calls **"nighttime thinking"** ("a few doses of vancomycin, ceftriaxone, and acyclovir won't hurt unless the kidneys are marginal; sort it out in the morning") — balanced against the **[[Acyclovir Nephrotoxicity|acyclovir]] "daytime thinking"** caution of not persisting once the picture argues against HSV. **CT** is normal, **EEG** shows diffuse slow waves, and the **LP** (opening pressure 15, normal) shows **10 WBCs, lymphocyte-predominant, protein 105, normal glucose**, with **negative HSV/VZV/enterovirus PCR** — a bland, viral-*pattern* picture. Berkowitz uses the protein-out-of-proportion-to-cells finding to teach that **[[Albuminocytologic Dissociation|albuminocytologic dissociation]]** signals inflammation broadly (not only Guillain-Barré) and can be immune-mediated, not just infectious. The normal glucose argues against fungal/TB; the bland viral pattern with negative HSV PCR lets him **peel off antibiotics** and lean toward the immune-mediated side.

**The diagnostic tension.** The first **MRI is normal** and the **pelvic ultrasound negative** for teratoma. Valeria raises a sharp point born of a recently read case — **PCR-negative HSV encephalitis** — asking whether to repeat the LP. Berkowitz validates the instinct (HSV PCR can be negative early; the multiplex "biofire" M/E panel is *less* sensitive than a dedicated HSV PCR, and cryptococcal antigen beats PCR for Cryptococcus) while gently naming her **[[Availability Heuristic|recency bias]]**: a patient this sick from HSV would rarely have a normal MRI and a normal PCR. A **repeat LP** is again bland with negative PCR — but a **second MRI at two weeks** shows **FLAIR/T2 hyperintensity in the left hippocampus with ipsilateral temporal diffusion restriction** — a **[[Limbic Encephalitis|limbic encephalitis]]** that can be infectious *or* inflammatory. He teaches the antibody logic: send NMDA from **CSF, not serum** (serum false-positives abound), and while the assay takes weeks, a persistently deteriorating, undiagnosed patient with acyclovir on board can reasonably receive **empiric high-dose steroids** rather than escalating blindly to higher-risk immunotherapy.

**Resolution.** She deteriorates into frank **[[Dysautonomia|dysautonomia]]** — worsening mental status, bradycardia, respiratory distress — is intubated and transferred to the ICU. At **twenty days** the **CSF autoantibody returns positive**, confirming **anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis**; no teratoma is ever found (roughly half of cases have none). She receives **10 sessions of plasma exchange**, improves, comes off the ventilator, and — with residual left-sided weakness tracking the hippocampal/temporal signal — recovers much of her function. Berkowitz closes on the hardest branch (empiric bilateral oophorectomy for a sub-radiologic teratoma in a patient failing plasma exchange — a decision he has faced with families, ethics, and hospital counsel) and a broader teaching arc: **neurology and psychiatry, once one field, are converging again** as treatable autoimmune causes of "psychiatric" and "demented" presentations are discovered. He ends with the organizing distinction between **[[Cell-Surface vs Intracellular Antibodies|cell-surface antibodies]]** (NMDA, LGI1 — pathogenic, often non-paraneoplastic, treatable) and **intracellular/onconeural antibodies** (anti-Hu, anti-Yo — almost always paraneoplastic, poor recovery).

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Localization × Time Course|Localization × time course]] as the master script.** With localization fixed (brain), tempo (acute-subacute, days) plus the *type* of altered mental status (psychiatric-flavored, then extrapyramidal) collapses the differential toward immune-mediated limbic encephalitis — the same Berkowitz frame that anchors [[CPS 153 - Intermittent Altered Mental Status and Non-Lesional Autoimmune Epilepsy|CPS 153]] and [[CPS 402 - Altered Mental Status and Disseminated Varicella-Zoster|CPS 402]].
- **The not-to-miss illness script.** "Young woman + new psychiatric symptoms + no psychiatric history + viral prodrome" is a compiled script for [[Anti-NMDA Receptor Encephalitis|anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis]]; recognizing it early is what separates a treatable diagnosis from years of misdiagnosed "schizophrenia."
- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring]], named by the teacher.** Berkowitz explicitly announces his early anchor on NMDA and keeps testing it against tetanus, rabies, and NMS rather than clinging silently — modeling how to hold a strong first impression *and* audit it.
- **[[Availability Heuristic|Recency/availability bias]], named by the presenter.** Valeria's recently read PCR-negative HSV case pulls her toward repeating the LP; the reasoning models honoring the possibility (repeat testing is reasonable) while weighting it against base rates (a patient this sick from HSV rarely has a normal MRI and negative PCR).
- **[[Test Interpretation|Test interpretation]] under specificity constraints.** Serum NMDA antibody is non-specific (CSF is the specific test); HSV PCR can be falsely negative early and the multiplex panel is less sensitive than a dedicated assay; a bland viral-pattern CSF with normal glucose reshapes the differential — each result weighted by its true operating characteristics.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Diagnostic humility]] and treating without proof.** The team peels off antibiotics on the CSF pattern, considers empiric steroids while the antibody is pending, and reserves higher-risk immunotherapy (plasma exchange, rituximab, oophorectomy) for a firmer diagnosis — comfortable acting under uncertainty without over-committing.

## Teaching Discussion

- Approach altered mental status as localization × time course over a first gate of medical vs neurologic vs psychiatric; because most hospital AMS consults are toxic-metabolic, the discipline is to protect the low-frequency, treatable neurologic and psychiatric diagnoses from being missed, and to specify whether the deficit is a global encephalopathy or a focal cognitive syndrome (aphasia, amnesia, apraxia).
- Learn the short list of ischemic strokes that cause pure altered mental status — bilateral thalami (artery of Percheron), brainstem/reticular activating system, bilateral ACA, PCA amnesia, shower emboli, and right inferior-division MCA agitated confusion — since most strokes otherwise localize focally and depressed consciousness favors hemorrhage.
- Treat a young person with new psychiatric symptoms, no psychiatric history, and a viral prodrome as anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis until proven otherwise; expect a prodrome → psychiatric phase (often mistaken for first-break schizophrenia, sometimes with amnesia) → orofacial dyskinesia → seizures and dysautonomia, and look for an ovarian teratoma (~50%).
- Read an unexpected, severe reaction to antipsychotics as a possible clue that the nervous system is already abnormal — weigh acute dystonic reaction, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, and the dysautonomia of the underlying encephalitis rather than assuming coincidence.
- Interpret CSF carefully: a lymphocytic pleocytosis with protein out of proportion to cells and normal glucose is a viral or immune-mediated pattern (albuminocytologic dissociation is not only Guillain-Barré); send the NMDA antibody from CSF (serum is non-specific and drives inappropriate treatment); and remember HSV PCR can be negative early, so a repeat LP is reasonable when suspicion is high.
- Practice "nighttime vs daytime thinking": start empiric vancomycin, ceftriaxone, and acyclovir when a sick febrile patient could have bacterial meningitis or HSV encephalitis, then de-escalate in daylight — peel off antibiotics once the CSF pattern is viral, and stop acyclovir once HSV is unlikely (a normal MRI in a very sick patient argues against HSV) to spare the kidneys.
- Act under uncertainty proportionately: while the CSF antibody is pending for weeks, empiric high-dose steroids are reasonable in a deteriorating undiagnosed patient, but reserve plasma exchange, rituximab, and empiric oophorectomy for a firmer diagnosis; hunt a sub-radiologic teratoma with transvaginal ultrasound, pelvic MRI, or PET, and escalate the teratoma search only as the stakes demand.
- Organize the autoantibody syndromes by cell-surface vs intracellular: cell-surface antibodies (NMDA, LGI1) are directly pathogenic, frequently non-paraneoplastic, and very treatable, while intracellular/onconeural antibodies (anti-Hu, anti-Yo) are almost always paraneoplastic with poor recovery because neuronal death has occurred — and appreciate that these discoveries are re-merging neurology and psychiatry.
