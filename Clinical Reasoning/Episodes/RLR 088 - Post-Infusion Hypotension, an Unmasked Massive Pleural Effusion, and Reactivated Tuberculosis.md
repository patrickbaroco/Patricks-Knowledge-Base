## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 28-year-old man with Crohn's disease on infliximab and 6-mercaptopurine becomes acutely hypotensive and tachycardic minutes into an infliximab infusion, initially raising concern for anaphylaxis. But a week of preceding exertional dyspnea and diaphoresis, decreased breath sounds, and a chest X-ray showing complete whiteout of the left hemithorax with contralateral tracheal deviation instead reveal a massive left pleural effusion — the true cause of his hypotension, "unmasked" rather than caused by the infusion. A neutrophilic, low-glucose exudate with negative initial cultures, pulmonary micronodules, and ultimately a falsely reassuring latent-TB screen obtained while the patient was on high-dose steroids converge on reactivated pleuropulmonary tuberculosis precipitated by starting a TNF-alpha inhibitor.

## Pearls

- Hypotension developing during or shortly after a medication infusion should not be reflexively attributed to the medication itself; the timing may instead "unmask" a pre-existing, compensated physiologic problem (here, obstructive physiology from a massive pleural effusion) in a patient with no cardiovascular reserve to buffer even a modest additional hemodynamic stress.
- Hypotension plus a plausible trigger is not sufficient to diagnose anaphylaxis — additional supporting features (rash, respiratory symptoms, GI symptoms, or a clear distributive-shock physiology on exam) are needed before committing to that diagnosis and its specific treatment.
- The four classic shock categories collapse into two physiologic groups: cardiogenic and obstructive shock reflect impaired blood flow through the thoracic cavity and are typically accompanied by thoracic symptoms (chest pain, dyspnea, hypoxia, tachypnea), while distributive and hypovolemic shock are vascular problems (a dilated or an empty vessel) that produce diffuse, whole-body symptoms without a thoracic localization — using the presence or absence of thoracic symptoms helps triage rapidly between the two groups.
- On chest X-ray, a "whiteout" hemithorax can result from a lung being pulled in (collapse from an endobronchial obstruction, with the trachea deviating toward the whiteout) or pushed down (a large effusion or mass, with the trachea deviating away from the whiteout); tracheal position is the key discriminator, and point-of-care ultrasound can confirm the diagnosis quickly.
- A very low pleural fluid glucose narrows the differential to a short list — empyema, autoimmune pleuritis, and malignancy — but the accompanying cell differential helps distinguish them further: a neutrophil-predominant exudate favors pyogenic infection, while autoimmune and malignant effusions are typically lymphocyte-predominant.
- An indeterminate or negative interferon-gamma release assay (e.g., QuantiFERON) drawn while a patient is on high-dose corticosteroids can be falsely negative, since steroids suppress the T-cell/macrophage response the test depends on; a negative result obtained under immunosuppression should not be trusted to exclude latent tuberculosis before starting a TNF-alpha inhibitor.

## Case Narrative

A 28-year-old man with Crohn's disease, diagnosed several years earlier and maintained on infliximab infusions and 6-mercaptopurine, presents to a San Francisco VA infusion center feeling well and begins a routine infliximab infusion. Shortly after it starts, he becomes lightheaded; his systolic blood pressure is found to be 70 with a heart rate of 140, and a rapid response is called.

Approaching this as the responding ED physician, the discussant frames hypotension with tachycardia and symptoms as "real" rather than a number to dismiss, and prioritizes supporting the patient: a quick check of oxygenation and airway, Trendelenburg positioning if it doesn't compromise the airway, and leveraging the existing IV access for fluids and/or pressors. Practical parallel steps include confirming code status, placing a backboard and monitor/pads in case of arrest, and drawing a full panel of labs before administering additional empiric treatment. He organizes the four classic shock categories into two physiologic groups: cardiogenic and obstructive shock reflect impaired blood flow through the thoracic cavity (a problem in the heart itself, or an obstructing lesion like a pulmonary embolism or pneumothorax) and are typically accompanied by thoracic symptoms, while distributive and hypovolemic shock are vascular problems (a dilated vessel or an empty one) that produce diffuse, whole-body symptoms without thoracic localization. He plans to use the presence or absence of thoracic symptoms to triage between these two buckets, while supporting the patient with fluids and, if needed, short-term peripheral pressors regardless of the ultimate cause. Given the timing relative to the infusion, his early leading hypotheses are an anaphylactoid reaction to the medication and, separately, a complication of the IV access itself, such as an air embolism.

Asked whether he would give intramuscular epinephrine, the discussant is explicit that IM epinephrine is life-saving for anaphylaxis but carries real risk — for instance, if the patient's hypotension instead reflected an acute coronary event from underlying cardiovascular disease, giving epinephrine would be harmful. He notes that hypotension plus a plausible trigger is not sufficient to diagnose anaphylaxis; additional features (a clear distributive physiology on exam, or supporting findings such as rash, respiratory symptoms, or GI symptoms) are needed first. He keeps IM epinephrine ready but would not yet give it.

On exam there is no wheezing, rash, or GI symptoms, but given ongoing concern for anaphylaxis, IM epinephrine is given along with wide-open IV fluids, and the patient is transferred to the ED. By the time he is evaluated there, his systolic blood pressure has improved to the 90s–low 100s and he is no longer symptomatic. History reveals about a week of progressively worsening exertional fatigue with diaphoresis but no fevers or chills, and progressive dyspnea on exertion to the point of avoiding his usual activities; his Crohn's disease remains well controlled with normal bowel movements and no abdominal pain. Vital signs now show a heart rate of 116, blood pressure 90/74, oxygen saturation 96% on room air, and a temperature of 37.0°C. His hospital gown is completely drenched with sweat, and he has decreased breath sounds on the left with no crackles.

The discussant reasons that this is a subacute (roughly one-week) story of exertional dyspnea and diaphoresis, and works through whether the diaphoresis reflects cytokine-mediated (inflammatory/infectious) sweating or a sympathetically mediated stress response — in effect, asking whether this points toward an underlying inflammatory process. He recognizes the story may not have begun at the infusion center at all, and would review the patient's vital signs from before the infusion began to see whether subtle abnormalities were already present. The decreased left-sided breath sounds are the most important new finding: this could reflect air in the pleural space (pneumothorax, possibly with tension physiology) or fluid in the pleural space (a large effusion), both of which can produce obstructive physiology and explain the hypotension; point-of-care ultrasound is proposed to distinguish the two quickly. He notes that the acute, infusion-associated timing favors anaphylaxis, while the preceding subacute symptoms weaken that hypothesis without being specific enough on their own to point clearly elsewhere.

A chest X-ray is obtained and shows complete whiteout of the left hemithorax. The discussant explains that a whiteout can reflect a lung being "sucked in" on itself (collapse from an endobronchial lesion, with the trachea pulled toward the affected side) or "pushed down" (from a large effusion or a mass, with the trachea pushed away from the affected side); here, the trachea is markedly deviated to the right, favoring a "push" phenomenon — most consistent with a large left pleural effusion, though a mediastinal mass causing tracheal deviation and ipsilateral lung collapse is also considered. Point-of-care ultrasound confirms a massive left pleural effusion.

Basic labs return: white blood cell count 3.4 (leukopenic), hemoglobin 9.3, platelets 364,000, with an absolute eosinophil count of zero; renal function, bicarbonate (26), and sodium (130, mildly low) are otherwise unremarkable, coagulation studies are normal, and AST/ALT are mildly elevated (34/54) with an otherwise normal liver panel. Thoracentesis removes 3.3 liters of serous fluid.

The discussant explains the core pleural fluid question: did fluid leak from an intact vessel under pressure (transudate), or from an inflamed, "leaky" vessel (exudate)? He would send LDH and total protein to apply this framework, but predicts an exudate even before results return, given the absence of any evidence of volume overload and the presence of systemic inflammatory features. Because infection and malignancy are the leading causes of an exudate, with autoimmune disease also warranted given the patient's Crohn's history, he prioritizes pleural fluid glucose and pH (to assess for empyema), Gram stain and culture, and cytology/flow cytometry alongside a cell count with differential.

Results: pleural fluid white count 249 with a neutrophil predominance; LDH markedly elevated at 2,155 (with a normal serum LDH); elevated protein; glucose less than 10 (though the fluid was not grossly purulent); no pH obtained. Serologies are also sent: ANA less than 1:40, dsDNA less than 200, rheumatoid factor less than 10 — all negative.

The discussant interprets this as meeting criteria for a complicated parapneumonic effusion (just short of frank empyema). He notes that a very low pleural glucose has a short differential — empyema, autoimmune disease, and malignancy — but that autoimmune and malignant effusions are typically lymphocyte-predominant, whereas this fluid's neutrophil predominance and markedly elevated LDH strongly favor a pyogenic bacterial process. He raises two further questions: first, does this explain the hypotension? Yes — obstructive physiology from a large pleural effusion, not just from air, can impair venous return and cardiac filling. Second, if this is an empyema, where did the infection originate, since empyema does not typically arise spontaneously — dental, esophageal, pulmonary, or, most concerning given the patient's known intra-abdominal disease, an intra-abdominal source.

The presenting discussant then reveals the connecting insight: infliximab infusions can cause some degree of histamine release and mild cardiovascular effect, but this patient had essentially no cardiovascular reserve because of his pre-existing, compensated obstructive physiology from the large pleural effusion — so the infusion did not directly cause anaphylactic hypotension, but rather "unmasked" the underlying obstructive process that had been silently building.

Initial Gram stain and cultures of the pleural fluid are negative. A CT chest is obtained after fluid removal to assess for a pleuroparenchymal process, showing a large residual left intrathoracic fluid collection favored to be pleural, significant compression of the left lung with rightward mediastinal shift, and scattered micronodularity throughout the right lung.

The discussant notes that the lack of culture growth is notable, since most empyemas do grow an organism (though a minority remain culture-negative), and pivots to weighing malignancy and autoimmune disease more heavily. The extensive pulmonary micronodularity raises real concern for malignancy, which he had been reluctant to entertain without this finding. On the autoimmune side, he revisits Crohn's-associated pleuropulmonary disease and considers TNF-inhibitor-induced secondary autoimmune disease (including drug-induced lupus), though the negative ANA argues against this. He notes that Crohn's disease and granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) are essentially the only diseases he associates with necrobiotic pulmonary nodules, and that the pulmonary and pleural findings here remain compatible with Crohn's-associated disease. Given the patient's immunosuppression, he also raises infliximab-associated reactivation of latent tuberculosis or histoplasmosis as causes capable of producing severe, sometimes neutrophilic, exudative pleural disease with pulmonary nodules. With infection, malignancy, and autoimmune disease all still viable, he proposes bronchoscopy to obtain culture data from the nodules and continued attention to a possible intra-abdominal source of infection.

At this point, additional history is revealed: the patient immigrated from China, and before starting the TNF-alpha inhibitor he had an equivocal QuantiFERON-TB Gold test; a repeat test was negative, and infectious disease cleared him to start infliximab. The discussant explains that an indeterminate QuantiFERON does not rule out latent tuberculosis, and describes the additional pleural-specific tests available: pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA), and AFB smear, PCR, and culture — noting that none of these are as sensitive as pleural biopsy, since tuberculous pleuritis is predominantly a disease of the pleura itself, with relatively few organisms shed into the fluid.

The final reveal: the patient's repeat QuantiFERON had been drawn while he was already on high-dose steroids, which suppressed the T-cell/macrophage response the assay depends on, producing a false negative. His pleural fluid ADA is markedly elevated, and the AFB smear is strongly positive. The diagnosis is pleural and parenchymal tuberculosis, reactivated as a complication of starting a TNF-alpha inhibitor in a patient whose latent infection had not been adequately excluded. The patient does well with treatment.

In discussion, the presenters highlight two "sixth sense"-style twists: the hypotension during the infliximab infusion was not true anaphylaxis but rather the infusion unmasking pre-existing obstructive physiology from a massive, TB-driven pleural effusion in a patient with no cardiovascular reserve; and the falsely reassuring repeat QuantiFERON was an artifact of testing while the patient was immunosuppressed on steroids, not a true absence of latent infection. They close by cautioning against "autopilot" trust in a subspecialist clearance ("the ID doctor said it was okay") without confirming the specific circumstances under which that clearance was obtained — quoting a program director's observation that many morbidity and mortality conferences begin with exactly that assumption going unexamined.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The case is explicitly framed around an "unmasking phenomenon": a medication (infliximab) is initially blamed for causing hypotension via anaphylaxis, when in fact it merely provided a modest hemodynamic stress that exposed a pre-existing, previously compensated obstructive process (the pleural effusion) in a patient with no physiologic reserve left to buffer it — a distinction the discussants flag as recurring and important across cases.

The discussants model deliberate diagnostic restraint around anaphylaxis: despite a temporally compelling trigger, IM epinephrine is withheld until either clear distributive physiology or additional supporting features emerge, illustrating that a plausible trigger plus hypotension is not sufficient for a specific, consequential diagnosis with its own treatment risks.

A second false-reassurance twist — the falsely negative QuantiFERON drawn on steroids — is used to teach against "autopilot" trust in subspecialist clearance; the discussants explicitly credit a program director's teaching point that morbidity and mortality reviews frequently trace back to an unexamined assumption that a specialist's prior clearance remains valid without checking the specific conditions (here, concurrent immunosuppression) under which that clearance was given.

## Teaching Discussion

Hypotension occurring during or shortly after a medication infusion should prompt consideration not only of a direct drug reaction but also of the possibility that the infusion has "unmasked" a pre-existing, compensated physiologic problem in a patient with minimal reserve — a distinction with direct implications for both diagnosis and treatment.

The four classic shock categories can be simplified into two physiologic groups for rapid bedside triage: cardiogenic and obstructive shock reflect impaired blood flow through the thoracic cavity and typically present with thoracic symptoms, while distributive and hypovolemic shock are vascular in origin and typically present with diffuse or absent localizing symptoms.

Hypotension plus a plausible trigger (such as a medication infusion) is not sufficient to diagnose anaphylaxis; additional supporting features — rash, respiratory symptoms, GI symptoms, or clear distributive physiology on exam — should be sought before committing to epinephrine and an anaphylaxis-specific treatment pathway, given that epinephrine carries its own risks.

A "whiteout" hemithorax on chest X-ray should prompt assessment of tracheal position: deviation toward the whiteout suggests volume loss from collapse (e.g., endobronchial obstruction), while deviation away from the whiteout suggests a space-occupying process (large effusion or mass) pushing the mediastinum; point-of-care ultrasound can rapidly confirm the diagnosis.

Pleural fluid analysis should proceed systematically: LDH and total protein establish transudate versus exudate; among exudates, a very low glucose narrows the differential to empyema, autoimmune disease, and malignancy, and the cell differential (neutrophilic versus lymphocytic) helps further discriminate a pyogenic process from autoimmune or malignant causes.

A negative or indeterminate interferon-gamma release assay (QuantiFERON) for latent tuberculosis should always be interpreted in the context of the patient's immunosuppression status at the time of testing; results obtained while on high-dose steroids or other significant immunosuppression may be falsely negative and should not be relied upon to clear a patient for TNF-alpha inhibitor therapy.

Reflexive trust in a prior subspecialist clearance ("ID already cleared this") should be tempered by actively confirming the specific circumstances of that clearance — including what testing was done, when, and under what conditions — since assumptions of this kind are a recurring root cause identified in morbidity and mortality review.
