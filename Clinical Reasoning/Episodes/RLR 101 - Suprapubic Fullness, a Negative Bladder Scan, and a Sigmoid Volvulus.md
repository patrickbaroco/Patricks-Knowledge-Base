## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> An 82-year-old, functionally excellent man presents with three days of suprapubic fullness and discomfort with urination, initially and reasonably framed as a possible bladder/urinary process. Two separately performed bladder scans are normal, ruling out urinary retention, leaving Reza intentionally "open-minded" and without a leading diagnosis. A repeated abdominal exam — prompted by nothing more than thoroughness — reveals a massively distended abdomen the patient himself never mentioned, confirmed on a portable KUB showing dilated large bowel. With normal inflammatory and iron labs and a clean colonoscopy four years prior, colon cancer (the most common cause of large bowel obstruction) becomes less likely, and CT ultimately reveals a sigmoid volvulus twisting directly over the bladder — explaining both the suprapubic fullness and the urinary symptoms as a mechanical mimicker rather than a true urologic process. The volvulus is successfully detorsed endoscopically by GI.

## Pearls

- Suprapubic fullness plus an unusual urinary sensation reasonably prioritizes the bladder anatomically, but a normal, twice-confirmed bladder scan should be allowed to meaningfully lower the probability of a urinary source rather than being dismissed as operator error.
- When the working framework doesn't fit the data, the right move is to explicitly acknowledge low diagnostic confidence and stay open rather than force the patient onto a default pathway (e.g., "older man, urinary symptom, therefore UTI or retention until proven otherwise").
- A sudden, precisely time-stamped onset of symptoms (patients or family recalling the exact moment something started) is a pattern classically associated with trauma, fracture, dissection, or other acute mechanical/vascular events rather than a gradually evolving inflammatory or infectious process.
- The physical exam can reveal findings a patient does not spontaneously report or even perceive — a massively distended abdomen was missed by the patient's own history and only found on direct examination, underscoring that a thorough exam is not a redundant formality even when the history seems to already point somewhere else.
- Colon cancer is the single most common cause of large bowel obstruction (roughly 70% in the discussion), but a recent, normal colonoscopy, normal iron studies, and excellent functional status meaningfully lower that pretest probability and should prompt consideration of alternative mechanical causes such as volvulus.
- Sigmoid volvulus classically occurs in older patients with long-standing constipation: chronic bowel dilation loosens the mesenteric ligaments over time, creating a redundant, mobile segment of sigmoid colon that becomes vulnerable to twisting on its own mesentery.
- Small bowel obstruction (more proximal) tends to produce more nausea and vomiting and benefits more from NG tube decompression; large bowel obstruction (more distal) often produces less nausea/vomiting and correspondingly "sneaky," relatively normal-looking labs, and NG tube decompression offers little benefit unless there is significant proximal extension of the obstruction.
- Endoscopic detorsion by GI is often successful as first-line management for sigmoid volvulus, but definitive surgery (e.g., sigmoidectomy) is typically recommended afterward given the high recurrence rate once the anatomic predisposition (redundant, lax sigmoid mesentery) remains in place.

## Problem Representation

An 82-year-old man with excellent baseline function presenting with sudden suprapubic fullness and urinary discomfort, a normal bladder scan, and a subsequently discovered massively distended abdomen, ultimately diagnosed with sigmoid volvulus twisting directly over the bladder.

## Case Narrative

The patient was an 82-year-old man who presented to the ER reporting that, three days earlier, he had abruptly noticed a sense of fullness in his suprapubic area, along with discomfort — not quite pain, but an unpleasant sensation — associated with urinating. Given the anatomic location and the urinary association, the initial reasoning prioritized the bladder: an organ capable of becoming distended in that specific location, raising the possibility of urinary retention or another process along the urinary tract.

A bladder scan was obtained after having the patient attempt to void, specifically to capture a true post-void residual rather than a pre-void volume that would be uninterpretable. The scan returned a low, unremarkable residual (approximately 92cc); when the finding seemed surprising given the patient's symptoms, it was repeated by the same operator and returned essentially the same normal result. This normal, twice-confirmed bladder scan was treated as genuinely informative — evidence against urinary retention — rather than as a technical artifact to be second-guessed.

Notably, the patient's wife reported that the symptom had started suddenly, at a specific time, right before dinner. This kind of precisely recalled onset was flagged as a pattern more typical of an acute mechanical or vascular event (trauma, fracture, dissection) than of a gradually developing inflammatory or infectious process, adding a note of concern even though the exam and initial data were otherwise unremarkable.

With a normal bladder scan and no other red flags yet identified, the case was deliberately framed as diagnostically uncertain: rather than defaulting to a reflexive "older man with a bladder complaint equals UTI or retention" pathway, the discussion modeled an explicitly open-minded stance, acknowledging that the fullness, negative bladder scan, and sudden onset did not yet connect to any specific leading diagnosis.

Direct conversation with the patient added detail: the fullness had come on abruptly over a minute or two, felt like an urge to urinate, and had persisted for three days with an ongoing sense that urination "just felt weird" each time; he also reported low-grade but persistently bothersome nausea, without vomiting, and no fevers, chills, or pain elsewhere in the abdomen. He was otherwise remarkably healthy for his age — his only medications were a statin and a laxative (Miralax) for constipation — and his vital signs were entirely normal.

At this point, before any exam findings were revealed, the discussion paused to consider what pretest probability could reasonably be assigned. The sudden onset and now the nausea (raising concern for an obstructive process, whether urinary or gastrointestinal) were flagged as more worrying than the case had initially seemed, even though the story did not clearly point to any single diagnosis, and the exam had not yet been performed.

The abdominal exam then provided the pivotal finding: on lifting the patient's shirt, his abdomen was massively distended — a finding the patient himself had never mentioned or, apparently, fully noticed. Because it was after hours, rather than waiting for a formal CT scan, a portable KUB was obtained at the bedside and showed dramatically dilated loops of bowel, with an appearance suggestive of a large bowel obstruction and a possible transition point, though a definitive localization was not possible from the plain film alone; the formal radiology read likewise flagged concern for large bowel obstruction without being able to specify the exact location of the blockage, and recommended CT follow-up. Basic labs returned entirely normal — a pattern explicitly noted as characteristically deceptive in bowel obstruction, especially when the obstruction is distal.

Before revealing the CT, the discussion worked through what would and would not be expected given a large bowel obstruction in this patient. Colon cancer, cited as the cause of roughly 70% of large bowel obstructions, was the leading statistical consideration, and management planning around it (involving surgery) was discussed. However, further chart review complicated this leading hypothesis: iron studies from two weeks prior were entirely normal, and the patient had undergone a colonoscopy at age 78 (four years prior) that was completely normal, alongside a functional status remarkable for his age — the patient routinely walked two to four miles a day with his wife. These findings meaningfully lowered the probability of colon cancer as the cause, prompting consideration of alternative mechanical causes such as volvulus, while the three-day symptom duration argued against something as acutely dramatic and short-fused as an acute volvulus with ischemia, since true bowel ischemia or perforation would typically declare itself faster.

The CT scan then revealed the answer directly and dramatically: a twisting, torsion point located immediately over the bladder — a sigmoid volvulus. This finding explained the entire clinical picture retrospectively: the twisted, distended sigmoid loop sitting directly atop the bladder produced the suprapubic fullness and urinary discomfort that had originally been mistaken for a urinary tract problem, while the bladder itself, correctly, showed no evidence of true retention on either bladder scan.

The mechanism of sigmoid volvulus was reviewed: it occurs disproportionately in older patients with long-standing constipation, in whom chronic bowel dilation over years progressively loosens the mesenteric ligaments supporting the sigmoid colon, creating a redundant, mobile loop of bowel that becomes prone to twisting upon itself. Management was organized around who to call and what to expect: general surgery is typically consulted first, but GI is often able to perform endoscopic detorsion via colonoscopy — as occurred in this case, successfully, confirmed by a follow-up KUB — with definitive surgery (commonly a sigmoidectomy) recommended in follow-up given the high rate of recurrence once the anatomic predisposition (a redundant, lax mesentery) remains unaddressed.

The discussion closed by returning to the paradox at the heart of the case: despite the presentation ultimately fitting a textbook description of sigmoid volvulus — abrupt onset, external compression on the bladder, a history of chronic constipation — it had been essentially impossible to predict from the initial history alone, and only became clear once the exam and imaging data accumulated. This was framed as an illustration of how a classic disease presentation can still be genuinely undiagnosable at the outset, and of the discipline required to keep gathering data rather than prematurely closing on the most available, symptom-matched diagnosis (urinary retention).

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| Suprapubic fullness + urinary discomfort | Initially raises probability of a bladder/urinary process |
| Bladder scan normal, repeated and confirmed | Substantially lowers probability of urinary retention |
| Precisely time-stamped, sudden onset | Raises concern for an acute mechanical/vascular event over an infectious/inflammatory one |
| Low-grade nausea without vomiting, no fevers | Nonspecific but keeps an obstructive process on the table |
| Massively distended abdomen found on exam (unreported by patient) | Strongly raises probability of an intra-abdominal process, specifically bowel-related |
| KUB showing dilated bowel loops | Confirms large bowel obstruction; localization still unclear |
| Normal recent iron studies, normal colonoscopy 4 years prior, excellent function | Lowers probability of colon cancer as the cause of the obstruction |
| CT showing torsion directly over the bladder | Confirms sigmoid volvulus and retrospectively explains the urinary symptoms as a mechanical mimicker |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Sigmoid volvulus illness script: an older patient with long-standing constipation and a redundant, mobile sigmoid colon, presenting with sudden-onset abdominal distension/fullness (sometimes with a mimicking symptom depending on the anatomic location of the torsion), a KUB showing massively dilated large bowel, and a CT confirming a twisting "whirl" pattern; managed first with endoscopic detorsion, followed by elective surgery given high recurrence.

The case is a caution against diagnostic momentum built purely from anatomic proximity of a symptom to an organ (suprapubic fullness → bladder) without confirmatory data — the negative bladder scan should have been (and ultimately was) allowed to meaningfully redirect the workup rather than being explained away. It also models "staying open" as an explicit diagnostic stance: rather than forcing the case into the most available label, acknowledging genuine uncertainty kept the door open for the exam and imaging to do the diagnostic work.

## Teaching Discussion

The central teaching point is the value of a genuinely thorough physical exam even when the history seems to already point toward a specific system — the massively distended abdomen here was a finding the patient did not report and did not himself perceive as remarkable, and it was only found because the exam was performed with full attention rather than as a formality after a seemingly localizing history.

A second teaching point is the discipline of updating on negative data: a normal, twice-confirmed bladder scan is real evidence against urinary retention and should be allowed to shift the differential, rather than being dismissed as a technical error simply because it conflicts with the initial leading hypothesis.

A third teaching point concerns using ancillary data (recent iron studies, a recent clean colonoscopy, excellent baseline function) to actively adjust the pretest probability of the single most common cause of a finding (colon cancer causing large bowel obstruction) rather than assuming the most common cause by default — this is what opened the door to considering and ultimately confirming volvulus.
