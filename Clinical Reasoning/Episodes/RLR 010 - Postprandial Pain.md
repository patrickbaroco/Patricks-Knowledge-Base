## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 29-year-old man presents with two months of ~15-lb weight loss, waxing-and-waning epigastric pain (partly postprandial), emesis, loose stools, and a measured fever to 103°F, on a background of twice-recurrent bacterial prostatitis. Rabih organizes the case into three deliberately separated columns — the fever, the abdominal pain, and the recurrent prostatitis — resisting the urge to force them into a single unifying thread before the data warrant it. Imaging reveals [[Hepatosplenomegaly|hepatosplenomegaly]] as the true center of gravity, and the abdominal pain is reabsorbed into it (postprandial pain loses localizing value once you remember the stomach can be *compressed* by an enlarged spleen or liver). Over days the patient defervesces without treatment while evolving [[Cytopenias|cytopenias]] and a marrow PET signal drive an escalating hematologic workup (bone marrow biopsy) against a lead differential of [[Lymphoma|lymphoma]], with Reza privately betting on [[Gaucher Disease|Gaucher disease type 1]] after normal liver chemistries and an Ashkenazi ancestry with a family history of splenomegaly. Everything returns negative — no malignancy, no Gaucher cells, negative viral serologies — and the patient recovers fully. The episode's payload is [[Diagnostic Uncertainty|the case with no answer]]: a self-limited viral syndrome NOS, and a meditation on how often such cases go untold.

## Pearls

- **On day one of a fever, prioritize infection but simultaneously exclude the [[Three H's of Fever|three H's]] — hyperthyroidism, hematologic causes ([[Thrombotic Microangiopathy|TTP/HUS]]), and hyperthermia ([[Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome|NMS]], heat stroke, exertional).** These belong on the day-one list not because they are common but because each is a diagnosis you must not spend extra days making. A [[TSH]] costs nothing and clears the first H.
- **Postprandial pain seems to localize to the digestive organs — stomach, duodenum, pancreas, biliary tree, mesenteric vasculature — but that localizing value is quietly destroyed by *compressive disease of the stomach*.** So many organs abut the stomach (liver, spleen, retroperitoneal colon, even ascites) that splenomegaly-driven early satiety is a classic mimic of a "gastric" complaint. The pain told the truth about *which organs*, but not *which disease*.
- **Bacterial prostatitis in a 29-year-old man, absent an STI or an anatomic lesion, should make you ask whether it arrived hematogenously rather than by ascension.** [[Hematogenous Prostatic Seeding|Hematogenous seeding]] of the prostate is a signature of indolent infections — [[Tuberculosis|TB]], brucellosis, endemic mycoses, even Cryptococcus — which flips the question toward the host: is recurrent prostatitis a clue to an underlying immunocompromise?
- **Lymphopenia forces a fork: is it the expected shadow of an inflammatory/viral response, or a window into an underlying immunodeficiency?** The same low lymphocyte count that fits a garden-variety viral illness also fits [[Acute HIV|acute HIV]] (which the antibody can miss for days) and prompts consideration of [[Common Variable Immunodeficiency|CVID]] when it travels with recurrent infection.
- **Fever plus months of weight loss plus [[Hepatosplenomegaly|hepatosplenomegaly]] plus evolving cytopenias and a marrow signal is [[Lymphoma|lymphoma]] until proven otherwise — and the absence of palpable nodes does not exempt you.** Lymphoma can be extranodal or arise in the spleen, "the biggest lymph node known to us," so the next step is a bone marrow biopsy, not reassurance.
- **Not every case resolves, and the ones that don't are the ones that never get told.** A young, otherwise healthy patient with a self-limited illness that defies serologic capture (HHV-6, an arboviral syndrome, an unnamed virus) is a real and undertaught endpoint; teaching only the solved cases distorts the base rate of certainty.

## Problem Representation

A 29-year-old man with recurrent bacterial prostatitis (currently on Bactrim) presents with two months of ~15-lb weight loss, intermittent night sweats, waxing-and-waning epigastric pain that is partly postprandial and partly food-independent, associated emesis, and loose stools, brought to the ED now by two days of intractable vomiting; he is febrile to 103°F with imaging showing isolated hepatosplenomegaly, a normal CBC that evolves into progressive [[Cytopenias|bicytopenia]] with lymphopenia, normal liver chemistries and ESR, mildly elevated CRP/LDH/lactate, hypophosphatemia, and a PET marrow signal — a multi-threaded febrile hepatosplenomegaly that, despite an exhaustive infectious, hematologic, and metabolic workup, defervesces and resolves without treatment and remains undiagnosed (viral syndrome NOS).

## Case Narrative

Reza presents a patient from his own service: a 29-year-old man with a temperature of 103°F, decreased oral intake, and roughly 15 pounds of weight loss over two months. He reports postprandial epigastric pain but also epigastric pain that waxes and wanes independent of eating, with episodes of emesis and some loose stools. Asked why he came *now* rather than earlier, he answers that the vomiting had made it impossible to keep food and fluids down. His only medical history is bacterial prostatitis diagnosed on two occasions — he is currently one week into Bactrim for the second episode, which falls in the middle of the two-month illness. He owns no thermometer but describes drenching night sweats a few times over the past month.

Rabih's opening move is organizational, not diagnostic. He instructs the audience to build **three separate columns** — the fever, the abdominal pain with its descriptors, and the working diagnosis of bacterial prostatitis — and to resist merging them prematurely, since the case has "so many parallel threads." On the fever, he offers a framework: day one of a fever demands prioritizing infection while simultaneously excluding the **[[Three H's of Fever|three H's]]** — hyperthyroidism, hematologic causes ([[Thrombotic Microangiopathy|thrombotic microangiopathy]] such as TTP/HUS), and hyperthermia ([[Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome|NMS]], heat stroke, exertional hyperthermia) — because each must be diagnosed on day one. He sends a [[TSH]] and otherwise trains the spotlight on infection, holding open whether it is related to the weight loss or independent of it.

On the abdominal pain, he makes the episode's signature localization argument. Postprandial pain implicates the organs of digestion — stomach, duodenum, pancreas, biliary tree, mesenteric vasculature — because the organs that digest food hurt when food arrives. But this localization has a "big, big problem": **compressive disease of the stomach**. So many structures abut the stomach — liver, spleen, retroperitoneal colon, ascites — that postprandial pain can arise from something pressing on the stomach rather than the stomach itself. His paradigm example is splenomegaly producing early satiety and postprandial discomfort. The pain reliably tells you the digestive organs are *involved*, but its localizing power is diminished by this fact.

On the prostatitis, Rabih flags that bacterial prostatitis in a 29-year-old man, absent a sexually transmitted infection or a local anatomic lesion, is unusual. He wonders whether the label is even correct, or whether the prostate was seeded **[[Hematogenous Prostatic Seeding|hematogenously]]** in the context of systemic disease — a pattern seen with indolent infections like [[Tuberculosis|tuberculosis]], brucellosis, and the endemic mycoses (he recalls a recent Cryptococcus case doing exactly this). That possibility reframes the recurrent prostatitis as a potential clue to an immunocompromised host.

Reza then supplies the pre-examination data. HIV is negative and the TSH normal. A CT abdomen/pelvis (non-contrast, owing to a contrast shortage) shows an enlarged liver at 19 cm and a spleen at 14.7 cm — isolated **[[Hepatosplenomegaly|hepatosplenomegaly]]** as the sole abnormality. The CBC is normal except for a lymphopenia around 400. The ESR is normal, CRP moderately elevated at 4.5, liver chemistries entirely normal, ferritin 243, LDH and lactate mildly elevated, with a moderate hypophosphatemia that the team dutifully sends (invoking "Uncle Bob" and the phosphate-with-thiamine teaching).

Rabih reasons that the crux is the hepatosplenomegaly, and that the abdominal pain has been "satisfactorily translated" into it. Staying true to the fever-means-infection principle, he asks which infections cause hepatosplenomegaly in a young man without exotic exposures and lands on [[Infectious Mononucleosis|infectious mononucleosis]] ([[Epstein-Barr Virus|EBV]], [[Cytomegalovirus|CMV]]) — tempered by the lymphopenia, which is less characteristic of mono — and [[Acute HIV|acute HIV]], which the antibody may miss early. He notes lymphopenia is characteristic of many viral infections but also raises the immunodeficiency question. The two-month time course and hepatosplenomegaly then pull him toward malignancy, especially [[Lymphoma|liquid malignancies]], and — given the normal CBC and normal liver enzymes at this stage — even toward hereditary [[Storage Diseases|storage diseases]]. His next steps: blood cultures to exclude endocarditis, a careful epidemiologic exposure history, viral serologies, and a vitamin D level for the hypophosphatemia.

Reza narrates the hospital course. Without antibiotics, antipyretics, or any therapy, the fever subsides within hours and never returns; the Bactrim is stopped. Viral studies stay negative throughout. The CBC evolves: platelets drift from a low-normal 150 to 112, and the white count falls to 2.1, while hemoglobin holds. The exam shows no JVD, no edema, no palpable lymph nodes — only the enlarged liver and spleen. A **PET-CT reveals a marrow signal** concerning for an infiltrative process. The patient, now afebrile, tolerating a diet, and feeling well, asks to go home. EBV and CMV serologies and PCR return negative.

Rabih escalates. Leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and splenomegaly raise both tick-borne bacterial infections and — in Southern California in summer — mosquito-borne arboviruses like [[West Nile Virus|West Nile]], though the long time course fits poorly. With liver, spleen, and now marrow involvement plus cytopenias, his concern crystallizes on **hematologic malignancy, specifically [[Lymphoma|lymphoma]]**. He reconciles the absent lymphadenopathy by noting lymphomas can be extranodal or arise in the spleen — "the biggest lymph node known to us" — and recommends a **bone marrow biopsy**. As peripheral due diligence he adds an [[Antinuclear Antibody|ANA]] for an atypical [[Systemic Lupus Erythematosus|lupus]] presentation, and — at the hematologist's prompting — the easy, non-invasive [[Vitamin B12 Deficiency|B12]], copper, and folate checks that might spare an invasive procedure, even though the B12 illness script does not fit.

The case then pivots on Reza's own reading. Underwhelmed by the inflammatory signal (a single transient fever, normal ESR, only modest ferritin), he fixes on the paradox of **hepatomegaly with stone-cold-normal liver chemistries** — a combination he had never seen in mononucleosis or lymphoma, which usually leave a hepatic signature. Searching case reports, he finds a young patient with hepatosplenomegaly, bicytopenia, and normal liver chemistries caused by **[[Gaucher Disease|Gaucher disease type 1]]**. He revisits the history: the patient is of Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry, his brother has splenomegaly, and his father has hepatomegaly (attributed to fatty liver). Gaucher type 1 becomes where Reza's "money" sits. Meanwhile the team, working the spleen with the "molecules" approach (no water, no red cells — probably white cells), also considers [[Common Variable Immunodeficiency|CVID]] (diarrhea plus recurrent prostatitis) and sends immunoglobulins (normal), and [[Celiac Disease|celiac disease]] for its extraintestinal manifestations (serology negative).

Crucially, before the invasive workup, Reza takes time to explain to the patient — who had wanted to leave — what was being considered (leukemia, lymphoma, or Gaucher type 1); the patient then chooses to stay and even requests the bone marrow biopsy. The biopsy and aspirate show **no leukemia, no lymphoma, and no Gaucher cells**. The patient is discharged and improves. Two weeks later at an ID appointment he is entirely well — weight recovering, no night sweats, tolerating a diet — and the final impression is a **viral syndrome NOS**. Rabih and Reza close by speculating: a transient viral process, possibly one that hijacked an atypical immune system (with the recurrent prostatitis as a faint immunologic clue), possibly a pathogen outside the usual serologic panel ([[HHV-6]], an arbovirus, or an unnamed virus), and both reflect that many such cases in otherwise healthy young people simply go unexplained.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Framing Effect|Framing]] by deliberate column-separation.** Rabih's opening instruction to keep the fever, the abdominal pain, and the prostatitis in three separate columns is an anti-premature-synthesis device. Multi-threaded cases tempt clinicians to force a single unifying diagnosis; the columns license each thread to be worked on its own merits and to merge only "if they merge at all." The abdominal-pain column ultimately dissolves into the hepatosplenomegaly column, but only after being examined independently.
- **The [[Background–Foreground Framing|background case within a case]].** The recurrent prostatitis is a pre-existing, only-partially-explained problem running underneath the acute presentation. Reza names the difficulty explicitly: you are evaluating one case against the noise of another, and it is hard to tell which findings belong to the new illness versus the old one. This is a recognized trap of comorbidity — the background disease can both mimic and mask the foreground.
- **[[Availability Heuristic|Availability]] cuts both ways.** Rabih's recall of a recent Cryptococcus-seeding-the-prostate case productively widens the prostatitis differential toward indolent hematogenous infection. Reza's discovery of a Gaucher type 1 case report that matches the hepatosplenomegaly-with-normal-LFTs pattern anchors his leading hypothesis. Availability generated genuinely useful hypotheses here — but the confidently held Gaucher hypothesis was ultimately wrong, a reminder that a vivid matching exemplar raises salience, not posterior probability.
- **Localization discipline: the "compressive stomach" caveat as an anti-anchoring move.** Rather than anchor on a gastric or pancreatic source for postprandial pain, Rabih pre-empts the anchor by naming how many extragastric organs can compress the stomach. This keeps the abdominal-pain thread open long enough for imaging to relocate it to the spleen and liver.
- **Discriminating the inflammatory signature.** Reza's read of the case turns on treating "inflammation" as a quantitative claim to be tested, not assumed: a single transient fever, a normal ESR, and an only-modest ferritin argued *against* a florid malignant or infiltrative process and *toward* a transient, self-limited one — the interpretation the outcome vindicated.
- **The [[Cognitive Autopsy|retrospective reckoning]] and [[Diagnostic Uncertainty|diagnostic humility]].** The episode's meta-lesson is that a rigorous, well-reasoned workup can end without a diagnosis, and that this is a legitimate — and systematically undertaught — endpoint. The hosts explicitly note that unsolved cases "never get told," biasing learners toward believing certainty is the norm. They also surface the humbling possibility of holes in the diagnostic panel itself (untested pathogens like HHV-6 or arboviruses).

## Teaching Discussion

- The day-one fever framework is the portable takeaway: prioritize infection, but in parallel rule out the three H's — hyperthyroidism, hematologic (thrombotic microangiopathy/TTP-HUS), and hyperthermia (NMS, heat stroke, exertional) — precisely because these are the fever etiologies you cannot afford to diagnose slowly.
- Postprandial pain is a seductive localizer. It genuinely implicates the digestive organs, but "compressive disease of the stomach" means an enlarged spleen, an enlarged liver, retroperitoneal disease, or ascites can all produce postprandial discomfort and early satiety without any primary gastric pathology — so treat the localization as a starting hypothesis, not a conclusion.
- Recurrent bacterial prostatitis in a young man without an STI or anatomic lesion should prompt two questions: is the diagnosis correct, and if so, did the organism arrive by ascension or by hematogenous seeding? Hematogenous prostatic involvement is a feature of indolent infections (TB, brucellosis, endemic mycoses, Cryptococcus) and can be a clue to an immunocompromised host.
- Lymphopenia is not self-interpreting: it is the expected companion of many viral illnesses *and* a potential flag for immunodeficiency (acute HIV, CVID). Let the company it keeps — recurrent infections, hepatosplenomegaly, time course — decide which reading to weight.
- The workup of febrile hepatosplenomegaly with cytopenias should escalate toward hematologic malignancy: lymphoma is the lead, absent nodes do not exclude it (extranodal and splenic primaries exist — the spleen is the largest lymph node), and a marrow PET signal justifies a bone marrow biopsy. Easy non-invasive labs (B12, copper, folate) are worth sending first if they might avert the procedure.
- Hepatomegaly with normal liver chemistries is a discriminating combination worth cataloguing: it argues against processes that scar or inflame the parenchyma (mononucleosis, most lymphomas) and toward infiltrative/storage processes — here Gaucher disease type 1, supported by Ashkenazi ancestry and a family history of organomegaly, even though the biopsy ultimately excluded it.
- The humanistic thread is inseparable from the reasoning: taking time to explain the stakes (leukemia, lymphoma, Gaucher) converted a patient who wanted to leave into one who requested the diagnostic biopsy. "We're not in charge" — the clinician's job is to inform and guide the patient's own decision, not to override it.
- Finally, the case models diagnostic humility. A fully worked, self-limited illness in a young person can end as "viral syndrome NOS," and the honest lesson is that certainty is not always available — and that the undiagnosed cases are the ones medicine tends to leave out of its teaching.

## Handbook Opportunities

- A **day-one fever card** — infection-first plus the three H's, with the one-line rationale (each must not be diagnosed slowly) and the reflex TSH.
- A **postprandial pain / compressive stomach** entry — the digestive-organ list alongside the extragastric compressors (spleen, liver, retroperitoneum, ascites) that erode its localizing value.
- A **prostatitis in the young man** card — STI vs anatomic vs hematogenous seeding, and the indolent-infection differential that reframes it as a host clue.
- A **hepatomegaly with normal liver chemistries** quick reference — the short differential (infiltrative/storage, including Gaucher type 1) that this combination should trigger.
- A **febrile hepatosplenomegaly with cytopenias** work-up map — infectious → hematologic → metabolic escalation, ending at bone marrow biopsy.
- A **"case with no diagnosis"** teaching entry — how to close, document, and follow a self-limited undiagnosed illness, and the calibration lesson it carries.
