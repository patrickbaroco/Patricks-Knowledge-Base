## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 71-year-old man is sent from hematology to the ED for a "sinister" calcium of 14, with chart labs (already several days old) showing a new anemia (Hgb 11→9 over three weeks), a mild [[Acute Kidney Injury|AKI]], mild hyperphosphatemia, and a mild non-gap acidosis. Reza first shows how *one* number can drive the rest — [[Hypercalcemia|hypercalcemia]] → [[Nephrogenic Diabetes Insipidus|nephrogenic DI]] → hypovolemia → prerenal AKI → low-EPO anemia and acidosis — a [[Unifying Diagnosis|pathophysiologic cascade]] rather than a list of separate lesions. He resists reflexively reading hypercalcemia+anemia+renal failure as [[Multiple Myeloma|myeloma]]'s CRAB, noting those are *late* findings and offering a tempo-corrected reordering ([[BARC Mnemonic|"BARC"]]: bone → anemia → renal → calcium). With PTH suppressed (the appropriate response), he runs the full non-PTH [[Schema-Based Reasoning|schema]] — [[PTHrP|PTHrP]], vitamin-D excess and granulomatous 1,25 conversion, lytic/myeloma, [[Milk-Alkali Syndrome|milk-alkali]], thyrotoxicosis, vitamin A, thiazides, and **immobilization** — and orders SPEP, PTHrP, and a skeletal survey. Then the history nobody had taken arrives: the man feels *great*, and after **sequential bilateral cataract surgery** left him functionally blind he barely moved for two weeks. The calcium normalizes on its own; PTHrP, SPEP, free light chains are normal and vitamin D is low. Both the hematologist and endocrinologist land on **[[Hypercalcemia of Immobilization|hypercalcemia of immobilization]]** — a diagnosis of exclusion marked by spontaneous resolution and no other lab abnormality. The lessons: the answer lived in an untaken history; an atypical trigger (cataracts) hid a textbook mechanism via a [[Representativeness Heuristic|representativeness]] trap; and, per Rabih, "to each their own schema" is wrong — you want [[Schema Multiplicity|many schemas per problem]], like extra EKG leads.

## Pearls

- **Two clinical clues should make you check a calcium before you ever see the number: constipation and polyuria together.** [[Hypercalcemia|Hypercalcemia]] causes [[Nephrogenic Diabetes Insipidus|nephrogenic DI]] (polyuria) and slows the gut (constipation); the same pairing is produced by hypokalemia. It is a diagnosis usually made on the lab, so the clinical prompt is worth memorizing.
- **Prefer a pathophysiologic *cascade* to a list of separate abnormalities.** One driver — hypercalcemia → nephrogenic DI → hypovolemia → prerenal [[Acute Kidney Injury|AKI]] → low erythropoietin and non-gap acidosis — can explain a new anemia, a bumped creatinine, and a low bicarbonate at once. Asking "can the [[Center of Gravity|center of gravity]] explain everything?" is more powerful than cataloguing each lesion.
- **CRAB misleads on tempo — in [[Multiple Myeloma|myeloma]], hypercalcemia is a *late* event, not the opening act.** Reza's tempo-corrected reordering, [[BARC Mnemonic|"BARC"]] (Bone lesions → Anemia → Renal failure → Calcium), keeps you from over-reading an early hypercalcemia+anemia+renal picture as classic myeloma when the sequence is wrong.
- **A *suppressed* PTH is the correct, expected response to hypercalcemia — it doesn't close the case, it opens the non-PTH [[Schema-Based Reasoning|schema]].** That schema (PTHrP, vitamin-D excess/granulomatous 1,25 conversion, lytic disease/myeloma, milk-alkali, thyrotoxicosis, vitamin A, thiazides, and immobilization) is where the answer lives; immobilization is the branch clinicians most reliably forget.
- **The diagnosis was in a history no one had taken.** The entire workup was assembled off the chart and the "green sheet" before anyone asked the patient how he was functioning; spontaneous resolution plus *no other lab abnormality* defines [[Hypercalcemia of Immobilization|immobilization hypercalcemia]], a diagnosis of exclusion. Talk to the patient before you commit to a workup.
- **An atypical trigger can hide a textbook mechanism — beware the [[Representativeness Heuristic|representativeness]] trap.** Cataract surgery "shouldn't" immobilize anyone, so it was dismissed; in fact *sequential bilateral* cataracts left a highly active man with excellent bone density functionally blind and couch-bound for two weeks — pure adult immobilization hypercalcemia diagnosed only after a negative search for a second contributing cause (cf. [[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]]).

## Problem Representation

A 71-year-old previously highly functional man is referred from hematology to the ED for hypercalcemia to ~14, with several-day-old chart labs showing a new normocytic anemia (Hgb 11→9 over ~3 weeks) with normal WBC/platelets, a mild AKI (Cr 1.5 from ~0.9), mild hyperphosphatemia (~5.5), and a mild non-gap metabolic acidosis; the reticulocyte count and smear are normal and PTH is fully suppressed (~1). On finally obtaining the history he is asymptomatic and had been nearly immobile for two weeks following sequential bilateral cataract surgery; repeat labs normalize spontaneously without intervention, and PTHrP, SPEP, and serum free light chains are normal with low 25- and 1,25-vitamin D — yielding a diagnosis of exclusion of hypercalcemia of immobilization, with the accompanying anemia, AKI, and acidosis attributable to a hypercalcemia-driven nephrogenic DI / prerenal cascade.

## Case Narrative

Rabih opens by admitting he was "completely stumped." A 71-year-old man is referred from a hematology clinic to the ED for concern of a sinister process: his calcium is 14. Reza notes that hypercalcemia is hard to predict clinically and is usually caught on the lab, then poses a teaching question — which two symptoms, combined, should prompt you to think of it? The answer is **constipation and polyuria** (the latter via [[Nephrogenic Diabetes Insipidus|nephrogenic diabetes insipidus]]), a pairing hypokalemia can also produce. He would want to assign a *tempo* to the calcium disorder — the "space-time continuum" — by mining the history for prior stones or an earlier calcium, since chronic hypercalcemia would reshape the differential.

Rabih gives the available data, all of it read off the chart because the consultant's handoff on the "green sheet" was in illegible handwriting: a hemoglobin drifting from 11 to 9 over about three weeks, normal white count and platelets, normal liver enzymes, a mild AKI (creatinine ~1.5 from a baseline ~0.9), a phosphorus risen to ~5.5, a bicarbonate around 20, and a calcium of ~13.7 — with, on questioning, a mild *non-gap* metabolic acidosis.

Reza asks which value to make the **[[Center of Gravity|center of gravity]]** and chooses the calcium, then asks whether it can explain everything. It can: hypercalcemia produces nephrogenic DI, which drives hypovolemia and prerenal [[Acute Kidney Injury|AKI]], which can generate a non-gap acidosis and, via a low-erythropoietin state, the anemia — a single **[[Unifying Diagnosis|pathophysiologic cascade]]**. Approaching the anemia separately (via Kara's isolated-anemia thought train: acute blood loss/hemolysis vs subacute marrow/substrate problems), he leans subacute given the three-week course and wants a smear and reticulocyte count. He notes the reflex of reading hypercalcemia + anemia + renal insufficiency as **[[Multiple Myeloma|myeloma]]**'s CRAB, but pushes back on tempo: those CRAB findings are *late* myeloma, so he proposes reordering it as **[[BARC Mnemonic|"BARC"]]** — bone pain/lesions first, then anemia, then renal insufficiency, then hypercalcemia.

Turning to the hypercalcemia itself, he corrects for albumin (which would only push it higher), calls it moderate, and invokes the outpatient **[[Base Rate|base rate]]** — hyperparathyroidism, then cancer, then a distant third of [[Milk-Alkali Syndrome|milk-alkali]] (citing Bob Center's article) — insisting the workup start with a PTH. A *normal* PTH would be unexpected and route toward hyperparathyroidism; a *suppressed* PTH is the physiologically appropriate response (negative feedback, since PTH drives [[1-Alpha-Hydroxylation|1-alpha-hydroxylation]] of vitamin D and GI calcium absorption). He flags that a non-gap acidosis can't even be called that without a VBG (it could be compensation for a respiratory alkalosis), but if real and without diarrhea could suggest a renal tubular acidosis, itself linkable to a paraproteinemia. He asks next for reticulocyte, smear, VBG, and PTH.

The returns: reticulocyte normal, smear normal, VBG not obtained ("a subpar attending"), and PTH fully suppressed (~1). Reza sets aside the acidosis and anemia and goes straight to the non-PTH **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|schema]]** for hypercalcemia: **[[PTHrP|PTHrP]]** (squamous cell, renal cell, breast cancers); excess vitamin D intake; excess conversion of 25- to 1,25-vitamin D by granulomatous processes ([[Granulomatous Disease|granulomatous disease]] — infection, sarcoid, occasionally lymphoma); lytic disease and myeloma (he would calculate a protein gap and send an [[Serum Protein Electrophoresis|SPEP]] and urine protein:creatinine); [[Milk-Alkali Syndrome|milk-alkali]] (excluded here by the acidosis, since it causes alkalosis); thyrotoxicosis; vitamin A toxicity; and a medication review (thiazides). He notes the degree of hypercalcemia is a bit higher than he'd expect for some of these. Given the age and cancer concern he would send SPEP and PTHrP, consider a 1,25-vitamin D, and get a **[[Skeletal Survey|skeletal survey]]** for lytic lesions (aware it would miss the blastic lesions of prostate cancer). Notably, he lists immobilization among the causes but reasons the patient "isn't immobilized."

Then Rabih finally supplies the history. The man arrives feeling *amazing*, with no complaints, and mentions he has been resting a great deal since **cataract surgery** three weeks earlier; the calcium had been drawn a week ago and the chart labs were four to five days old. Repeat bloodwork is entirely normal, including the calcium, with PTH again suppressed. The added-on workup returns normal PTHrP, normal SPEP, normal serum free light chains, and *low* 25- and 1,25-vitamin D. He gets a liter of fluid and is sent back to hematology-oncology, who had not yet evaluated him — and the calcium normalizes on its own with essentially no intervention.

Reza reasons that the sinister pathologies are excluded, that the hypercalcemia was real and drove the nephrogenic-DI cascade (anemia, AKI, acidosis), and that the PTH may simply need time to recover; he would arrange follow-up in a month with return precautions for polyuria or constipation. Rabih reveals the answer: the man had **sequential bilateral cataracts** and was effectively blind, and this highly active person with excellent bone mineral density stopped moving for roughly two weeks. Both the hematologist and the endocrinologist diagnosed **[[Hypercalcemia of Immobilization|hypercalcemia of immobilization]]** — a diagnosis of exclusion defined by spontaneous improvement and the *absence of any other lab abnormality*. Rabih confesses that despite reviewing his own hypercalcemia schema hundreds of times he had forgotten immobilization, partly because cataract surgery didn't seem immobilizing; Reza, in turn, is candid that in real time he would not have felt confident *committing* to immobilization — the normal calcium would reassure him, but he lacks command of that illness script and would offer it only tentatively.

The reflections turn meta. Rabih argues the old CP-Solvers line "to each their own schema" is wrong: one clinician should hold **[[Schema Multiplicity|as many schemas as possible]]** for a single problem — like reading an EKG with more than twelve leads — so that hypercalcemia can be approached as PTH-dependent, PTHrP-driven, part of a systemic syndrome, paired with metabolic alkalosis, or **spontaneously resolving**, each a sub-schema under a master schema. He closes by crowdsourcing: the literature on hypercalcemia of immobilization is thin, and he invites listeners to help build the illness script.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Schema Multiplicity|Schema multiplicity]] — the episode's headline thesis.** Rabih explicitly retires "to each their own schema" in favor of holding *many* schemas per problem (the extra-EKG-leads metaphor), with hypercalcemia decomposed into PTH-dependent, PTHrP-driven, systemic-syndrome, alkalosis-paired, and spontaneously-resolving sub-schemas beneath a master schema. Breadth of scaffolding — not a single favored framework — is what surfaces the rare branch.
- **[[Illness Script Command|Illness-script command and its limits]].** The immobilization branch existed on both hosts' schemas yet was forgotten (Rabih) or held only tentatively (Reza, who admits he lacks command of that script and would not confidently commit even with the calcium normalizing). Having a diagnosis *listed* is not the same as owning its script well enough to act on it — a candid map of the gap between recognition and commitment.
- **[[Representativeness Heuristic|Representativeness]] as the trap that hid the answer.** Cataract surgery does not match the prototype of an immobilizing event, so immobilization was dismissed — until "sequential bilateral cataracts → functional blindness → two couch-bound weeks" revealed a textbook mechanism behind an atypical trigger. The prototype, not the physiology, misled.
- **The untaken history and [[Diagnostic Momentum|referral framing]].** The entire workup was constructed from the chart and an illegible green sheet under a hematology-supplied "sinister" frame, before anyone asked the patient how he was functioning; the single question that resolved the case ("what have you been doing?") was the one step deferred. History precedes workup.
- **[[Unifying Diagnosis|Unifying diagnosis]] done well.** Rather than tally anemia, AKI, and acidosis as separate problems, Reza drives them from one cause through an explicit pathophysiologic chain — Occam applied via mechanism, and a model of how to test whether a single center of gravity accounts for scattered data.
- **[[Base Rate|Base-rate discipline]] and tempo.** He anchors the outpatient differential in frequency (hyperparathyroidism > malignancy > milk-alkali) and repeatedly invokes tempo — the "space-time continuum," the BARC reordering of CRAB — to keep late-stage findings from masquerading as early ones.

## Teaching Discussion

- Constipation plus polyuria is the clinical pairing that should prompt a calcium; hypercalcemia produces both (constipation and nephrogenic-DI polyuria), as can hypokalemia.
- Ask whether one center of gravity explains the whole panel before working up each abnormality: a hypercalcemia-driven nephrogenic-DI/prerenal cascade can account for anemia (low EPO), AKI, and a non-gap acidosis together.
- CRAB encodes myeloma's features but not their order; because hypercalcemia is late, a tempo-aware reordering (bone → anemia → renal → calcium) prevents over-reading an early hypercalcemia+anemia+renal picture as classic myeloma.
- A suppressed PTH is the appropriate feedback response to hypercalcemia and should trigger the full non-PTH schema (PTHrP malignancy, vitamin-D excess and granulomatous 1,25 conversion, lytic disease/myeloma, milk-alkali, thyrotoxicosis, vitamin A, thiazides, immobilization); milk-alkali is distinguished by alkalosis, so a coexisting acidosis argues against it.
- Immobilization hypercalcemia is a diagnosis of exclusion recognized by spontaneous resolution and no other persistent lab abnormality; in adults it is uncommon as the sole cause and warrants a search for a second contributor before being attributed entirely to immobility, but an intensely active person with excellent bone density can manifest it from a short but near-total immobilization — even from something as unassuming as bilateral cataract surgery.
- Take the history before committing to a workup: the diagnosis here was available in one question about function, obscured by a chart-driven, referral-framed evaluation.
- Hold multiple schemas for a single problem rather than a single preferred framework; the odds of retrieving the rare correct branch rise with the number and granularity of schemas you can bring to bear.
