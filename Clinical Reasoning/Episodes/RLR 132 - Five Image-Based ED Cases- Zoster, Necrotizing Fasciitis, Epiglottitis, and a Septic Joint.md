## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A rapid-fire, image-based format: Robbie shares photos and imaging from his last several ED shifts, and Reza reasons through each case live with minimal context, describing exactly what he sees before Robbie reveals the history and outcome. Five cases in roughly thirty minutes: dermatomal zoster on an unusual (ankle/L5) distribution; hemorrhagic zoster in an immunosuppressed patient with a normal CBC and coagulation panel; gas-forming necrotizing fasciitis (ultimately Staph aureus, despite the gas suggesting Clostridium); Haemophilus influenzae epiglottitis in an unvaccinated young adult; and a septic shoulder joint from recurrent Streptococcus dysgalactiae bacteremia. A sixth case (a "juicy" CBC) is teased but deliberately saved for a future episode.

## Pearls

- Vesicular, papular-to-vesicular lesions in a dermatomal distribution should prioritize herpes zoster; lesions at different stages of development (some intact, some deroofed and scabbing) within the same eruption support this over other vesicular processes.
- Zoster pain classically precedes the rash — patients often describe radiating neuropathic pain or numbness (sometimes misattributed to sciatica or another musculoskeletal cause) days before the rash itself is noticed.
- Zoster confined to multiple contiguous levels of a single dermatome (e.g., widespread across the L5 distribution) is not the same as disseminated zoster (multiple, non-contiguous dermatomes) — confirm the distribution carefully before applying the "disseminated" label, since it has different immunocompromise implications.
- Standard treatment for herpes zoster is valacyclovir (e.g., 1g three times daily for seven days) to reduce the duration of symptoms, decrease viral shedding/transmission, and — most importantly — reduce the risk of postherpetic neuralgia; add steroids if there's facial nerve involvement causing facial palsy (Ramsay Hunt-type presentation).
- Petechiae, purpura, and ecchymosis share the same underlying pathophysiology (red blood cells extravasating from vessels) and differ only by lesion size (pinpoint, larger, and bruise-like/large, respectively).
- When evaluating non-blanching skin lesions, check whether they're palpable: palpable purpura points toward leukocytoclastic (small-vessel) vasculitis, reflecting vessel wall inflammation causing RBC extravasation.
- Small-vessel vasculitis is classically gravity-dependent (lower extremity predominant) due to the added hydrostatic stress of blood pooling in dependent limbs — involvement of the arms rather than the legs is atypical and should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis.
- Hemorrhagic zoster (bleeding into vesicles, producing an ecchymotic/purpuric appearance overlying a vesicular eruption) is a well-described variant seen in patients who are on blood thinners, thrombocytopenic, or — for reasons not fully understood — immunosuppressed even with an entirely normal CBC and coagulation panel.
- A high index of suspicion justifies confirmatory PCR testing (of an unroofed vesicle) for zoster even when the presentation is atypical enough to raise other diagnoses, rather than relying on clinical diagnosis alone as you would for a classic dermatomal case.
- Radiolucency ("air") seen on plain film within soft tissue/muscle, especially with disproportionate limb swelling, severe pain, high fever, and marked leukocytosis, should immediately raise concern for necrotizing fasciitis (or another gas-forming soft tissue infection) — this is a limb- and life-threatening emergency requiring broad-spectrum antibiotics, a toxin-binding agent, and emergent surgical consultation.
- Necrotizing fasciitis subtyping by likely organism: Type 1 is polymicrobial and classically associated with diabetes; Type 2 is monomicrobial, classically Group A Streptococcus, and more typical in patients without diabetes; Type 3 is classically clostridial and specifically associated with gas production.
- The presence of gas on imaging does not reliably confirm a clostridial etiology — anaerobic organisms capable of gas production are notoriously difficult to culture, so their absence on culture doesn't rule them out, and a sufficiently necrotic tissue environment can produce gas from anaerobic activity even when the dominant organism identified (here, Staph aureus) isn't classically gas-forming.
- A markedly edematous, heterogeneous-appearing structure obstructing the oropharynx/airway on CT should prompt consideration of both a necrotic/outgrown-blood-supply tumor and an acute infectious process (like epiglottitis) — the vaccination history is a critical, fast discriminator between these possibilities.
- "Hot potato voice" (a muffled voice from severe oropharyngeal swelling) with severe sore throat, fever, and poor secretion management is classically associated with epiglottitis, historically most often caused by Haemophilus influenzae — now a rare "ancient disease" in fully vaccinated populations, but still very much possible in unvaccinated patients.
- For synovial fluid interpretation, the two questions that resolve the vast majority of cases are: is the fluid inflammatory or non-inflammatory, and — if inflammatory — is it infected or crystalline (remembering these two can coexist, since an infection can trigger a concurrent gout flare)?
- Synovial fluid white count doesn't reliably distinguish infection from crystalline disease at any single cutoff, but counts north of 100,000 increasingly favor infection. A negative crystal exam does not exclude crystalline disease (crystal detection is imperfect, similar to gram stain sensitivity for infection) — a positive finding (crystals or organisms) is much more useful than a negative one.
- A high synovial fluid neutrophil percentage further favors infection over crystalline disease as the operative process.
- The most common organisms in septic arthritis (and in osteomyelitis, spinal infections, and cellulitis broadly) are Staph and Strep species — empiric coverage should reflect this (e.g., vancomycin plus ceftriaxone) pending cultures.
- Diagnosing and treating a septic joint is not the end of the workup — always ask why the joint became infected (hematogenous seeding from a separate source, direct inoculation from trauma or injection drug use, or a persistent/recurrent bacteremia source) rather than treating the joint infection as a self-contained problem.
- Recurrence of the same organism in blood and a joint shortly after completing a prior antibiotic course for bacteremia from that same organism should raise concern for an unresolved or undrained source (e.g., an endovascular or other seeded focus) rather than being treated as an unrelated new infection.

## Problem Representation

Five separate emergency-department cases, worked through from images and minimal history alone: (1) dermatomal zoster on the ankle/L5 distribution in an immunocompetent patient; (2) hemorrhagic, widespread zoster in a patient with small cell carcinoma and a normal CBC/coagulation panel; (3) gas-forming necrotizing fasciitis in a diabetic man, ultimately due to Staph aureus; (4) Haemophilus influenzae epiglottitis in an unvaccinated young adult; and (5) a recurrent Streptococcus dysgalactiae septic shoulder joint in a patient with lymphedema and recent bacteremia from the same organism.

## Case Narrative

**Case 1 — Dermatomal zoster on the ankle.** From an image of ankle and calf lesions alone, Reza identified papular-to-vesicular lesions in what appeared to be a dermatomal distribution, prioritizing shingles despite the unusual (ankle) location; lesions at different stages of development (an eruption on the medial calf that had already deroofed and begun scabbing) reinforced the diagnosis. Robbie confirmed the eruption spanned the L5 dermatome. Reza correctly predicted the history before being told: the patient had radiating leg pain first (initially dismissed by his wife as sciatica) and only noticed the rash two days later while putting on his shoes, presenting with the correct self-diagnosis of shingles. He was treated with valacyclovir 1g three times daily for seven days (to reduce postherpetic neuralgia risk, symptom duration, and transmission) and discharged with gabapentin and lidocaine for neuropathic pain control. This patient had no immunocompromising condition.

**Case 2 — Hemorrhagic zoster.** A second image, from a different patient with more richly pigmented skin, showed a similar vesicular eruption — but this time with visible hemorrhage into the vesicles, giving an ecchymotic appearance. Reza reasoned through the combination of vesicles and ecchymosis, correctly identifying this as hemorrhagic zoster (recalling that Robbie had taught him about the entity previously) and noting the eruption appeared widespread/scattered rather than confined to one dermatome — raising the question of what causes bleeding into zoster vesicles (blood thinners, thrombocytopenia, or immunosuppression) and prompting him to recommend checking a CBC, coagulation studies, and medication history. Robbie revealed this patient had small cell carcinoma with associated immunosuppression, but — notably — a completely normal CBC and INR, illustrating that hemorrhagic zoster can occur in immunosuppressed patients without any laboratory evidence of a bleeding diathesis. Given the atypical appearance, a PCR of an unroofed vesicle was sent (unlike the first, more classic case) and returned positive for zoster; the rash improved dramatically with valacyclovir.

**Case 3 — Gas-forming necrotizing fasciitis.** From a plain film showing asymmetric leg swelling with radiolucency extending from the tibia into the surrounding soft tissue and muscle, Reza raised concern for a puncture wound or gas-producing infection. Told this was a 72-year-old man with diabetes presenting with one day of high fever and severe leg pain (white count 32,000), Reza prioritized necrotizing fasciitis, initially favoring Type 1 (polymicrobial, associated with diabetes) before reconsidering and favoring Type 3 (clostridial, gas-producing) given the radiographic gas. He recommended broad-spectrum antibiotics, a toxin-binding agent, and emergent surgical consultation given the limb- and life-threatening nature of the process. The actual causative organism was Staph aureus (bacteremia and necrotizing fasciitis, confirmed intraoperatively), treated with vancomycin and meropenem and emergent surgical debridement. Robbie explained that the presence of gas doesn't reliably implicate Clostridium specifically — anaerobic gas-forming organisms are notoriously difficult to culture, so a necrotic enough tissue environment can produce gas from unidentified anaerobes even when a more easily cultured organism like Staph aureus is what's ultimately recovered.

**Case 4 — Haemophilus influenzae epiglottitis.** Scrolling through a CT starting at the head, Reza identified a markedly edematous, heterogeneous structure in the oropharynx appearing to obstruct the airway, initially favoring a necrotic tumor (with areas of differing density reflecting outgrown blood supply) causing upper airway obstruction. Given the actual history — a 24-year-old, otherwise healthy man with three days of severe sore throat, fever, hot potato voice, poor secretion management, and a history of declining all vaccinations — Reza pivoted to a peritonsillar or deep oropharyngeal infection, specifically favoring Haemophilus given the hot-potato voice and lack of vaccination, and recommended ENT involvement for possible drainage. On intubation, the team found a hugely swollen, edematous epiglottis, and blood cultures grew Haemophilus influenzae — a diagnosis of classic epiglottitis, now rare in vaccinated populations.

**Case 5 — Recurrent streptococcal septic shoulder.** From a synovial fluid panel alone (white count 74,000, red count 8,000, cloudy appearance, no urate crystals seen, 89% segmented neutrophils), Reza worked through his synovial fluid framework: first, is the fluid inflammatory (yes, clearly); second, if inflammatory, is it infected or crystalline, noting these can coexist. The red count argued against hemarthrosis. He noted crystal negativity doesn't exclude crystalline disease (imperfect test sensitivity, like gram stain for infection) but that the very high neutrophil percentage favored infection. Concluding the joint was infected until proven otherwise, he recommended empiric coverage for the most common septic arthritis organisms (Staph and Strep) with vancomycin and ceftriaxone, blood cultures, and an orthopedic consult for washout — and, critically, asked why the joint became infected in the first place (trauma, injection drug use, or hematogenous spread from another source). The actual patient was a 74-year-old man with lymphedema who had developed Streptococcus dysgalactiae bacteremia one month earlier, completed antibiotics only a week prior, and then represented with the same organism recovered from both blood and synovial fluid — prompting operative washout, with the outcome still pending at the time of recording.

A sixth case — a "juicy" CBC — was deliberately teased and saved for a future episode rather than covered here, given the thirty-minute format constraint.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Impact |
|---|---|
| Vesicular lesions in a dermatomal distribution, at varying stages of eruption | Strongly supports herpes zoster over other vesicular or purpuric processes |
| Pain preceding rash by days, radiating/neuropathic quality | Reinforces the zoster diagnosis and explains common misattribution to a musculoskeletal cause (e.g., sciatica) |
| Vesicles with hemorrhage/ecchymotic change, widespread distribution | Raises suspicion for hemorrhagic zoster specifically; prompts evaluation for coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, or immunosuppression |
| Normal CBC and INR in an immunosuppressed (malignancy) patient with hemorrhagic zoster | Confirms hemorrhagic zoster can occur even without demonstrable coagulopathy — immunosuppression alone can be sufficient |
| Radiolucent gas in soft tissue/muscle on plain film + fever + severe pain + marked leukocytosis | Strongly supports necrotizing fasciitis/gas-forming soft tissue infection as a surgical emergency |
| Gas present but Staph aureus (not Clostridium) grown on culture | Confirms gas production does not specifically implicate Clostridium — anaerobic co-organisms may go uncultured |
| Heterogeneous, markedly edematous oropharyngeal structure on CT + hot potato voice + no vaccination history | Shifts the differential from tumor toward acute infectious epiglottitis, specifically Haemophilus influenzae |
| Synovial WBC 74,000 with 89% neutrophils, cloudy fluid, no crystals seen | Favors an infected (rather than purely crystalline) joint, though crystal-negativity does not fully exclude gout/pseudogout |
| Recurrence of the same organism (Strep dysgalactiae) in blood and joint shortly after completing prior antibiotics | Suggests an unresolved or persistent source rather than a new, unrelated infection |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Reasoning from images and minimal history alone, then updating with the full story**: this entire episode's format is itself a deliberate exercise in generating and testing hypotheses with limited information, then checking calibration against the eventual reveal — a useful practice format for visual/pattern-recognition diagnoses.
- **Gas on imaging anchoring toward Clostridium**: both hosts explicitly flag this as a common but incomplete inference — gas indicates an anaerobic, necrotic process, not necessarily a clostridial one specifically.
- **Vaccination history as a fast, high-yield discriminator**: used explicitly to shift from a oncologic hypothesis (tumor) to an infectious one (epiglottitis) for the sore-throat case, illustrating how a single social/historical detail can dramatically re-rank a differential.
- **"Why did this happen" as a mandatory final step**: explicitly modeled in the septic joint case — diagnosing and treating the joint infection is necessary but insufficient without identifying (or continuing to search for) the underlying source.

## Teaching Discussion

This episode functions as a deliberate visual pattern-recognition exercise across five distinct organ systems and specialties, showing how a disciplined, framework-driven approach (dermatomal distribution and lesion staging for zoster; palpable-vs-non-palpable and gravity-dependence for purpura; gas-on-imaging reasoning for soft tissue infection; vaccination history for epiglottitis; the inflammatory/infected-or-crystalline ladder for synovial fluid) allows rapid, structured progress even with minimal clinical context. It also reinforces, across several of the cases, that a single test's positive result (gas on imaging, a positive crystal exam, a specific organism on culture) is often more informative than its negative counterpart, and that diagnostic momentum shouldn't stop at "what is it" without also asking "why did this happen."
