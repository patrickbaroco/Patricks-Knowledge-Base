## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Robbie runs Reza through a discharge-triage exercise built around three simultaneous ED chief complaints: severe asymptomatic hypertension, bilateral severe scrotal pain, and severe atraumatic shoulder pain. The hypertension resolves quickly with a symptomatic-versus-asymptomatic framework. The scrotal pain case becomes a race against Fournier's gangrene after a constricting ring injury, with a deceptively reassuring urology exam nearly closing the case before a remembered case citation reopens it. The shoulder pain case starts as a septic-joint concern in a pacemaker patient and resolves into calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease (CPPD) presenting simultaneously as pseudogout, pseudo-rheumatoid arthritis, and pseudo-osteoarthritis, cracked open only once the patient's hands — not just his shoulder — are examined.

## Pearls

- Hypertension should be sorted into symptomatic versus asymptomatic before deciding how aggressively to treat it — the vast majority of elevated blood pressures found incidentally in the ED or clinic are asymptomatic, and treating them aggressively causes more harm than good because the patient's cerebral autoregulation has often adapted to their chronic baseline.
- The bar for "symptomatic" hypertension should be high and specific: dyspnea (flash pulmonary edema), severe chest pain (dissection), or new anuria (renal involvement) — not headache alone, which is too nonspecific a symptom to act on.
- The rationale against aggressively treating asymptomatic hospital hypertension parallels not reducing a hospitalized diabetic's insulin dose for a temporary restricted diet — both risk overcorrecting for an environment the patient won't remain in.
- A five-to-seven-minute triage-room visit, home antihypertensive restart, and outpatient follow-up can be entirely appropriate management for severe but asymptomatic, chronic hypertension.
- When a patient presents with a chief complaint framed around a piece of hardware ("could my pacemaker be causing this?"), consider both the direct mechanical implausibility and the indirect risk the hardware still carries — a pacemaker doesn't cause shoulder pain directly, but as prosthetic material it's still a nidus for hematogenous seeding and bacteremia.
- An eye-pain framework (is it compression or is it ischemia?) can be repurposed for the scrotum: a tight constricting ring left in place can cause transient ischemia, and ischemic tissue is then vulnerable to secondary infection.
- Fournier's gangrene should be treated as present "until proven otherwise" even at a low pretest probability, given the catastrophic consequences of a missed diagnosis — and the superficial skin exam can be deceptively normal even with significant deep extension.
- SIRS criteria (tachycardia plus fever) in a patient with a plausible genitourinary source should prompt a source-directed sepsis workup, stat cross-sectional imaging, and empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics rather than waiting for more dramatic exam findings.
- A low serum sodium is a recognized part of the clinical gestalt (the "learning score") that raises suspicion for necrotizing fasciitis, though a normal sodium doesn't rule it out.
- Diabetes is a major risk factor for Fournier's gangrene and should be specifically screened for (A1c, glucose) whenever the diagnosis is on the differential.
- Ultrasound alone can be falsely reassuring in suspected necrotizing fasciitis or deep pelvic/perineal infection — it may show only superficial findings (edema, normal organ perfusion) while missing deep fascial or retroperitoneal extension that CT with contrast would reveal.
- Don't relinquish your own clinical judgment the moment a consultant offers a reassuring read — a specific, well-remembered case citation (even from a personal reference library, not just memory) can be enough to justify pushing back and pursuing further imaging.
- Delay in diagnosis is the single largest driver of mortality in necrotizing fasciitis, making a clinician's willingness to keep pushing after an initially reassuring consult genuinely life-saving.
- For any joint complaint, distinguish intra-articular processes (infection, crystal disease) from extra-articular ones (tendon/rotator cuff): a tendon problem hurts with active movement but not with passive range of motion, while an intra-articular process hurts with both active and passive movement because any disruption of the joint space itself causes pain.
- Warmth and swelling in a joint further raise concern for an intra-articular process over a purely tendinous one.
- Completely normal inflammatory markers (CBC, ESR, CRP) make septic arthritis less likely but do not exclude it outright, especially with a new joint effusion — arthrocentesis remains the definitive test when in doubt.
- Correctly framing the underlying problem matters as much as testing: reclassifying an "isolated monoarticular shoulder arthritis" as a symmetric small-joint polyarthritis with a superimposed acute monoarticular flare, simply by examining the hands, unlocks a completely different and more accurate differential.
- Calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease (CPPD) can present as any (or all) of three recognized "pseudo" phenotypes: pseudogout (acute crystalline monoarthritis), pseudo-rheumatoid arthritis (a symmetric small-joint polyarthritis mimicking RA), and pseudo-osteoarthritis (accelerated osteoarthritis in atypical joints, like the shoulder, that don't usually develop primary OA).
- Chondrocalcinosis on plain films (classically visible in the wrist/hand and knee) is the key radiographic signature that confirms CPPD once the clinical picture raises suspicion.
- Subspecialty pattern recognition can be extraordinarily fast once the right structures are examined — a rheumatologist reaching the CPPD diagnosis in about two minutes by simply looking at the hands, shoulder, and X-ray after a much longer independent workup — a reminder that knowing when to lean on a specialist's gestalt, rather than only their formal workup, has real value.
- The same shift can showcase both "expertise going wrong" (an initially reassuring urology read that nearly missed a necrotizing infection) and "expertise going very right" (rheumatology's rapid bedside diagnosis) — neither should be read as a blanket indictment or endorsement of a specialty, but as a reminder that the clinician coordinating care has to know when to defer and when to push.

## Problem Representation

**Case 1:** A patient presenting with severe hypertension (BP 202/120) but no symptoms suggestive of end-organ threat, ultimately representing new-onset chronic primary hypertension rather than a hypertensive emergency.

**Case 2:** A man in his 60s with bilateral severe scrotal pain beginning after methamphetamine use and a prolonged constricting ring injury to the penis and scrotum, progressing over several days to fever and nausea, ultimately representing Fournier's gangrene (necrotizing fasciitis of the perineum) in the setting of previously undiagnosed diabetes.

**Case 3:** A 91-year-old man with a cardiac pacemaker presenting with three days of atraumatic, warm, swollen left shoulder pain limited on both active and passive range of motion, ultimately representing calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease manifesting simultaneously as pseudogout, pseudo-rheumatoid arthritis, and pseudo-osteoarthritis.

## Case Narrative

Robbie opened the episode by giving Reza three ED chief complaints at once — bilateral severe scrotal pain, severe left shoulder pain, and severe hypertension (202/120) — and asking him to decide, based on the chief concern alone, which patient could be safely discharged before hearing any further details.

### Case 1: Asymptomatic Severe Hypertension

Reza chose to discharge the hypertension patient first, explaining that blood pressure findings should be sorted into symptomatic and asymptomatic categories: the overwhelming majority of elevated blood pressures found incidentally are asymptomatic, and aggressively treating them — for instance with medications like hydralazine — causes more harm than benefit, since patients' cerebral autoregulation has often adapted to a chronically elevated baseline and rapid correction risks hypoperfusion. He cited teaching from Things We Do For No Reason on not adjusting home antihypertensives for asymptomatic hospitalized patients, drawing an analogy to not cutting a hospitalized diabetic's insulin dose in response to a temporary restricted diet, since their glucose will simply rebound once they resume their normal habits. He set a high bar for what counts as "symptomatic" — dyspnea (raising concern for flash pulmonary edema), severe chest pain (raising concern for dissection), or new anuria (raising concern for renal involvement) — explicitly excluding headache as too soft a symptom to act on. His plan was simply to restart the patient's home antihypertensive regimen and discharge.

Robbie confirmed this was exactly right and shared the real story: this chronically hypertensive veteran, with many prior blood pressure readings above 190, had recently entered a new relationship after losing his previous partner a decade earlier — his new partner noticed the elevated readings and encouraged him to seek care. Robbie diagnosed new, severe primary hypertension, and rather than simply discharging the patient without treatment (which many emergency departments would do), used his own primary care background to start a combination antihypertensive pill, arrange primary care follow-up, and schedule a renal function check for one week later — the entire encounter completed in five to seven minutes without the patient ever leaving the triage room.

### Case 2: Bilateral Scrotal Pain — Fournier's Gangrene

Robbie then gave the history for the second patient: a man in his 60s who had used methamphetamine five days earlier and, during a sexual encounter, had a tight ring wrapped around his penis and scrotum for an unknown duration. He woke the next day with severe bilateral scrotal pain that worsened over several days, eventually accompanied by low-grade fevers and nausea, which finally prompted him to seek care.

Before hearing the exam, Reza said he was already more worried about this patient than the shoulder-pain patient he hadn't yet heard about, invoking a framework Robbie had previously taught him for eye pain — is the problem compressive or ischemic? — and applying it to the scrotum: the constricting ring likely caused transient local ischemia, and the subsequent fever and nausea suggested secondary infection on top of that ischemic insult. He framed this as Fournier's gangrene until proven otherwise, noting that even at a low pretest probability the catastrophic stakes justified aggressive workup, and that the superficial skin exam in Fournier's gangrene can be deceptively normal despite significant deep extension.

The exam: low-grade fever (100°F), tachycardia to 126, normal blood pressure, normal orthostatics; the scrotum was enlarged, red, and warm, but there was no perineal tenderness, rash, or other extension noted on exam. Reza identified SIRS criteria (heart rate and temperature) and reasoned toward sepsis with a likely genitourinary source, which would guide empiric antibiotic selection. He was not reassured by the lack of perineal findings, reasoning that any process has to start somewhere, and ordered a stat contrast CT of the abdomen and pelvis regardless of the creatinine, blood cultures and routine labs before starting antibiotics, and empiric vancomycin plus piperacillin-tazobactam (dosed at 4.5 grams for pseudomonal coverage) — reasoning through, and ultimately deciding against, adding clindamycin given weak evidence for it in this presumed polymicrobial gram-negative/anaerobic picture rather than a toxin-mediated process. He called this a probable type 3 necrotizing fasciitis and contacted urology simultaneously.

Labs returned: WBC 18, sodium 118 — otherwise normal. Reza recalled his mentor Lawrence Tierney ("LT"), describing a personal morning-report memory from training in which a physician-patient with atraumatic thigh pain and a completely normal exam was found to have Group A strep necrotizing fasciitis, flagged specifically by Tierney because of the patient's low sodium — a formative teaching moment that left Reza weighting sodium heavily as a clue in necrotizing fasciitis, even while acknowledging a normal sodium wouldn't rule it out.

Robbie then quizzed Reza on the major risk factor he should be considering; Reza correctly answered diabetes, and Robbie revealed the patient's A1c of 12 and glucose of 345, with the corrected sodium remaining low despite accounting for the hyperglycemia. Urology examined the patient and initially reassured the team that the perineal exam was unremarkable, recommending only an ultrasound. Robbie's gut instinct persisted despite this reassurance; he recalled — via a personal "external brain" Evernote of saved teaching pearls and case citations — a specific American Journal of Medicine case report describing Fournier's gangrene presenting with a deceptively normal superficial exam. He re-engaged urology with that specific citation and pushed for a CT rather than accepting ultrasound alone. The CT showed fat stranding extending from the scrotum deep into the perineum and glutes, confirming a deep necrotizing infection; urology and the team escalated urgently, the patient was given vancomycin and a carbapenem pending pseudomonal coverage, and an operative intervention was planned. Notably, an ultrasound performed opportunistically while the patient was already in the CT queue showed only scrotal edema with normal testicular perfusion and no epididymitis — a falsely reassuring, unremarkable result that would have completely missed the diagnosis had it been relied upon alone.

Reza and Robbie both emphasized how easy it would have been to relinquish clinical judgment once a consultant offered a reassuring read, and how directly that persistence likely affected the outcome, given that delay in diagnosis is the single largest driver of mortality in necrotizing fasciitis.

### Case 3: Atraumatic Shoulder Pain — CPPD and the Three Pseudos

The third patient was a 91-year-old man with a pacemaker (placed for sick sinus syndrome) who presented with three days of severe, atraumatic left shoulder pain, preceded by a milder twinge two weeks earlier that had resolved. He specifically asked whether his pacemaker could be responsible. On exam, the shoulder was warm (though not erythematous) with pain on range of motion; the patient was otherwise well, without fever or nausea.

Reza reasoned through a framework separating intra-articular processes (infection, crystal disease) from extra-articular ones (rotator cuff or tendon pathology): a tendon problem should hurt with active movement but not with passive range of motion, whereas an intra-articular process causes pain with both, because any disruption of the joint space itself triggers pain regardless of active muscle engagement. Given the pacemaker, he treated this as a possible Staph aureus septic joint until proven otherwise — reasoning that while the hardware doesn't directly cause shoulder pain, it is a classic risk factor for hematogenous bacteremia and seeding, which could indirectly explain a septic joint. He ordered a CBC, renal panel, ESR, CRP, uric acid, and a shoulder X-ray, with plans for arthrocentesis given the joint was swollen. Robbie then added that the shoulder was also swollen, which further strengthened Reza's suspicion of an intra-articular process.

The results were unrevealing: CBC, ESR, CRP, and uric acid were all normal, arguing against an acutely inflammatory or infectious process, though Reza noted normal uric acid doesn't exclude gout and a septic joint with completely normal inflammatory markers, while unusual, isn't impossible. He considered an MRI to evaluate extra-articular structures if a joint tap didn't clarify things.

Robbie then shared two images: a shoulder X-ray showing age-appropriate osteopenia without an obvious fracture, and a photo of a clenched fist showing prominent MCP joints. Reza noted the ability to make a full fist argued against active synovitis at that moment, but the prominent MCP involvement raised the possibility of rheumatoid arthritis. Robbie then revealed the fuller picture: the patient in fact had synovitis of all MCPs, PIPs, and both wrists — a previously unnoticed symmetric polyarthritis — in addition to the acute monoarticular left shoulder arthritis, reframing the underlying problem from an isolated joint question into "what causes a symmetric polyarthritis plus a superimposed acute monoarticular flare."

Reza's differential for the symmetric polyarthritis included rheumatoid arthritis (though DIP involvement argued more toward superimposed osteoarthritis given the patient's age), lupus, and crystalline disease; he ordered hand X-rays along with RF and anti-CCP antibodies, and — given the reassuringly normal inflammatory markers — considered empiric prednisone as a reasonable alternative to putting the patient through arthrocentesis, while acknowledging he wasn't fully certain whether a septic joint could truly present with completely normal labs and planning to ask rheumatology directly.

Robbie then revealed the actual shoulder X-ray read: advanced glenohumeral and AC joint osteoarthritis plus a large calcific deposition. This prompted Reza to correctly guess calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease once shown the calcification, recalling that CPPD can mimic both osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis. Robbie confirmed: hand X-rays showed extensive chondrocalcinosis, and the full picture was a classic presentation of CPPD manifesting as all three of its recognized "pseudo" phenotypes at once — pseudogout (the acute crystalline shoulder monoarthritis), pseudo-rheumatoid arthritis (the symmetric small-joint polyarthritis), and pseudo-osteoarthritis (the accelerated glenohumeral arthritis, an atypical joint for primary OA). Robbie credited rheumatology with reaching this diagnosis in roughly two minutes at the bedside, simply by examining the hands and shoulder together with the X-ray — after he himself had been stumped by the case. Rheumatology performed a subacromial injection and arranged follow-up to discuss chronic management for CPPD-associated pseudo-rheumatoid arthritis.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The hypertension case is a compact illustration of the harm-from-overtreatment illness script — the danger isn't missing a diagnosis but overcorrecting a chronic, adapted physiologic state. The scrotal pain case is built on resisting anchoring to a reassuring consultant read: urology's exam-based reassurance could easily have ended the workup, and the case models both healthy skepticism of subspecialty input and appropriate deference to it once new, decisive imaging is obtained. The shoulder case demonstrates the cost of narrow problem framing — treating the shoulder as an isolated monoarticular process nearly missed the far more informative symmetric polyarthritis hiding in plain sight in the patient's hands, and the eventual diagnosis was reached almost entirely through re-framing the problem rather than through any single new test.

## Teaching Discussion

This episode's throughline across all three cases is the discipline of matching the intensity of workup to the actual stakes and actual data, in either direction. The hypertension case models restraint — recognizing when aggressive intervention causes more harm than the asymptomatic finding itself. The scrotal pain case models persistence — refusing to let a single reassuring subspecialty read close a life-threatening diagnosis prematurely, particularly when it conflicts with the systemic data (sodium, SIRS criteria, diabetes) already in hand. The shoulder case models re-framing — recognizing that the "problem" as initially defined (an isolated monoarticular process) was incomplete, and that simply examining an anatomically adjacent area (the hands) transformed the entire differential. Together, the three cases form a compact tutorial on calibrating clinical intensity: know when to back off, when to push through resistance, and when to step back and ask whether you're even solving the right problem.
