## Pearls

- **Sequential diagnosis is a live [[Anchoring Bias|anchoring]] trap — and falling for it is not a competence failure.** When two patients in a row have the same slam-dunk diagnosis and a third opens with the *identical* line, the [[Base Rate|base rate]] you just built primes [[Premature Closure|premature closure]]. Rabih was confident it was hernia number three *before examining the patient*; what saved him was refusing to skip the exam — and asking colleagues (Jack Penner) to double-check. The defense against a cognitive trap is a fixed process (examine every bulge), not more confidence.
- **A bulge that does not cross the midline is boxed by the [[Linea Alba|linea alba]] — that one exam finding localizes it to the [[Rectus Sheath Hematoma|rectus sheath]].** The [[Anatomical Approach|anatomical approach]] to any visible bulge asks what *substance* it is (solid, hollow, gas, or liquid) and what *plane* it sits in (skin, fascia, muscle, viscus); [[Point-of-Care Ultrasound|POCUS]] answers substance at the bedside and CT confirms. A geographic pattern that isn't contiguous with the inguinal canal argues against hernia.
- **[[Background Affects Foreground|The background always reframes the foreground]].** A bulge with ecchymosis in a man on [[Warfarin|warfarin]] is spontaneous bleeding until proven otherwise — and patients often can't recall the trivial trauma (a cough, a bump) that triggered it, because the vulnerability, not the force, is what changed. A dark hue over a bulge raises [[Ecchymosis|ecchymosis]] (blood tracking outward through tissue planes) and, at the worrying end, necrosis moving outward-in.
- **A [[Rectus Sheath Hematoma|rectus sheath hematoma]] is an *arterial* bleed, and arterial-vs-venous guides reversal.** It bleeds from the (usually inferior, sometimes superior) epigastric arteries. Tracy Minichiello's pearl: a venous hematoma is more often *not* reversed, but an arterial one tips toward **reversal** — here, PO vitamin K reversed an INR of 2.3 and the bulge receded. The tight sheath often tamponades the bleed, which is why many are self-limited.
- **Match the intervention to the severity, not the diagnosis (the [[SIADH|SIADH]] analogy).** Just as SIADH starts with fluid restriction rather than tolvaptan, a rectus sheath hematoma without end-organ compromise needs only *holding* anticoagulation — IR embolization is reserved for expansion or nerve compression (Reza's prior apixaban patient with femoral neuropathy from a hematoma). The [[Return to the Bedside|neuro exam]] assigns the severity.
- **Screen every hernia for the surgical emergency, then let symptoms drive repair.** Red flags are strangulation (erythema — you're occluding blood flow) and incarceration; counterintuitively, the *bigger* the hernia the *lower* the incarceration/strangulation risk (a wider defect traps less bowel). Absent red flags, refer to general surgery for symptom-based elective repair.

## Problem Representation

A 61-year-old man with atrial fibrillation on warfarin (switched from apixaban for GI intolerance) and hypertension presents after a bout of coughing three days earlier was followed by a right groin bulge that has persisted. Examination shows a non-reducible inguinal-region bulge with subtle overlying ecchymosis extending from the right groin to the periumbilical area, tender on Valsalva, with intact hip range of motion and no lymphadenopathy — and, critically, the bulge and ecchymosis **do not cross the midline**. Vital signs are normal; INR is 2.3, CBC is normal, and CT with contrast shows a 7 × 3 cm rectus sheath hematoma without active extravasation — a spontaneous, cough-provoked arterial (epigastric) bleed unmasked by anticoagulation, presenting in the slipstream of two preceding classic inguinal hernias.

## Case Narrative

Rabih presents three patients seen the same day, each with a right inguinal bulge, as a deliberate exercise in speed and in cognitive bias. **Patient one** is a 75-year-old man Rabih already knows from a recent bout of left-sided diverticulitis (resolved, scans normal); he now reports a right inguinal bulge, present about two weeks, most noticeable at the *end of the day* and gone by morning, with no fever, obstruction, or systemic symptoms. Reza solves it in one word — hernia — and, importantly, *explains the reasoning* rather than resting on the pattern: a bulge that worsens with upright activity and resolves with recumbent rest points to a process governed by **gravity and intra-abdominal pressure**. He generalizes into a small schema of conditions that change at night (a supine brain tumor swelling and worsening headache; nocturnal aspiration or reflux) and predicts that standing, coughing, and a head-turn Valsalva will reproduce a reducible bulge. The exam confirms an easily reducible inguinal hernia. On management, Reza screens for the surgical emergency first — strangulation (erythema from occluded blood flow) and incarceration — finds none, and refers to general surgery, noting he doesn't manage enough hernias to know the operative thresholds. Rabih fills in the nuance: repair is largely symptom-driven, incarceration/strangulation rates are low, and, counterintuitively, larger defects incarcerate *less*.

A **second** "61-year-old with a right inguinal bulge" is a setup — Rabih jokes the recording is over — which lands precisely because the [[Base Rate|base rate]] has become overwhelming. Then the **real** second case: this 61-year-old was well until, three days ago, he coughed a few times and an hour later noticed a right-wall bulge that has not gone away. Reza notes the tempo is faster and the bulge *persistent*, which — if it is a hernia — raises concern for incarceration; he withholds System 2 pending the exam and vitals. He also flags the reasoning risk aloud: patients (and clinicians) reflexively justify a new problem with a recent event, so the cough may or may not be causal.

The exam breaks the pattern. The bulge is **not reducible**, carries **subtle overlying ecchymosis**, and extends from the right groin to nearly the periumbilical region; Valsalva is painful. Reza seizes on the fact that there is a *visible anatomical abnormality*, not merely referred inguinal pain (which would open kidney stone, renal tumor, or hip pathology). He works the [[Anatomical Approach|anatomical approach]]: the geographic, non-contiguous pattern argues against a simple inguinal or ventral hernia; the question becomes what *substance* and what *plane* — reach for [[Point-of-Care Ultrasound|POCUS]] (air vs fluid vs vessel) and, if unrevealing, CT, plus a CBC, chem-7, and liver tests. The ecchymosis's dark hue makes him briefly consider necrosis tracking outward, which he down-weights given the non-toxic appearance.

The history then lands: AFib on **warfarin** (intolerant of apixaban), hypertension on amlodipine, normal vitals. On a fuller exam the bulge and ecchymosis are seen to stop **abruptly at the midline**. Reza invokes "[[Background Affects Foreground|the background always affects the foreground]]": an anticoagulated man with a bulge and ecchymosis is now a **spontaneous bleed**, with the cough as the pressure trigger and the drug as the vulnerability — trauma the patient may not even register. He likens the dermatome-respecting, plane-bound spread to shingles (territorial, not diffuse, bleeding) and recalls a prior apixaban patient with a spontaneous hematoma causing nerve compression. He orders platelets/PT/PTT and imaging, favoring CT to gauge mass effect (compartment-syndrome physiology on nearby nerves/vessels), noting a beta-blocker could blunt the tachycardic response to blood loss.

Results: CBC normal, INR 2.3; POCUS shows an ambiguous structure; CT with contrast confirms a **7 × 3 cm rectus sheath hematoma without active extravasation**. Rabih supplies the anatomic key — the rectus sheath is bounded in the midline by the **[[Linea Alba|linea alba]]**, so it *cannot* cross it, which is exactly the exam cutoff. On management, Reza reasons by severity (the [[SIADH|SIADH]] "start X, escalate" analogy): with a normal neuro exam and no expansion, no IR embolization is needed — reserve that for nerve compression or ongoing bleeding. Immediately, **stop the anticoagulation** (known bleeding, no known clot); he initially would *not* reverse an INR of 2.3, expecting it to drift down on holding alone. Long-term, weigh a [[CHA2DS2-VASc|CHA₂DS₂-VASc]] of 2 (which will only rise with age): hold two weeks, then decide with hematology whether re-challenge is possible, and if not, refer for a **[[Watchman Procedure|Watchman]]** (occlude the left atrial appendage where AFib clots form) as the durable alternative — analogous to a temporary IVC filter for the patient who can't tolerate DVT anticoagulation.

The twist that changes management comes from Rabih's ten-minute consult with anticoagulation expert **Tracy Minichiello**: reverse-or-not hinges on the *source* of the hematoma — **venous** bleeds are more often left alone, but an **arterial** bleed favors reversal. A rectus sheath hematoma is arterial (inferior or superior epigastric arteries), so the patient received **PO vitamin K**, INR reversal, and warfarin held; the bulge receded without exam worsening, and he was discharged with cough suppressants and an outpatient plan to time re-anticoagulation. In the [[Cognitive Autopsy|debrief]], Rabih is candid that only the ecchymosis saved him — a day-one painless bulge would likely have been called hernia number three — and both hosts frame the episode as the honest anatomy of a cognitive trap: [[Anchoring Bias|sequential-diagnosis bias]] is real, largely blameless, and defeated by process (examine every patient) rather than willpower.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Anchoring Bias|Sequential-diagnosis anchoring]] as the episode's spine.** Two consecutive slam-dunk hernias manufacture a base rate that makes hernia number three feel certain *before the exam*. Rabih names the honest lesson: cognitive traps are not competence failures, and the countermeasure is a non-negotiable process (examine and, when primed, get a second set of eyes) rather than trying to be more careful.
- **[[Premature Closure|Premature closure]] averted at the bedside.** The reducibility check, the ecchymosis, and the midline cutoff are the data that reopen a closed case — a concrete instance of the [[Return to the Bedside|physical exam rescuing]] reasoning that pattern-matching had already finished.
- **[[Anatomical Approach|Anatomical approach]] to a visible bulge.** Reza converts "bulge" into a tractable problem by asking *what substance* (solid/hollow/gas/liquid) and *what plane* (skin/fascia/muscle/viscus), sequencing POCUS then CT — and uses the non-contiguous geography and the midline boundary to localize to the rectus sheath.
- **[[Framing Effect|Background affects foreground]].** The single most decisive datum is a medication: anticoagulation converts an unremarkable bulge-with-bruise into spontaneous bleeding. The trigger (a cough) may be trivial or unremembered because the change is host *vulnerability*, not applied force.
- **Mechanism drives management (arterial vs venous).** The reverse/observe decision is not by protocol but by pathophysiology — rectus sheath hematomas are arterial, which tips toward reversal — a specialist pearl that reorganizes a whole management branch.
- **Severity-graded intervention and [[Diagnostic Humility|knowing your limits]].** The SIADH analogy (escalate to match severity) keeps IR in reserve for nerve compression/expansion, and both hosts repeatedly flag the boundaries of their own hernia and anticoagulation expertise, consulting surgery and an anticoagulation authority.

## Teaching Discussion

- Treat sequential diagnosis as a predictable anchoring trap: when consecutive patients share a diagnosis, the base rate you just built biases the next identical presentation toward premature closure. Defend with process — examine every patient fully and, when you feel certain before the exam, invite a second opinion — not with self-reproach.
- Localize a visible bulge with the anatomical approach: ask what substance (solid, hollow, gas, liquid) and what tissue plane, use POCUS at the bedside and CT to confirm, and let geography (contiguity with the inguinal canal, crossing vs respecting the midline) narrow the structure.
- A bulge or ecchymosis that stops abruptly at the midline is bounded by the linea alba and localizes to the rectus sheath; a rectus sheath hematoma is an arterial bleed from the inferior (or superior) epigastric arteries, often self-tamponading in the tight sheath.
- Let the background reframe the foreground: in an anticoagulated patient, a bulge with ecchymosis is spontaneous bleeding until proven otherwise, and the provoking trauma may be trivial or unrecalled because the change is bleeding vulnerability, not force.
- Decide reversal by the source of bleeding: venous hematomas are more often observed, arterial hematomas (like the rectus sheath) tip toward reversal; here, PO vitamin K reversed an INR of 2.3 and the hematoma receded.
- Match intervention to severity (the SIADH analogy): with a normal neuro exam and no expansion, holding anticoagulation suffices; reserve IR embolization for nerve compression or ongoing bleeding, guided by the bedside neuro exam.
- Plan the anticoagulation future explicitly: hold, reassess with hematology on re-challenge, and if the patient can't be re-anticoagulated, consider a Watchman (left-atrial-appendage occlusion) for durable stroke prevention in AFib — using CHA₂DS₂-VASc, which climbs with age, to frame the risk.
- Screen every hernia for strangulation (erythema) and incarceration; absent red flags, repair is symptom-driven, and larger defects paradoxically carry lower incarceration risk.
