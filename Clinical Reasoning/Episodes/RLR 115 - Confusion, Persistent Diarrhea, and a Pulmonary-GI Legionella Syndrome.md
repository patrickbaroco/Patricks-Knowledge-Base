## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> An older man falls in the bathroom and arrives confused after a week of poor intake and several days of profuse, non-bloody diarrhea. A trauma workup is appropriately prioritized first, then the reasoning pivots to linking a GI process to a systemic encephalopathy via routine metabolic derangement. The case looks like ordinary volume-depleted gastroenteritis until the patient fails to improve, develops an incidental lingular opacity on chest X-ray, and — despite a strongly diarrhea-focused workup that keeps coming back negative — is ultimately diagnosed with Legionella, a systemic disease with a pulmonary predilection that in this case presented primarily as diarrhea and confusion before frank pneumonia emerged. A second, more severe case from the following week reinforces the same lesson and adds an epidemiologic teaching point about closed-loop water systems as the true risk factor, rather than travel destination itself.

## Pearls

- When a patient is found confused after a fall, resist anchoring on the fall itself as the origin of the story; a thorough trauma evaluation (including a low threshold for cervical spine precautions and a careful search for occult fractures a confused patient cannot localize) should proceed in parallel with investigating what caused the confusion in the first place.
- A patient's ability to have performed a purposeful, multi-step task shortly before a fall (e.g., independently starting and using a shower) is itself a data point about their baseline cognitive function at that time, and can help establish when a decline in mental status actually began relative to the fall.
- Seizure, and specifically postictal confusion, is an easy-to-underweight cause of altered mental status in older adults, particularly when there is no known seizure history — internists in particular can be prone to underemphasizing this possibility relative to metabolic or infectious causes.
- A simple, high-yield way to link a GI process to a systemic encephalopathy is through basic metabolic derangement: uremia from poor oral intake and volume loss, electrolyte disturbances (sodium, potassium), and glucose are efficient, low-cost tests that can explain confusion without invoking a primary CNS process.
- Hypertensive patients at baseline can still be clinically orthostatic without ever becoming frankly hypotensive by conventional thresholds — what matters is a meaningful relative drop in blood pressure with positional change, not an absolute cutoff, so orthostatic vital signs remain worth obtaining even in a patient whose supine blood pressure looks unremarkable or elevated.
- A reported symptom like "diarrhea" should not be assumed to be actively ongoing just because a patient describes it in the history; directly observing for the symptom (e.g., with a stool kit and hourly monitoring) can reveal that a reported symptom has already resolved or was overstated, and this base-rate caution is worth applying before building an extensive workup around a symptom that may not currently be present.
- "I couldn't put my finger on it, but something feels off" is a valuable, experience-derived clinical instinct, distinct from anchoring on a specific diagnosis — it reflects allowing accumulating data to generate appropriate concern rather than forcing premature closure on a reassuring-sounding explanation (like simple volume-depleted gastroenteritis) and prematurely discharging or de-escalating a patient's care.
- An incidental finding on chest X-ray (such as a subtle lingular opacity) in a patient without respiratory symptoms is easy to deprioritize, but should regain diagnostic weight if the primary working hypothesis (e.g., an infectious diarrheal illness) fails to explain the patient's full clinical course, especially once more specific stool studies return negative.
- A practical mental checklist for the evaluation of diarrhea includes: (1) test for intraluminal/intraintestinal pathogens (stool studies), (2) ask about recent antibiotic exposure (C. difficile risk), (3) ask about travel (raising the pretest probability of Salmonella, Shigella, or Campylobacter), (4) assess for immunocompromise (HIV, IBD), and (5) actively consider whether an extraintestinal focus of disease is the true primary process, with diarrhea as a secondary consequence rather than the site of origin — an important reframe once intraluminal testing is unrevealing.
- Several systemic infections (including certain respiratory viruses and Legionella) can produce severe GI symptoms as part of a broader, extraintestinal-driven illness, meaning diarrhea does not have to originate from primary gut pathology to be a prominent presenting symptom.
- Legionella should be conceptually reframed as a systemic disease with a predilection for the lungs rather than as a pneumonia syndrome per se; its pulmonary manifestations can be delayed, subtle, or even absent relative to systemic and gastrointestinal symptoms, and useful supporting clues include hyponatremia and elevated liver transaminases or CK, reflecting its broader multisystem physiology.
- The epidemiologic risk factor for Legionella is not travel to a specific destination but exposure to standing or recirculating water in a closed-loop system (hotel water systems, certain hospital water systems, window air conditioning units) that provides a favorable growth environment for the organism — a history of any such exposure is worth eliciting specifically, independent of travel history alone.
- Just because an initial workup was reassuring does not mean the diagnostic door should be closed — repeating imaging, revisiting an incidental finding, and maintaining active reassessment as a patient's course evolves are all appropriate and often necessary, and the value of a diagnostic process should be judged by its rigor and process, not solely by whether the final diagnosis turns out to be common or exotic.

## Problem Representation

An older man with poor recent oral intake and several days of diarrhea, presenting with altered mental status following a fall, initially explained by volume-depletion-related metabolic derangement, but ultimately diagnosed with Legionella — a systemic infection presenting predominantly with diarrhea and confusion before pneumonia became clinically apparent.

## Case Narrative

Uncle Bob presented a man in his 70s who arrived at the emergency department confused after a fall in his bathroom, with about a week of poor oral intake (tolerating mostly Pepsi) and two to three days of profuse, non-bloody diarrhea. Rabih's first priority was a thorough trauma evaluation, cautioning against assuming the story began with the fall itself; he noted that the patient's demonstrated ability to independently start and use the shower shortly before the fall was itself informative about his cognitive baseline at that time, helping to frame whether the confusion preceded or followed the fall. Reza linked the GI symptoms directly to the fall, reasoning that volume depletion from poor intake and diarrhea could have caused orthostatic hypotension and a resulting fall, and flagged the GI tract as the likely primary site of pathology.

Uncle Bob reported the patient denied head trauma, had a negative CT, and — per his family, with whom he lived — had become progressively more confused and forgetful over the preceding two to three days, a clear decline from an already-imperfect baseline. Initial vitals were notably unremarkable: temperature 98.9°F, heart rate 69, blood pressure 162/91 (no orthostatics obtained), normal respiratory rate and oxygen saturation.

Reza reframed the fall as likely a "proximal," not "terminal," finding — a consequence of an already-present vulnerability (the confusion) rather than an independent inciting event — and organized his thinking around a combined CNS-plus-systemic framework, explicitly flagging seizure (with postictal confusion) as a possibility often underweighted by internists in older patients. He proposed a simple route linking GI illness to systemic encephalopathy through metabolic derangement: uremia from poor intake and diarrhea-related volume loss, sodium, and glucose as key screens, with hypercalcemia judged less likely given the presence of diarrhea (more typically associated with constipation) — and referenced a prior RLR case involving thyrotoxicosis-driven hyperdefecation as an example of a related endocrine-GI link worth keeping in the back pocket.

Rabih praised this reasoning and added two practical points: hypertensive-at-baseline patients can be clinically orthostatic without becoming frankly hypotensive, since what matters is a meaningful relative drop rather than an absolute threshold (making the absence of documented orthostatics in this history notable); and, drawing on his ED experience, that reported "diarrhea" frequently fails to reproduce under direct observation once a patient is admitted and monitored, cautioning against building the workup entirely around the symptom as reported without confirming it directly.

Initial labs showed a mildly elevated white count (~12), a chronic baseline anemia (10-11), mild hyponatremia, mild hypokalemia, a mildly low bicarbonate, creatinine 1.8, and BUN 25-30 — a picture broadly consistent with volume depletion from diarrhea, without yet suggesting a distinct systemic process. Rabih noted the low sodium was reassuring in this context, reflecting likely hypotonic fluid intake (Pepsi) to replace ongoing losses, and specifically contrasted this with how much more concerning hypernatremia would have been in the same clinical picture. Exam findings (unremarkable HEENT, cardiopulmonary, and abdominal exam; fluctuating attentiveness) and the labs together did not, in Rabih's assessment, elevate this patient above a routine, explainable diarrheal illness with a resulting mild encephalopathy in a patient with reduced baseline cognitive reserve.

The diarrhea, this time, was confirmed as ongoing (not merely reported). The patient received fluids and his creatinine improved, but a chest X-ray incidentally showed a finding in the lingula; he had no fever and no rigors despite the leukocytosis, so this was initially deprioritized. A stool sample was mislabeled and had to be redrawn; blood cultures remained pending. By hospital day two, the patient appeared clinically worse — a change Uncle Bob candidly described as something he "couldn't put his finger on." Reza specifically praised this as valuable diagnostic instinct rather than premature closure, an ability to let accumulating, non-specific data generate genuine concern rather than settling for a comfortable explanation. Reza reasoned that the persistent leukocytosis, taken together with the previously deprioritized lingular finding, now deserved reconsideration; he framed the case as three overlapping problems (altered mental status, unresolved diarrhea, and a lung finding) requiring a unifying explanation, and used the acute (rather than subacute) tempo to favor an infectious over a neoplastic cause for the lung opacity. He proposed a pulmonary-GI syndrome differential prioritizing Legionella (recommending CK, AST, and ALT, consistent with the patient's hyponatremia) and Mycoplasma (typically with other extrapulmonary features), judging classic bacterial (S. pneumoniae) and viral (influenza) pneumonias less likely given the overall picture; he sent urinary Legionella and urinary pneumococcal antigen testing and held off on empiric antibiotics for the time being, reasoning there was no acute instability mandating immediate treatment and that antibiotics could theoretically worsen GI symptoms via disruption of the gut microbiota.

Rabih generalized this into a practical mental checklist for diarrhea: test for intraluminal pathogens; ask about recent antibiotic exposure (C. difficile risk); ask about travel (raising Salmonella, Shigella, or Campylobacter probability); assess for immunocompromise (HIV, IBD); and, critically, consider whether an extraintestinal focus of disease might be the true primary process, with the diarrhea itself a secondary consequence — citing recent personal experience with severe GI symptoms as an extraintestinal manifestation of a respiratory viral illness. He noted he would likely deprioritize the lingular finding clinically unless the patient developed cough or hypoxia, but that it would gain significant weight the moment stool studies returned unrevealing.

Later that same day, the patient developed fever and sweats; a repeat chest X-ray showed frank pneumonia, and a cough emerged. Stool C. difficile testing had already returned negative, and comprehensive stool pathogen testing ultimately returned entirely negative as well; the urinary Legionella antigen was sent as a send-out test with an anticipated several-day turnaround. At the bedside on day three, a targeted environmental history revealed a window air conditioning unit in the patient's bedroom — a classic Legionella exposure risk via standing or recirculating water. The Legionella test ultimately returned positive; the patient was switched from empiric ceftriaxone (started by the admitting intern) to azithromycin and improved. Both hosts emphasized reframing Legionella as a systemic disease with a pulmonary predilection rather than a pneumonia syndrome per se — a predilection that in this patient's case manifested initially and predominantly through diarrhea and confusion, with pneumonia only becoming apparent later in the course.

Uncle Bob then briefly presented a second, related case from the following week: a man in his 40s who developed a flu-like illness during recent overseas travel, from which everyone else in his travel group recovered except him; he developed worsening diarrhea progressing to fecal and urinary incontinence, severe confusion, and shortness of breath, with a chest X-ray confirming pneumonia. Because of the travel and hotel-stay context, Legionella was empirically suspected and treated, and confirmed positive; this patient was notably sicker than the first, bordering on requiring ICU-level care. Reza used this second case to clarify a key epidemiologic point: Legionella risk is not truly about the travel destination itself, but about exposure to standing water within a closed-loop recirculating system (hotel water systems, some hospital water systems), which provides an ideal growth environment for the organism — citing his own institution's documented water-system contamination as a real-world illustration of the same principle.

Both hosts closed by emphasizing that maintaining an open diagnostic mind — being willing to revisit a previously reassuring workup, repeat imaging, and continue reassessing as a patient's clinical course evolves — was the true teaching point of the case, independent of whether the eventual diagnosis turned out to be common or unusual; the value of the underlying clinical reasoning process holds regardless of the final answer.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Legionella pulmonary-GI syndrome illness script: a patient develops prominent diarrhea and altered mental status, with pulmonary findings initially subtle, incidental, or entirely absent, before frank pneumonia and fever emerge later in the course; supporting systemic clues include hyponatremia and elevated transaminases/CK, and the true epidemiologic risk factor is exposure to a closed-loop water system rather than travel destination per se.

The case models disciplined avoidance of premature closure: an initially reassuring, well-explained presentation (volume-depleted gastroenteritis causing mild encephalopathy) is correctly not treated as fully resolved once the patient's trajectory diverges from the expected course, even without a single dramatic new finding — the diagnostic "door" is kept open based on accumulating non-specific concern (persistent leukocytosis, clinical worsening, a previously dismissed incidental imaging finding) rather than any one decisive data point.

## Teaching Discussion

The central teaching point is the reframing of Legionella from a "pneumonia" to a systemic disease with a pulmonary predilection that can manifest predominantly through GI and constitutional symptoms, with pulmonary findings sometimes delayed or radiographically silent early in the course — a reframe that meaningfully changes when and how the diagnosis should be considered.

A second teaching point is the discipline of not prematurely closing a diagnostic workup simply because an initial explanation is plausible and partially supported by the data — Uncle Bob's "I couldn't put my finger on it" instinct, and the decision to keep re-evaluating the patient's trajectory rather than accepting an incomplete resolution, is presented as a skill built through experience rather than a single identifiable red flag.

A third teaching point is epidemiologic: Legionella risk tracks with exposure to standing or recirculating water in closed-loop systems, not with travel to a particular destination — a distinction that changes what exposure history is actually worth eliciting from a patient.
