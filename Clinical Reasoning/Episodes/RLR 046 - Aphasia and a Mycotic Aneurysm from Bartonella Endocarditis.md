## Pearls

- **[[Localization × Time Course|Neuro DDx = time course × localization]] — and "[[Illness Tempo|tempo is queen]]."** Sudden isolated [[Aphasia|aphasia]] localizes to the dominant (usually left) **language cortex** and, by its hyperacute onset, prioritizes **[[Stroke|stroke]]**; isolated aphasia (or even isolated vertigo, confusion, or a thalamic/inferior-division MCA syndrome) is *fair game* for stroke, so "if it might be acute neurology, call — and check the fingerstick glucose."
- **White on a non-contrast CT is [[Intraparenchymal Hemorrhage|blood until proven otherwise]] — gauge it by density.** Most masses/tumors are *hypodense* (you're seeing edema); a hypercellular tumor like lymphoma can be mildly hyperdense, but only **frank blood** reaches the density seen here. Then work the **blood-vs-vessel schema**: a problem with the *blood* ([[Coagulopathy|coagulopathy]], trauma) or the *vessel* ([[Vasculopathy|vasculopathy]]/[[Vasculitis|vasculitis]], aneurysm, or a **[[Hemorrhagic Conversion|tumor that hemorrhaged]]**).
- **Location breaks the [[Base Rate|base rate]].** The great majority of spontaneous [[Intraparenchymal Hemorrhage|IPH]] is *deep* (basal ganglia/thalamus) from **hypertensive small-vessel disease**; a **cortical/lobar** bleed — especially in a *young, normotensive* patient — is unusual enough that even full vascular risk factors wouldn't explain it, redirecting to aneurysm/AVM, **[[Reversible Cerebral Vasoconstriction Syndrome|RCVS]]**, **[[Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy|amyloid angiopathy]]**, or a **[[Cortical Vein Thrombosis|venous]]** cause (edema out of proportion to the clot, and "[[Could This Be Venous|could this be venous?]]").
- **A [[CT Angiography|CTA]] is one picture in time; a [[Digital Subtraction Angiography|catheter angiogram]] is a video.** When the CTA/MRI are negative but the hemorrhage is unexplained in a young patient, escalate to **digital subtraction angiography**, which watches contrast move through arterial and venous phases and can reveal what a single snapshot misses — here a **tiny distal (M4) aneurysm**.
- **Honor the [[Law of Proportionality|law of proportionality]] — don't [[Search Satisficing|stop at the first finding]].** A tiny distal aneurysm is too small to explain a 3×3×3 cm bleed, and its location is wrong for a **berry aneurysm** (which cluster around the circle of Willis) — but *right* for a **[[Mycotic Aneurysm|mycotic (infectious) aneurysm]]**, which favors distal vessels and has a friable, rupture-prone wall. The coiled aneurysm "could have been the end of the case"; proportionality kept it open.
- **Let the *lack* of systemic features filter [[Infective Endocarditis|endocarditis]].** A distal mycotic aneurysm points to endocarditis, but an **afebrile, systemically well** patient with **severe [[Aortic Regurgitation|aortic regurgitation]]** who is still *compensated and alive* argues for a **subacute, [[Culture-Negative Endocarditis|culture-negative]]** process — and in the right epidemiology (San Francisco, no cat/farm exposure) that means **[[Bartonella quintana|Bartonella quintana]]**, the body-louse "five-day (quintan) fever."

## Problem Representation

A 49-year-old man, well the previous night, presents with the sudden inability to speak — awake and alert with a mixed expressive and receptive aphasia and no other clear focal deficit, localizing to the dominant (left) language cortex with a hyperacute tempo that prioritizes stroke. Last-seen-normal was the night before (outside the acute reperfusion window), vital signs and fingerstick are normal, and non-contrast CT shows a 3×3×3 cm lobar (left frontoparietal, subcortical) hemorrhage with surrounding edema — a spontaneous intraparenchymal hemorrhage in a young, normotensive patient whose cortical location breaks the base rate for hypertensive small-vessel bleeding. Coagulation studies, toxicology, and HIV are negative, and CTA, CT-venogram, and MRI exclude an aneurysm, vascular malformation, venous thrombosis, and underlying mass — until digital subtraction angiography reveals a tiny distal (M4) MCA aneurysm. Applying the law of proportionality (too small a lesion, too distal a location for a berry aneurysm), the team pursues a mycotic aneurysm, and echocardiography shows a mobile aortic-valve vegetation with severe aortic regurgitation in an afebrile, compensated patient — a subacute, culture-negative infective endocarditis. With negative cultures, elevated inflammatory markers, and no cat/farm exposure, the diagnosis is **Bartonella quintana endocarditis** (serology-confirmed), managed with aneurysm coiling, empiric antibiotics, and valve surgery.

## Case Narrative

Guest **Aaron Berkowitz** — neurologist, educator, and the source of the "neuro DDx = time course × localization" mantra the hosts invoke constantly — presents a **49-year-old man** found by a friend unable to speak and brought in by 911; he had been fine the night before. He is **awake, alert, attentive, and visibly frustrated**, getting out only fragments (a sound, a piece of profanity, "man"), unable to reliably follow commands like "close your eyes" except by imitation, with no other obvious focal deficit.

Reza takes "pathology with expression" globally rather than anchoring on [[Dysarthria|dysarthria]], and reaches for Berkowitz's framework: **[[Localization × Time Course|neuro DDx = time course × localization]]**. The tempo — a sudden inability to express oneself that brought him straight in — is **hyperacute to acute**, and the localization is the **[[Aphasia|language center]]**: reception seems less affected than expression, placing a lesion in the dominant (in a right-hander, usually **left**) hemisphere's language cortex. He runs the full [[Anatomical Approach|anatomical ladder]] (cortex → brainstem → cranial-nerve nuclei → junction → muscle) and clears the **oral cavity** (dentures/aligners can mimic dysarthria) and intoxication before concluding this is **true neuroanatomical pathology**; and he closes with the rule to always ask *what else* might be abnormal, hard here because the patient can't fully cooperate. Because it's hyperacute, he prioritizes a **[[Stroke|vascular]]** cause (ischemic or hemorrhagic). Berkowitz affirms the expressive/receptive split — noting the pearl that those categories "crystallize" in *chronic* recovery and are far messier acutely — and that the suddenness made them most worried about **stroke**.

Rabih would spend a minute establishing the **time course** (a vertical "normal → abnormal" line vs a gentler slope) to set the pretest probability, but activates a **code stroke** regardless (under 24 hours), following the **[[Pump Pipes Plasma|pump / pipes / plasma]]** frame with a fingerstick, blood pressure, and EKG. Berkowitz uses the moment to teach why neurology says "call us for anything acute" — isolated aphasia, isolated vertigo, even isolated confusion (a thalamic stroke, showered emboli, an inferior-division right-MCA stroke) can all be stroke — "better to run and slow down than walk and have to sprint." **Last-seen-normal** was the night before, outside the window for acute intervention, but they run to the scanner. Vitals and glucose are normal, and the **non-contrast CT** shows exactly where Reza localized: a **3×3×3 cm, relatively well-circumscribed hyperdensity** in the **left frontoparietal** region, subcortical but not deep, with surrounding edema — and, by *density*, this is **[[Hemorrhage|blood]]**, not a mass (most masses are hypodense from edema; even hypercellular lymphoma isn't as dense as frank blood).

"**[[Illness Tempo|Tempo is queen]]**," Reza says — and white on a non-con CT is **[[Intraparenchymal Hemorrhage|blood until proven otherwise]]**. He reframes the problem as a **49-year-old with sudden expressive aphasia and a lobar IPH**, and asks *why do we bleed?* — the **blood-vs-vessel schema**: a problem with the **blood** ([[Coagulopathy|coagulopathy]] — platelets, INR, PTT, though a brain bleed is an odd *first* manifestation; or **trauma**, sometimes occult) or with the **vessel** ([[Vasculopathy|vasculopathy]]/[[Vasculitis|vasculitis]] — including [[Scurvy|scurvy]], collagen-vascular disease like [[Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome|Ehlers-Danlos]]/[[Marfan Syndrome|Marfan]], or a CNS-tropic vasculitis; or a **[[Hemorrhagic Conversion|tumor that outgrew its supply and bled]]**). He even flags the missing headache — *do we truly know there's none?* — with a callback to their recent [[RLR 045 - Recurrent Monoarticular Arthritis and Post-Rituximab Hypogammaglobulinemia|lupus-without-antibodies case]] (absent findings can be absent only because the patient can't report them).

Rabih supplies the case's first decisive move — **breaking the [[Base Rate|base rate]] by location**. The great majority of spontaneous [[Intraparenchymal Hemorrhage|IPH]] is *deep* (hypertensive small-vessel disease of the basal ganglia/thalamus); a bleed **this cortical** is unusual enough that *even if* the patient had every vascular risk factor, the location wouldn't fit — so he prioritizes an **aneurysm**, a **[[Vasculopathy|vasculopathy]]/vasospasm ([[Reversible Cerebral Vasoconstriction Syndrome|RCVS]])**, **[[Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy|amyloid angiopathy]]**, and — "most importantly, because it's so often missed" — a **[[Cortical Vein Thrombosis|venous]] cause with secondary hemorrhage**. He is also struck that the patient is **not hypertensive** (unusual for a brain bleed) and that the **edema is out of proportion** to the clot (a venous clue). Berkowitz is delighted — young, cortical, edema-heavy: exactly the profile where you must think *venous*, easily missed on CTA unless you scrutinize the venous phase — and adds the taught causes (warfarin hemorrhages, once nightly, now rare with the NOACs).

The workup then goes **negative across the board**: normal coags/CBC, negative tox (including cocaine) and HIV; a **CTA with a CT-venogram** (a "brilliant resident" adding the venous phase) shows **no aneurysm, malformation, or venous thrombosis**; and **MRI shows no mass**. Left with an unexplained hemorrhage, the hosts refuse to stop. Rabih reasons "purely analogically" that a [[CT Angiography|CTA]] is a *single snapshot* and escalates; Reza asks the "medical-student" question of whether iodinated contrast is even safe over a fresh bleed (it is — the vessel tamponades; Berkowitz teaches the **[[Spot Sign|spot sign]]** of active extravasation as an aside). Both land on a **[[Digital Subtraction Angiography|catheter angiogram]]** — a *video*, watching contrast fill from arterial to venous phase — and Rabih, recalling a Berkowitz VMR on how hard **[[Dural AV Fistula|AV fistulas]]** are to see, urges "let's all look at the screen together." The angiogram finds a **tiny distal (M4) MCA aneurysm** near the hemorrhage, which is **coiled** as the presumed culprit — "but that's not the final diagnosis."

Reza applies the **[[Law of Proportionality|law of proportionality]]**: is a *tiny* aneurysm enough to explain a **3×3×3 cm** bleed, and is that **distal** location even right for an aneurysm? Berry aneurysms cluster around the **circle of Willis**; an **M4** (fourth-order MCA branch, "way out there") aneurysm is wrong for that — but exactly where **[[Mycotic Aneurysm|mycotic (infectious) aneurysms]]** live. He names the reasoning explicitly: his instinct would be to take the coiled aneurysm and declare victory, but proportionality keeps the case open. Rabih adds the key management principle for any aneurysm — *rule out a systemic disease seeding the brain*, the most important being **infection** ([[Infective Endocarditis|endocarditis]]), then vasculitis (infectious/autoimmune) and cancer ([[Intravascular Lymphoma|intravascular lymphoma]]). An **echocardiogram** clinches the pivot: a **mobile aortic-valve vegetation with severe [[Aortic Regurgitation|aortic regurgitation]]**.

Now the *lack of systemic features* becomes the filter — the same discipline as the cortical-location break. Reza's [[Infective Endocarditis|endocarditis]] schema: confirm it's a **vegetation** (not a mass); **left-sided** here (arterial embolization; right-sided reaches the arterial tree only via a **[[Paradoxical Embolism|PFO]]** or because the organisms are small enough to bypass the lung filter); and **infective vs [[Nonbacterial Thrombotic Endocarditis|non-infective]]** ([[Marantic Endocarditis|marantic]] with malignancy, [[Libman-Sacks Endocarditis|Libman-Sacks]] with lupus). Get **three blood cultures**; ~98% of IE is common bacteria (staph, strep) — but an **afebrile, systemically well** man with **severe AR** who is nonetheless *compensated and alive* argues **against** an acute staph process and **toward** a **subacute, [[Culture-Negative Endocarditis|culture-negative]]** one. Rabih reinforces it: the discordance between severe AR and a well patient marks a **subacute-to-chronic** disease, favoring culture-negative causes. The culture-negative shortlist — **[[Bartonella|Bartonella]]** and **[[Coxiella|Coxiella]]** (± [[HACEK Organisms|HACEK]], [[Syphilis|syphilis]]) — narrows on epidemiology: **no cat exposure** (against [[Bartonella henselae|B. henselae]]), **no farm exposure** (against Coxiella), but this is **San Francisco**. Both hosts bet **[[Bartonella quintana|Bartonella quintana]]** — the **body-louse**, urban/homelessness-associated "**quintan** (five-day) fever." Berkowitz confirms: **Bartonella henselae *and* quintana serologies are strongly positive**, and ID favors **quintana** by exposure and local base rate. The aneurysm was coiled, empiric antibiotics started, and the aortic valve replaced; the patient did well.

Berkowitz frames the whole case as a **"fairy tale in reverse"** — the wizard who grows *younger* through the story. Endocarditis usually reaches neurology *forward*: a febrile, unwell patient with known IE has a stroke or hemorrhage and neurology is consulted about timing or anticoagulation. Here it ran **backward** — a bread-and-butter hemorrhage in a young, normotensive, non-traumatic patient (worry: vascular malformation; think **venous**, per his mentor **Steve Feske's** "[[Could This Be Venous|for every stroke or hemorrhage, ask: could this be venous?]]") → a **weirdly distal** aneurysm → "what causes distal aneurysms? *mycotic*" → an echo that was "not hiding once we went looking." The humbling notes: the disease was so **neurologically dominant and systemically quiescent** that he sat on a *neurology* primary service with severe AR the neurologists' rudimentary exam missed (the cardiologists found every sign); and the aphasia itself was a profound **barrier** — a patient who couldn't report fever, headache, or history at all.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Localization × Time Course|Time course × localization]] and "[[Illness Tempo|tempo is queen]]."** Aphasia is localized to the dominant language cortex and, by hyperacute tempo, routed to stroke; the reminder that acute aphasia is "messier" than the textbook expressive/receptive split (which crystallizes in recovery) keeps the localization honest.
- **[[Intraparenchymal Hemorrhage|Blood-until-proven-otherwise]] and the blood-vs-vessel schema.** Density reads the CT as **blood**, and "why do we bleed?" organizes the differential into a **blood** problem ([[Coagulopathy|coagulopathy]], trauma) vs a **vessel** problem ([[Vasculopathy|vasculopathy]]/[[Vasculitis|vasculitis]], aneurysm, tumor with [[Hemorrhagic Conversion|hemorrhagic conversion]]).
- **[[Base Rate|Base-rate]] break by *location*.** Deep IPH = hypertensive small-vessel; a **cortical/lobar** bleed in a young normotensive patient is off the base rate no matter the risk factors, redirecting to aneurysm/AVM, **[[Reversible Cerebral Vasoconstriction Syndrome|RCVS]]**, **[[Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy|amyloid]]**, and **[[Cortical Vein Thrombosis|venous]]** causes — the same *break-the-symmetry* move later reused on endocarditis.
- **"[[Could This Be Venous|Could this be venous?]]" (Feske).** Edema out of proportion, cortical location, and a young patient flag a venous etiology that CTA misses without the venous phase — with talking to radiology and hunting **[[Dural AV Fistula|AV-fistula]] flow voids** as the practical follow-through.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Test interpretation]]: snapshot vs video.** A [[CT Angiography|CTA]] is one instant; a [[Digital Subtraction Angiography|catheter angiogram]] watches arterial→venous filling and finds what a snapshot misses — plus the safety of CTA over a fresh bleed (vessel tamponade) and the **[[Spot Sign|spot sign]]** of active extravasation.
- **[[Law of Proportionality|Law of proportionality]] + [[Search Satisficing|not stopping at the first finding]].** The coiled distal aneurysm "could have been the end," but it's too small for a 3-cm bleed and too distal for a berry aneurysm — proportionality forces the pursuit that uncovers the **[[Mycotic Aneurysm|mycotic]]** mechanism.
- **[[Infective Endocarditis|Endocarditis]] schema and the *lack of systemic features* as a filter.** Vegetation-vs-mass, left-vs-right sided, infective-vs-[[Nonbacterial Thrombotic Endocarditis|non-infective]], and culture-positive-vs-negative — with an afebrile, compensated patient (severe AR yet alive) marking a **subacute, [[Culture-Negative Endocarditis|culture-negative]]** process, epidemiologically **[[Bartonella quintana|B. quintana]]**.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Humility]]: the fairy tale in reverse.** Discovering endocarditis *backward* (hemorrhage → aneurysm → valve → bug), a systemically quiescent disease that hid on a neurology service, and the barrier of an aphasic patient who could report nothing — a case designed to be missed forward.

## Teaching Discussion

- Localize difficulty speaking by time course × localization: separate dysarthria (a mechanical/oral problem) from aphasia (a language-cortex problem), localize aphasia to the dominant hemisphere, and let a hyperacute tempo prioritize stroke — remembering that isolated aphasia, vertigo, or confusion can all be stroke, so activate the code and check the glucose.
- Read white on a non-contrast CT as blood until proven otherwise, using density to separate blood from a (usually hypodense) mass, then work the blood-vs-vessel schema: coagulopathy and trauma versus vasculopathy/vasculitis, aneurysm, and tumor with hemorrhagic conversion.
- Break the base rate by location: deep intraparenchymal hemorrhage is hypertensive small-vessel disease, but a cortical/lobar bleed — especially in a young, normotensive patient — should redirect to aneurysm/AVM, RCVS, amyloid angiopathy, and venous causes, regardless of vascular risk factors.
- Ask "could this be venous?" for every stroke or hemorrhage: edema out of proportion to the clot and a cortical location suggest cortical vein or sinus thrombosis, which requires the venous phase (CT-venogram/MRV) and often a careful second read with radiology.
- Escalate from CTA (a single snapshot) to digital subtraction angiography (a dynamic video) when a young patient's hemorrhage remains unexplained; it can reveal small or distal lesions a static study misses.
- Apply the law of proportionality and resist premature closure: a tiny distal aneurysm cannot explain a large bleed and is anatomically wrong for a berry aneurysm — distal aneurysms are the signature of mycotic (infectious) aneurysms, which are friable and rupture-prone, so pursue a systemic source.
- Work up an aneurysm/mycotic aneurysm for endocarditis, and let the presence or absence of systemic features refine it: an afebrile, compensated patient with severe valvular regurgitation who remains well points to a subacute, culture-negative endocarditis — Bartonella (quintana in urban/louse exposure; henselae with cats) and Coxiella (farm/Q fever) — confirmed by serology when cultures are negative.
