## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> This recording is, chronologically, the earliest Neurology Virtual Morning Report captured in this vault — **Aaron Berkowitz's** on-air introduction to the Clinical Problem Solvers community and his framing of the **[[Neurophobia|end-neurophobia]]** mission, built here around his "**[[Localization × Time Course|E=mc² of neurology]]**" schema. A 36-year-old woman with two months of recurrent neck pain wakes, takes her usual cyclobenzaprine and naproxen, and an hour later develops abrupt right arm and leg weakness and numbness with a sense of falling to the right. The discussion works the case in two axes — abrupt *tempo* (vascular, toxic-metabolic, seizure/Todd's paralysis, migraine) and *localization* (the corticospinal tract's single-sided course argues for one lesion, either left brain/brainstem or right spinal cord) — before a single cranial-nerve finding, left tongue deviation, flips the case to a "crossed" brainstem sign: contralateral body weakness plus ipsilateral CN XII palsy. Using the **[[Rule of Four (Brainstem Localization)|Rule of Four]]** (CN 9–12 = medulla), this localizes to a left **medial medullary (Dejerine) syndrome**, confirmed on MRI as a left medial medullary infarct — caused, on CT angiogram, by high-grade stenosis of the *right* proximal vertebral artery, itself attributed to months of the patient repeatedly cracking her own neck for pain relief.

## Pearls

- **Berkowitz's "E=mc²" schema — [[Localization × Time Course|differential diagnosis = localization × time course]].** Sudden ("abrupt") tempo narrows the etiology list to vascular (stroke/TIA), toxic-metabolic, seizure (including an unwitnessed seizure's post-ictal Todd's paralysis), and migraine (aura can precede the headache, or the headache may never come); localization then asks *where* within the nervous system.
- **One-sided face-sparing arm-and-leg weakness together implicates one CNS lesion, not a peripheral one.** Because the corticospinal tract runs as a single bundle from cortex through corona radiata, internal capsule, and brainstem before crossing at the cervicomedullary junction, right arm+leg weakness together points to either the left brain/brainstem (above the crossing) or the right spinal cord (below it) — not an isolated root, plexus, or nerve problem.
- **A single cranial-nerve finding can re-localize an entire case.** Left tongue deviation (ipsilateral CN XII weakness) paired with contralateral (right) body weakness is a classic "crossed" brainstem sign; applying the **[[Rule of Four (Brainstem Localization)|Rule of Four]]** (CN 9–12 = medulla) localizes the lesion to the left medulla — specifically **medial medullary (Dejerine) syndrome**, brainstem's less-famous sibling to lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome.
- **Cervical artery dissection illness script: neck pain, often with minor or unremembered trauma — including vigorous chiropractic manipulation or self-manipulation — followed by abrupt stroke-like symptoms.** Dissection and arrhythmia are the leading causes of stroke under age 50. Classically the stroke follows the onset of neck pain within 24 hours, with an outer window of about a week; this patient's pain had been present for two months, an atypical gap the group flagged rather than let rule out the diagnosis.
- **Posterior-circulation collaterals can defy simple ipsilateral vessel-to-infarct logic.** This patient's *left* medial medullary infarct arose from stenosis of the *right* vertebral artery — a reminder that vertebrobasilar anatomy (paired vertebrals feeding a shared basilar and anterior spinal system) can produce lateralization that looks paradoxical at first glance.
- **The antithrombotic choice for cervical dissection is a genuine open question.** The CADISS trial found aspirin roughly equivalent to anticoagulation for secondary stroke prevention after dissection — though the trial was underpowered by the fact that recurrent stroke after a dissection-related stroke is itself rare (~1%); many clinicians still favor short-term anticoagulation in young patients given the presumed embolic mechanism.

## Problem Representation

A 36-year-old woman with two months of recurrent, atraumatic bilateral posterior neck pain (managed with as-needed naproxen and cyclobenzaprine) wakes with a resurgence of her usual neck pain, takes both medications, and roughly an hour later develops abrupt-onset right arm and leg weakness and numbness with a subjective sense of falling to the right when walking. She has no headache, vision change, dysarthria, or dysphagia, no past medical or family history, no tobacco/alcohol/drug use, and works night shifts as a security guard. Exam localizes to a left medial brainstem lesion — 4/5 right arm and leg weakness with diminished right-sided sensation, plus left tongue deviation (ipsilateral CN XII involvement) — confirmed by MRI as a left medial medullary infarct, caused by high-grade stenosis of the proximal right vertebral artery on CT angiography, most consistent with a vertebral artery dissection related to two months of repeated self-manipulation ("cracking") of her neck.

## Case Narrative

This is the first Neurology Virtual Morning Report captured in this vault's build order — the recording opens with Aaron Berkowitz's introduction to the Clinical Problem Solvers community (Johns Hopkins medical school, a Harvard-system neurology residency and chief year, recently relocated from Boston to Los Angeles) and his framing of the series' mission to **[[Neurophobia|end neurophobia]]**, a term coined by Ralph Josefowitz in a 1994 *Archives of Neurology* article for the fear of clinical neurology that follows when teaching fails to connect basic neuroscience to the bedside. Berkowitz's antidote, restated across this vault's neurology episodes, is his "**[[Localization × Time Course|E=mc² of neurology]]**": differential diagnosis = localization × time course. Neurology, he argues, uniquely allows a clinician to localize a lesion to within centimeters using history and exam alone, after which the differential for *what* is happening follows naturally from *where* it is happening.

**Chief complaint: abrupt-onset right arm and leg weakness and numbness.** Working from the tempo axis first, the discussants (Kiara, presenting from Peru; Tahir, a fourth-year student at Baylor) generate a sudden-onset differential: vascular (stroke or TIA), toxic-metabolic (intoxication), and — prompted by Berkowitz — a broader "sudden in neurology" list that also includes seizure (an unwitnessed seizure can present as pure post-ictal weakness, a Todd's paralysis) and migraine (the aura can mimic a stroke before any headache appears, or the headache never comes at all). On the localization axis, right arm-and-leg weakness together is worked through the corticospinal tract's anatomy — precentral gyrus, corona radiata, internal capsule, brainstem, crossing at the cervicomedullary junction, then spinal cord to root/plexus/nerve — concluding that a single right-sided arm-and-leg deficit points to either the left brain/brainstem (above the crossing) or the right spinal cord (below it), not an isolated peripheral process.

**History reframes the case as subacute-then-abrupt.** The fuller history reveals two months of recurrent, atraumatic bilateral posterior neck pain, managed with naproxen and cyclobenzaprine; on the morning of presentation she woke with her usual pain, took both medications as usual, and about an hour later developed the abrupt weakness, numbness, and a sensation of falling to her right side while walking — with no vision change, dysarthria, dysphagia, headache, nausea, or vomiting, and an unremarkable past medical, family, and social history apart from night-shift work as a security guard. The group briefly entertains a slow-growing structural process (an expanding spinal or paraspinal mass finally decompensating) but sets it aside as poorly explained by two months of pain in a non-immunosuppressed patient, and instead converges on **cervical artery dissection**: age under 50 with no vascular risk factors, dissection and arrhythmia being the two leading causes of stroke in that age group, and a plausible link between the chronic neck pain and an eventual embolic or hemodynamic stroke. Berkowitz notes the atypical timing explicitly — most dissection-related strokes occur within 24 hours of pain onset, with an outer tail out to about a week, making a two-month gap unusual — and floats a possible precipitant: an extra or unusually sedating dose of cyclobenzaprine on a background of pre-existing vertebral stenosis, dropping perfusion pressure just enough to tip a marginally patent vessel into occlusion.

**Exam: a "crossed" sign relocalizes the lesion.** Vitals are unremarkable, including a blood pressure of 118/78 — notably not elevated, despite Kiara's reasonable prediction that an acute stroke might provoke a compensatory hypertensive response to preserve cerebral perfusion. Cranial nerve exam reveals **left tongue deviation** with an otherwise normal cranial nerve exam. Both discussants immediately recognize this as new information requiring re-localization: a lesion above the neck. Using the **[[Rule of Four (Brainstem Localization)|Rule of Four]]** — CN 1–4 midbrain, 5–8 pons, 9–12 medulla — CN XII localizes to the medulla. Because the corticospinal tract has not yet crossed at that level while the CN XII nerve/nucleus is already ipsilateral, a single medullary lesion produces **ipsilateral tongue weakness with contralateral body weakness** — here, left tongue and right body — a classic brainstem "crossed" sign. This is **medial medullary (Dejerine) syndrome**, the less-famous counterpart to lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome, involving the medial medulla's corticospinal tract, medial lemniscus, and CN XII nucleus/fascicles, vascularized by the vertebral artery and anterior spinal artery. The remainder of the exam — 4/5 right arm and leg strength with diminished right-sided sensation — fits the same lesion via the medial lemniscus, before it too has crossed.

**One test, then confirmation.** Asked to commit to a single confirmatory study, the group proposes a CT angiogram; Berkowitz agrees, noting the practical sequence would be a non-contrast head CT first (to exclude hemorrhage, reasonable given the patient is awake and conversant), then CT or MR angiography of the head and neck for the vessels — flagging that a brainstem infarct this small might not show on CT at all, and that even MRI is imperfectly sensitive for small brainstem strokes. The actual results confirm the clinical localization precisely: **MRI showed restricted diffusion in the left medial medulla**; **CTA of the head was normal**, but **CTA of the neck showed pathological narrowing and high-grade stenosis of the proximal (V1 segment) right vertebral artery** — a laterality mismatch (left-sided infarct, right-sided vessel disease) that Berkowitz flags as a reminder that vertebrobasilar collateral anatomy does not always produce simple ipsilateral correspondence between diseased vessel and infarct side.

**Etiology and treatment.** Asked directly, the patient had no history of chiropractic care or trauma — but disclosed that she had been cracking her own neck frequently over the preceding two months because it relieved her pain, the presumed mechanical precipitant for the dissection. She was treated with aspirin and a heparin bolus, transferred to a higher level of care, improved, and was discharged after two days without need for endovascular or surgical intervention. Berkowitz uses the case to raise the CADISS trial, which found aspirin and anticoagulation roughly equivalent for secondary stroke prevention after cervical artery dissection — a result some read as license to use either agent, while others note the trial enrolled patients too late to settle the question and point out that recurrent stroke after a dissection-related stroke is itself rare (~1%), which limits the trial's power to detect a real difference; many clinicians, given the presumed embolic mechanism and the safety of anticoagulation in young patients, still favor a period of anticoagulation.

**Synthesis and teaching wrap.** The closing discussant reprises the case through the E=mc² lens: vascular causes are triaged first because they are the most time-sensitive, with toxic-metabolic, seizure/post-ictal states, and even headache-free migraine kept on the initial sudden-onset list; localization proceeds from peripheral-versus-central to brain-versus-spinal-cord; the neck pain immediately raised cervical artery dissection as an illness script even before trauma was confirmed; and the single hypoglossal finding — via the Rule of Four and the ipsilateral-cranial-nerve/contralateral-body crossed-sign logic — completed the localization to medial medullary syndrome. Berkowitz closes with a brief aside on cerebral perfusion pressure (mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure) as the physiologic rationale behind Kiara's blood-pressure hypothesis, even though this particular patient's pressure was not elevated, and a closing plea, not entirely in jest: don't crack your neck.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Localization × Time Course|Localization × time course]] as the master script**, restated from the series' earliest recorded appearance in this vault: fixing tempo (abrupt) and location (via the corticospinal tract's single-sided anatomy) narrows an initially broad differential before any imaging is obtained.
- **Cervical artery dissection illness script.** Neck pain, often with trivial or unremembered trauma (chiropractic manipulation, or here, self-manipulation), followed by an abrupt focal deficit; dissection and arrhythmia are the leading causes of stroke under 50. The group's willingness to keep dissection on the differential despite the atypical two-month latency — rather than discarding it because it didn't fit the classic 24-hour-to-one-week window — is an explicit act of **[[Diagnostic Humility|diagnostic humility]]**: hold the textbook pattern loosely when the rest of the picture still fits.
- **Crossed brainstem signs as an illness script.** Ipsilateral cranial nerve deficit plus contralateral body findings is taught here as a reliably localizing pattern to the brainstem, cashed out precisely via the **[[Rule of Four (Brainstem Localization)|Rule of Four]]** into medial medullary (Dejerine) syndrome.
- **Revising rather than anchoring.** Kiara's early exam-based hypothesis leaned toward a spinal cord lesion; when the cranial nerve exam produced a new, higher-yield data point (tongue deviation), the group revised cleanly to a brainstem localization rather than forcing the new finding to fit the old hypothesis — the opposite of anchoring.
- **A falsified prediction treated as calibration, not failure.** Kiara's physiologic reasoning that acute stroke should provoke a compensatory hypertensive response (to preserve cerebral perfusion pressure) was sound in principle, but this patient's blood pressure was normal — Berkowitz revisits the physiology (cerebral perfusion pressure = mean arterial pressure − intracranial pressure) afterward rather than treating the miss as an error, modeling **[[Test Interpretation|pretest calibration]]** applied to a clinical prediction rather than a lab test.
- **Explicit dual-process framing.** Berkowitz names Kahneman's "thinking fast and slow" directly: an abrupt right-sided weakness triggers a fast pattern-match to stroke, but the group is repeatedly asked to slow down and weigh what's on "the other side of the scale" as each new detail (the neck pain, the cranial nerve finding, the vessel-side mismatch) arrives — a recurring, idiosyncratic teaching device worth tracking as its own candidate concept.

## Teaching Discussion

- Sudden-onset neurologic deficits deserve a short, explicit tempo-based differential before localization even begins: vascular (stroke/hemorrhage), toxic-metabolic, seizure (including post-ictal Todd's paralysis after an unwitnessed seizure), and migraine (aura can precede or occur without headache).
- One-sided weakness spanning more than one limb, without a clear peripheral (root/plexus/nerve) pattern, should be localized centrally first — using the corticospinal tract's single continuous course from cortex to its crossing at the cervicomedullary junction to decide whether the lesion is above (contralateral brain/brainstem) or below (ipsilateral spinal cord) that crossing.
- A cervical artery dissection illness script — neck pain with minor or unremembered trauma, sometimes including chiropractic care or vigorous self-manipulation, followed by an abrupt stroke-like deficit — is worth invoking even when the timing between pain onset and stroke falls outside the classic 24-hour-to-one-week window; the textbook window describes the majority, not a rule-out threshold.
- A single, well-chosen cranial nerve finding can relocalize an entire case: ipsilateral cranial nerve involvement with contralateral body findings is a reliable brainstem "crossed" sign, and the Rule of Four (cranial nerves 1–4 midbrain, 5–8 pons, 9–12 medulla) converts that single data point into a specific level.
- Medial medullary (Dejerine) syndrome deserves equal billing with its better-known sibling, lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome — both are testable, both follow directly from brainstem vascular and cranial nerve anatomy.
- Vertebrobasilar collateral anatomy can decouple the side of vessel disease from the side of the resulting infarct; don't be thrown when CTA laterality doesn't match the clinical/MRI localization at first glance.
- The choice between antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy after a dissection-related stroke remains genuinely unsettled in the literature (CADISS trial), and clinical practice reasonably varies based on presumed mechanism, patient age, and bleeding risk rather than a single clear answer.
