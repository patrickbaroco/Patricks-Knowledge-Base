> [!summary] Summary
> A 75-year-old woman with 20 years of diabetes develops sudden binocular horizontal diplopia, worse on right gaze. Exam shows the right eye simply cannot abduct — no ptosis, no fatigability, no other cranial nerve findings — alongside an incidental, unrelated length-dependent sensory polyneuropathy (diminished vibration and depressed ankle reflexes). The discussion works through the full diplopia localization pathway before landing on an **isolated right CN VI (abducens) palsy**, almost certainly a diabetic microvascular nerve infarct, using the "neighborhood sign" argument (the sixth nerve nucleus sits right beside the seventh in the pons) to rule out a brainstem lesion. The polyneuropathy is treated as a *separate* diabetes-related problem rather than folded into one unifying brainstem lesion — an explicit [[Hickam's Dictum]] teaching moment.

## Pearls

- **Isolated CN VI palsy with no facial weakness argues strongly against a brainstem (nuclear/fascicular) lesion.** The abducens nucleus is medial in the pons, and its exiting fascicle loops around the facial (CN VII) nucleus before leaving the brainstem — a "neighborhood sign": a lesion small enough to hit only CN VI and spare CN VII in the brainstem is essentially impossible, so an isolated sixth palsy points to a lesion of the nerve itself, outside the brainstem.
- **Two findings in one patient don't have to share one lesion.** A distal, symmetric, length-dependent polyneuropathy (diminished vibration, depressed ankle jerks) and an isolated cranial neuropathy can be two separate manifestations of the same systemic risk factor (here, diabetes) rather than a single anatomic lesion — [[Hickam's Dictum]]: "a patient can have as many diseases as they darn well please."
- **CN VI has the longest intracranial course of any cranial nerve**, which is why it is the classic **false localizing sign** in elevated intracranial pressure — a sixth palsy from high ICP reflects nerve stretch, not a focal lesion at the site of the palsy.
- **Diabetic (microvascular) ocular motor nerve palsies classically spare pupillary and, when CN VI is affected, facial function** — a small-vessel infarct hits the nerve's core (the muscle fibers), sparing peripherally-located fibers (pupillomotor in CN III) and neighboring structures, and typically resolves over weeks to months without treatment.
- **Giant cell arteritis deserves consideration in any new neurologic symptom in a patient over 60**, even an isolated ocular motor palsy without the "classic" headache, jaw claudication, or scalp tenderness — those textbook features are often absent in practice.
- This episode explicitly foreshadowed the possibility that an isolated cranial neuropathy could evolve into a full **[[Miller Fisher Syndrome]]** picture if more cranial nerves and reflex loss appeared over subsequent days — see [[CPS 263 - Diplopia, Numbness, and Miller Fisher Syndrome|CPS 263]], where that is exactly what happened in a different patient.

## Problem Representation

A 75-year-old woman with a 20-year history of diabetes, hypertension, heart failure, and B12-deficiency anemia develops sudden-onset binocular horizontal diplopia, worse on right gaze, without pain, ptosis, fatigability, or other cranial nerve symptoms. Exam localizes the diplopia to failure of right eye abduction with an otherwise normal cranial nerve exam, alongside an incidental distal symmetric sensory polyneuropathy (diminished vibration, depressed Achilles reflex). Brain MRI is unremarkable. The findings localize to an isolated right CN VI (abducens) nerve lesion, most consistent with a diabetic microvascular infarct, with the polyneuropathy representing a separate, unrelated complication of the same underlying diabetes.

## Case Narrative

This episode doubled as a birthday celebration: the case was presented by Gabriela (from Brazil, celebrating her birthday) and discussed by Gabrielle and Valeria ("Vale," also celebrating a birthday that day, jokingly dubbed Gabriela's "twin" for the occasion), with [[Aaron Berkowitz]] guiding the discussion and Maria providing closing teaching points.

Given the chief concern — diplopia — Gabrielle opened with the standard binocular-versus-monocular framework: binocular diplopia resolves when either eye is covered and points to a problem of ocular alignment (weakness of an extraocular muscle from a vascular lesion, myasthenia gravis, or thyroid eye disease), while monocular diplopia persists with one eye covered and points to a refractive/lens problem within that eye. Berkowitz elaborated on thyroid eye disease as a distinct mechanism worth remembering: rather than weakness misaligning the eyes, an inflamed and restricted muscle (classically the inferior rectus) mechanically prevents movement in the opposing direction.

Berkowitz then built out the full **[[Diplopia]]** localization pathway from the eye inward, noting eye movements are among the fastest in the body (up to roughly 700 degrees per second) and must be tightly coordinated to avoid double vision. Six muscles move each eye — four recti (superior, inferior, medial, lateral) and two obliques (superior, inferior) — controlled by three cranial nerves: **CN VI** (lateral rectus, abduction), **CN IV** (superior oblique), and **CN III** (everything else, plus the levator palpebrae and pupillary parasympathetics). Using the rule of four, these nuclei sit in the brainstem: III and IV in the midbrain, VI in the pons. To move the eyes together — for example, looking left requires the left lateral rectus (CN VI) and right medial rectus (CN III) simultaneously — the CN VI nucleus on one side communicates with the contralateral CN III nucleus via the **medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF)**, itself part of a "highway" also connecting CN VIII for the vestibulo-ocular reflex; a lesion here produces internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO). One level higher, the **paramedian pontine reticular formation (PPRF)** commands the ipsilateral CN VI nucleus to look toward that side, and the PPRF itself is commanded by the *contralateral* **frontal eye field (FEF)** — which is why a large hemispheric stroke classically deviates the eyes toward the side of the lesion (away from the resulting hemiparesis). Critically, FEF and PPRF lesions cause a *conjugate* gaze palsy (both eyes move together, just not in one direction) rather than diplopia — true diplopia requires a lesion at or below the level of the MLF: the nuclei, the nerves, the neuromuscular junction, or the extraocular muscles themselves.

**History**: a 75-year-old woman with sudden-onset binocular horizontal diplopia noticed by her son two days prior (he observed her eyes were misaligned), worse on right gaze, without dysphagia, dysarthria, eye pain, red eye, fever, headache, or fatigability. Past medical history: 20 years of diabetes, hypertension, heart failure, B12-deficiency anemia (treatment started five months prior), breast cancer ten years earlier, and GERD. Medications included insulin, empagliflozin, atenolol, furosemide, metformin, B12 supplementation, and omeprazole (relevant as a potential ongoing contributor to B12 malabsorption). No allergies, smoking, or alcohol use; family history noncontributory.

Valeria organized her thinking around tempo: the diplopia was acute but not hyperacute (evolving to noticeable over roughly a day, not seconds), keeping vascular and inflammatory causes on the table, and she raised the possibility of a diabetic ocular motor nerve palsy specifically, wondering how pupil involvement or ptosis would help narrow which nerve was affected. Gabrielle likewise prioritized a vascular etiology given the cardiovascular risk factor burden, and specifically proposed the **MLF** as a candidate localization given the horizontal, gaze-dependent nature of the diplopia. Berkowitz affirmed both directions and added a teaching point about tempo and myasthenia, attributed to one of his mentors: "diplopia is always sudden," because even a slowly progressive process crosses a perceptual threshold at some discrete moment — patients often unconsciously compensate (squinting, head-tilting) until they can't any longer — so an acute onset does not, by itself, exclude myasthenia gravis.

Berkowitz then posed the localizing puzzle directly: if diplopia worsens on right gaze, what could cause that? Valeria proposed a left INO (the left eye fails to adduct on attempted right gaze because it can't receive the signal from the right PPRF/CN VI nucleus across a damaged MLF), with an abducting nystagmus expected in the right eye. Berkowitz confirmed this as one real possibility and used it to clarify the MLF's confusing naming convention: since the crossing fibers spend most of their course on the destination (adducting) side, a "left INO" refers to a lesion affecting left eye adduction — but naming aside, the localization is what matters (the dorsal brainstem, between the medulla and midbrain). He then asked for the two other broad possibilities producing failure to look right: either the right eye fails to abduct (a right lateral rectus or right CN VI problem) or the left eye fails to adduct (a left medial rectus, left CN III, or MLF/INO problem). Gabrielle correctly reasoned that an isolated right CN VI problem was more parsimonious than a partial left CN III palsy, since CN III governs so many functions (pupil, lid, multiple muscles) that a lesion severe enough to affect only adduction while sparing everything else would be unusual.

Given the sudden tempo, the leading hypotheses became: an MLF stroke (if this proved to be an INO), or a diabetic microvascular infarct of CN VI (if the right eye itself failed to abduct) — plus myasthenia and thyroid eye disease remaining live possibilities given the diplopia's gaze-dependence could in principle also reflect a restrictive or fatigable process rather than a discrete nerve lesion.

**Exam**: the right eye was unable to abduct, producing horizontal diplopia worse on right gaze; no ptosis, no fatigability, and no other cranial nerve abnormalities. Additionally, the exam revealed diminished vibration sensation at the toes and tibial tuberosities bilaterally (somewhat asymmetric), an Achilles reflex of 1/4 with all other reflexes 2/4, and an otherwise normal neurologic exam.

With the right eye specifically failing to abduct, the localization narrowed to a right CN VI (or lateral rectus) problem — settling the INO/left-CN-III branch of the differential. Gabrielle then tried to unify this finding with the incidental sensory/reflex findings by proposing a single dorsal brainstem (pontine) lesion broad enough to explain both the CN VI palsy and the sensory changes, reasoning that the brainstem packs sensory, motor, and cranial nerve pathways closely together. Valeria agreed a pontine stroke was plausible for the eye finding but separated it conceptually from the sensory/reflex findings, favoring a distal symmetric diabetic polyneuropathy for those instead, given the length-dependent, bilateral distribution.

Berkowitz sided with Valeria's separation and used the moment to introduce **[[Hickam's Dictum]]** explicitly by name (attributed to one of his professors) as the counterpoint to reflexive Occam's-razor unification: a patient "can have as many diseases as they darn well please." He then explained precisely why a single brainstem lesion was anatomically implausible here: the brainstem is extremely small and densely packed, so a lesion large enough to affect bilateral sensory pathways and reflex arcs while also selectively hitting only the CN VI nucleus (and nothing else nearby) would be a near-impossible pin-sized coincidence. The decisive **"neighborhood sign"** argument: the CN VI nucleus is one of the most medial motor nuclei in the pons (grouped with III, IV, and XII, all sharing multiples-of-a-dozen numbering, per Valeria's mnemonic), and critically, the CN VI fascicle physically loops around the CN VII (facial) nucleus — the internal genu — before exiting the brainstem. This means any brainstem lesion large enough to affect the CN VI nucleus or fascicle would almost certainly also produce facial weakness. Since this patient's facial function was entirely normal, a pontine nuclear or fascicular lesion was excluded, and the localization moved to the CN VI nerve itself, outside the brainstem — either in the subarachnoid space, the cavernous sinus, the orbit, or (favored, given the absence of any other cranial nerve or meningeal signs) simply an isolated microvascular infarct of the nerve, presumed related to the patient's 20-year history of diabetes.

Berkowitz also reviewed CN VI's uniquely long intracranial course as the anatomic basis for its being the classic **false localizing sign** of elevated intracranial pressure, and reiterated (as in prior discussions) that a diabetic microvascular third nerve palsy classically spares the pupil because the infarct affects the nerve's core (motor fibers) while sparing the peripherally-located pupillomotor fibers — contrasting this with a compressive lesion (aneurysm, uncal herniation) that hits those peripheral fibers first and "blows" the pupil while eye movements may still be intact, a genuinely ominous combination. He shared a light anecdote about a nebulizer inadvertently blowing medication into one eye mimicking a blown pupil and triggering an unnecessary herniation panic, and noted a case of neurolymphomatosis (lymphomatous infiltration of a cranial nerve) that similarly spared the pupil.

**Labs and imaging**: HbA1c 8.8% (poorly controlled diabetes); HIV, syphilis, and hepatitis serologies negative; electrolytes normal; brain MRI showed no acute ischemic lesion on diffusion-weighted imaging and no other abnormality — as anticipated, since the sixth nerve is too small to visualize a discrete infarct on standard MRI. ESR/CRP were sent to screen for giant cell arteritis, prompted by a case report Gabriela had read recommending GCA screening even for isolated ocular motor palsies without classic GCA features; results were still pending at the time of discussion.

**Final diagnosis**: an isolated right CN VI palsy, presumed microvascular/diabetic in etiology (the most common identifiable cause of isolated ocular motor palsies, alongside a substantial idiopathic proportion), expected to improve over several months without specific treatment beyond an eye patch for symptomatic relief and optimization of diabetes control. The distal sensory polyneuropathy was attributed separately to longstanding diabetes, with ongoing evaluation for a contributing role from B12 deficiency (potentially perpetuated by omeprazole-associated malabsorption).

Berkowitz closed by half-jokingly floating the possibility that this could be the opening act of a **Miller Fisher syndrome** if further cranial nerves and reflexes became involved over subsequent days — a hypothetical that, notably, plays out for real in a different patient in [[CPS 263 - Diplopia, Numbness, and Miller Fisher Syndrome|CPS 263]]. Maria's closing teaching points reinforced the localization framework and the rule of four (with Berkowitz gently correcting two fine points: CN XI does not actually arise from the brainstem, and CN VIII's afferent pupillary light reflex fibers relay through the midbrain's pretectal nucleus). The discussion closed by revisiting giant cell arteritis as a "don't miss" consideration for any new neurologic symptom — including an isolated ocular motor palsy — in a patient over 60, since classic features like jaw claudication and scalp tenderness are frequently absent in real practice.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

**Isolated CN VI (abducens) palsy** — most commonly microvascular (diabetes, hypertension) or idiopathic; classically resolves over weeks to months; the longest intracranial nerve course of any cranial nerve, making it the classic false localizing sign of elevated ICP; an isolated palsy without other neurologic findings argues against a brainstem, subarachnoid, cavernous sinus, or orbital process, in favor of a simple nerve infarct.

**[[Hickam's Dictum]] (exemplar)** — explicitly invoked by name: rather than forcing the CN VI palsy and the distal polyneuropathy into a single unifying brainstem lesion (which the anatomy makes nearly impossible), the two findings are recognized as separate complications of the same systemic risk factor (diabetes). This is a direct counterpoint to the [[Unifying Diagnosis]] instinct that has driven several other episodes in this series — here, the correct move is to *resist* unification once the anatomy rules it out.

**[[Unifying Diagnosis]] (contrast exemplar)** — Gabrielle's valiant attempt to explain both findings with one pontine lesion illustrates the pull toward parsimony/Occam's razor; the teaching value is in showing precisely how to test and reject that impulse using neuroanatomic "neighborhood sign" reasoning rather than intuition alone.

**[[Diagnostic Humility]]** — screening for giant cell arteritis in an isolated ocular motor palsy despite the absence of classic GCA features (jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, headache), because those features are frequently absent in real-world GCA presentations.

## Teaching Discussion

- **Extraocular muscle/nerve map**: CN VI → lateral rectus (abduction); CN IV → superior oblique; CN III → medial/superior/inferior rectus, inferior oblique, levator palpebrae, and pupillary parasympathetics.
- **Full diplopia localization pathway**, eye-outward: extraocular muscle → neuromuscular junction → cranial nerve (III/IV/VI) → cranial nerve nucleus → MLF (internuclear connections) → PPRF (ipsilateral conjugate gaze center) → contralateral frontal eye field. Only lesions at or below the MLF cause true diplopia; PPRF/FEF lesions cause a conjugate gaze palsy instead.
- **The "neighborhood sign"**: cranial nerve nuclei that sit close together in the brainstem tend to be affected together by any lesion large enough to reach them. CN VI's fascicle loops around the CN VII nucleus (the internal genu) before exiting the pons, so an isolated CN VI palsy without facial weakness essentially excludes a brainstem nuclear/fascicular lesion.
- **Rule of four**, reinforced with two corrections from Berkowitz: CN XI does not arise from the brainstem (despite sometimes being grouped with 9/10/11/12); CN VIII's pupillary light reflex afferents relay through the midbrain pretectal nucleus, technically extending its "levels" beyond pons/medulla.
- **CN VI as the classic false localizing sign**: because of its unusually long intracranial subarachnoid course, elevated intracranial pressure can stretch and injure CN VI far from any structural lesion, producing a palsy that does not reflect the true site of pathology.
- **Diabetic (microvascular) cranial nerve palsy anatomy**, reinforced: a small-vessel infarct affects the core of the nerve (motor fibers), sparing peripherally-located fibers — pupillomotor sparing in CN III palsies, facial-nucleus sparing in CN VI's brainstem "neighbors" — versus a compressive lesion, which characteristically affects peripheral fibers first (pupil-involving CN III palsy from aneurysm or uncal herniation).
- **Hickam's Dictum vs. Occam's razor**: not every constellation of findings in one patient needs (or has) a single unifying lesion, especially when the patient carries a systemic risk factor (like long-standing diabetes) capable of independently causing multiple, anatomically unrelated problems.
- **Giant cell arteritis** as a "don't miss" in any new neurologic symptom in a patient over 60, including isolated ocular motor palsies, even without classic jaw claudication/scalp tenderness/headache; GCA-related stroke has a possible predilection for the posterior circulation.
- **Foreshadowing/companion case**: this episode explicitly raises the hypothetical of an isolated cranial neuropathy evolving into full-blown Miller Fisher syndrome — realized in a different patient in [[CPS 263 - Diplopia, Numbness, and Miller Fisher Syndrome|CPS 263]].
