## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Responding to a listener request (Andrew Sanchez) to cover how they think through sudden clinical deterioration, Reza narrates a real overnight rapid response he personally ran, quizzing Robbie step by step on how he'd approach it. The scenario opens as presumed hypoxemic respiratory failure with alarming vitals (BP 190/105, HR 130, on 10L oxygen), but a single conversation with the awake, communicative patient completely reframes the case: an oncology patient who'd just resumed oral intake developing acute chest pain radiating down his left arm. The episode is a real-time demonstration of resisting premature framing, de-escalating a chaotic room, reasoning through when tachycardia argues against ACS, and — after a negative CTA, negative troponins, and a retrospective chart-review discovery that this had happened once before — landing on anxiety/panic as the final diagnosis, with an explicit "cognitive autopsy" defending the soundness of the diagnostic process despite the ultimately benign outcome.

## Pearls

- When called to a rapid response or code, deliberately suppress secondhand information at first and go directly to your own observation of the patient — verbal reports (like "the patient is pulseless") can be wrong, and your own eyes should be the foundation everything else gets layered onto.
- No single bedside sign, including pulse palpation, is perfect — use it alongside other proxies (like whether the patient is spontaneously breathing) to build an overall pretest probability rather than relying on any one maneuver in isolation. If there's genuine doubt about a pulse, starting chest compressions is the safer error.
- Even from a distance, checking for visible chest rise is a fast, useful way to screen for whether a patient is breathing — a lapse in this basic observation can have serious consequences, underscoring that "looking fine" and "resting" are not interchangeable with "breathing."
- A well-timed goals-of-care conversation the night before a patient's death can allow that death to occur peacefully, without unwanted resuscitation attempts — a family's gratitude for that conversation is as real and important an outcome as any acute intervention.
- Managing a chaotic rapid response room is itself a skill: deliberately restrict the number of people present (e.g., naming a target number and asking everyone else to step just outside, available if needed), and explicitly designate roles — the primary nurse, the primary team, respiratory therapy, and whoever knows the patient best.
- Quieting a noisy room and asking for a single, structured one-liner summary from whoever has the most current information is a fast way to reorient everyone (including yourself) to the same shared picture before proceeding.
- Elevated blood pressure alongside tachycardia is an internally inconsistent pattern if you assume the tachycardia is purely a compensatory reflex (since tachycardia's core purpose is to protect against impending hypotension) — this mismatch should prompt you to ask whether the "tachycardia" is actually sinus, or whether it might be an arrhythmia (which pairs well with hypoxemia from causes like cardiogenic pulmonary edema).
- Sinus tachycardia paired with hypertension, in the absence of another clear explanation, often reflects excess catecholamine drive — most commonly from active substance use/withdrawal or from severe pain/anxiety/discomfort. Recognizing this lets you treat both vital sign abnormalities as nonspecific markers of distress rather than the primary diagnostic target, and refocus on the truly objective abnormality (such as hypoxemia) instead.
- Immediate bedside troubleshooting for unexplained hypoxemic distress: escalate oxygen support as needed, but also actively look for reversible contributors — suction the airway if the patient cannot manage their own secretions (based on their mental status and observed cough/gag, not reflexively in an awake, effectively-breathing patient), and remember the pleural space can also need decompression (consider pneumothorax).
- Empiric first-line pharmacologic options for unexplained hypoxemic respiratory distress with hypertension include diuretics (for possible flash pulmonary edema) and blood pressure control (e.g., nitroglycerin paste, which is easy to apply and easy to remove if a rapid reversal is needed) — reasonable low-risk moves while further workup proceeds.
- Do not criticize the emergency department in hindsight for tests that seem unnecessary once results are back (the "Monday morning quarterback" trap) — EDs make real-time decisions with the information available at the time, without the benefit of the eventual negative result.
- A powerful debiasing exercise when a hospitalized patient develops a new, acute symptom: explicitly ask, "if this patient walked into the emergency department right now with this presentation, what would be done?" This can strip away the false reassurance of "they're already admitted and stable" framing and restore appropriate urgency.
- Absent underlying heart failure, classic acute coronary syndrome typically does not present with tachycardia — significant tachycardia in a patient with chest pain should lower (not raise) your estimate of straightforward ACS as the cause, prompting broader consideration (arrhythmia, PE, anxiety/pain-driven sympathetic activation, and other causes).
- In an undifferentiated, high-acuity chest pain workup where "you need an answer today," get the EKG first: a clear STEMI bypasses the need for CTA entirely and goes straight to the cath lab pathway; clear, bilateral breath sounds meaningfully lower the probability of pneumothorax; if the EKG doesn't show clear ACS, a CTA chest can efficiently evaluate for aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, and (in a patient with a recently placed feeding tube) esophageal injury/rupture in a single study.
- A cognitive autopsy after a negative high-stakes workup should evaluate the soundness of the decision-making process, not just the outcome — a negative CTA does not retroactively mean the CTA was the wrong call, especially when the alternative (missing a PE or ACS in a hypercoagulable oncology patient) would have been far worse.
- Base-rate reality check: if you were to order 100 CTAs for suspected PE in patients you cared about, you would want the overwhelming majority to be negative — a high negative rate is the expected, desired signature of appropriately sensitive testing for a rare-but-deadly diagnosis, not evidence of overtesting.
- The ratio of routine tests ordered (like EKGs) to higher-acuity tests ordered (like CTPE) is dramatically more lopsided than the actual prevalence ratio between the diseases they're meant to catch (ACS vs. PE) — a useful reminder that clinicians may be more reflexively willing to order cheap, fast tests than appropriately-indicated higher-cost tests, even when a life-threatening diagnosis is genuinely on the table.

## Problem Representation

An overnight rapid response for a 72-year-old man with metastatic upper-GI-origin cancer, who had resumed oral intake earlier that day after a period of tube feeding, presenting with alarming vital signs (severe hypertension, tachycardia, apparent hypoxemic distress on high-flow oxygen) that, once the patient could be directly interviewed, reframed into an evolving chest pain syndrome ultimately attributed to anxiety/panic — after a negative EKG for ACS, a negative CTA, and negative serial troponins — and found on chart review to be a recurrent phenomenon tied to reintroducing oral food.

## Case Narrative

Prompted by a listener request (from Andrew Sanchez) to cover how they reason through a sudden change in a patient's clinical status, Reza set up a real scenario he had personally lived through as the overnight hospitalist, whose job that night was to respond to rapids and codes across the hospital (not to supervise, but to assist the primary team). Before giving any case details, he asked Robbie to describe his general approach to being urgently summoned to a deteriorating patient.

Robbie described intentionally suppressing whatever secondhand information he's told en route and going directly to his own observation of the patient as the foundation for everything else — citing a common pitfall where a patient reported as "pulseless" is found, on the responder's own exam, to have a thready pulse. Reza shared a story from his own residency at UCSF, where he began chest compressions on an unresponsive-appearing patient in a chair based on being unable to palpate a pulse, only to be struck in the face as the patient — who had simply been asleep with a normal QRS on telemetry — woke up. He asked Robbie how to get genuinely good at pulse palpation. Robbie's answer: no bedside test, including pulse-checking, is perfect, so use it alongside other cues — most importantly, whether the patient is spontaneously breathing, since respiratory effort is often easier to observe reliably than a pulse and correlates strongly with the probability of having a pulse. He added that practice matters, and that when genuinely in doubt, starting compressions is the safer error to make.

Reza shared a second story from Hopkins: glancing into the room of an 80-year-old with advanced dementia who "looked comfortable" while walking to get coffee, only to be called back minutes later to find the patient had actually died, not merely been resting. He drew two lessons: actively look for chest rise, even from a distance, rather than assuming a peaceful appearance means adequate breathing; and, separately, he reflected on how a goals-of-care conversation held with the family the evening before had allowed that same patient to die peacefully without unwanted resuscitation, referencing a family's gratitude for that conversation (recalling an anecdote from a Vivek Murthy podcast) as a meaningful outcome in its own right.

Turning to the actual case, Reza described walking into a brightly lit room: the bed inclined, the patient wearing a face mask, visibly sweating and appearing to gasp for air, with a resident and nurse present and a blood pressure cuff cycling. The monitor showed a systolic blood pressure around 190, diastolic around 105, oxygen saturation 100%, and heart rate 130 — on 10 liters of supplemental oxygen.

Robbie reasoned that, despite the seemingly reassuring 100% saturation, the fact that it required 10 liters of flow to achieve kept a respiratory problem plausible, while the combination of severe hypertension and tachycardia was internally puzzling: tachycardia's physiologic purpose is to protect against impending hypotension, so tachycardia in a markedly hypertensive patient suggested either that this wasn't simple sinus tachycardia (raising the possibility of an arrhythmia, which could pair with hypoxemia from something like cardiogenic pulmonary edema) or that both vital sign abnormalities were nonspecific markers of a catecholamine surge — most commonly from substance use/withdrawal or severe pain/discomfort — rather than the primary diagnostic clue. He suggested treating the hypertension and tachycardia as a probable epiphenomenon of distress and refocusing on the hypoxemia as the most objective abnormality. His immediate bedside plan: continue to optimize oxygen support, then actively troubleshoot ways to reduce the oxygen requirement — suctioning the airway if the patient couldn't manage secretions himself (contingent on his level of alertness and observed cough/gag, not reflexively), keeping pneumothorax on the differential (with pleural-space decompression as a consideration), and using empiric diuretics (for possible flash pulmonary edema) and blood pressure control (his preference being nitroglycerin paste, easy to apply and wipe off if needed) as low-risk empiric moves.

Reza then asked how Robbie manages the room itself during a rapid response, since the actual scene had felt chaotic with no clear leader. Robbie described relying on established relationships in his usual closed unit, but generalized a strategy for anyone entering a less familiar situation: explicitly limit the number of people in the room (e.g., "I only want five people here"), and name specific roles — the primary nurse, the primary team, respiratory therapy, and whoever knows the patient best — asking everyone else to step just outside. Reza described his own version, used in an open unit where he meets new people at every code: asking the room to quiet down, identifying who's involved, and then asking the resident for a structured one-liner to reorient everyone simultaneously.

The one-liner from the overnight resident (relayed from the cross-covering intern): a 72-year-old man with metastatic cancer originating from the upper GI tract, on chemotherapy, who had just had his oral diet restarted that day after previously being on tube feeds — and the rapid was called because he'd developed severe shortness of breath and chest pain. Reza then quieted the room and asked the patient directly what was happening; the patient reported chest pain radiating down his left arm and an inability to breathe. Notably, the rapid had not actually been triggered by true hypoxemia — retrospectively, oxygen had only been added out of comfort, and the monitor had never shown a genuinely low saturation.

Robbie reflected that this illustrates how noisy and misleading secondhand framing can be, and how dramatically the problem representation changes once the patient can participate directly: from hypoxemic respiratory failure, to hypertension-and-tachycardia of uncertain cause, to — now — a chest pain syndrome in an oncology patient who had just resumed oral intake. Recognizing the patient was tachycardic and hypertensive but not imminently unstable, and not truly hypoxic, he recommended deliberately lowering the energy in the room, reassuring the patient calmly (to avoid worsening his tachycardia and hypertension through panic), and pursuing a standard, calm chest pain workup: EKG, basic labs, chest x-ray, and specifically weaning the oxygen down to determine his true saturation.

Reza confirmed they weaned the oxygen and the saturation remained above 97% on room air; lung exam was clear bilaterally; cardiac exam showed only tachycardia, no murmurs or abnormal sounds; the patient remained visibly distressed.

The next decision point was how aggressively to pursue imaging — proceed straight to CTA, or wait for initial labs — and whether to transfer the patient to a telemetry unit, since he was currently on a non-tele floor. Robbie framed the first and most important question in any acute chest pain evaluation as: do you need an answer today? Here, clearly yes. The first concrete step regardless of downstream plans is the EKG — a STEMI would bypass the need for CTA entirely and proceed straight to the cath lab pathway. He also flagged pneumothorax as a "don't miss" diagnosis requiring a different disposition (chest tube capability), though clear, bilateral breath sounds significantly lowered that probability. If the EKG didn't show clear ACS, he'd proceed directly to a CTA chest, which could efficiently evaluate for aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, and — given the patient's recent feeding tube manipulation — esophageal injury, all in one study. He added a specific pearl: this patient's degree of tachycardia itself lowered the probability of classic ACS, since the vast majority of ACS presentations, absent concurrent heart failure, do not produce tachycardia — a teaching point Reza recalled learning from a Hopkins cardiology fellow.

The EKG showed sinus tachycardia with diffuse ST depressions and no ST elevation. At this point, Reza and the residents paused to explicitly re-anchor their reasoning: the problem had evolved from presumed hypoxemic respiratory failure, to hypertension-and-tachycardia (understood as a catecholamine/distress marker), to now simply asking what was causing this patient's chest discomfort in that moment. Reza emphasized not retrospectively criticizing the emergency department's imaging decisions in general ("Monday-morning quarterbacking"), since EDs act on real-time information without the benefit of eventual negative results.

To help the residents move past treating the patient as "already admitted and stable," Reza used a specific reframing exercise: asking them to imagine this same patient walking into the emergency department right now with these exact symptoms, and to say plainly what they'd do. The team unanimously agreed they'd proceed straight to CTA without hesitating over the creatinine or the somewhat vague, subjective quality of some symptoms — illustrating how the reframing exercise cut through the false reassurance of the inpatient setting.

They proceeded with CTA and troponin. In a real-world logistical aside, Reza described a hospital-policy lapse: he had let the patient go to CT without a physician escort, only to be called by the charge nurse (a friend, addressed with characteristic humor) pointing out that policy required a doctor to accompany the patient. Reza went down personally, was surprised (to his own comedic embarrassment) to learn CT contrast is delivered by an automated power injector rather than manually administered, reviewed the CT images himself without seeing an obvious PE, but — appropriately not fully trusting his own informal read — awaited the formal tele-radiology interpretation.

The final results: troponin negative twice, CTA negative, and the patient reported feeling better shortly afterward. A subsequent chart review revealed the patient had experienced a strikingly similar episode roughly a year earlier, also temporally linked to resuming oral food intake after a period of tube feeding — reframing the final impression as a recurrent, food-reintroduction-associated anxiety/panic phenomenon rather than a one-off event.

Robbie's closing reflection emphasized that hypertension and tachycardia together most often signal either a substance-related or a pain/anxiety-driven catecholamine surge, and that the genuinely important work in this case was making sure the patient wasn't having pain from a serious, cancer-associated risk (specifically pulmonary embolism) before settling on a benign explanation — while acknowledging that the diagnostic journey to reach that "simple" final answer is considerably harder in real time than it appears in hindsight.

Reza reinforced the base-rate reality that most CTAs ordered for suspected PE will be negative, and that this is expected and appropriate, not a sign of diagnostic failure. He performed an explicit cognitive autopsy on the case, concluding the reasoning process had been sound throughout, regardless of the negative outcome, and predicted (with equanimity) that the primary team might tease them the next day for "just anxiety" — arguing that outcome should never be used to retroactively judge a well-reasoned process, especially in a hypercoagulable oncology patient where a missed PE or ACS would have been far more costly than a negative workup.

The episode closed with a "math riddle" from Robbie: estimating the prevalence ratio of ACS to PE (roughly 5-10 to 1) against the vastly more lopsided ratio of EKGs ordered to CTPEs ordered — used to argue that clinicians are, if anything, systematically under-ordering CTPEs relative to how comparably common and lethal PE is next to ACS, and that clinicians shouldn't feel guilty about liberal CTPE ordering when genuinely working up a potentially deadly process, even while acknowledging that formal cost-effectiveness literature suggests overordering does occur at a population level.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Impact |
|---|---|
| SpO2 100% only on 10L supplemental oxygen | Keeps a respiratory process plausible despite a reassuring-looking saturation value |
| Severe hypertension with tachycardia | Internally inconsistent if the tachycardia is assumed purely compensatory — raises suspicion for either a non-sinus arrhythmia or a catecholamine-driven distress state (substance-related or pain/anxiety-related) |
| Patient able to directly report chest pain radiating down the left arm | Completely reframes the case from a presumed primary respiratory problem to a chest pain syndrome |
| No true hypoxemia once oxygen weaned (SpO2 >97% on room air), clear bilateral lung exam | Substantially lowers concern for a primary respiratory or cardiogenic pulmonary edema process |
| Marked tachycardia in the setting of chest pain | Lowers (rather than raises) probability of classic ACS, since ACS without concurrent heart failure typically does not produce tachycardia |
| EKG: sinus tachycardia with diffuse ST depressions, no ST elevation | Argues against a STEMI requiring immediate cath lab activation; keeps CTA-evaluable causes (PE, dissection, esophageal injury) and non-structural causes (anxiety) on the table |
| Recent resumption of oral intake after tube feeding, cancer history | Raises hypercoagulability-related PE concern, justifying CTA despite low pretest probability of a positive result |
| Negative CTA, negative serial troponins, symptomatic improvement | Supports a functional/anxiety-driven process over a structural cardiopulmonary emergency |
| Chart review reveals an identical prior episode also linked to resuming oral intake | Confirms a recurrent, situational (likely anxiety/panic) phenomenon rather than an isolated acute event |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Secondhand-information suppression as a deliberate first move**: explicitly naming the discipline of not trusting what you're told until you've verified it yourself (the "pulseless" patient with a thready pulse) as a core rapid-response skill.
- **Vital sign internal-consistency checking**: using the mismatch between severe hypertension and tachycardia (which "shouldn't" coexist if tachycardia is purely compensatory) as an active diagnostic signal rather than accepting both abnormalities at face value.
- **Reframing exercise to defeat "already admitted" false reassurance**: explicitly asking "what would the ED do with this patient right now" as a structured technique for stripping away the misleading calm of an inpatient setting.
- **Non-punitive treatment of ED imaging decisions in hindsight** ("Monday-morning quarterbacking") — an explicit named bias to avoid.
- **Cognitive autopsy performed regardless of a benign outcome**: modeling that a negative high-acuity workup should be evaluated on the soundness of the decision process at the time, not backward-rationalized as unnecessary once the results are known.
- **Anticipating and preemptively defending against outcome-based criticism from colleagues** ("the team tomorrow will make fun of us... let them") — an explicit, healthy response to the social pressure that can otherwise discourage appropriately aggressive workups.

## Teaching Discussion

This episode is fundamentally about the discipline of re-framing in real time: a case that begins as apparent hypoxemic respiratory failure is completely restructured, twice, first by direct conversation with the patient and then by the EKG result — each time requiring the team to consciously discard their prior framing rather than cling to it. It also offers concrete, transferable operational skills (room management during a code, the "what would the ED do right now" reframing technique, suppressing secondhand information) alongside a clean teaching point about tachycardia's relationship to ACS, and closes with an explicit, non-punitive model of cognitive autopsy — evaluating decision quality independent of outcome — that is broadly applicable well beyond this specific case.
