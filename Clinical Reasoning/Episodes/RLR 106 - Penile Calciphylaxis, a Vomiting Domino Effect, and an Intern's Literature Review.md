## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Reza, picking up a new hospitalist service, finds a patient with end-stage renal disease and a recent STEMI actively vomiting at the bedside — the presenting complaint on sign-out was a years-long, recurrent nausea and vomiting syndrome tentatively labeled gastroparesis. Digging into the actual admission reveals the real story: the patient came in ten days earlier for two months of glans penis pain that progressed to a black, dry patch, ultimately diagnosed clinically as penile calciphylaxis — confirmed by markedly deranged calcium-phosphate metabolism and a KUB showing diffuse vascular calcification. Round-the-clock opioids started for the excruciating pain of penile infarction then worsened his underlying gastroparesis, producing the vomiting Reza actually walked in on. The episode closes with an intern's literature review that reframes the family's understanding of prognosis and delivers a genuinely practice-changing intervention: a dorsal penile nerve block.

## Pearls

- For undifferentiated nausea and vomiting, focus the initial bedside evaluation on the two organs most responsible for morbid causes — the brain and the GI tract — assessable directly with history, exam, and inspection, plus an EKG to screen for myocardial ischemia as an underrecognized cause of vomiting.
- Isopropyl alcohol pads, simply inhaled, have shown antiemetic efficacy comparable to ondansetron in some studies and are a useful bridge option before IV access is obtained; the antiemetic escalation ladder runs ondansetron first-line, then atypical antipsychotics (olanzapine, prochlorperazine, haloperidol), with benzodiazepines specifically useful when the underlying driver is withdrawal.
- A long-standing, recurrent symptom pattern described as leaving "no permanent scars" should be actively verified, not assumed — look specifically for weight loss, malnutrition, and electrolyte derangements as evidence of cumulative harm before accepting that framing.
- Gastroparesis is a diagnosis that gets applied too readily to recurrent postprandial nausea and vomiting without confirmatory nuclear gastric emptying testing; when a patient is on chronic opioids (which independently slow gastric motility and must typically be held to get an interpretable study), a presumptive gastroparesis label may remain unconfirmed indefinitely.
- Prucalopride is a prokinetic option for gastroparesis notable for not prolonging the QTc — a meaningful advantage in patients (like this one, with a baseline QTc of 520) where other prokinetic/antiemetic options carry real arrhythmia risk; it typically requires GI prescribing.
- Black, dry tissue on exam should be read as ischemic/infarcted rather than infectious — infection typically produces moist, edematous, erythematous tissue from the inflammatory response, so dryness is itself a discriminating clue pointing away from a primary infectious process.
- Calciphylaxis should be considered in any ESRD patient with unexplained, severe, ischemic-appearing skin/soft tissue necrosis, especially in extremity- or pelvis-predominant distributions; risk correlates with the calcium-phosphate product, PTH level, and dialysis adherence, and — independent of uremia — with warfarin use (via its effects on protein C and S), a recognized driver of "non-uremic calciphylaxis."
- Penile calciphylaxis is rare enough that many clinicians will never see it, but carries an even worse prognosis than calciphylaxis at other sites — some series report survival on the order of months rather than the roughly one-year median survival quoted for calciphylaxis overall — and amputation does not improve prognosis, since the disease is systemic vascular calcification, not a localized penile process.
- When a new problem appears on roughly hospital day 10 or later, the first working hypothesis should be that something the care team did is responsible, rather than that a new, spontaneous process has begun — this patient's vomiting was not a coincidental new complaint but a direct downstream consequence of opioids started days earlier for penile pain control.

## Problem Representation

An ESRD patient on hemodialysis with a recent STEMI, admitted for penile pain found to be penile calciphylaxis, whose subsequent round-the-clock opioid therapy for that pain precipitated a flare of underlying, previously unconfirmed gastroparesis.

## Case Narrative

Reza described the case as a "day in the life" hospitalist story, presented as it actually unfolded rather than reconstructed for teaching clarity. Picking up a new two-week inpatient service and reading through overnight sign-out, he walked in to find a man in his mid-50s actively vomiting into a basin, visibly uncomfortable. Before addressing the specific case, the discussion built a general approach to a vomiting patient at the bedside: controlling the symptom early (since any further workup — imaging, further history — is difficult while a patient is actively retching), with isopropyl alcohol pad inhalation as an underused bridge option before IV access, ondansetron as first-line pharmacotherapy, atypical antipsychotics as a second-line option, and benzodiazepines specifically useful when withdrawal is the underlying driver. The recommended bedside approach then focuses on the brain and the GI tract — the two organs responsible for most morbid causes of vomiting — assessable through history and exam, plus an EKG to screen for cardiac ischemia as a cause. The volume of material produced was flagged as a rough clue: profuse vomiting points more toward a GI-predominant process, whereas a lot of retching with little material often reflects a different mechanism.

On history, the patient denied neurologic symptoms, abdominal pain, diarrhea, or dysphagia, but described food consistently irritating him and provoking vomiting — a pattern present intermittently for years without a prior diagnosis, now worse than ever. An EKG showed no STEMI but a QTc of approximately 520. Reviewing the chart, Reza found the patient had end-stage renal disease on thrice-weekly hemodialysis via a permacath, long-standing diabetes with a recent A1C of 4.8, and — two to three weeks prior to this admission — a STEMI treated with two stents, now on dual antiplatelet therapy.

Rabih's initial framing treated the recurrent nausea and vomiting as a chronic, episodic syndrome without an established diagnosis, but cautioned against assuming it left "no permanent scars" without actively looking for weight loss, malnutrition, or electrolyte derangements as evidence to the contrary. Given the patient's extensive vascular disease burden, recurrent myocardial ischemia and mesenteric ischemia were flagged as considerations that should be actively excluded (via troponin/EKG trending, and via a lower threshold to think about gut hypoperfusion than in a lower-risk patient) rather than passively assumed absent because pain — usually a prominent feature of ischemia — was not present. Diabetic gastroparesis was raised as an elegant but frequently under-confirmed diagnosis in exactly this kind of recurrent, postprandial nausea/vomiting presentation.

Reza then filled in what the workup had already shown: a GI e-consult roughly a year earlier had reviewed this recurrent syndrome, and an endoscopy had shown retained food, raising concern for gastroparesis — but confirmatory gastric emptying scintigraphy could not be performed because the patient was on chronic opioids for bony pain, which independently slows gastric motility and would confound the study. GI had instead recommended prucalopride, a prokinetic notable for not prolonging the QTc (a meaningful consideration given this patient's baseline QTc of 520), though it requires GI prescribing.

Turning to what had actually brought the patient into the hospital ten days earlier, Reza described a presentation of two months of glans penis symptoms: initial minor bleeding, progressing to exquisite, 10-out-of-10 pain, without urethral discharge or dysuria. Before revealing the exam, the general approach to penile pain was discussed: infectious causes (cellulitis, candidal balanitis — particularly relevant in a diabetic patient — recurrent HSV), and trauma (zipper injuries being a classically cited mechanism), with the expectation that direct visual inspection should almost always update and sharpen the initial "penile pain" problem representation into something more specific.

The exam revealed a black, dry patch on the glans penis, unresponsive to a course of topical clindamycin prescribed at an outside hospital. The dryness of the lesion was specifically probed and used to argue against an infectious process (which would typically produce moisture, erythema, and edema from the local inflammatory response) and toward ischemia — black, dead tissue reflecting infarction from either infection or, more concerning given this patient's profile (recent STEMI, diabetes, ESRD), a primary vascular/ischemic process. Calciphylaxis was raised specifically, given its tendency to produce ischemic lesions in unusual body sites in ESRD patients.

To build the case for or against calciphylaxis, the discussion identified a specific data-gathering strategy: examining the rest of the skin for similar lesions in the classically extremity- and pelvis-predominant distribution typical of calciphylaxis; trending the patient's adherence to dialysis along with calcium, phosphorus, and PTH values (with a higher calcium-phosphate product and higher PTH both raising concern); and — somewhat counterintuitively for a presenting complaint of penile pain — asking specifically about warfarin use, since warfarin is an independent risk factor for calciphylaxis (implicated via its effects on protein C and S) and is specifically associated with a recognized entity of non-uremic calciphylaxis occurring even outside the setting of renal failure.

The actual data confirmed the hypothesis: phosphorus was markedly elevated at 10 (against a typical reference range around 2.5-4.5) with calcium at 8 (low end of the 8-10 reference range), yielding an elevated calcium-phosphate product. A KUB obtained during one of the earlier nausea/vomiting episodes — initially framed as being shown to look for a sigmoid volvulus — instead revealed extensive, diffusely scattered radio-dense material following a vascular distribution pattern, most prominent in the epigastric region consistent with the celiac artery distribution, but present throughout the abdomen. This systemic vascular calcification was proposed as a plausible unifying substrate connecting the patient's recent STEMI (via coronary artery calcification) to his current penile presentation (via calcification of the vessels supplying the glans) — the same underlying disease process manifesting in multiple vascular beds.

Urology and dermatology were consulted and were confident enough in the clinical diagnosis of calciphylaxis from visual inspection alone that they did not pursue a biopsy. Palliative care became involved and started the patient on round-the-clock hydromorphone to control the severe pain of active penile infarction — and it was this opioid escalation that precipitated the flare of his underlying gastroparesis, producing the vomiting Reza had walked in on that morning. The presenting complaint on sign-out (recurrent nausea and vomiting) was, in other words, not the primary problem at all, but a downstream consequence of treatment for the actual admitting diagnosis.

The final portion of the case credited a specific intervention: Reza's intern researched the literature on penile calciphylaxis and returned with findings that changed the clinical course. Sodium thiosulfate, the standard treatment for calciphylaxis generally, has limited demonstrated efficacy, and calciphylaxis overall carries roughly 60% one-year mortality. A case series of approximately 30 patients with specifically penile calciphylaxis (many diagnosed by inspection alone, as in this patient) showed an even worse prognosis than calciphylaxis at other sites, with survival more typically measured in months rather than approaching a year — prompting an urgent, rather than routine, goals-of-care family meeting and a shift toward comfort-focused management, especially given the patient was also developing hypoglycemic episodes from malnutrition and cachexia related to his inability to keep food down. The intern also found that penile amputation would not improve prognosis, since the underlying problem is systemic vascular disease rather than a localized penile process, and — most practically impactful — that a dorsal penile nerve block (lidocaine and steroid injection) could provide meaningful relief; this was arranged through the pain service and reduced the patient's pain from 8/10 on round-the-clock hydromorphone to 4/10 within an hour, opening the door to a more durable nerve ablation for longer-term comfort and to reducing the opioid burden that had been driving the gastroparesis flare.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| Years-long recurrent postprandial nausea/vomiting, prior EGD with retained food | Raises suspicion for gastroparesis, though unconfirmed by gastric emptying study |
| Chronic opioid use precluding gastric emptying scintigraphy | Explains why gastroparesis remains a presumptive, not confirmed, diagnosis |
| Black, dry glans penis lesion unresponsive to topical antibiotics | Lowers probability of an infectious process; raises probability of ischemia/infarction |
| ESRD, recent STEMI, diabetes | Raises pretest probability of a systemic vascular/ischemic process (calciphylaxis) over a localized dermatologic diagnosis |
| Elevated calcium-phosphate product (Phos 10, Ca 8) | Supports calciphylaxis risk |
| KUB showing diffuse vascular-pattern calcification | Confirms a systemic vascular calcification process, plausibly also explaining the recent STEMI |
| Round-the-clock opioids started for penile pain, followed by worsening vomiting | Supports opioid-precipitated gastroparesis flare as the cause of the presenting vomiting, rather than a new spontaneous process |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Calciphylaxis illness script: an ESRD (or, less commonly, non-uremic/warfarin-associated) patient develops painful, ischemic, black/necrotic skin and soft tissue lesions in extremity- or pelvis-predominant distributions, supported by an elevated calcium-phosphate product and elevated PTH, sometimes with radiographically visible diffuse vascular calcification; carries a poor prognosis, particularly severe when it involves the penis.

The case is an explicit, named demonstration of "hospital day 10" reasoning — the instinct to treat a new inpatient symptom as a fresh, independent diagnostic puzzle rather than first asking what the care team itself has done recently that could explain it. Rabih explicitly reflects that his comfort zone (outpatient/ED encounters with patients presenting from home) had made him under-prioritize this iatrogenic-first framing, a bias correction he names directly as a takeaway.

## Teaching Discussion

The episode's central teaching point is iatrogenic-first reasoning for new inpatient symptoms arising well into a hospitalization: a symptom appearing on or after roughly hospital day 10 should prompt an active search for what has been added to or changed in the patient's regimen before constructing an entirely new differential, since inpatients are subject to a steady stream of new medications and interventions that can produce their own downstream effects.

A second teaching point is the discriminating value of tissue appearance (black and dry versus moist and inflamed) in distinguishing ischemic from infectious causes of skin necrosis, and the importance of updating an initial, vague problem representation ("penile pain") immediately and specifically once direct inspection is possible.

A third, more systems-level teaching point is the value of trainee-driven literature review in actively changing management — not just academically, but practically: reframing a family's understanding of prognosis from roughly a year to a matter of months prompted an urgently-timed goals-of-care conversation rather than a delayed one, and surfacing the dorsal penile nerve block delivered concrete, immediate symptomatic relief that a senior clinician had not independently identified.
