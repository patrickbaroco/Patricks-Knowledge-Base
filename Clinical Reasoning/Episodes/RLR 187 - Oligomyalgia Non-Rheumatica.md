## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Rabih presents an older San Francisco VA man with indolent **[[Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia|CLL]]** (watch-and-wait, baseline lymphocytosis ~21k) and type 2 diabetes who comes to the ER with three days of **atraumatic left [[Hip Pain|hip pain]]** — morning-predominant, mildly eased by activity, over the greater trochanter, still weight-bearing. Reza opens with the joint-pain schema: work the area the patient points to, and split first by **traumatic vs atraumatic** and then **[[Inflammatory vs Mechanical Pain|inflamed vs not]]**, noting the hip is a *deep* joint that can look normal while harboring pathology. The initial exam is near-normal and the entire first-pass workup — CBC (at CLL baseline), renal, LFTs, **[[Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate|ESR]]**, **[[C-Reactive Protein|CRP]]**, uric acid, and hip/pelvis radiograph — is normal, so he is treated symptomatically and sent home. Over roughly **three outpatient months** the illness slowly declares itself: pain becomes bilateral, then adds the **neck**, then both **shoulders**, in a symmetric, proximal, stiffness-dominant pattern. Along the way it generates two non-contrast spine MRIs (degenerative disease with moderate canal stenosis), an orthopedics visit (thoughtfully *declining* to operate), and a neurology visit — where the exam is normal and the neurologist declares it **not neurologic**. Only when a junior ER physician finally rechecks inflammatory markers do they read **ESR 70, CRP 50**, with **negative [[Rheumatoid Factor|RF]] and anti-[[Anti-CCP Antibody|CCP]]** and normal hand/foot exams and radiographs; **15 mg of prednisone** produces a dramatic recovery, clinching **[[Polymyalgia Rheumatica|polymyalgia rheumatica]]** (with seronegative **[[Elderly-Onset Rheumatoid Arthritis|elderly-onset RA]]** kept on the differential and **[[Giant Cell Arteritis|GCA]]** to screen for). Rabih's framing device — the arc from **[[Oligomyalgia Non-Rheumatica|monomyalgia non-rheumatica → oligomyalgia → polymyalgia rheumatica]]** — captures why the case was *unsolvable early*: too few sites and, critically, normal markers, because nobody re-ordered the ESR/CRP as the syndrome matured. The enduring lesson is one of **[[Illness Tempo|tempo]]** and **[[Diagnostic Humility|humility]]**: some illnesses cannot be pattern-matched until they finish arriving, and an unexplained, persistently high ESR/CRP is the saving clue.

## Pearls

- **An illness that presents "too early" can be genuinely unsolvable — the pattern has to finish arriving before you can name it.** A single body region with a non-inflammatory look and normal markers offers no pattern to match; the correct move is not a forced diagnosis but a deliberate follow-up plan that lets **[[Illness Tempo|tempo]]** reveal the syndrome. This case only became solvable at three months, and both hosts frame that as skill *and* [[Diagnostic Humility|humility]], not failure.
- **Frame any musculoskeletal complaint by two branch points before anything else: traumatic vs atraumatic, and inflamed vs not inflamed.** For the **[[Hip Pain|hip]]** — a deep joint that can look and image normal while harboring disease — the exam (passive hip flexion, weight-bearing, range of motion) and the vitals/ESR carry more weight than inspection, and asking about an unaccustomed exertion (not just obvious trauma) is part of the atraumatic-vs-traumatic split.
- **Morning-predominant stiffness that *improves* with activity points to inflammatory, not mechanical, disease.** The same signal that flags inflammatory back pain (better with movement, worse with rest) applies at any joint; here it was the first quiet clue toward a **[[Inflammatory vs Mechanical Pain|rheumatic]]** process long before the markers turned.
- **Two body regions separated in space, with an unaffected zone between them, argue against a single mechanical or contiguous process.** Hip *and* neck (sparing the thoraco-abdominal region) should push you off degenerative disease and toward something **systemic** — infiltrative, metastatic, or a **[[Symmetric Polyarthritis|systemic inflammatory]]** disease that lights up multiple joint territories at once.
- **An unexplained, persistently elevated ESR/CRP is the saving clue in evolving polymyalgia — and the classic miss is failing to re-order it.** Inflammatory markers were checked exactly once, at the too-early first visit, and read normal; had any single later ESR/CRP been drawn, the diagnosis would have declared itself. When a syndrome is evolving, **[[Do-It-Again Sign|repeat the discriminating test]]** rather than trusting one early normal.
- **PMR versus seronegative elderly-onset RA is a real, sometimes unresolvable overlap; the steroid response confirms the rheumatic disease, and the dose helps separate them.** A markedly high ESR with negative **[[Rheumatoid Factor|RF]]/[[Anti-CCP Antibody|CCP]]** and proximal (shoulder/hip/neck) predominance favors **[[Polymyalgia Rheumatica|PMR]]**, but **[[Elderly-Onset Rheumatoid Arthritis|elderly-onset RA]]** also targets the axial and proximal skeleton; both melt with steroids (RA typically needs less, ~5–10 mg; PMR ~15 mg), so a dramatic low-dose response is a **[[Treatment as a Diagnostic Test|diagnostic maneuver]]** — and every PMR patient must be screened for **[[Giant Cell Arteritis|GCA]]**.

## Problem Representation

An older man (San Francisco VA) with indolent CLL under active surveillance (baseline lymphocytosis ~21,000) and well-controlled type 2 diabetes presents with three days of atraumatic, morning-predominant left greater-trochanteric hip pain that is mildly relieved by activity, with a near-normal musculoskeletal and neurologic exam and a completely normal first-pass workup (CBC at his CLL baseline, renal panel, LFTs, ESR, CRP, uric acid, and a hip/pelvis radiograph). Over roughly three outpatient months the pain becomes bilateral and then involves the cervical spine and finally both shoulders in a symmetric, proximal, stiffness-dominant, morning-predominant pattern; two non-contrast spine MRIs show only degenerative disease with moderate canal stenosis, and orthopedic and neurologic evaluations (including a normal neurologic exam) find no structural or neurologic explanation. When inflammatory markers are finally rechecked they are markedly elevated (ESR 70, CRP 50) with negative rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP and normal hand and foot exams and radiographs; a dramatic response to 15 mg of prednisone confirms polymyalgia rheumatica — with seronegative elderly-onset rheumatoid arthritis remaining on the differential — subsequently complicated by steroid-induced hyperglycemia (glucose 580).

## Case Narrative

Rabih flags one framing detail up front, because it changes how the case should be read: unlike his usual ER-and-hospital cases, this is a **three-month outpatient story** that he followed largely through the chart. The patient is a man he has come to know over years of visits — indolent **[[Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia|CLL]]** that is simply monitored, and type 2 diabetes on an SGLT2 inhibitor and a GLP-1 agonist (the latter for weight loss), with an A1c around 6.7. He arrives in the ER with **three days of left [[Hip Pain|hip pain]]**.

Reza builds the joint schema before taking more data. You examine the area the patient indicates, and the hip is harder than the knee or wrist because it is **deep-seated** — it can look completely normal over serious intra-articular pathology, so range of motion, weight-bearing status, and a careful history for *trauma* (including unaccustomed exertion, not just an obvious injury) matter most. He names the two organizing branch points: **[[Inflammatory vs Mechanical Pain|traumatic vs atraumatic]]** first, then **inflamed vs not** — the latter judged by range of motion, vital signs, and erythema. Rabih layers on the history: it is **atraumatic**, began as a morning ache three days ago and has progressed, is **worst in the morning and a little better through the day**, sits over the **greater trochanter**, and does not change with weight-bearing; there are no fevers, chills, swelling, or skin changes, and Tylenol and ibuprofen barely touched it. The **white count of 21,000** is his CLL baseline (a normal differential), not a new alarm.

Reza flags the morning-predominant, activity-eased quality as a possible **inflammatory** signal — the same logic used for inflammatory back pain — while noting that a deep joint's lack of visible redness is uninformative. On exam Rabih finds almost nothing: a mild limp, no systemic signs, hip range that produces discomfort but nothing excruciating or direction-specific, and no sensory, motor, or skin findings. Facing genuine uncertainty, he does what he does when he doesn't know: **blood work and imaging**. CBC, renal, LFTs, **[[Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate|ESR]]**, **[[C-Reactive Protein|CRP]]**, and uric acid, plus a hip/pelvis radiograph, all return **normal** (the WBC again just his baseline; the film shows no arthritis or degenerative change). Reza reasons through what a normal early film does and doesn't exclude — an occult fracture can precede radiographic change, an intra-articular effusion or subtle bony lesion can hide, and a CT might have added sensitivity — but with no trauma, no inflammatory signal, and a reassuring exam, the shared-decision plan is **symptomatic treatment** (lidocaine, muscle relaxers) with return precautions. This is visit one, day three; there is **no emergency to force**.

Intrigued by a pattern he hasn't seen, Rabih puts the patient on his follow-up list and watches the chart. **One week later** the patient sees his primary care physician, now reporting pain on the **right side as well** and a sense of **weakness**; the PCP orders a **lumbar spine MRI**. Reza reads the evolution against prior RLR cases — asymmetric processes can become symmetric as the underlying disease matures, and **symmetry itself reshapes the differential** — while cautioning that "weakness" is hard to separate from pain-limited effort. He would broaden rather than narrow: repeat the labs (including the inflammatory markers), and extend imaging to the pelvis and hips. The **non-contrast lumbar MRI** shows **severe degenerative disease with moderate canal stenosis**; interval labs (WBC, renal, LFTs) are unchanged, and **no inflammatory markers are repeated**.

The degenerative findings pull the workup toward the spine. The PCP refers to **orthopedic surgery**, who — to both hosts' admiration — decline to anchor: the absence of urinary symptoms and the *upper-body* distribution don't fit a simple stenosis, and the hips seem intrinsically normal on their exam, so before operating they send him to **neurology**. Reza uses this as a **[[Diagnostic Momentum|diagnostic-momentum]]** teaching point: radiographic degenerative disease is nearly universal in older patients, clinical-radiographic correlation for radicular/back surgery is notoriously poor, and the disciplined question is *who actually benefits* — not merely whether an image is abnormal. He also notes a limitation of the **non-contrast** study: gadolinium would reveal an inflamed or irritated cord through blood–brain-barrier breakdown and enhancement. Neurology (Aaron Berkowitz) finds a **normal exam** and concludes the problem is **not neurologic**.

Then the illness widens again. The patient develops **new neck pain**; a **cervical MRI** shows the same picture — degenerative disease with moderate canal stenosis — and another lab round (CBC, renal, LFTs, uric acid, **CPK**) is normal. He has visit after visit for severe pain, declines opioids, and gets by on cyclobenzaprine, Tylenol, and lidocaine patches. Rabih encounters him once by chance in the ER waiting room, leaning on a large wooden **cane**, hip and neck both tormenting him; the patient, worn down, declines further evaluation. Reza reasons over the accumulating data: the uric acid checks reflect **[[Gout|gout]]** risk from CLL cell turnover, but nothing looks gouty; and, invoking Mike Nagata's teaching about **two body parts far apart in space**, he notes that hip *and* neck with a spared thoraco-abdominal zone is **not a contiguous mechanical process** — it suggests something systemic (infiltrative, metastatic, or systemic inflammatory) reaching multiple joint territories, though CLL of only ~21k makes lymphocytic infiltration unlikely. No unifying **[[Illness Script|illness script]]** yet fits, so he too asks for more imaging (gadolinium-enhanced, targeting the symptomatic joints).

At about **three months** the syndrome finally declares itself. Seen by a junior ER physician (Dr. Permal), the patient now has the problem representation that was missing all along: **bilateral, morning-predominant hip stiffness with severe pain; neck stiffness and pain; and now severe bilateral shoulder pain and stiffness.** The physician notes that the *only* inflammatory markers ever obtained were the normal set at the initial visit. Reza's reasoning snaps into place: a **[[Symmetric Polyarthritis|symmetric]]** process of the proximal/axial regions with stiffness that improves with activity is "yelling" a rheumatic disease. His first instinct is **[[Rheumatoid Arthritis|rheumatoid arthritis]]** (RA is the paradigm of relief-with-activity), so he would obtain hand radiographs, **[[Anti-CCP Antibody|anti-CCP]]** and **[[Rheumatoid Factor|RF]]**, and activate a symmetric-polyarthritis schema with emphasis on the large joints and neck (RA notoriously involves the cervical spine).

The final twist inverts the RA anchor: the **hand and foot exams and radiographs are normal**, **RF and anti-CCP are negative**, but the **ESR is 70 and CRP is 50**, and the patient can barely move his shoulders and hips. The **diagnostic maneuver** that clinches it is **15 mg of prednisone** — and the response is dramatic. The diagnosis is **[[Polymyalgia Rheumatica|polymyalgia rheumatica]]**. Rabih delivers the case's title as its thesis: at presentation the patient had the *opposite* of PMR — **[[Oligomyalgia Non-Rheumatica|monomyalgia non-rheumatica]]** (one region, normal markers), an unsolvable problem; he slowly became *oligomyalgic* (more sites) but nobody restored the "**rheumatica**" — the inflammatory-marker axis — by re-checking ESR/CRP; only at three months did he become **truly polymyalgic *and* rheumatica**, with markedly elevated markers. Rabih is candid that he suspected the answer while the patient suffered through months of visits, MRIs, and referrals, and that a single repeat ESR/CRP — ordered by anyone — would have cracked it far sooner.

The rheumatology follow-up preserves the honest uncertainty. The consultant (Dr. Carol/Kara Al) favors **PMR** on the strength of the very high ESR and the proximal predominance but cannot fully exclude **seronegative [[Elderly-Onset Rheumatoid Arthritis|elderly-onset RA]]** with RF/CCP negative — the two overlap substantially, and elderly-onset RA (unlike younger RA) prominently involves the shoulders, hips, and axial skeleton; RA also responds to steroids, typically at lower doses (~5–10 mg) than PMR (~15 mg). The patient is transformed on 15 mg — mobile, functional, "a whole new person" — but the steroids drive a glucose to **580**, sending him back to Rabih for glycemic control (**[[Steroid-Induced Hyperglycemia|steroid-induced hyperglycemia]]**). Reza closes on the analogy to an earlier case (unilateral ataxia that only became diagnosable once it went bilateral and declared as EBV rhombencephalitis): some illnesses are **impossible to solve at first contact**, and arriving "too early" — before the pattern and the markers exist — is its own diagnostic trap.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Tempo as the organizing lesson ([[Illness Tempo|tempo-based reasoning]]).** The case is a study in an illness that is *unsolvable too early*: at day three there was one region and normal markers, so no pattern existed to match. The disciplined response was a follow-up plan, not a forced label — and the diagnosis became available only once the syndrome finished arriving at three months. Arriving "too early" (or too late) is itself a recognized trap.
- **Anchoring on degenerative imaging ([[Anchoring Bias|anchoring bias]]).** Two MRIs showing near-universal degenerative disease with canal stenosis pulled the workup toward the spine and generated ortho/neuro referrals; the abnormal-but-incidental images risked anchoring the whole team on a mechanical explanation the clinical picture never supported.
- **Diagnostic momentum, resisted ([[Diagnostic Momentum|diagnostic momentum]]).** Orthopedics is explicitly praised for *not* letting the stenosis carry them to an operation — recognizing poor clinical-radiographic correlation and asking "who actually benefits" instead of treating the scan.
- **Framing and problem representation ([[Framing Effect|framing]]).** The initial "atraumatic musculoskeletal hip pain" frame was accurate but too narrow to be solvable; the case turned only when the data supported a new **[[Symmetric Polyarthritis|symmetric proximal/axial]]** problem representation. Reza's meta-point: how you frame the problem matters more than how much you know.
- **Test interpretation and the repeat-the-test miss ([[Test Interpretation|test interpretation]]).** A single early-normal ESR/CRP is not a persistent-normal ESR/CRP; the failure to **[[Do-It-Again Sign|re-order the discriminating test]]** as the syndrome evolved was the central diagnostic gap. Interpreting a normal film (occult fracture can precede change) and preferring baseline markers over one-time values are the same discipline.
- **Treatment as a diagnostic test ([[Treatment as a Diagnostic Test|therapeutic trial]]).** The prednisone response is the confirmatory maneuver; its *magnitude and dose* also help separate PMR from seronegative elderly-onset RA, and it is what distinguishes the two clinically overlapping entities in the absence of serology.
- **Two-parts-apart heuristic (schema/[[Illness Script|illness script]]).** Nagata's "two body parts far apart in space, with a spared zone between" is used to reject a contiguous mechanical process and demand a systemic one — the reasoning bridge from degenerative-spine to systemic-inflammatory.
- **Diagnostic humility ([[Diagnostic Humility|humility]]).** Both hosts hold the honest tension: the answer was suspected while the patient suffered, the PMR-vs-EORA distinction may never fully resolve, and the case is offered as a common, humbling illustration of medicine unfolding in real time.

## Teaching Discussion

- Treat "too early" as a diagnostic state in its own right: when a complaint is confined to one region with a bland exam and normal markers, there may be no pattern to name yet, and the skilled move is a concrete follow-up plan rather than a premature label or an exhaustive early workup.
- Organize musculoskeletal pain with two branch points — traumatic vs atraumatic, then inflamed vs not — and respect that the hip is a deep joint where the exam (passive flexion, weight-bearing, range of motion) and inflammatory markers outperform inspection.
- Read morning-predominant stiffness that eases with activity as an inflammatory signal at any joint, the same way it flags inflammatory back pain, even before laboratory confirmation.
- When symptoms occupy two regions separated in space with a spared zone between, step off a contiguous mechanical explanation and consider a systemic process (infiltrative, metastatic, or systemic inflammatory) that can seed multiple joint territories.
- Recognize that near-universal degenerative changes on spine MRI in older adults are easy to over-weight; clinical-radiographic correlation for radicular and back surgery is poor, so ask who actually benefits rather than treating the image, and remember non-contrast studies miss enhancement of an inflamed cord.
- Re-order the discriminating test as an illness evolves: a single early-normal ESR/CRP does not exclude an inflammatory disease that has not yet declared itself, and an unexplained, persistently elevated ESR/CRP is often the clue that breaks an evolving polymyalgic syndrome open.
- Use symmetric proximal and axial pain with morning stiffness to activate a symmetric-polyarthritis schema; distinguish PMR from seronegative elderly-onset RA knowing they overlap substantially, that RF/CCP negativity and a very high ESR favor PMR, and that a dramatic response to low-dose prednisone both confirms the rheumatic disease and — by the dose required — helps separate the two.
- Screen every patient diagnosed with PMR for giant cell arteritis (jaw claudication, headache, visual symptoms, scalp tenderness), and anticipate and manage steroid-induced hyperglycemia, particularly in a diabetic patient started on ~15 mg of prednisone.

## Handbook Opportunities

- A **polymyalgia rheumatica** card — proximal/axial stiffness in older adults, ESR/CRP, EULAR/ACR features, low-dose steroid response, mandatory GCA screening, and steroid-taper/hyperglycemia caveats.
- A **PMR vs elderly-onset seronegative RA** card — overlapping features, distinguishing labs (ESR height, RF/CCP), axial/proximal involvement, and steroid-dose differences.
- An **inflammatory vs mechanical joint pain** card — morning stiffness/relief-with-activity, traumatic-vs-atraumatic and inflamed-vs-not branch points, and the deep-joint (hip) exam.
- An **"evolving/unsolvable-early syndrome"** card — when to defer a diagnosis, set follow-up, and *repeat the discriminating test* (serial ESR/CRP) rather than trust one early normal.
- A **degenerative spine imaging** card — incidental DJD/stenosis prevalence, poor clinical-radiographic correlation, "who benefits" before surgery, and contrast vs non-contrast MRI.
