## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A Neurology VMR built around a **73-year-old man** with **sudden-onset, constant horizontal diplopia for one day** and no other neurologic symptoms. Berkowitz and Valeria build the full **[[Anatomical Approach|diplopia pathway]]** — muscle → neuromuscular junction → cranial nerves III/IV/VI → cavernous sinus → brainstem nuclei/**[[Medial Longitudinal Fasciculus|MLF]]**/**PPRF** — and use the sudden tempo, patient age, and vascular risk factors to favor a microvascular cranial nerve palsy or small brainstem stroke over demyelinating disease. The exam, however, is more than a simple sixth or third nerve palsy: on left gaze, neither eye moves at all; on right gaze, the right eye abducts (with nystagmus) but the left eye fails to adduct — a pattern Valeria correctly recognizes as **one-and-a-half syndrome**, localizing precisely to the **left dorsal pons** (a combined left MLF plus left PPRF/CN VI nucleus lesion). MRI confirms a tiny dorsal pontine infarct, but Berkowitz uses the case to push back on reflexively labeling small deep infarcts "lacunar" and skipping an embolic workup, and the presenter closes with a memorable tour through the extended cranial-nerve-eponym family (eight-and-a-half, thirteen-and-a-half, and beyond) that these dorsal pontine syndromes belong to.

## Pearls

- **Binocular vs. monocular diplopia is settled with the cover test**: if covering either eye makes the double vision resolve, it's binocular (neurologic, misalignment); if double vision persists in one eye even when the other is covered, it's monocular (ophthalmologic).
- **"Diplopia is always acute"** (Marty Samuels, via Miller Fisher's tradition): ocular fusion holds an image together right up until the moment it doesn't, so a "sudden-onset" report doesn't by itself imply a sudden-onset lesion.
- **One-and-a-half syndrome** = an ipsilateral conjugate horizontal gaze palsy ("one," from a PPRF or CN VI nucleus lesion) plus an ipsilateral **[[Internuclear Ophthalmoplegia|internuclear ophthalmoplegia]]** ("half," from an MLF lesion) — the only preserved horizontal eye movement is abduction (often with nystagmus) of the eye contralateral to the lesion. It localizes with remarkable precision to a roughly 1 cm patch of dorsal pons.
- **The MLF is named for where it's *going*, not where it starts** — because it crosses almost immediately after leaving the CN VI nucleus, it spends nearly its entire course on the side of the CN III nucleus it's headed toward, which is the side that determines the localization of an internuclear ophthalmoplegia.
- **Age reframes the same eye-movement finding.** An isolated INO in a young patient raises demyelinating disease (MS); the same finding in an older patient with vascular risk factors raises a small ischemic brainstem lesion first.
- **Don't reflexively equate "small and deep" with "lacunar, no embolic workup needed."** Small dorsal brainstem infarcts fed by distal circumferential (AICA/PICA) branches may be embolic rather than classic hypertensive lipohyalinosis-type lacunar strokes; an embolic workup is low-risk and can be management-changing (anticoagulation) if it turns up atrial fibrillation or another source — and multiple simultaneous small infarcts should specifically raise suspicion for embolism over lacunar disease.
- **Even frequent screening can have a near-zero yield and still be worth doing.** Berkowitz notes he sends ESR/CRP for giant cell arteritis on nearly every older patient with new headache or visual symptoms but has personally never made the diagnosis without jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, or constitutional symptoms — a reminder that a screening habit's value isn't measured only by personal hit rate.

## Problem Representation

A 73-year-old man with hypertension and hyperlipidemia (recently non-adherent to his statin) presents with sudden-onset, constant horizontal binocular diplopia for one day, without fatigability, vision loss, prior similar episodes, headache, pain with eye movement, seizure, limb weakness, sensory symptoms, or bowel/bladder dysfunction, and with normal activities of daily living. Exam shows hypertension (155/90) with an otherwise normal general and systemic exam; cranial nerve exam shows binocular diplopia (resolving with covering either eye) with a specific pattern on horizontal gaze testing — on attempted left gaze, neither eye moves; on attempted right gaze, the right eye abducts with nystagmus while the left eye fails to adduct — with normal pupils, no facial weakness, normal tongue movement, and an otherwise entirely normal neurologic exam (5/5 strength throughout, normal sensation, 1+ reflexes, downgoing plantar responses). This pattern localizes to a combined left MLF and left PPRF/CN VI nucleus lesion in the left dorsal pons — one-and-a-half syndrome — most consistent, given the sudden tempo, age, and vascular risk factors, with an acute ischemic stroke; MRI confirms a small dorsal pontine infarct, and the patient is started on antiplatelet therapy with vascular risk factor optimization.

## Case Narrative

The presenter (referred to here as "A," a Neurology Clinical Research Team member at Washington University in St. Louis) gives the chief concern: a 73-year-old man with sudden-onset diplopia. Berkowitz asks Valeria how she approaches double vision, and she gives what he calls a "masterclass": first distinguishing monocular (ophthalmologic — refractive error, orbital structural lesion) from binocular diplopia (a true eye misalignment — her problem, as a neurologist), then, assuming binocular, walking through the extraocular muscles (innervated by CN III, IV, and VI — she correctly assigns VI to lateral rectus, IV to superior oblique, and III to the rest), the neuromuscular junction (flagging myasthenia gravis as a can't-miss diagnosis), the nerves' course through the cavernous sinus (where compromise of CN V, facial sensation, would be a clue), and on to the brainstem, where she notes "the rule of fours" places these nuclei in the pons and midbrain. Berkowitz praises the discussion and adds the key bedside branch point: ask whether covering one eye resolves the double vision (binocular/neurologic) or not (monocular/ophthalmologic) — Valeria correctly anticipates this question before he finishes asking it.

Berkowitz builds out the anatomy further, noting he prefers teaching the extraocular movement pathway from the bottom up: six muscles (four rectus, two oblique) per eye, innervated by CN III, IV (superior oblique), and VI (lateral rectus), interfacing via the neuromuscular junction, arising from brainstem nuclei (III/IV in the midbrain, VI in the pons) — "nucleus" here meaning the cell bodies of the neurons, not DNA. He then asks how the left CN VI nucleus (controlling left lateral rectus) and the right CN III nucleus (controlling right medial rectus) stay synchronized for leftward conjugate gaze; Valeria, after briefly confusing the term with the medial lemniscus, correctly identifies the **[[Medial Longitudinal Fasciculus|MLF]]** — a white-matter tract connecting the CN VI nucleus on one side to the CN III nucleus on the other, "a highway with exits" for CN VIII, III, IV, and VI, coordinating eye movements with each other and with head movements. Berkowitz completes the circuit: to look left voluntarily, the right frontal eye field signals the left **PPRF** (paramedian pontine reticular formation, the brainstem's horizontal gaze center), which tells the left CN VI nucleus to fire (abducting the left eye) and sends a crossing signal through the MLF to the right CN III nucleus (adducting the right eye). This lets him enumerate every possible lesion site for diplopia: frontal eye field (would cause gaze deviation, not diplopia — a different problem), PPRF (conjugate gaze palsy, not misalignment), or, further down, a cranial nerve, its brainstem nucleus or fascicle, the MLF, the neuromuscular junction, or the muscle/orbit itself — plus processes that can hit multiple of these at once, like a skull-base/meningeal process, Guillain-Barré/Miller Fisher variant, or cavernous sinus disease (thrombosis, carotid-cavernous fistula, sellar/pituitary pathology).

Asked about the significance of sudden onset, Valeria (correctly anticipating Berkowitz's next question) reasons that acute onset raises vascular causes — a brainstem stroke affecting ocular motor nuclei, or an aneurysm compressing a nerve (though she'd expect headache with the latter) — while also flagging that patients with myasthenia or Miller Fisher variant Guillain-Barré sometimes describe their symptoms as sudden simply because that's when they first noticed them, even though the underlying process isn't truly hyperacute. Berkowitz affirms this and credits her with independently rediscovering Marty Samuels's pearl that "diplopia is always acute," since ocular fusion holds until it suddenly doesn't. He runs through the tempo-consistent causes: a tiny brainstem stroke (MLF or ocular motor nerve/nucleus infarction — sometimes loosely called a "diabetic" cranial nerve palsy, though any vascular risk factor can cause it, and these are classically pupil-sparing); an acute cavernous sinus process (thrombosis, fistula, aneurysm rupture — though he'd expect more accompanying findings); or myasthenia, which can present as an apparently sudden fluctuating diplopia. Given the patient's age and this presentation, his first-pass leading hypothesis is a microvascular third, fourth, or sixth nerve palsy, or a small brainstem stroke.

The presenter gives the rest of the history: a 73-year-old man with one day of constant diplopia, no fatigability, no visual loss, no prior similar episodes, no headache or seizure, no weakness, tingling, or numbness, normal bowel/bladder function, no weight loss, and normal activities of daily living; an otherwise unremarkable review of systems; a history of hypertension (on amlodipine) and hyperlipidemia (recently non-adherent to his statin); a family history of stroke in his father; and no smoking, alcohol use, or allergies. Valeria asks about limb weakness (to screen for Guillain-Barré or a broader brainstem process) and, prompted by a chat comment, emphasizes that asking about fatigability is important for a neuromuscular junction disorder. Berkowitz asks specifically about pain around the eye or with eye movement (none present), and asks Valeria why he's interested in that — she correctly guesses **giant cell arteritis**. Berkowitz confirms this was one consideration: GCA classically causes headache, visual blurriness or loss, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, and constitutional symptoms, but can rarely cause an ocular motor cranial neuropathy (III, IV, and/or VI collectively — as distinct from "oculomotor," referring to III specifically), presumably via small-vessel vasculitic nerve infarction. He notes he screens for GCA (ESR/CRP) in essentially every older patient with new headache or visual symptoms but has never personally diagnosed a case without the more classic features — and that microvascular third or sixth nerve palsies themselves often do cause some periorbital pain, so its absence here doesn't fully exclude either. He also asks whether the double vision is side-by-side (horizontal) or stacked (vertical); the presenter confirms horizontal, which lets the group set aside the more complex vertical diplopia framework.

With the localization narrowed to horizontal diplopia, Berkowitz has Valeria enumerate every possible cause of diplopia specifically on rightward gaze: right lateral rectus, left medial rectus, right CN VI, left CN III, or the MLF connecting them — walking her through the logic that any of these five sites could produce the same symptom, and that the exam (or bedside maneuvers like the cover-uncover test or Maddox rod, for subtler misalignment) is needed to distinguish them. Asked which of these possibilities she'd favor given the patient's age and the acute tempo, Valeria reasons that a young patient (especially a young woman) would raise an **[[Internuclear Ophthalmoplegia|internuclear ophthalmoplegia]] (INO)** from demyelinating disease (MS), but given this patient's age, she'd favor an ischemic microvascular event — and, asked to choose between a third or sixth nerve lesion, initially picks III (she admits this may just reflect having seen more third nerve palsies). Berkowitz favors VI instead, reasoning that CN III innervates several muscles plus the lid, so a lesion selectively affecting only the fascicle serving the medial rectus (sparing everything else III does) would be an anatomically improbable "single strand of spaghetti" injury — though he cautions with a recent example of a patient whose apparent classic microvascular sixth nerve palsy turned out, on MRI, to be the "world's tiniest" stroke localized to the sixth nerve fascicle itself, so isolated ocular motor nerve infarcts aren't always purely peripheral.

The presenter gives the exam: blood pressure 155/90, otherwise normal vitals; alert and oriented; no ptosis; no facial weakness; normal tongue movement; binocular diplopia confirmed by resolution with covering either eye. On attempted left lateral gaze, both eyes remain in the midline (neither adducts nor abducts). On attempted right lateral gaze, the right eye abducts (with nystagmus on abduction) but the left eye fails to adduct. Pupils are small and reactive; strength is 5/5 throughout with normal sensation, 1+ reflexes, and downgoing plantar responses. Berkowitz confirms the pattern explicitly with the presenter before proceeding.

He then invites Valeria to localize this pattern, calling neuro-ophthalmology "the calculus of neurology." She recognizes it — a case her colleague Gabby had previously presented and diagrammed — as **one-and-a-half syndrome**: a complete conjugate gaze palsy to the left (the "one") plus, on attempted rightward gaze, failure of left eye adduction despite intact right eye abduction (the "half," an internuclear ophthalmoplegia). Berkowitz walks through the logic: on rightward gaze, the right CN VI nucleus and nerve are clearly intact (right eye abducts), but the expected simultaneous left eye adduction (normally driven by a signal crossing through the MLF from the right — actually left — sixth nerve region to the contralateral third nerve nucleus) fails, localizing a lesion to the **left MLF**. He clarifies the naming convention that can confuse people: because the MLF crosses almost immediately after leaving the CN VI nucleus, it spends nearly all of its course on the side of the CN III nucleus it's headed toward — which is the side used to localize the lesion, and Valeria correctly identifies it as the left MLF using her own mnemonic ("all MLF things are the third, the third, the third"). Since the patient also can't look left at all (a full conjugate gaze palsy, not just disconjugate diplopia), Berkowitz reasons through the additional structure needed: it's not a frontal eye field lesion (would cause other findings), so it must be the left PPRF or left CN VI nucleus itself — anatomically adjacent structures that can't be reliably distinguished clinically. This combination of an ipsilateral (left) MLF lesion plus an ipsilateral (left) PPRF/CN VI nucleus lesion, both in the left dorsal pons, is **one-and-a-half syndrome**.

Berkowitz credits **C. Miller Fisher** with first describing one-and-a-half syndrome (in a paper on neuro-ophthalmologic observations), alongside Miller Fisher syndrome and the classic lacunar stroke syndromes — noting Fisher was famous for both meticulous bedside observation and for giving his discoveries memorable names, a tradition passed to Berkowitz's own mentor, Marty Samuels, who trained under Fisher. He explains the anatomic basis for the related "eight-and-a-half syndrome" (one-and-a-half plus an ipsilateral facial, CN VII, palsy): the CN VII fascicle wraps directly around the CN VI nucleus in the dorsal pons, so lesions there often catch both. He confirms the localization is specifically the left dorsal mid-pons, and reviews general brainstem organization: cranial nerve nuclei live dorsally (motor nuclei III/IV/VI/XII near the midline, branchial motor nuclei V/VII/IX/X slightly more lateral, and sensory/special sensory nuclei V/VIII most lateral), while long tracts (corticospinal, etc.) run in the anterior/ventral brainstem — precise enough, he notes, that this localization would hold even against a technically "normal" MRI if the lesion were simply too small for the slice thickness to catch.

Asked for a cause, Valeria reasons through the differential: a very small stroke (favored, especially outside a young/demographic-atypical MS presentation), a demyelinating lesion (possible but felt less likely given age/tempo), or a compressive process like an AVM (though that wouldn't typically present this suddenly) — she "puts her coin down" on stroke, and Berkowitz agrees, noting that MS classically causes INO by affecting the MLF (a very common board-exam association) but that getting a full one-and-a-half syndrome from MS would require also hitting the adjacent gray-matter PPRF/CN VI nucleus, which he's never personally seen from a demyelinating lesion, though he doesn't rule it out entirely.

The presenter reveals the outcome: this was a real case seen a day or two after symptom onset; CT/MRI showed a very small "lacunar" infarct in the dorsal pons; basic labs were unremarkable aside from hyperlipidemia, and the patient had recently stopped taking his statin. He was started on antiplatelet therapy and risk-factor management.

Berkowitz uses the word "lacunar" as a launchpad for an extended teaching point: he's skeptical of reflexively applying the term, since it typically implies a hypertensive small-vessel (lipohyalinosis) mechanism and often leads clinicians to skip an embolic workup — but nothing about a small infarct's *size* on imaging actually proves its *mechanism*. He distinguishes the classic anterior/ventral pontine "wedge" infarcts (fed by paramedian pontine perforators branching at right angles off the basilar artery, more confidently hypertensive/lacunar) from small dorsal pontine dot-like infarcts like this one, which he suspects may more often be embolic — potentially fed by the distal ends of circumferential branches (AICA/PICA) rather than classic penetrators, since atherosclerosis tends to cluster at vessel branch points rather than at the very distal ends of small arteries. He notes this is a genuine ongoing debate with his own residents, and argues that since an embolic workup carries little downside and could meaningfully change management (anticoagulation, if atrial fibrillation or another embolic source is found), it's reasonable to pursue even for a "lacunar-appearing" infarct — especially since eye movements are evolutionarily critical enough that he doubts they'd be left dependent on the most distal, precarious end of a tiny penetrating vessel. He references a "neuromythology" paper by William Landau (circulated by his mentor Marty Samuels) titled something like "Wholly, Holy, and Holey," critiquing how far the lacunar concept has been stretched clinically beyond its original pathologic/autopsy basis. Valeria adds a supporting principle from a vascular neurology rotation: strictly, only a single small infarct should be called "lacunar" — multiple simultaneous small infarcts should specifically raise suspicion for an embolic source instead. Berkowitz agrees, and lists Fisher's classic (truly hypertensive-appearing) lacunar syndromes — pure motor hemiparesis (internal capsule), pure hemisensory loss (thalamic VPL/VPM), ataxic hemiparesis, and dysarthria-clumsy hand syndrome — as the ones he's more comfortable calling lacunar outright, while still keeping an open mind about mechanism for less classic small deep infarcts.

The presenter closes with a visual teaching addendum on the extended family of these dorsal pontine eponymous syndromes: one-and-a-half syndrome (PPRF/CN VI nucleus plus MLF); eight-and-a-half syndrome (plus ipsilateral CN VII); further combinations adding corticospinal tract involvement (contralateral hemiparesis) and additional cranial nerves (V, trigeminal; VIII, vestibulocochlear; bilateral VII) are described in case reports under names like thirteen-and-a-half, fifteen-and-a-half, and sixteen-and-a-half syndrome — most reported from lesions like pontine lymphoma rather than stroke, given how much brainstem real estate they require. Berkowitz, delighted, admits he knew of eight-and-a-half but not the further extensions, and closes by honoring Miller Fisher and Marty Samuels's shared legacy in stroke neurology and neuro-ophthalmology.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Illness Script|Recognizing a named pattern from partial data.]]** Valeria matches the described eye movement findings to the one-and-a-half syndrome script (learned from a colleague's prior case), then works backward to justify the localization anatomically rather than only pattern-matching superficially.
- **[[Base Rate|Age-based reframing of the same finding.]]** An isolated INO is explicitly weighted differently by patient age and sex — demyelinating disease in a young woman, ischemia in an older patient with vascular risk factors — illustrating how demographic base rates shape differential weighting for an identical exam finding.
- **[[Anchoring Bias|Resisting the pull of a reassuring imaging label.]]** Berkowitz explicitly pushes back against letting the word "lacunar" (assigned from imaging appearance alone) foreclose consideration of an embolic mechanism, arguing that size and location on a scan don't prove mechanism — a deliberate anti-anchoring move against premature closure from a comforting diagnostic label.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Owning a personally low hit rate on a routinely ordered test.]]** Berkowitz's candid admission that he screens broadly for GCA but has never personally confirmed a case without classic features models comfort with imperfect diagnostic yield from a reasonable screening habit.
- **[[Test Interpretation|A "normal" MRI wouldn't have changed the diagnosis.]]** Berkowitz notes that the clinical localization here is precise enough that even a false-negative MRI (missed by slice thickness) wouldn't undermine the diagnosis — an explicit statement of when clinical localization should outrank an imaging result.

## Teaching Discussion

- Use the cover test to separate monocular (ophthalmologic) from binocular (neurologic) diplopia before doing anything else.
- Build the full diplopia localization pathway: muscle → neuromuscular junction → CN III/IV/VI → cavernous sinus → brainstem nucleus/fascicle → MLF/PPRF, and remember a frontal eye field lesion causes gaze deviation (not diplopia) while a PPRF lesion causes a conjugate gaze palsy (not misalignment).
- Recognize one-and-a-half syndrome (ipsilateral conjugate gaze palsy plus ipsilateral INO) as a highly precise localizer to the dorsal pons (combined PPRF/CN VI nucleus + MLF), and know that the MLF is named/localized by its destination (the CN III nucleus side), not its origin.
- Remember "diplopia is always acute" and that CN III lesions rarely present as a pure, isolated partial deficit given how many muscles CN III innervates.
- Reframe an isolated INO by age/demographic: young patient → demyelinating disease; older patient with vascular risk factors → ischemia first.
- Don't let the label "lacunar" foreclose an embolic workup for a small deep infarct — distinguish classic hypertensive-pattern lacunar syndromes (pure motor, pure sensory, ataxic hemiparesis, dysarthria-clumsy hand, all described by Fisher) from small dorsal brainstem infarcts that may be embolic, and treat multiple simultaneous small infarcts as a red flag for embolism over lacunar disease.
- Know the extended cranial-nerve-eponym family built on one-and-a-half syndrome (eight-and-a-half with CN VII, and rarer case-report combinations reaching into the teens with additional cranial nerves or long-tract involvement), usually from tumor/lymphoma rather than stroke given the territory required.
- Screen broadly for giant cell arteritis in older patients with new headache/visual symptoms even if your personal confirmed-diagnosis rate is low, and remember "ocular motor" (CN III/IV/VI collectively) is a distinct term from "oculomotor" (CN III specifically).
