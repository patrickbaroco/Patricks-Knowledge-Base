## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A man wheeling his elderly father into the emergency department for routine follow-up is himself found obtunded minutes later — eyes closed, mouth open, unresponsive — after being completely normal moments before. Working through a hyperacute change in mental status with a neurologist joining by phone, the treating physician manages evolving generalized tonic-clonic status epilepticus while piecing together a rapidly worsening picture of hyponatremia, fluctuating glucose, and hypotension. A single CT image reveals acute blood products in the midline that are ultimately recognized — after first being mistaken for a ruptured anterior communicating artery aneurysm — as pituitary apoplexy, with the resulting acute secondary adrenal insufficiency explaining the refractory hypotension and prompting empiric steroids before any official imaging read, in a case where a neurologic catastrophe drove medical collapse rather than the reverse.

## Pearls

- When mental status becomes abnormal in seconds to minutes with a clearly witnessed last-known-normal, the differential narrows sharply to a short list: vascular catastrophe (ischemic stroke, intracerebral or subarachnoid hemorrhage), seizure (often unwitnessed, with the patient found post-ictal), an acute and typically injected or ingested toxic-metabolic insult, or trauma — ask "did he fall, or was he pushed?"
- Most ischemic strokes are focal, but a handful of specific vascular territories can produce a diffuse, encephalopathy-like or acutely comatose presentation without focal deficits: bilateral thalamic infarction (artery of Percheron occlusion), showers of small emboli, and basilar artery occlusion are the classic examples to keep in mind when a "stroke" doesn't look focal.
- "Seated syncope" — a syncopal episode that occurs while a patient is seated and therefore cannot achieve the recumbent position that would normally restore cerebral perfusion — can produce prolonged hypoxic jerking and twitching that mimics a seizure, and is worsened by bystanders trying to rouse the patient rather than laying them flat.
- Modern status epilepticus is defined as five minutes of continuous seizure activity, or repeated seizures without return to baseline consciousness between them — not the historical 30-minute threshold — and management should proceed in three simultaneous tracks: determining why the patient is seizing, treating the seizure itself, and securing the ABCs.
- In status epilepticus, escalate proactively rather than reactively: request the next tier of medication (e.g., a fosphenytoin or levetiracetam load) as soon as benzodiazepines are given, not after they've failed, since preparing and delivering the next agent can take 20-25 minutes even once ordered.
- A seizure is classified as "provoked" only when it has an acute and reversible cause (electrolyte disturbance, hypo/hyperglycemia, drugs or toxins, acute systemic infection, acute head trauma or hemorrhage); a fixed structural lesion such as an old stroke or a tumor causes technically "unprovoked" seizures even though a clear anatomic cause exists, because the lesion itself is neither acute nor reversible — the real question becomes why the seizure happened today rather than another day.
- Acute hemorrhage into the pituitary gland (apoplexy) can cause acute secondary adrenal insufficiency by disrupting ACTH production, producing hypotension that will not respond to fluids and pressors alone and requires empiric corticosteroid replacement, sometimes before any confirmatory imaging read is available.

## Problem Representation

A man accompanying his elderly father to the emergency department becomes acutely, hyperacutely obtunded (eyes closed, mouth open, unresponsive) within minutes of being entirely normal; evolving generalized tonic-clonic status epilepticus, progressive hyponatremia (sodium 123), fluctuating point-of-care glucose, and worsening hypotension accompany a single CT image showing acute midline blood products, ultimately recognized as pituitary apoplexy with resultant acute secondary adrenal insufficiency, in a patient found later to harbor a previously silent 1.8 cm non-functioning pituitary macroadenoma.

## Case Narrative

A man wheels his 90-year-old father into the emergency department for a routine follow-up of electrolyte issues and is asked to take his father up to the second-floor lab for blood work. Roughly ten minutes later, a rapid response is called at exactly that location. The treating physician, expecting the father to be the patient given his recent electrolyte issues, is startled to find instead that it is the son — now referred to as "the future patient" — who is affected: seated in a wheelchair, eyes closed, not moving, mouth wide open, being wheeled toward a bed.

Joining the discussion as a neurologist, the discussant frames the localization as straightforward given the depressed mental status — the problem is "upstairs," in the brain. The critical variable is time course: because the patient was witnessed completely normal only minutes earlier, this is a hyperacute (seconds-to-minutes) change, narrowing the differential to a short list — vascular causes (ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage), seizure (often unwitnessed, with the patient found post-ictal), an acute toxic-metabolic insult (usually not instantaneous unless something is injected or an error such as a wrong insulin dose occurs), or trauma, recalling a mentor's teaching maxim from case-conference exercises: "the patient was found in coma" — was he pushed, or did he fall? He further reasons that most ischemic strokes are focal rather than producing this kind of diffuse presentation, with a few specific exceptions worth remembering: showers of small emboli (a "starry night" pattern, for example after cardiac surgery), bilateral thalamic infarction from occlusion of the artery of Percheron (can cause acute coma), right MCA inferior-division strokes (agitated confusion, uncommon), rare ACA territory strokes, and basilar artery occlusion (also classically associated with sudden obtundation). He also raises "seated syncope" — a syncopal event that cannot resolve normally because the patient remains upright, producing hypoxic jerking that can be mistaken for a seizure and is often worsened by bystanders trying to rouse rather than recline the patient — and cardiac arrhythmia as a cause of sudden collapse.

Practically, the plan is to get the patient to CT quickly to rule out catastrophic structural lesions while sending basic labs and a fingerstick glucose to screen for toxic-metabolic causes in parallel.

The treating physician steps away briefly to check the record, discovering the patient is a veteran but that the local chart is empty, requiring a separate cross-system viewer that takes several minutes to load. By the time he returns, the patient is on a monitor with IV access, afebrile, with a normal heart rate and blood pressure in the 100s, saturating well on room air, and a point-of-care glucose of 205 (later re-checked at 90 roughly 45 minutes afterward) — a value not clearly explanatory on its own. As the physician begins a neurologic exam, the patient has a generalized tonic-clonic seizure in all four limbs with no clearly witnessed focal onset.

The neurologist walks through simultaneous management priorities: understanding why the patient is seizing (already in motion via labs and the planned head CT), treating the seizure itself, and securing the ABCs, since seizures carry real risk of arrhythmia, hypoxia, and aspiration (invoking sudden unexpected death in epilepsy, SUDEP, as an example of how seriously seizures should be treated as medical emergencies). He clarifies that status epilepticus is now defined as five minutes of continuous seizure activity, or repeated seizures without return to baseline consciousness between them — not the older 30-minute threshold. Treatment proceeds in three tiers: first, benzodiazepines (commonly underdosed; incremental doses toward roughly 0.1 mg/kg of lorazepam, typically given as repeated 2 mg IV boluses); second, if unresponsive, an antiseizure medication load (historically phenytoin, though a major trial found IV phenytoin, valproate, and levetiracetam similarly effective at terminating status, with levetiracetam — dosed around 40 mg/kg — now widely favored for its more favorable toxicity profile); and third, if seizures persist, escalation to intubation and induced coma with midazolam, propofol, or pentobarbital, alongside continuous EEG monitoring, since it becomes impossible to distinguish a post-ictal state from ongoing non-convulsive status epilepticus once clinical convulsions stop. He emphasizes escalating proactively — calling for the next tier of medication as soon as the current one is given, rather than waiting to see if it fails, since preparation of the next agent alone can take 20-25 minutes.

In real time, the patient is given 2 mg IV lorazepam for the ongoing seizure and a code stroke is called to expedite the fastest possible path to the CT scanner. The event coincides almost exactly with a shift change, leaving the on-call neurology resident roughly 30 minutes away and available only by phone. En route to CT, the patient seizes three more times. Anticipating the next step, the team calls ahead for fosphenytoin (roughly 20 mg/kg) and for anesthesia to be ready for a controlled intubation outside the scanner rather than inside it.

While in the scanner, point-of-care labs return: hemoglobin normal; sodium 123 with otherwise normal electrolytes; a repeat fingerstick glucose down to 90; and a blood pressure trending down through the 90s into the 80s and high 70s.

Asked whether the sodium of 123 is significant enough to explain the seizures, the neurologist introduces the distinction between provoked and unprovoked seizures: "provoked" specifically means an acute and reversible cause — hypo- or hyperglycemia, hyponatremia, hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, drugs or toxins, acute systemic infection or high fever, or acute head trauma or hemorrhage — while a fixed structural lesion (an old stroke, an area of encephalomalacia, a brain tumor) causes technically "unprovoked" seizures even with a clear anatomic explanation, because the lesion itself is neither acute nor reversible; the real question in such cases is why the seizure happened today rather than any other day. He notes that the rate of sodium decline likely matters more than the absolute value in determining seizure risk, and that hypernatremia, potassium derangements, and hypercalcemia are not classically associated with seizures the way hyponatremia, hypoglycemia/hyperglycemia, hypocalcemia, and hypomagnesemia are. He wants the head CT regardless, to assess for both acute and chronic structural lesions that might explain why this particular day was the tipping point.

A single CT image is shared. The neurologist identifies acute blood products in the midline, between the hemispheres rather than intraparenchymal, and — noting that arteries and the Sylvian fissures are visible, suggesting a CTA — raises concern for a ruptured anterior communicating artery aneurysm causing subarachnoid hemorrhage. Asked what else occupies that midline space, he reconsiders and lands on pituitary apoplexy: hemorrhage into a cystic or mass-like structure at that location, potentially explaining both the endocrine and hemorrhagic features of the case — a diagnosis he says he has never personally encountered despite knowing it as a textbook cause of thunderclap headache. This proves to be the correct diagnosis, later confirmed by MRI roughly ninety minutes afterward.

Discussion turns to imaging strategy: non-contrast CT is obtained first specifically to detect blood before contrast is given, and CTA is added because an acute stroke code pursues both simultaneously — an intracerebral hemorrhage may stem from an underlying vascular malformation or aneurysm relevant to neurosurgical planning, and an ischemic stroke workup requires assessing for a large-vessel occlusion amenable to thrombectomy. The "spot sign" (a focus of contrast extravasation within a hematoma, associated with expansion risk) is mentioned, along with reassurance that CTA contrast does not meaningfully worsen an existing intracranial hemorrhage, since the causative vessels are typically tiny penetrating vessels that tamponade themselves off once bleeding begins (barring severe coagulopathy).

With no neurologist or neuroradiologist physically present — coverage was entirely remote and the on-call resident was still en route — the treating physician describes leaning on cross-specialty pattern recognition rather than deep expertise in any single field to reach the diagnosis: worsening hyponatremia, a fluctuating glucose, hypotension, and blood in that specific midline location together suggested an evolving pituitary/adrenal crisis. Without an official radiology read, IV corticosteroids were given empirically for presumed acute secondary adrenal insufficiency, addressing hemodynamic instability that would otherwise have been managed with fluids and pressors alone.

Despite steroids, the patient continued in status epilepticus, was intubated and started on propofol, seized through the propofol infusion, and then developed a malignant bradyarrhythmia progressing to a PEA arrest; he was quickly resuscitated with epinephrine, achieved return of spontaneous circulation, and was subsequently loaded with phenobarbital, ultimately achieving EEG-confirmed seizure suppression. He was transferred to a specialized neurosurgical center and underwent transsphenoidal resection of a non-functioning pituitary macroadenoma (the majority of pituitary adenomas are non-secreting). He returned to the ED three days before this recording with expected post-operative complications — presyncope and difficulty with medication administration — and was doing well overall.

In discussion, both physicians reflect on how rarely a neurologic process drives a medical resuscitation of this severity, rather than the more typical pattern of medicine consulting neurology for diagnostic help; on the pituitary's anatomic proximity to the optic chiasm and the cavernous sinus (containing cranial nerves III, IV, VI, and V1/V2), noting the patient notably lacked any eye movement abnormalities despite this proximity; on the likelihood of resulting panhypopituitarism requiring hormone replacement (steroid, and likely thyroid and gonadal hormone, though this was not confirmed since subsequent care occurred elsewhere); and on the broader theme of a previously silent predisposing lesion — here, a 1.8 cm macroadenoma present at the time the patient was walking his father in normally — suddenly tipping into catastrophe.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The case is built as a deliberate "Sixth Sense"-style structure in which the initial read of the CT image (ruptured ACOM aneurysm) is a reasonable, pattern-matched first impression that is revised once additional anatomic and clinical context (the endocrine and hemodynamic picture) is incorporated — an explicit example of updating a first impression rather than anchoring on it.

The treating physician's diagnostic leap is framed as a triumph of cross-specialty bridging rather than deep specialty expertise: recognizing the "fingerprints" of a pituitary/adrenal crisis required connecting knowledge from neurology, endocrinology, and general internal medicine simultaneously, in the absence of any single specialist physically present.

The discussants explicitly note the dissociation between textbook ("Step 1") epidemiology and real-world clinical experience — pituitary apoplexy is taught as a classic, must-not-miss cause of thunderclap headache, yet the neurologist discussant had never encountered an actual case, illustrating that rehearsed illness scripts for rare conditions may not translate into confident real-time recognition until the moment demands it.

## Teaching Discussion

A hyperacute change in mental status (seconds to minutes, with a reliable last-known-normal) narrows the differential sharply to vascular catastrophe, seizure, an acute toxic-metabolic insult, or trauma; most ischemic strokes are focal, but bilateral thalamic infarction (artery of Percheron), basilar artery occlusion, and showers of small emboli are recognized exceptions that can produce diffuse, non-focal presentations including coma.

"Seated syncope" is a useful bedside concept: syncope that occurs while a patient remains upright cannot resolve through the normal recumbency-restores-perfusion mechanism, producing prolonged hypoxic jerking that mimics seizure and is worsened, not helped, by attempts to rouse the patient rather than lay them flat.

Status epilepticus is now defined as five minutes of continuous seizure activity or repeated seizures without return to baseline consciousness — not the older 30-minute threshold — and should be managed along three simultaneous tracks: identifying the cause, treating the seizure, and securing the ABCs, given the real risks of arrhythmia, hypoxia, aspiration, and SUDEP.

Status epilepticus treatment follows an escalating three-tier ladder — benzodiazepines, then an antiseizure medication load (levetiracetam, phenytoin/fosphenytoin, or valproate, roughly equally effective), then intubation with induced coma (midazolam, propofol, or pentobarbital) plus continuous EEG — and each tier should be requested proactively as soon as the prior one is given, since preparation time alone can take 20-25 minutes.

A seizure is "provoked" only when caused by an acute, reversible process (electrolyte derangement, hypo/hyperglycemia, drugs or toxins, acute infection or fever, acute head trauma or hemorrhage); a chronic structural lesion causes technically "unprovoked" seizures despite having a clear anatomic cause, and the tempo of any accompanying acute derangement (such as the rate of sodium decline) is likely more relevant to seizure risk than the absolute value.

Acute hemorrhage into the pituitary gland (apoplexy) can cause acute secondary adrenal insufficiency via loss of ACTH, producing hypotension that will not respond to fluids and pressors alone; when this diagnosis is suspected from imaging and clinical context, empiric corticosteroids should be given without waiting for a formal confirmatory read, given the low harm of empiric treatment relative to the risk of untreated adrenal crisis.

Reaching a difficult diagnosis without immediate access to the relevant specialist often depends less on deep expertise in any one field than on the ability to bridge partial knowledge across several domains (here, neurology, endocrinology, and general medicine) to recognize a coherent, if unfamiliar, clinical pattern.
