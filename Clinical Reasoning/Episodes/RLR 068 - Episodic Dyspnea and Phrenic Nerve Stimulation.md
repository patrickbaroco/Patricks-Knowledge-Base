## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A man with ischemic cardiomyopathy — EF once 10%, recovered to 55% on GDMT, with a **CRT-D** for LBBB — has two to three weeks of **out-of-the-blue, self-resolving dyspnea** and an odd chest sensation, seconds to minutes long, many times a day, with no trigger. Reza applies the [[Episodic Symptoms and Triggers|intermittent-symptoms-look-for-a-trigger]] rule (from the [[RLR 065 - Orthopnea and Compressive Goiter|goiter case]]) and, finding it non-exertional/non-positional/non-emotional, reasons that **short intermittent dyspnea is heart or lung**: in the heart only an **arrhythmia** is truly intermittent (→ [[Ambulatory ECG Monitoring|Ziopatch]]); in the lung, **bronchospasm**. Telemetry, troponin, and BNP are normal and no arrhythmia is captured — but he has an episode **in the ED**. Reza pivots to a **[[Thoracic Spasm|spasm]]** frame (the "arrow through the chest"): bronchospasm (lungs clear), [[Esophageal Spasm|esophageal spasm]], [[Coronary Vasospasm|coronary vasospasm]] (EKG paced, no ischemia). Then the decisive **[[Return to the Bedside|bedside observation]]**: with the gown off, his **abdominal wall is visibly quivering/fluttering** during the episode — a *muscle* phenomenon ([[Myokymia|myokymia]]). Cardiology interrogates the device: the **[[CRT|LV lead]] is intermittently stimulating the [[Phrenic Nerve Stimulation|phrenic nerve]]**, driving forceful diaphragmatic contractions that displace the abdominal muscles — "his own EMG inside him." Reprogramming fails; the LV lead is turned off, and a month later he returns in **heart failure** from lost resynchronization. Lesson: like the goiter, **you had to be there when the episode happened** — the clue was hidden under the gown.

## Pearls

- **Intermittent symptoms → look for a trigger, and refine the frame by pattern.** Short, self-resolving, out-of-the-blue episodes that are **non-exertional, non-positional, non-emotional** narrow the space; a resting exam is unhelpful because you aren't capturing the pathology — so **arrange to observe the episode** (walk them, monitor, or catch it in the ED). ([[RLR 068 - Episodic Dyspnea and Phrenic Nerve Stimulation|RLR 068]])
- **Short *intermittent* dyspnea is heart or lung — and in the heart, only arrhythmia is truly intermittent.** Pericardial, myocardial, and valvular disease don't switch on and off; **arrhythmia** does (age → premature beat → tachyarrhythmia), so reach for [[Ambulatory ECG Monitoring|ambulatory ECG]]. In the lung, the on/off cause is **bronchospasm** (not pneumonia/effusion/atelectasis/PE). Anemia, deconditioning, and body-habitus don't cause *intermittent* dyspnea. ([[RLR 068 - Episodic Dyspnea and Phrenic Nerve Stimulation|RLR 068]])
- **Sudden-on, sudden-off symptoms invite a [[Thoracic Spasm|spasm]] frame — "what's in the chest can spasm."** The vessels ([[Coronary Vasospasm|coronary vasospasm]]), the airways (bronchospasm), the esophagus ([[Esophageal Spasm|esophageal spasm]]), and — as this case adds — the **diaphragm** can all spasm; think of an "arrow through the chest," like renal colic in the flank. ([[RLR 068 - Episodic Dyspnea and Phrenic Nerve Stimulation|RLR 068]])
- **A quivering, fluttering muscle points to a [[Myokymia|muscle]] phenomenon, not a deep visceral one.** Visible abdominal-wall fluttering during the episode reframes "chest sensation" toward superficial muscle activity; consider metabolic/electrolyte, medication/exposure, and (less likely with age) hereditary myotonic causes — or an **external driver**. ([[RLR 068 - Episodic Dyspnea and Phrenic Nerve Stimulation|RLR 068]])
- **A CRT/biventricular device can stimulate the [[Phrenic Nerve Stimulation|phrenic nerve]] — a real, well-described complication.** The **LV lead** sits near the left phrenic nerve, so lead position/anatomy can cause intermittent phrenic capture and **forceful diaphragmatic contractions** (felt as dyspnea/chest oddness; seen as abdominal-wall fluttering — the diaphragm displacing the abdomen). Interrogate the device; reprogramming may fix it, but disabling the LV lead sacrifices resynchronization. ([[RLR 068 - Episodic Dyspnea and Phrenic Nerve Stimulation|RLR 068]])
- **You often have to *be there* for an episodic diagnosis.** As in the [[RLR 065 - Orthopnea and Compressive Goiter|goiter case]] (cough only when supine), the answer here appeared only during an episode and only once the **gown was off** — the [[Return to the Bedside|bedside]] observation, not a test, was the key. ([[RLR 068 - Episodic Dyspnea and Phrenic Nerve Stimulation|RLR 068]])

## Problem Representation

A middle-aged man with hypertension, diabetes, and ischemic cardiomyopathy (prior EF 10%, recovered to ~55% on guideline-directed medical therapy) with a CRT-D placed for a left bundle branch block presents with two to three weeks of sudden, unprovoked, self-resolving episodes of dyspnea and an odd chest sensation lasting seconds to minutes, multiple times daily, and is entirely well between episodes with normal vitals and exam. Telemetry over hours shows no arrhythmia, and troponin, BNP, CBC, renal panel, and chest x-ray are normal; during a witnessed ED episode his cardiopulmonary exam is normal but his abdominal wall is visibly quivering, and a paced EKG shows no ischemia. Device interrogation reveals the left ventricular lead intermittently stimulating the phrenic nerve, causing forceful diaphragmatic contractions (the abdominal fluttering) — the diagnosis — with reprogramming unsuccessful, LV-lead deactivation relieving the symptom, and subsequent heart failure one month later from loss of cardiac resynchronization.

## Case Narrative

Rabih presents a case he frames as a sequel to the [[RLR 065 - Orthopnea and Compressive Goiter|episodic-cough/goiter]] case. The patient reports two to three weeks of **out-of-the-blue severe dyspnea** with an "odd chest sensation," lasting seconds to minutes, resolving on its own, and recurring multiple times a day. Reza invokes the earlier lesson — **intermittent symptoms mean look for a trigger** — and mentally tests posture, exertion, and exposure. He refines the frame to **short, self-resolving, intermittent** episodes and reasons that such dyspnea is **heart or lung** (anemia, deconditioning, and body-habitus don't produce *intermittent* symptoms). In the **heart**, only an **arrhythmia** switches on and off (not pericardial/myocardial/valvular disease); aging can bring a premature beat triggering a tachyarrhythmia, so he'd consider a **[[Ambulatory ECG Monitoring|Ziopatch]]** to capture an event. In the **lung**, the on/off cause is **bronchospasm**. He also names **panic attack** (spontaneous, doom-tinged, short) but the flavor doesn't fit.

The history adds a striking cardiac course: HTN, diabetes, ischemic cardiomyopathy with an EF once **10%**, recovered to **~55%** on GDMT, and a **CRT-D** for LBBB. Vitals and exam are normal, and he is asymptomatic at the visit. Reza sets a clean frame — **non-exertional, non-emotional, non-positional intermittent dyspnea** — and notes the exam can't help because it isn't capturing the pathology. He'd **walk the patient**, get an EKG and chest x-ray and basic labs, and, if unrevealing, **discharge with a Ziopatch** covering arrhythmia and bronchospasm.

The case turns because he has an **episode in the ED**. He'd been on **telemetry for hours** with **no captured arrhythmia**, and troponin, BNP, CBC, renal panel, and chest x-ray are normal. During the episode he is **visibly uncomfortable**, says his chest "feels very weird," and feels like he's gasping. Reza pivots to a **[[Thoracic Spasm|spasm]] frame** — sudden on, sudden off — picturing "an arrow through the chest": **bronchospasm** (excluded by clear lungs), **[[Esophageal Spasm|esophageal spasm]]** (can't be excluded by exam), and **[[Coronary Vasospasm|coronary vasospasm]]** (get an EKG for ST changes). The repeat EKG shows a **paced rhythm without ischemia**, and the repeat troponin is negative.

Then the decisive **[[Return to the Bedside|bedside observation]]**. Because the initial EKG had been done in triage and the gown was now covering him, the team removes the gown to repeat it during the episode — and notices his **abdominal wall quivering/fluttering** while he reports the chest sensation. Reza reframes: this is a **superficial [[Myokymia|muscle]] phenomenon**, not a deep visceral spasm. He runs a muscle-quivering differential — **metabolic/electrolyte** (e.g., potassium affecting depolarization), **medication/exposure**, and **hereditary myotonic disease** (less likely at this age, favoring a *secondary* disturbance) — and would review meds, supplements, and electrolytes.

The reveal comes from cardiology at the bedside within 30 minutes. Prompted that a **diagnostic test** clinches it, Reza intuits an **external electrical driver** — the **device** — and cardiology's **interrogation** confirms it: the **LV lead is intermittently stimulating the phrenic nerve**, causing random **forceful diaphragmatic contractions** so strong that the **diaphragm displaces the abdominal wall** (the observed fluttering) — "his own EMG inside him." Turning the pacemaker off stops the quivering. Reprogramming to avoid phrenic capture **fails**, so the **LV lead is disabled** — and **one month later he returns in heart failure** from lost resynchronization (which is how Rabih later met him). Rabih notes this is a **rare but well-described** complication (more often described with dual-chamber pacing; the **left phrenic nerve courses right next to the LV**), driven by lead position/anatomy rather than necessarily malposition.

Both close on the recurring lesson: as with the [[RLR 065 - Orthopnea and Compressive Goiter|goiter]] (cough only when supine), an **episodic diagnosis often requires being present for the episode** — and here the clue was literally **hidden under the gown**, on the abdomen (unprotected by the rib cage), where the fluttering diaphragm showed itself. The case also **extends the thoracic-spasm list**: vessels, airways, esophagus — and now the **diaphragm** — can spasm (and, Rabih adds, may even present as **hiccups**). Reza celebrates it as reasoning by **[[Analogical Reasoning|analogy]]** and bedside observation over technology, linking it to prior favorites (dialysis steal syndrome; a hiccup-as-CLL case).

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|Pattern-refined schemas]].** "Intermittent dyspnea = heart or lung," then "only arrhythmia is intermittent in the heart," then a **spasm** frame — each step prunes the differential by the *pattern* (episodic, self-resolving) rather than the organ alone.
- **[[Analogical Reasoning|Spasm by analogy]].** The "arrow through the chest / renal-colic" image transports the spasm concept across vessel, airway, esophagus, and finally diaphragm — the analogical move that made an exotic diagnosis reachable.
- **[[Return to the Bedside|Capturing the episode]].** The resting exam was worthless; the diagnosis lived in the *episode*, and specifically in a body region revealed only when the gown came off — a pure bedside win echoing the goiter case.
- **[[Anatomical Approach|Superficial vs deep localization]].** Reclassifying the "chest" sensation as a **muscle** (superficial) rather than visceral (deep) phenomenon, then localizing to diaphragm/phrenic, was the pivot from "spasm of an organ" to "stimulated muscle."
- **[[Tempo-based reasoning|Episodic tempo as data]].** The seconds-to-minutes, many-times-daily, self-resolving pattern is itself the strongest clue, steering away from fixed structural disease toward an intermittent electrical/spastic driver.
- **Technology as confirmation, not substitute.** Device interrogation clinched it, but only after bedside observation generated the hypothesis — a reminder that the exam sets up the test.

## Teaching Discussion

- Treat intermittent symptoms as a prompt to find a trigger and to observe the episode; refine the frame by whether episodes are exertional, positional, or emotional, recognizing a resting exam won't capture the pathology.
- Reason that short intermittent dyspnea is heart or lung, that only arrhythmia is truly intermittent among cardiac causes (use ambulatory ECG), and that bronchospasm is the on/off pulmonary cause.
- Apply a spasm frame to sudden-on/sudden-off thoracic symptoms — coronary vasospasm, bronchospasm, esophageal spasm, and diaphragmatic spasm — analogous to renal colic.
- Reclassify a quivering, fluttering sensation as a superficial muscle phenomenon and work its causes (metabolic/electrolyte, medication/exposure, hereditary myotonic, or an external electrical driver).
- Know that CRT/biventricular devices can stimulate the phrenic nerve (LV lead near the left phrenic nerve), causing diaphragmatic contractions felt as dyspnea and seen as abdominal fluttering; interrogate the device, and recognize that disabling the LV lead forfeits resynchronization.
- Remember that episodic diagnoses often require being present for the episode, and that exposing the relevant body region (here, the abdomen) can reveal the clue.
