## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Two triage-room back pain patients, presented as if seen simultaneously, seem identically reassuring at first glance. A general red-flag framework for back pain is applied to both: a 72-year-old woman with two weeks of gradually improving lumbar pain after gardening is safely discharged, while a 54-year-old man with a month of worsening thoracic pain — on closer questioning, actually a 10-year truly chronic and progressively worsening course — prompts imaging that ultimately reveals diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH), a chronic, non-infectious, non-malignant cause of unrelenting back pain that both physicians initially struggled to recall.

## Pearls

- The vast majority (roughly 95% or more) of back pain is a benign, self-limited process ("MSK, not otherwise specified") that improves within about four weeks; the diagnostic task is not to name every case precisely but to reliably screen for the minority that carries a red flag.
- Back pain red flags come in two tiers: "hot" red flags (a focal neurologic deficit) demand emergency-level urgency — STAT imaging, specialist involvement, and consideration of immediate treatment to preserve neurologic function — while "lukewarm" red flags (signs of inflammation such as fever, weight loss, or elevated inflammatory markers; a history of cancer; sudden onset suggesting disc herniation, fracture, or vascular event; and non-lumbar location) should prompt a more urgent, thorough workup without necessarily being a same-minute emergency.
- More than half of patients with a spinal epidural abscess do not have a fever; the combination of back pain and an elevated ESR, rather than fever alone, should be the clue that triggers concern for this diagnosis.
- Never let an incidental detail in the history (a recent bout of gardening, a minor twist of the back) explain away a genuine red flag; a benign-sounding precipitant does not, by itself, account for systemic inflammatory signs or a focal neurologic deficit.
- A single dose of ketorolac (IM, commonly 15 mg — with no demonstrated efficacy difference versus 30 mg) has essentially no meaningful contraindications outside of anaphylaxis; a one-time dose will not cause a GI bleed, heart failure, or renal failure, so pre-dose labs are unnecessary, and relief is often apparent within 30 minutes.
- Reflexively dismissing "chronic" back pain as automatically benign is a trap: while most dangerous causes of back pain (infection, malignancy) present acutely or subacutely and become implausible over a truly unrelenting 10-year course, a small set of specific, non-infectious, non-malignant conditions can cause genuinely chronic, progressive back pain — and deserve their own illness scripts rather than being lumped into "chronic, therefore benign."
- Diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH) causes chronic, progressive spinal pain from exuberant ossification bridging the anterior aspect of the vertebral bodies; on imaging, this ossification characteristically spares the side directly adjacent to the aorta, since the vessel's pulsation mechanically inhibits new bone formation there — a striking, specific imaging clue once recognized.

## Problem Representation

**Patient 1:** A 72-year-old woman with a lifelong history of back trouble presents with two weeks of lower back pain following increased garden work, gradually improving on ibuprofen and Tylenol, without red flags; a single dose of ketorolac and reassurance, grounded in the patient's own long familiarity with her condition, safely resolve the visit.

**Patient 2:** A 54-year-old man with a decade-long history of back pain from prior military parachute-jump injuries presents with a month of markedly worsening pain that, on further history, represents the leading edge of a truly chronic, progressively worsening 10-year course rather than a new acute process; the pain localizes specifically to the thoracic spine (a lukewarm red flag), and CT imaging ultimately reveals diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH), with anterior vertebral ossification characteristically sparing the side adjacent to the aorta.

## Case Narrative

To illustrate a real-world diagnostic challenge — distinguishing a tricky case from a simple one when seeing a high volume of patients with the same chief concern — the presenting discussant reconstructs two real patients (seen at different times, but presented here as though evaluated back-to-back) who both walked into triage with back pain and, at first glance, seemed to carry the same low-intensity "vibe."

**Patient 1** is a 72-year-old woman who appears comfortable, sitting calmly, with normal vital signs. She describes lifelong intermittent back trouble, with the last two weeks notably worse after helping her husband with yard work. She has been taking ibuprofen and Tylenol and reports gradual improvement, just not as quickly as she'd like. She denies fevers, chills, weight loss, or any other symptoms. She is triaged to an urgent care room and given a ketorolac injection for pain relief, with plans for a more detailed evaluation shortly after.

**Patient 2** is a 54-year-old man with a decade of back pain dating to rough parachute landings during his military service. He reports his pain has become much worse over the past month — bad enough that his wife insisted he seek care. He has been taking ibuprofen with some relief but finds the pain increasingly bothersome. He denies fevers, chills, weakness, or other systemic symptoms, and gives off the same low-intensity impression as the first patient. He, too, is triaged to a room for ketorolac.

Before returning to each patient in more detail, the responding discussant lays out a general framework for back pain: roughly 95% or more is a benign, self-limited process that will improve within about four weeks — the back-pain equivalent of a "fever, not otherwise specified." The essential task is recognizing red flags, a concept broadly applicable across many common symptoms (chest pain, jaundice, the red eye, headache), not unique to back pain. "Hot" red flags — a focal neurologic deficit — represent a true emergency requiring STAT imaging, specialist involvement, and consideration of immediate treatment (such as steroids) to preserve neurologic function, out of concern for spinal cord compression. "Lukewarm" red flags still warrant urgency, though not the same immediacy: signs of inflammation (weight loss, fever, or abnormal inflammatory labs such as leukocytosis or an elevated ESR — noting that more than half of spinal epidural abscess patients lack fever, making back pain plus an elevated ESR the more reliable clue), a history of cancer (given the propensity of certain cancers to metastasize to the spine via Batson's venous plexus), sudden onset (raising concern for disc herniation, vertebral fracture, or a vascular event such as spinal infarct), and non-lumbar location (since the reassuring majority of benign back pain is lumbar). He cautions against explaining away a genuine red flag with an incidental detail from the history, such as a recent awkward movement, when that detail doesn't actually account for the red flag itself.

On first pass, neither patient has an obvious red flag. The only distinguishing detail is trajectory: Patient 1 reports gradual improvement (just not fast enough for her liking), while Patient 2 reports progressive worsening over the past month, culminating in his wife's insistence that he seek care. Based on this alone, the responding discussant's plan is a brief spinal exam and quick neuro check for both, with Patient 1 requiring little beyond a reminder to avoid overusing NSAIDs (with consideration of acid suppression if needed) and Patient 2 warranting somewhat more concern given his lack of improvement.

The presenting discussant then reveals additional detail that flips the expected concern: on closer questioning, Patient 1 mentions that she feels cold whenever she exerts herself — a detail immediately flagged (in an explicit callback to the immediately preceding episode's pacemaker case) as a deliberate joke rather than real clinical information. In reality, her exam is completely normal, she improves significantly with ketorolac, and she is discharged with lidocaine patches. Discussing the encounter, the responding discussant confirms his approach to single-dose ketorolac (typically 15 mg IM, with no demonstrated efficacy difference from 30 mg): essentially free of contraindications outside of anaphylaxis for a one-time dose, with no need for prior labs and often noticeable relief within about 30 minutes. Rather than generic return precautions, he tells this patient — who has lived with her condition for decades and knows it better than anyone new to her care — simply to return if she feels she needs to, respecting her long-standing familiarity with her own baseline.

Patient 2's visit unfolds differently. On more detailed questioning, the presenting discussant realizes that what was initially described as "a month of worsening pain" is actually the leading edge of a truly chronic story: the patient has, in fact, been getting worse every day for about 10 years, and the past month simply represents the point at which the pain became severe enough to meaningfully limit his function. Asked to localize the pain, he points specifically to his thoracic spine. The rest of his exam is unremarkable.

The responding discussant identifies two "lukewarm" red flags here: a specific, point-localizable, non-lumbar (thoracic) location, and a stepwise escalation in severity rather than a smooth, gradual worsening. He considers two possibilities: either the patient's chronic back pain now has a new acute component superimposed on it, or this new thoracic pain represents an entirely separate, subacute process layered on top of his baseline chronic pain. He recommends an anatomical approach to thoracic back pain, starting with palpation and percussion of the spine (percussion tenderness — testing away from the suspected area first, then working toward it with adequate firmness — being a classic clue to vertebral osteomyelitis), while remaining open to a paraspinal rather than truly spinal process: skin and soft tissue (such as zoster sine herpete, a dermatomal pain syndrome without a visible rash), muscle (myositis, since a simple muscle spasm would be unlikely to persist this long), or referred pain from a thoracic organ. His initial workup, approached as an ED physician, is an x-ray of the thoracic spine to screen for fracture, with a low threshold to send inflammatory markers to evaluate for an inflammatory or infectious spinal or perispinal process.

Reflecting afterward (having not had an answer in real time), the presenting discussant articulates an important gap: nearly every dangerous cause of back pain that clinicians are trained to fear — cancer, osteomyelitis, and the like — is an acute or subacute process, so a truly chronic, unrelenting, progressively worsening story (confirmed here by a patient who, as a reliable historian, was unambiguous that this was not an acute-on-chronic mismatch) tends to get reflexively dismissed as benign, much as a headache present every day for 10 years would be recognized as a primary headache syndrome rather than a dangerous secondary cause. Yet there exists a specific, if small, category of genuinely chronic, progressive, non-infectious, non-malignant causes of back pain that deserve their own illness scripts.

Reasoning through this live, the responding discussant rules out infection and malignancy as implausible over a truly unrelenting decade-long course (barring a rare, initially indolent tumor that later becomes locally destructive) and considers osteoporosis — common, but typically most symptomatic at weight-bearing sites (hips, knees, lumbar spine) and usually painless unless complicated by an acute fracture (which would itself present as an acute-on-chronic event, not smooth progression) — and rheumatoid arthritis, which he associates primarily with cervical spine involvement, sparing the thoracic and lumbar regions.

In real time, the presenting discussant's own leading hypothesis was chronic osteoporosis with a cascade of microfractures. Because plain x-ray sensitivity for compression fractures is only around 60%, he goes straight to CT of the thoracic spine, expecting to confirm osteoporotic compression fractures.

The CT is shared as images. On first look at the coronal view, the responding discussant misidentifies a lucent, "punched-out" area in a thoracic vertebra, initially suspecting a lytic lesion — an incorrect read. The presenting discussant redirects attention: scanning down the coronal view from the cervical spine into the thoracic spine, the vertebral bodies themselves are structurally normal, but there is a large area of fused, bridging ossification along the anterior aspect of the vertebrae, beginning at the thoracic level. On the axial and sagittal views, the aorta sits anteriorly and to the left of the vertebral column, and the anterior ossification is present only on the right side — completely absent on the left, where the aorta lies.

This asymmetric pattern is diagnostic of diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH) — a diagnosis the responding discussant reaches largely by process of elimination and a quick search, acknowledging he'd never developed a solid illness script for it despite having encountered the term before. The presenting discussant confirms this, describing the case as a genuine, extended personal learning exercise (many hours spent reading and reflecting afterward). There is no strongly effective treatment for DISH, but the patient was nonetheless relieved simply to receive a name for his condition after a decade without one. On re-examination after the diagnosis, the presenting discussant identifies additional hyperostosis at the patient's Achilles tendons, elbows, and shoulders — further supporting a systemic enthesopathic process. Closing the case, he explains the specific reason the ossification spares the side adjacent to the aorta: the vessel's pulsation mechanically inhibits new bone formation immediately next to it, a detail he considers "beautiful pathophysiology" once understood.

| Additional hyperostosis found at Achilles tendons, elbows, and shoulders on re-exam | Confirms a systemic enthesopathic process consistent with DISH rather than an isolated spinal finding |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The case explicitly models a reflexive bias: clinicians are trained to treat "chronic" as synonymous with "benign" when screening for dangerous causes of back pain, because the classic feared etiologies (infection, malignancy) are acute or subacute — but this heuristic has a blind spot for genuinely chronic, progressive, non-infectious, non-malignant conditions like DISH, which require their own dedicated illness script rather than being grouped indiscriminately with "chronic, therefore reassuring" pain.

The presenting discussant's initial hypothesis (osteoporotic compression fractures) and the responding discussant's initial misreading of the CT (a lytic "punched-out" lesion) both illustrate anchoring on more common or more feared patterns before the actual, less familiar imaging pattern was correctly recognized — resolved not by first-pass pattern matching but by deliberately, systematically scanning the entire visible spine for asymmetry.

The deliberate, in-episode callback joke (Patient 1 "getting cold" with exertion, mimicking the prior episode's real finding) reinforces, through humor, the importance of not over-pattern-matching a symptom from a recent case onto an unrelated new patient without genuine supporting data.

## Teaching Discussion

Roughly 95% of back pain is benign and self-limited; the primary diagnostic task is reliably screening for red flags rather than reaching a precise etiology in every case, using a two-tier framework — "hot" (focal neurologic deficit, demanding emergency-level urgency) and "lukewarm" (inflammatory signs, cancer history, sudden onset, non-lumbar location, all warranting a more thorough but less immediately emergent workup).

More than half of spinal epidural abscess patients lack fever; an elevated ESR combined with back pain, rather than fever alone, should trigger consideration of this diagnosis.

A single dose of ketorolac carries essentially no meaningful contraindications outside of anaphylaxis and does not require baseline labs; individualized return precautions (leaning on a patient's own long-standing familiarity with a chronic condition) can be more appropriate than generic instructions.

When a patient's back pain story sounds like recent worsening, actively probe whether it is truly new/subacute or actually the leading edge of a much longer, genuinely chronic and progressive course — the distinction changes the differential entirely, since most feared causes of back pain (infection, malignancy) become implausible over a true decade-long unrelenting course, while a specific set of chronic, progressive, non-infectious conditions (such as DISH) should instead be considered.

Diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis causes chronic, progressive spinal pain from ossification bridging the anterior vertebral bodies, with a specific, recognizable imaging signature — ossification that spares the side adjacent to the aorta due to the vessel's pulsatile inhibition of bone formation — and is often accompanied by enthesopathy at other tendon insertion sites (Achilles, elbows, shoulders).
