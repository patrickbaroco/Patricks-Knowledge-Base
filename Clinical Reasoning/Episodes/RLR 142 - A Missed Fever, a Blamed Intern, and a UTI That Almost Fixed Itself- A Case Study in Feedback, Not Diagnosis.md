## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Reza takes over a weekend medicine consult on a 76-year-old kidney transplant recipient with a subacute stroke, consulted only for diabetes management — and, reviewing the vitals independently before rounds, notices a subtle low-grade fever trend and a resolving leukocytosis that his excellent intern never presented. What follows is less a diagnostic mystery than a case study in how oversights actually happen in a hospital: an initially-dismissible UA (asymptomatic bacteriuria, correctly not treated), a bedside conversation with the patient's wife that surfaces unreported dysuria through a condom catheter, and — on deeper chart review — the discovery that the patient had been spiking a true, unambiguous fever of up to 102°F all weekend, missed simultaneously by the primary neurology team, the medicine consult service, transplant nephrology, and nursing. Reza uses the case to model calibrated, non-punitive feedback across an entire hierarchy of colleagues, while Robbie adds a humbling final twist: between the two data points that mattered, no antibiotic had been given at all, and the patient's own immune system had already cut the fever and white count roughly in half unassisted.

## Pearls

- Independently reviewing a patient's raw data (vitals, labs) before hearing a trainee's presentation is a habit worth cultivating — it lets an attending catch trends a busy or still-developing learner may not have retrieved or may have undervalued.
- When a learner fails to mention an important finding, distinguish between two different failure modes before responding: they never retrieved the information at all, versus they retrieved it but didn't recognize its significance (forgot it, or judged it unimportant) — how you probe for this should be calibrated to how well you know the learner (direct and casual with someone you know well; more exploratory and open-ended with someone new).
- A trainee's intense self-blame after being shown a missed finding is a favorable prognostic sign, not a problem to be soothed away — the far more concerning response is apathy. The goal isn't to eliminate a learner's emotional investment but to help them modulate its intensity relative to the actual stakes of the miss.
- Explicitly teach the distinction between a minor oversight (missing a mild fever) and a consequential error (causing direct patient harm through a wrong action) — both deserve reflection, but only proportional self-scrutiny.
- When a new fever is discovered in a hospitalized patient with a distant, unrelated presenting problem (like a subacute stroke), establish the actual timeline before assuming causation: a fever temporally close to a stroke raises concern for an endovascular source (like infective endocarditis causing a febrile/septic stroke), while a fever appearing well after the inciting event, in a hemodynamically stable patient, should be approached like any new hospital-acquired fever.
- In immunosuppressed hosts, the ability to localize an infection through history is often impaired, but physical exam findings (crackles, rash, nasal discharge) remain a reliable source of localizing information — go examine the patient directly rather than relying solely on their symptom report.
- A UA showing pyuria, bacteria, and positive leukocyte esterase but a negative nitrite, in a patient without urinary symptoms, does not automatically mean urinary tract infection requiring treatment — recognize asymptomatic bacteriuria and apply the standard exceptions (pregnancy, prior to a urologic procedure) before treating; being immunosuppressed and elderly does not, by itself, mandate treatment.
- Always ask a family member or caregiver directly whether the patient has mentioned symptoms to them — a patient who denies symptoms to the care team may still be reporting real discomfort (like dysuria into a condom catheter) to a spouse who simply wasn't asked.
- Dysuria in an older man without another clear source should prompt consideration of an external cause (a perineal/genital exam, keeping HSV in mind particularly in immunocompromised hosts) and, since fever is atypical for simple cystitis, consideration of prostatitis — a rectal exam is reasonable to keep "in the back pocket" for suspicious cases even if not always performed.
- Empiric antibiotics for a non-septic, non-neutropenic, non-decompensated-cirrhosis patient can typically wait for a UA/culture to result — treating reflexively based on risk factors alone, without meeting empiric-treatment criteria, isn't necessary when there's time to get better data.
- Be cautious trusting a urine culture (or its absence) obtained by another service for a different indication — a UA sent only to assess urine specific gravity, without a symptom-driven reason to reflex to culture, is a different and less reliable data point than a culture sent specifically to investigate a urinary complaint.
- Switching a condom catheter to a design with a lower catheter-associated UTI risk is a simple, easily overlooked nursing-communication-order fix worth remembering in catheterized patients with recurrent urinary infections.
- Mixed urine culture growth (e.g., E. coli plus E. faecalis) doesn't necessarily require broadening coverage to treat both organisms — a lower-virulence isolate can represent colonization rather than true pathogenic co-infection, and it's reasonable to hold a working antibiotic (with a reasonable local resistance profile) pending sensitivities rather than broadening reflexively.
- Templated, boilerplate exam documentation (e.g., reflexively charting "scalp atraumatic" or similar unremarkable findings for organs not actually relevant to the complaint) dilutes the signal-to-noise ratio of a note and makes genuinely important findings easier to miss — documenting only what was purposefully examined is more useful than filling out a template.
- Feedback is not an unconditional good — delivering it to someone you don't know well or don't have a trusting relationship with can do net harm; reserve direct, potentially uncomfortable feedback for relationships where you have the standing and trust to deliver it constructively, and consider quietly redirecting patient care instead when that relationship doesn't exist.
- Growth in medical training isn't linear — it's better modeled as a sinusoidal wave, with expected periods of overcorrection before a learner settles into calibrated practice; tolerate and even welcome a trainee's temporary overcorrection after a missed finding rather than treating it as a new problem.
- Not every mistake or near-miss should change a practice pattern — weigh the cost of a systemic behavior change (like reviewing every lab pre-emptively) against the rarity and severity of the miss it would have prevented; sometimes the right lesson from a single bad outcome is "this doesn't justify changing what I do broadly."
- When investigating why a finding was missed across multiple providers or services, default to a non-judgmental, curious posture ("what happened here?") rather than an accusatory one — and consider whether a systems-level fix (like ensuring the right primary contact information reaches nursing) will prevent recurrence more effectively than assigning individual blame.
- Even in immunocompromised hosts, the body frequently controls infections like cystitis without any antibiotic intervention at all — a useful humility check against assuming that catching something "on time" always changes the outcome, and a reminder that antibiotics for mild, self-limited infections (strep throat, uncomplicated cystitis) often provide only modest symptom-relief benefit over supportive care.
- When calibrating how much a bad outcome should weigh on you personally, consider anchoring to the patient's own experience and expressed satisfaction rather than to what any individual consultant or colleague thinks — reserve real self-scrutiny for outcomes that caused the patient serious harm, and distinguish diligent-but-imperfect effort from true neglect (the latter deserving much less self-forgiveness).

## Problem Representation

A 76-year-old kidney transplant recipient on tacrolimus and low-dose prednisone, admitted with a subacute stroke and consulted to medicine only for diabetes management, is found on independent chart review to have an unmentioned, multi-day true fever (up to 102°F) missed simultaneously by every service following him, ultimately explained by a urinary tract infection (E. coli and E. faecalis) that resolves with brief antibiotic treatment — a case built less around diagnostic difficulty than around how such a miss happens and how to respond to it well.

## Case Narrative

Reza set up the case as one presented exactly as it unfolded, without any narrative smoothing, to preserve the real complexity of practice. He was taking over a weekend medicine consult, inheriting a sign-out from the Friday attending with no flagged updates. The one patient being followed was a 76-year-old man with a subacute stroke, on neurology as the primary service, consulted to medicine solely for diabetes management — sliding scale insulin, nutrition, and daily dosing recommendations.

Before hearing his intern's presentation, Reza reviewed the chart himself, as he described doing habitually. He noticed the temperature: 100.3, and scrolling back, a trend — 100.2, 100.2, then roughly 99 about 30 hours earlier. He also noticed the white count, normal that day but 16,000 the day before. None of this had been presented to him by the intern, who was focused on the diabetes plan — the actual purpose of the consult.

Reza asked Robbie two things: how he'd handle noticing an unpresented finding like this, and what he'd want to learn from the trainee in response. Robbie distinguished two possible failure modes — the learner never retrieved the information, or retrieved it but undervalued or forgot it — and said how you probe for the difference should depend on how well you know the person: a direct, informal question with someone you've worked with for years, a more open-ended one ("did anything in the vitals catch your eye?") with someone newer, to figure out where the gap actually occurred.

Reza then added a complicating detail: the patient was status post kidney transplant, on tacrolimus and a small dose of prednisone. When he pointed out the temperature to his new intern — someone he described as excellent — she immediately spiraled into self-blame, stunned she'd missed it. He asked Robbie how to handle that reaction.

Robbie's answer reframed the moment entirely: a strong emotional response to a missed finding is a favorable sign, not a problem — it's nearly impossible for anyone, especially someone early in training, to respond to a growth opportunity perfectly, and the two failure modes to worry about are apathy or overinvestment; overinvestment carries a far better prognosis. His approach in the moment: express genuine pride in how much the trainee cares, explicitly reassure her that forgetting a piece of information while juggling ten priorities as an intern would be completely normal and forgivable, and gently distinguish the stakes — overlooking a low-grade fever is a fundamentally different category of error than causing direct harm through a wrong action, like an overdose. He'd use the moment to explore why she thinks it was missed, potentially surfacing the teaching point that a low-grade fever means something different in an immunocompromised host than in anyone else — and, overall, aim to help her calibrate how much emotional weight to carry, not to suppress the caring itself.

With that groundwork laid, Reza asked what Robbie would actually do next, clinically. Robbie's first move was establishing the timeline: is this fever temporally connected to the stroke (raising concern for a febrile stroke and an endovascular source like infective endocarditis) or is it a genuinely new, hospital-acquired process, separate from a stroke that's now more remote? Reza confirmed the stroke was remote and the fever was new. Asked next about localizing symptoms, Reza reported the intern had found none on history.

Robbie explained that immunosuppressed hosts often can't localize an infection well through symptoms, but exam findings remain a reliable source of information — his plan would be to go examine the patient directly, watching for crackles, nasal congestion, or a rash, targeting the most common infectious sources (respiratory, urinary, skin/soft tissue). Given the gentle tempo of the fever (low-grade, otherwise well-appearing patient) despite the patient's high vulnerability, he'd stay calm rather than act aggressively, gathering more information first.

Reza described doing exactly that. He first checked in with the outgoing Thursday/Friday covering colleague, who pointed to a UA showing white cells, bacteria, and positive leukocyte esterase, with a negative nitrite — and who called it a UTI. Reza's own read was different: this looked like asymptomatic bacteriuria, which by guideline only warrants treatment in specific situations (pregnancy, pre-urologic procedure) — not simply because a patient is older and immunosuppressed with white cells on a UA.

He then went to the bedside, where the patient's wife was present. The patient himself, freshly woken, was appropriately interactive and denied any symptoms — no cough, no burning with urination — despite having a condom catheter in place. His wife, however, revealed he'd told her privately that it burned every time he urinated into the catheter, and that they'd previously used preventive measures for recurrent UTIs.

Robbie's response to this discrepancy reframed the goal of the workup: the patient, subjectively, felt fine and wasn't asking for help with the dysuria — but the team's job wasn't only to address his stated discomfort, it was to prevent a foreseeable future deterioration that the current data (low-grade fever, immunocompromised status) hinted at. He'd tell the patient directly that despite feeling well, some numbers were raising a flag worth investigating further. His planned workup: a perineal exam to rule out an external cause of dysuria (specifically flagging HSV as a mimicker worth considering in immunocompromised hosts), a chest x-ray, a respiratory viral panel, blood cultures (given the immunocompromise), a repeat UA with review of the prior urine culture, and — importantly, in an older man — a post-void residual to exclude urinary retention. He also raised prostatitis as a specific concern given fever plus dysuria without flank pain in an older man, and said he'd weigh whether the case warranted the discomfort of a rectal or perineal exam, keeping it as an option even if not always performed.

Asked whether he'd start antibiotics at that point, Robbie was systematic: did the patient meet criteria for empiric treatment — sepsis, neutropenia, decompensated cirrhosis? No. So he'd wait for the UA to result rather than treat blind, noting he generally distrusts data collected under another service's watch (a prior UA sent for an unrelated reason, like assessing urine specific gravity for the nephrology team, rather than a symptom-driven culture) and would only lean toward earlier treatment if that prior culture had already come back positive.

Reza described his actual course: the earlier UA, it turned out, had been sent by nephrology purely to check specific gravity — not reflexed to culture for symptoms. He got a chest x-ray (negative), skipped the respiratory viral panel Robbie had suggested (a miss he acknowledged in hindsight), and focused on the urinary source based on what the wife had shared. Given the WBC trend (16,000 down to 10,000), the persistent low-grade fever, and the patient's immunocompromised status, he sent a repeat UA and urine culture and started empiric IV ceftriaxone (choosing IV over the team's initial preference for oral, given the immunocompromise). He also acted on his colleague's suggestion to switch the patient's condom catheter to a lower-catheter-associated-UTI-risk design, putting in the nursing communication order himself — prompting Robbie to share a cautionary ED anecdote about a patient who hadn't changed his condom catheter in five days, with predictably unpleasant consequences.

The repeat UA came back with more definitive findings: pyuria, now-positive nitrite (previously negative), bacteria, and few squamous cells (suggesting a cleaner sample).

Then came the chart-review twist that reframed the whole case. Reviewing the nephrology attending's note, Reza saw a passing mention that the patient had had a documented temperature of 101.4 two days earlier — information he hadn't yet seen, since he'd only been looking at Sunday-into-Monday data. Scrolling back further, he found the patient had actually spiked a genuine, unambiguous fever as early as Friday evening (101.4), fluctuating between roughly 99 and 102 across the entire weekend. Checking every service's notes from that stretch: the primary neurology team's notes made no mention of fever at all; the medicine consult notes not only omitted it but explicitly documented the patient as "afebrile" and "hemodynamically stable"; the transplant nephrology notes likewise made no mention. A real, sustained, multi-day fever had been missed simultaneously by every team following the patient, and by nursing as well.

After starting ceftriaxone, the patient's temperature dropped below 98°F for the first time in days, his white count fell to 5,000, and his dysuria completely resolved. The urine culture grew more than 100,000 CFU of both E. coli and E. faecalis. The team, on seeing the culture, asked whether to switch from IV ceftriaxone to oral augmentin to cover both organisms.

Reza framed two questions for Robbie: the narrow medical question about antibiotic coverage, and the much larger question of how to handle feedback across an entire hierarchy of people who'd all missed the same finding — the intern (already primed to self-blame), the senior resident, the primary neurology team, and his own colleague attending on transplant nephrology.

Robbie's feedback philosophy centered on trust and relationship: feedback isn't automatically beneficial — delivered to someone without an established trusting relationship, it can do net harm rather than good, despite how reflexively it's championed in modern medical education. He limits direct, potentially difficult feedback to people he has an active developmental responsibility for; for teams or colleagues he doesn't know well, he'd rather quietly redirect the patient's care than deliver unsolicited correction. He described growth as a "sinusoidal wave" rather than a straight line, and said he welcomes a learner's temporary overcorrection after a miss like this as a normal, healthy part of that process rather than something to worry about.

Reza described his own approach in detail. With the intern and senior resident, he opened by genuinely telling them they hadn't done anything wrong — he'd only caught the trend because he happened to look at a narrower recent window of data, not scroll all the way back to Friday. He explicitly separated near-misses that should change practice from those that shouldn't, invoking a colleague's story: that colleague never pre-reviews labs before rounding with the team, and in thousands of patients, only once did the whole team miss a potassium of 2.7 as a result — and that colleague's own conclusion was that a single miss like that doesn't justify the cost of reviewing every patient's labs preemptively going forward. Reza said internalizing that principle — that a bad outcome doesn't automatically mean the antecedent behavior was wrong — took him a long time to learn.

With the primary neurology team (a new weekend team, following two separate handoffs), he approached the conversation with explicit curiosity rather than judgment, asking what had happened rather than assigning blame — and learned the fragmented handoff chain across the weekend was likely a major contributor. He then escalated to the chief of neurology, who responded constructively, conducting a real-time root cause analysis and committing to ensuring reliable primary-contact information reaches nursing going forward (while explicitly deciding an automated EMR-triggered alert for fever thresholds would be overkill for a case of this severity). Reza also flagged, as an aside, his frustration with templated, boilerplate exam documentation (like reflexively charting "scalp atraumatic") that dilutes a note's real signal and makes genuinely important findings easier to overlook.

With his own colleague, the covering transplant nephrology attending, Reza deliberately took the most empathetic approach of all: he put himself in that colleague's shoes, reasoning that if he'd been signed out simply to "monitor sugars," he likely wouldn't have gone looking for a fever either. He told the colleague as much directly, framing it as "I would have done the same thing you did" rather than criticism — while gently flagging the intern's habit of writing templated "afebrile and hemodynamically stable" language as something worth addressing, since it was part of how the fever stayed invisible.

On the medical question, Reza held the patient on IV ceftriaxone rather than switching to oral augmentin, reasoning the E. faecalis was more likely a colonizer than a true co-pathogen and that roughly 20% of E. coli show real-world resistance to augmentin — better to wait for sensitivities, which later confirmed susceptibility, and the patient did well.

Robbie offered a final, more radical reframe. He asked Reza to lay out the facts plainly: Saturday, fever 102, white count 16,000; Monday, fever 100.2, white count 10,000; and in between, no treatment had been given at all. The patient's own body had already cut both numbers roughly in half unaided. Robbie connected this to a broader point about antibiotic benefit in self-limited infections — citing that antibiotics for conditions like strep throat or uncomplicated cystitis often provide only around 12 hours of extra symptom relief compared to NSAIDs alone — and to a shared prior case (with a colleague, Maddie) where a fleeting moment of suprapubic discomfort had triggered an unnecessarily broad antibiotic regimen. His point wasn't that treatment was wrong here, but that had this fever been caught the instant it began, the patient likely would have received a much longer, more aggressive course of antibiotics than turned out to be strictly necessary — a genuinely humbling illustration that even immunocompromised patients can substantially fight off infection on their own, and that catching something "in time" doesn't automatically mean the outcome would have been better.

Reza closed by distinguishing textbook cases — clean, complete, perfectly presented, like something out of NEJM — from how mistakes actually happen in real hospitals: not through negligence, but through a single overlooked data point, the EKG with ST elevations that never gets read. His summary lesson for the audience was twofold: understand how oversights like this genuinely occur, and default to assuming good faith rather than judgment — innocent until proven guilty, not the reverse — while still being willing to fix the underlying system (in this case, ensuring nursing has clear, reliable primary-contact information).

Robbie's closing reflection turned personal: most clinicians are their own harshest critics, which is part of why external feedback can feel so much heavier than intended. He described having settled on a personal bar for how much a missed finding should weigh on him — anchored not to what any consultant or colleague thinks, but to how the patient himself experienced the outcome. If the patient says he's fine, Robbie is at peace with it; a severe outcome (an ICU admission, critical illness) would be a different matter entirely, one he says he wouldn't easily forgive himself for. Reza agreed, describing his own shift away from needing to feel infallible or externally validated, and shared that he'd told his intern directly: she'd made zero mistakes in this case, but she would inevitably make real mistakes that cause patient harm someday, and learning to live with that — without collapsing into either denial or despair — is itself a skill worth developing deliberately. Both hosts drew a final line between diligent, good-faith effort that still produces an imperfect outcome (forgivable, part of growing) and true neglect (not something either of them could live with as easily).

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The episode is a sustained study of hindsight bias and blame attribution rather than a single diagnostic pitfall: once the fever is discovered, the natural instinct — in the intern, and implicitly in the listener — is to search for who is at fault, and the case deliberately resists that instinct at every level (intern, resident, cross-covering neurology team, colleague attending). Reza's explicit habit of imagining himself in his colleague's position before drawing any conclusion is a concrete debiasing technique against reflexive blame. Robbie's closing observation — that the fever and white count had already substantially self-resolved before any treatment was given — is a pointed correction to outcome bias: the instinct to assume that catching a finding earlier would necessarily have produced a better outcome is not automatically true, and should be checked against what actually would have changed.

## Teaching Discussion

Unlike most RLR episodes, this one centers less on differential diagnosis and more on the "medicine-adjacent" meta-skills that surround diagnosis: how to review data independently, how to give feedback that helps rather than harms, how to investigate a systems-level miss without assigning blame, and how to calibrate self-scrutiny to the actual severity of an outcome rather than to the mere fact that something was missed. The clinical content itself reinforces a clean, generalizable fever-workup sequence in a hospitalized, immunosuppressed patient (timeline first, then targeted exam and staged workup, treating only once empiric criteria or confirmatory data are met) and a clear example of correctly declining to treat asymptomatic bacteriuria despite pressure to do so. The most memorable teaching point may be the least conventional one: that a missed finding doesn't automatically imply a worse outcome, and that humility should extend not just to admitting an error but to accurately assessing whether that error actually mattered.
