## Pearls

- **A continuous headache lasting three weeks falls outside the natural history of the common primary syndromes.** Migraine resolves in days and tension/cluster do not run continuously for weeks, so tempo alone reframes a "cheap" complaint as possibly the bystander of a focal or systemic process — the same way a headache accompanies most systemic illness. ([[Illness Tempo|tempo]] as a red flag.)
- **Approach headache in three buckets, then localize the intracranial one anatomically.** Extracranial head/neck (sinus, glaucoma, tooth), systemic (headache as bystander), or intracranial — and within intracranial, walk meninges, parenchyma, vasculature, and the easily forgotten layer, **bone**. The [[Anatomical Approach|anatomical approach]] to the scalp (skin/soft tissue, nerve, vessel, muscle, bone) is what eventually catches a skull lesion.
- **Reproducible tenderness a patient pinpoints with one fingertip is a real anatomical clue, not reflexively a distractor.** Use [[Analogical Reasoning|analogical reasoning]] with care — chest-wall tenderness does not exclude ACS, so tenderness alone proves little — but a pinpoint, patient-pointed spot earns a careful look at every layer beneath it, and here it localized the disease.
- **Scalp tenderness raises [[Giant Cell Arteritis|GCA]], but demographics and inflammation gate it.** GCA is a disease of the aging; 50 is the very floor and cases below it are rare, so a 50-year-old with a normal ESR and no systemic inflammation makes GCA unlikely by [[Base Rate|base rate]] before any biopsy.
- **A [[Lytic Bone Lesion|lytic bone lesion]] forks first into primary-bone versus systemic disease.** Systemic = metastasis, [[Multiple Myeloma|myeloma]], infection (including TB), or sarcoid/crystalline; a *solitary* lesion in a well-appearing patient with no systemic features points to a **primary** bone process, then splits benign ([[Paget Disease of Bone|Paget]], bone island/cyst, [[Fibrous Dysplasia|fibrous dysplasia]]/McCune-Albright) versus malignant (osteosarcoma, Ewing, osteoblastoma). Normal SPEP/free light chains, PSA, alk phos, and a solitary PET lesion prune the systemic arm.
- **FDG-avidity is not synonymous with malignancy.** A benign lesion densely replaced by proliferating vessels and lymphatics can be hypermetabolic; the [[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive autopsy]] here is that the patient's wellness and absent systemic features should have out-weighed the PET avidity toward benign bone disease — pathology clinched [[Gorham-Stout Disease|Gorham-Stout (vanishing bone) disease]].

## Problem Representation

A previously healthy 53-year-old man on no medications presents with three weeks of continuous, non-throbbing left-sided headache without nausea, vomiting, focal neurologic deficit, fever, or neck stiffness, and appears entirely well with a normal neurologic exam and normal vitals. The single localizing finding is reproducible tenderness — which he pinpoints with one fingertip — over the left occipito-parietal scalp, with a normal ESR and otherwise normal basic labs. Non-contrast head CT reveals a solitary lytic lesion of the parietal bone with cortical thinning and a pristine brain; SPEP and serum free light chains, PSA, urinalysis, and alkaline phosphatase are normal, RPR is negative, and PET-CT shows a single hypermetabolic lesion with no other bony disease. Bone biopsy shows near-complete replacement of bone by vascular and lymphatic tissue, yielding a diagnosis of Gorham-Stout (vanishing/disappearing bone) disease — a rare, benign primary osteolytic process.

## Case Narrative

Rabih presents a 53-year-old man with three weeks of worsening, continuous left-sided headache — no nausea, vomiting, neurologic change, fever, or neck stiffness — just a constant ache on the left. Reza frames headache as one of medicine's "cheap" complaints that is frequently the bystander of a systemic illness (like a GI symptom or an AKI), so the first move is to hunt red flags: focal neurologic deficit, signs of inflammation, positional or persistent severe pain. Here the red flag is tempo — the common primary syndromes do not behave this way, since migraine resolves within days and neither tension nor cluster runs continuously for three weeks — so this is not an atypical migraine but something demanding an explanation. He lays out the 30,000-foot approach: extracranial head/neck causes (glaucoma, sinus disease, a toothache), a systemic process for which the headache is a bystander, or an intracranial process broken down anatomically into meninges, parenchyma, and vasculature. With a purely left-sided, isolated headache, he is concerned for something localized to the left side of the cranium.

Rabih adds the exam: normal vitals, a completely normal neurologic exam, a well-appearing man — but pressing on the left occipital scalp hurts, and a basic panel including an ESR is normal. Reza weighs [[Giant Cell Arteritis|giant cell arteritis]] given scalp tenderness, but down-weights it hard on [[Base Rate|demographics]] — GCA is a disease of the aging, and at 50 the patient sits at the very floor of risk with rare reports below it — reinforced by the absence of systemic inflammation. He pauses on the tenderness itself, cautioning with [[Analogical Reasoning|analogical reasoning]] that a positive "tender to palpation" can mislead, the way reproducible chest-wall tenderness does not actually exclude ACS; press hard enough and any scalp hurts. Assuming Rabih did not press hard, he takes an [[Anatomical Approach|anatomical approach]] to the scalp — skin and soft tissue, cutaneous nerves (drawing analogies to anterior cutaneous nerve entrapment and meralgia paresthetica, and to occipital neuralgia as a nerve-irritation headache), vessels (GCA, thought unlikely), and muscle — while flagging that the absence of any focal neurologic deficit argues the process may not be deep in the hemisphere.

Rabih reveals the detail that changed his index of suspicion: the patient localized the pain with a single *fingertip*, not a vague palm — unusual enough that, despite how well he looked, it prompted a closer look and then a CT for a spot Rabih "couldn't see with his eyes." The CT is the pivot: a lytic lesion of the parietal bone with cortical thinning, in exactly the tender spot, with a pristine brain above and below. Reza notes he should have included **bone** in his anatomical layers from the start (recalling a colleague's case of syphilitic osteolytic skull disease) and reframes the problem as a [[Lytic Bone Lesion|lytic bone lesion]].

He builds the lytic-lesion [[Schema-Based Reasoning|schema]]. Bone lesions run lytic-to-blastic (myeloma the lytic prototype, adenocarcinoma the blastic), but the first fork is *primary bone process versus systemic disease entity*. Systemic causes — pyogenic infection or TB, widespread metastatic malignancy, and autoimmune disease (sarcoid, crystalline) — all fit poorly here: there is no flavor of inflammation, no metastatic pattern, and the patient is too well and too focal. That pushes him to a primary bone process, split into benign ([[Paget Disease of Bone|Paget]] — but the solitary lesion and normal alk phos argue against it — bone islands, bone cysts, and [[Fibrous Dysplasia|fibrous dysplasia]]/McCune-Albright) versus malignant (Ewing, osteosarcoma, osteoblastoma, whose scripts he holds loosely). He would consult radiology for characteristic advanced imaging and, if unrevealing, biopsy to exclude a primary bone cancer.

The added labs prune the systemic arm: SPEP and serum free light chains normal (no secretory [[Multiple Myeloma|myeloma]]), PSA normal, UA normal, alk phos normal — and a [[PET-CT|PET-CT]] shows the lesion is solitary and *hypermetabolic*. Reza reads FDG-avidity as "cells taking up glucose" — infection or cancer — sends an RPR (recalling the syphilitic-bone case) with a sexual history, but leans toward a primary bone cancer given the focality, and plans a biopsy. The RPR is negative; after a delay the biopsy returns bone almost completely replaced by vessels and lymphatic tissue. Reza Googles "osteolytic lesion replaced by lymphatic and vascular vessels" in real time and lands on **[[Gorham-Stout Disease|Gorham-Stout disease]]** — disappearing/vanishing bone disease — the diagnosis the pathologist gave. It is rare and idiopathic, with a predilection for the skull and femur, a characteristic pathology that clinches it, and speculative treatment (bisphosphonates, of unproven efficacy); both hosts openly rate their command of the illness script near 50-50 and plan to read into it.

In reflection, Reza performs a [[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive autopsy]]: the complete absence of systemic features — normal labs, no inflammation, a well-appearing patient — was "yelling primary bone disease," and honestly should have pushed him toward a *benign* process rather than the aggressive sarcomas he entertained; what misdirected him was the FDG-avidity, a reminder that a benign lesion replaced by vessels can be hypermetabolic. The larger lessons ([[Diagnostic Humility|humility]]): you do not need to know a diagnosis to reason your way into its category and know when to be concerned, and colleagues — here the radiologist — are part of the reasoning. Rabih recaps the arc: headache → focal fingertip tenderness → CT lytic lesion → the isolated-versus-systemic fork → a purely isolated skull lesion biopsied to vanishing bone disease.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Anatomical Approach|Anatomical localization]] carries the case.** Reza walks the scalp layer by layer — skin/soft tissue, nerve, vessel, muscle — and the one layer he initially omits, **bone**, is exactly where the answer sits. The CT then converts a vague "left-sided headache" into a single parietal lesion, and the whole subsequent workup is an anatomical narrowing from "somewhere in the head" to "this bone, and nowhere else."
- **[[Analogical Reasoning|Analogical reasoning]] used on purpose.** Reza reaches for analogies repeatedly — chest-wall tenderness not excluding ACS (don't over-read a positive palpation), anterior cutaneous nerve entrapment and meralgia paresthetica (a compressed cutaneous nerve as a pain generator), occipital neuralgia — to reason about an unfamiliar finding from familiar ones. The technique both *raises* hypotheses and *calibrates* how much to trust the tenderness.
- **[[Base Rate|Base-rate discipline]] on GCA and on rarity.** Age and inflammatory markers down-weight GCA before any biopsy, and the same base-rate lens keeps the aggressive primary bone cancers plausible-but-unlikely; the twist is that the *rarest* answer (Gorham-Stout) still had to be reached by category, since no base rate would surface it directly.
- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|Schema over recall for a never-seen diagnosis]].** Neither host has command of the final entity, yet the lytic-lesion schema (primary vs systemic; benign vs malignant) lets them make near-complete progress and localize the answer to "solitary benign primary bone disease" before the name is even known — the episode's explicit thesis that you can reason into a category without knowing the diagnosis.
- **[[Cognitive Autopsy|Cognitive autopsy]] on the FDG anchor.** Reza names the misstep cleanly: the patient's wellness and absent systemic features pointed to benign disease, but the PET's hypermetabolic signal pulled him toward malignancy — a worked example of one salient test over-weighting an otherwise coherent benign picture, and of the fact that FDG-avidity indexes cellular glucose uptake (including proliferating vessels), not malignancy per se.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Humility and distributed cognition]].** Both hosts rate their Gorham-Stout script at "50-50," treat Googling the biopsy description as a legitimate real-time move, and credit radiology as part of the reasoning — modeling that knowing *when to be concerned* and *how to categorize* matters more than having memorized every rare entity.

## Teaching Discussion

- Treat headache tempo as a primary red flag: three weeks of continuous pain is incompatible with the common primary syndromes (migraine resolves in days; tension and cluster are not continuous for weeks), so an unremitting headache demands a structural or systemic explanation rather than an "atypical migraine" label.
- Structure headache as extracranial head/neck, systemic-bystander, or intracranial, and localize the intracranial bucket anatomically through meninges, parenchyma, vasculature, and bone — remembering that the skull itself is a layer, easy to omit until a lytic lesion appears.
- Read reproducible, pinpoint, patient-localized tenderness as a genuine anatomical clue while using analogical reasoning to avoid over-reading it — chest-wall tenderness does not exclude ACS, so tenderness neither confirms nor excludes on its own, but a fingertip-localized spot warrants imaging of the structures beneath it.
- Gate giant cell arteritis on age and inflammation: it is a disease of the aging with 50 as the practical floor, so a normal ESR and no systemic inflammatory features in a 50-year-old make it unlikely despite scalp tenderness.
- Work a lytic bone lesion by first asking primary-bone versus systemic (metastasis, myeloma, infection including TB, sarcoid/crystalline); a solitary lesion in a well patient with normal SPEP/free light chains, PSA, and alkaline phosphatase, and a solitary lesion on PET, points to a primary bone process, then split benign (Paget, bone island/cyst, fibrous dysplasia/McCune-Albright) from malignant (osteosarcoma, Ewing, osteoblastoma) and biopsy when uncertain.
- Do not equate FDG-avidity with malignancy: hypermetabolism reflects cellular glucose uptake, and a benign lesion densely replaced by proliferating vascular and lymphatic tissue — as in Gorham-Stout disease — can be intensely avid; let the patient's overall wellness and the absence of systemic features weigh against malignancy.
- Recognize Gorham-Stout (vanishing/disappearing bone) disease as a rare, idiopathic osteolysis with a skull and femur predilection, a characteristic histology (bone replaced by lymphovascular proliferation) that clinches the diagnosis, and speculative treatment (bisphosphonates of unproven efficacy) — and accept that reasoning to the correct *category* is a legitimate endpoint when the specific entity is one you have never seen.
