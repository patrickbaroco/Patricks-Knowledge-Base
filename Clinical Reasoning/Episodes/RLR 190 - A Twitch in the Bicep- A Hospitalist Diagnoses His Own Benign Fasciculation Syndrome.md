## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Guest Lev — a hospitalist and longtime member of the CP Solvers community — presents a case that turns out to be his own. A 45-year-old man develops a single twitch in his right bicep that, over the following weeks, spreads into random, painless [[Fasciculation|fasciculations]] across both arms, both legs, the face, back, and abdomen, worse at night and unrelieved by stopping caffeine. A single episode of hours-long generalized fatigue and weakness raises the stakes, but the neurologic exam stays essentially normal aside from fasciculations in the right calf, and an exhaustive workup — electrolytes, CK, thyroid, inflammatory markers, a cervical spine MRI, an EMG, and a paraneoplastic/autoimmune panel — comes back reassuringly unrevealing apart from an incidental, non-correlating finding and a mild asymptomatic ulnar neuropathy. Reza and Robbie spend the case wrestling with a genuinely rare diagnostic shape: a purely positive neurological phenomenon with no accompanying deficit to anchor a localization. The symptoms resolve spontaneously over the following months — relief tracing not to any treatment but to the reassurance of a normal EMG — and Lev reveals the diagnosis as [[Benign Fasciculation Syndrome]], a condition disproportionately self-diagnosed with dread by healthcare workers who know just enough about ALS to be dangerous to themselves.

## Pearls

- A "muscle twitch" is not one entity but a family of mimics — [[Fasciculation]], myoclonus, and tremor — so before building any framework, clarify exactly what the patient means; most twitches are benign and related to overexertion or caffeine, and only rarely reflect something devastating like ALS.
- [[Positive vs Negative Neurological Phenomena|Positive neurological phenomena]] (things gained — a twitch, a seizure, a hallucination) are far harder to diagnose than negative phenomena (things lost), because most movement disorders reveal themselves through an accompanying deficit; a purely positive phenomenon with a persistently normal exam is the rare, genuinely difficult case.
- An incidental imaging finding has to earn its explanatory role: this patient's cervical spine MRI showed degenerative changes correlating anatomically with a single, unilateral C8 distribution, which could not account for diffuse, bilateral, non-dermatomal fasciculations — a distractor, not an answer.
- An exhaustive, entirely normal workup is not a dead end; it is itself diagnostic information, progressively narrowing the differential away from motor neuron disease, metabolic derangement, and autoimmune peripheral nerve hyperexcitability toward a benign, self-limited process.
- [[Benign Fasciculation Syndrome]] is common — some estimates put lifetime prevalence around 70% — and is diagnosed by an EMG showing occasional fasciculation potentials without electrodiagnostic evidence of motor neuron disease, neuropathy, or radiculopathy; reassurance from that normal study is often the only "treatment" needed.
- The [[Availability Heuristic]] disproportionately drives healthcare workers toward catastrophizing benign fasciculations as ALS, because vivid clinical exposure to the disease makes that association cognitively available far out of proportion to its true base rate.

## Problem Representation

A 45-year-old man (a hospitalist, presenting his own case) with a history of migraines and eczema and no family history of neurologic disease developed a single episode of painless right bicep twitching that, over the following weeks, spread into recurrent, random, non-painful muscle twitching across both arms, both legs, the face, back, and abdomen — worse at night and unchanged by stopping caffeine — punctuated by one self-limited episode of generalized fatigue and weakness lasting several hours, in the setting of a normal neurologic exam aside from focal fasciculations in the right calf, normal electrolytes, CK, TSH, and inflammatory markers, an incidental and non-correlating cervical spine MRI finding, and an EMG showing only a mild asymptomatic ulnar neuropathy and occasional fasciculation potentials without evidence of motor neuron disease — ultimately resolving spontaneously over several months without any intervention beyond reassurance.

## Case Narrative

Rabih introduced Lev as barely a "guest" at all — a member of the CP Solvers orbit since its earliest days, a classmate of Sharmin's, and a recent, enthusiastic reader of Reza's book. Before Lev presented anything beyond the chief concern, Reza used the moment to teach: abnormal muscle movement, he explained, is chronically under-practiced territory for internists, the neurologic equivalent of "transient loss of consciousness" being prematurely labeled "syncope." Working through a framework he and Aaron Berkowitz had built together, Reza walked from the skeletal muscle back to its neuron, its neuromuscular junction, and the muscle itself, noting that irritation anywhere along that chain can produce a twitch — and that before landing on "twitch," it's worth confirming the patient isn't actually describing a mimic like myoclonus or tremor. Most twitches, he stressed, are benign overexertion or caffeine effects; only rarely do they signal something as devastating as ALS.

Lev then gave the history: a 45-year-old man, sitting at home one summer day, noticed an involuntary movement in his right bicep. It recurred two or three more times that day and continued for the next few days, then, about a week later, spread to his left thumb without any effort on his part. Over the following weeks it involved both arms, both legs, his face, back, and abdomen — random, distracting, never painful, and worse at night when he was trying to fall asleep. Stopping coffee didn't change anything. At one point at work, after a cluster of twitches, he developed generalized weakness in his arms and legs severe enough that he needed to sit down; it resolved over a few hours, followed by generalized soreness.

Robbie's first reaction reframed the whole problem: most movement disorders are diagnosable precisely because they come bundled with a deficit — a seizure implies focal cortical dysfunction that eventually declares itself once the seizure clears, a tremor in Parkinson's disease arrives alongside bradykinesia. This case, so far, was pure positive phenomenon — abnormal movement without any accompanying loss — and Robbie flagged that as both intriguing and genuinely difficult to reason through, since the vast majority of systemic neurologic processes affecting all four limbs would also be expected to touch the sensorium, which here remained completely intact.

Additional history added little to localize the problem: migraines treated with sumatriptan, eczema treated with topical creams, no surgical history, no allergies, a family history notable only for hypothyroidism, hypertension, and coronary disease but no neurologic disease, and no alcohol, tobacco, or drug use. Review of systems was negative for fevers, dysphagia, tongue twitching, or weight loss. Reza framed his approach as first asking whether this sits in the world of neurology, medicine-presenting-as-neurology, or psychiatry — reserving primary psychiatric explanations for last — and noted that the muscle soreness following a wave of fasciculations made physiologic sense, akin to a diffuse, whole-body version of an ordinary nighttime muscle cramp.

On exam, vitals were normal. The patient was alert and oriented with normal strength throughout, no cranial nerve, sensory, or motor deficits, a negative Romberg, no pronator drift, and normal coordination, gait, finger-to-nose, heel-to-shin, and rapid alternating movements — with fasciculations visible only in the right calf. Robbie underscored how unusual this was: a movement disorder without any deficit to explain, breaking the pattern he relies on (Parkinson's with its constipation and mood changes, seizures with their post-ictal deficits) where the search for a "loss" typically drives progress. Absent any obvious environmental trigger, he proposed starting simply — checking electrolytes as a stretch explanation for episodic symptoms — while acknowledging low pretest probability, and getting expert neuromuscular hands on the patient. Reza agreed, adding a neuromuscular consult with EMG and nerve conduction studies, TSH, and basic electrolytes as the next steps, while flagging that the patient was almost certainly worried about ALS despite the absence of the weakness and atrophy that typically accompany it.

Labs returned entirely normal: CBC, electrolytes, LFTs, TSH, CK, ESR, and CRP were all unremarkable. Robbie reframed these pertinent negatives as meaningfully narrowing rather than unhelpful — either this was an isolated neurologic disease, or, as with anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis, a disproportionate neurologic syndrome was hiding a process elsewhere in the body that simply hadn't shown itself yet on standard testing.

An MRI of the cervical spine showed multilevel degenerative changes most pronounced at C7–T1, with left ventral thecal sac indentation and moderate left neural foraminal narrowing correlating with a left C8 distribution. Both hosts immediately treated this as a distractor rather than an explanation: the finding was unilateral and dermatomal, while the patient's fasciculations were diffuse and bilateral, and — as Reza put it — nearly anyone imaged at this age would show some degenerative changes. There was no clinical myelopathy, and the imaging finding simply didn't correlate with the presenting syndrome.

The EMG, obtained about a month later — by which point the fasciculations, insomnia, and fatigue persisted unchanged, and the patient described the twitches as feeling like a painless electrical shock he couldn't stop noticing — sampled multiple sensory and motor nerves bilaterally along with several perispinal and cranial muscles. It showed a mild increase in distal left ulnar sensory peak latency with focal slowing across the elbow, and occasional fasciculation potentials in the left tibialis anterior; the impression was a mild left ulnar neuropathy at the elbow, with no evidence of motor neuron disease, peripheral neuropathy, or multiple radiculopathies. Robbie again noted how hard this made things: the tests designed to find loss weren't finding any, because there wasn't any to find — and he began reaching toward exceptionally rare positive-phenomena syndromes, drawing an analogy to anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis's antibody-mediated positive phenomena in the brain and wondering aloud about a peripheral nervous system cousin — antibody-mediated hyperexcitability syndromes such as Isaac syndrome and anti-CASPR2 disease — while being explicit that this was a low-confidence, last-resort direction driven by diagnostic uncertainty rather than real suspicion.

A neurologist sent a paraneoplastic and autoimmune serum panel, which returned negative. Over the following two to three months, without any treatment, the fasciculations persisted but the insomnia and fatigue gradually improved, and by follow-up the twitches were down to about once a day.

At that point, Lev revealed that the patient was himself — a fact that had happened about two years earlier, and that the moment of relief traced not to any intervention but to receiving the EMG result. Robbie noted, in retrospect, the identifying details (San Francisco, nursing/hospital work, wife, one child) had been hiding in plain sight. Lev named the final diagnosis as [[Benign Fasciculation Syndrome]] — fasciculations occurring in an estimated 70% of the population, most commonly tied to poor sleep, stress, anxiety, and caffeine, though in his own case none of the usual triggers were clearly present except an emotionally loaded conversation about a family living trust around the same time. He noted the condition is markedly overrepresented as a self-diagnosis among healthcare workers, that fasciculations almost always persist even after diagnosis, and that almost none of these patients ever progress to ALS — in his own case, the twitches never fully went away, he simply stopped noticing or caring about them once reassured.

Reza closed by reflecting that medicine still has real work to do shedding the stigma around psychiatric and anxiety-adjacent presentations, and that Lev's story models something valuable: that an anxious reaction to a genuinely alarming symptom in someone who understands its worst-case implications can be proportionate rather than pathological, much as the pre-antibody-discovery patients later found to have anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis were, for a time, wrongly assumed to be purely psychiatric.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Illness script — positive phenomena lack a "loss" landmark.** [[Positive vs Negative Neurological Phenomena]] organized the entire case: Robbie's central observation was that most movement disorders are diagnosable because they are paired with an underlying deficit (a seizure implies focal cortical dysfunction; myoclonus in an encephalopathic patient implies loss of mental status; Parkinsonian tremor arrives with bradykinesia and constipation). This case offered none of that — a persistently normal exam alongside a diffuse positive phenomenon — which is why both hosts repeatedly, and explicitly, described themselves as working without a schema.
- **Tempo and spread as diagnostic information.** The escalation from a single, localized bicep twitch to a diffuse, multi-region process over several weeks, together with an episode of generalized weakness, was treated as genuinely concerning rather than dismissed — Robbie flagged the preserved sensorium amid whole-body involvement as an "interesting paradox" worth tracking, not a reason to stop taking the symptom seriously.
- **Incidentaloma discipline.** The cervical spine MRI finding was interrogated for anatomic correlation (unilateral, single-dermatome match against a diffuse, bilateral symptom pattern) before being set aside as a distractor — the same discipline the show has applied to incidental findings in other episodes (see [[RLR 189 - Static Left, Racing Right- A Six-Month Chase from Suspected Lung Cancer to Acute Fibrinous and Organizing Pneumonia|RLR 189]]'s "organ inventory" reasoning).
- **Availability heuristic — exemplar.** Lev's closing teaching point is a textbook description of the [[Availability Heuristic]]: benign fasciculation syndrome is disproportionately over-diagnosed with dread by healthcare professionals, precisely because clinicians' vivid, salient exposure to ALS makes that association cognitively available far out of proportion to its true base rate.
- **Diagnostic humility — exemplar.** Both hosts repeatedly and explicitly admitted the limits of their schema ("I don't have any real insight to share," "there's no way I'm gonna figure this out") rather than forcing a premature label onto a case that resisted their existing scripts, choosing honest uncertainty over false closure — a further [[Diagnostic Humility]] exemplar.
- **Proportionate anxiety, not pathologized.** Reza's closing framing — that an anxious response to a genuinely alarming symptom, in someone who understands its worst-case implications, can be proportionate rather than a sign of an anxiety disorder — deliberately separates normal human anxiety from psychiatric illness, echoing the historical mislabeling of pre-discovery anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis patients as purely psychiatric.

## Teaching Discussion

Reza's opening framework for abnormal muscle movement — co-developed with Aaron Berkowitz — gives internists a rare, structured entry point into territory they otherwise see infrequently: localize irritation to the neuron, the neuromuscular junction, or the muscle itself, and first confirm the patient is describing a true fasciculation rather than a mimic like myoclonus or tremor. Most twitches are benign and tied to overexertion or caffeine; the base rate for anything as severe as ALS is very low, and that base rate should anchor the initial approach even when the patient (or the clinician) fears the worst.

The case's real teaching arc, though, is Robbie's positive-versus-negative-phenomena framework. Most neurologic movement disorders are tractable precisely because they come paired with a deficit that localizes them — and when that deficit is absent, as it was here throughout, the standard toolkit (exam, labs, EMG) is built to find loss, not to explain pure excess. That mismatch is exactly why the case felt so resistant to a confident diagnosis in real time, and why an entirely reassuring, deficit-free workup was itself the diagnostic signal rather than a disappointment.

Benign fasciculation syndrome, as Lev laid it out, is a genuinely common, EMG-confirmed diagnosis of exclusion: occasional fasciculation potentials without electrodiagnostic evidence of motor neuron disease, neuropathy, or radiculopathy, most often tied to poor sleep, stress, anxiety, and caffeine, and markedly overrepresented as a self-diagnosis among healthcare workers who know just enough about ALS to catastrophize a benign finding. Reassurance — not treatment — is usually the intervention, and the fasciculations themselves often persist indefinitely without functional consequence.

Finally, the episode's autobiographical reveal does double duty as a lesson on physician wellness: Reza's closing point, that a clinician's anxious reaction to their own alarming symptom can be proportionate rather than pathological, pushes back on any reflexive urge to relabel every instance of healthcare-worker anxiety as a psychiatric disorder, while still leaving room to acknowledge that some fraction of such reactions genuinely do warrant treatment.
