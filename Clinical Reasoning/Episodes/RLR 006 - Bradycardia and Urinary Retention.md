## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 68-year-old man on carvedilol presents feeling he has "aged 10 years in three days," with exertional fatigue and lightheadedness that Reza reframes into a heart/lung/blood problem before the monitor supplies the answer: a heart rate of 27. The problem is [[Bradycardia|bradycardia]], and the EKG classifies it as *sinus* bradycardia — which Reza works through the episode-coined [[LIED Mnemonic (Sinus Bradycardia)|LIED mnemonic]] (lytes, ischemia/infiltration, endocrinopathy, drugs). Labs, TSH, troponin, and a stable VA-packed Mediset progressively exonerate each cause; the mild AKI plus beta blocker briefly makes the ED team anchor on [[BRASH Syndrome]] and admit to the ICU. The reveal is a post-void residual of 900 cc: [[Urinary Retention|urinary retention]] from [[Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia|BPH]]. Reza reasons in real time through an apparent physiologic paradox — retention should mean pain and *sympathetic* tone, and anticholinergic-driven retention should cause *tachycardia* — before landing on a vagally mediated [[Bladder–Heart Reflex|bladder-to-heart reflex]]. A Foley drains 1.2 L and the heart rate climbs from 32 to 98 within minutes, confirming it.

## Pearls

- **Don't blame the [[Bradycardia|bradycardia]] until it's severe enough to own the presentation.** Just as a mildly low glucose shouldn't be blamed for a patient's collapse, a heart rate has to be genuinely, unphysiologically low before it becomes *the* problem. At 27, exertional fatigue and lightheadedness stop being the complaint to solve and become downstream effects of the rhythm.
- **Classify bradycardia before building a differential — [[Sinus Bradycardia|sinus]] versus [[AV Dissociation|AV dissociation/block]] have different DDx.** The only way to tell is a 12-lead plus a rhythm strip, "surfing" the P waves and QRS to look for dropped beats or dissociation. A rate of 27 sounds ventricular and should prioritize [[Complete Heart Block|AV dissociation]] until the tracing proves otherwise. Sinus bradycardia actually makes infiltrative and exotic conduction disease *less* likely.
- **For sinus bradycardia, run [[LIED Mnemonic (Sinus Bradycardia)|LIED]] — and start with drugs.** Lytes (electrolytes: K, Mg, Ca), Ischemia/Infiltration, Endocrinopathy (hypothyroidism), Drugs. The highest-base-rate first pass in the hospital is the medication list; here a beta blocker ([[Carvedilol|carvedilol]]) is on board, prompting a check for accidental over-ingestion or [[Beta Blocker Toxicity|beta blocker toxicity]].
- **Increased [[Vagal Tone|vagal tone]] belongs on the sinus-bradycardia differential — and is easy to forget when the bradycardia is continuous.** Vagal causes come to mind readily for a *transient* event (vasovagal syncope), but a sustained rate of 27 makes a reflex mechanism feel implausible, which is exactly why it gets missed.
- **A distended bladder can drive [[Bladder–Heart Reflex|reflex bradycardia]] through the vagus.** [[Urinary Retention|Urinary retention]] here produced a heart rate of 27 that corrected to 98 within minutes of draining 1.2 L — the same abnormal vagal wiring behind Cushing-reflex bradycardia in raised ICP, insufflation bradycardia during laparoscopy, and carotid sinus hypersensitivity.
- **[[BRASH Syndrome]] is a real trap when the ingredients are present but sub-threshold.** Bradycardia, Renal insufficiency, AV block, Shock, Hyperkalemia — this patient had a beta blocker, mild AKI, and marked bradycardia, enough for the team to label it BRASH, but a normal potassium (4.8) and hemodynamic stability argued against it.

## Problem Representation

A 68-year-old man with hypertension and CKD on low-dose carvedilol presents with three days of exertional fatigue and lightheadedness, found to have marked, symptomatic sinus bradycardia (rate 27) that is not explained by electrolytes, ischemia, thyroid disease, or beta blocker toxicity, and that resolves abruptly with bladder decompression — vagally mediated reflex bradycardia driven by BPH-related urinary retention.

## Case Narrative

The patient, a 68-year-old man, opens by telling Rabih he feels he has "aged 10 years in the last three days" — unbelievably tired, unable to walk to the exam room without help, dizzy whenever he stands. Reza dwells on the phrasing: had the complaint stopped at "I feel old," most listeners would have coded it as nonspecific fatigue, a symptom under which anything can hide. But the qualifier — fatigue and lightheadedness *on exertion*, severe enough that his wife and the ED volunteers had to bring him back — narrows the field. Exertional fatigue, Reza argues, points to the heart or the lung, with one competitor prioritized ahead of both: [[Anemia|anemia]], which the blood count will help sort from a cardiac or pulmonary cause. He is triaging the presentation into a heart/lung/blood problem when Rabih shows the monitor: heart rate 27, blood pressure soft at 100s/70s, normal respiratory rate and saturation.

The patient falls squarely into the cardiac bucket, and Reza reframes the problem as [[Bradycardia|bradycardia]] — insisting no human, however athletic, should sit at 27, and in a 68-year-old this is dangerous. This is symptomatic bradycardia, so the [[ACLS|ACLS]] algorithm is activated in parallel with the diagnostic work: telemetry, IV access, cycling blood pressures, and transcutaneous pacer pads placed and ready, with pain medication on hand should pacing be needed. Diagnostically, the first move is to classify the rhythm — [[Sinus Bradycardia|sinus bradycardia]] versus [[AV Dissociation|AV dissociation]] and [[Complete Heart Block|complete heart block]] versus [[Second Degree AV Block|second-degree block]] — which requires a 12-lead and a rhythm strip, "surfing" the P waves and QRS complexes for dropped beats or dissociation. A rate of 27 sounds ventricular to Reza, so he leans toward AV dissociation but withholds the leap until the tracing is read.

The EKG comes back as marked sinus bradycardia, and the history fills in: hypertension, CKD with a baseline creatinine near 1.3, carvedilol 6.125 mg, hyperlipidemia on a statin. Reza organizes the sinus-bradycardia differential with the episode's mnemonic — [[LIED Mnemonic (Sinus Bradycardia)|LIED]], because "the EKG don't lie": **L**ytes (potassium, magnesium, calcium), **I**schemia (or Infiltration of the conduction system, more relevant in AV block, as with sarcoidosis), **E**ndocrinopathy (specifically hypothyroidism), and **D**rugs. The base-rate first pass is the medication list. The [[Carvedilol|carvedilol]] is a beta blocker that slows the rate, and even at a low dose Reza wants to exclude an inadvertent over-ingestion — [[Beta Blocker Toxicity|beta blocker toxicity]] — by asking the patient directly. He sends electrolytes (guarding against acute-on-chronic kidney injury with [[Hyperkalemia|hyperkalemia]]), a troponin, a TSH, and plans an echocardiogram.

The workup returns an hour later with the patient stable, his rate drifting up to 34–35 without intervention. His wife administers his medications from a VA-pharmacy-packed Mediset unchanged for years, arguing against a dosing error. Creatinine is 1.6 (up from 1.3), potassium 4.8, magnesium and calcium normal, troponin negative, TSH normal, chest x-ray clear. LIED is now largely exhausted: ischemia, electrolytes, endocrinopathy, and beta blocker toxicity are all off the table. Reza widens the net to over-the-counter supplements and herbs (invoking [[Digoxin Toxicity|digoxin]] as the archetype of an occult ingestion), considers tick-borne conduction disease ([[Lyme Carditis|Borrelia]], anaplasmosis) only to dismiss it given geography, proposes provoking the patient to test for chronotropic competence, and — finding no reversible cause — would keep him on telemetry, get the echo, and consult EP about a pacemaker. Before Rabih moves on, Reza flags [[BRASH Syndrome]] (Bradycardia, Renal insufficiency, AV block, Shock, Hyperkalemia) and then dismisses it — and Rabih reveals the ED team had in fact anchored there, admitting the patient to the ICU as BRASH on the strength of the beta blocker, mild AKI, and bradycardia despite a normal potassium.

The reveal is a nurse's bladder scan: a post-void residual of 900 cc. Reza, caught off guard, reasons aloud through what becomes the teaching centerpiece. He proposes adding a category to his mnemonic — increased [[Vagal Tone|vagal tone]] — noting how readily a vagal mechanism comes to mind for *transient* bradycardia (vasovagal syncope) yet how hard it is to invoke for something continuous. Then he wrestles with a genuine physiologic paradox in real time: [[Urinary Retention|urinary retention]] should cause pain and therefore *sympathetic* activation and tachycardia; and if retention were driven by an [[Anticholinergic|anticholinergic]] on board, blocking parasympathetic input should *also* produce tachycardia. Neither fits a rate of 27. He reasons that the bradycardia argues against an anticholinergic (which would raise the rate), and instead favors a mechanical obstruction — [[Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia|BPH]], overwhelmingly likely in a 68-year-old man — producing retention that, through some unidentified connection, drives *parasympathetic* rather than sympathetic tone. His final hypothesis: BPH-related retention causing reflex bradycardia.

Rabih confirms it in the case's payoff. A Foley drained 1.2 L, and the heart rate rose from 32 to 98 within seconds to a few minutes — one of his most joyful ED experiences. The mechanism is a [[Bladder–Heart Reflex|reflex arc]] between the distended bladder and the heart mediated by the vagus, the same abnormal wiring behind [[Cushing Reflex|Cushing-reflex]] bradycardia in raised intracranial pressure (which Rabih had, by coincidence, learned was vagally mediated at UCSF morning report the day before), the marked bradycardia some patients develop on abdominal insufflation during laparoscopy, and [[Carotid Sinus Hypersensitivity|carotid sinus hypersensitivity]]. The patient's BPH was borne out by improvement on tamsulosin and finasteride weeks later. Notably, the workup was efficient because the ED nurses reflexively obtain a urinalysis and bladder scan whenever the creatinine is up — the scan was provided before anyone thought to ask.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring]] on [[BRASH Syndrome]].** The ED team locked onto BRASH once a few ingredients lined up (beta blocker, mild AKI, bradycardia) and admitted to the ICU under that label — despite a normal potassium and hemodynamic stability that should have loosened the grip. Reza names BRASH explicitly *to dismiss it*, modeling the counter-move of testing rather than accepting a pattern-match.
- **[[Framing Effect|Framing]] by chronicity blocked the vagal hypothesis.** A vagal mechanism is cognitively available for a *transient* event (vasovagal syncope) but feels implausible for a *continuous* rate of 27; the sustained framing of the bradycardia is precisely what kept increased vagal tone off the differential until the bladder scan forced it on.
- **Reasoning through a physiologic paradox in real time.** Reza openly contradicts himself — retention "should" produce pain and sympathetic tachycardia, and anticholinergic retention "should" also produce tachycardia — and uses the observed bradycardia as the discriminating datum to reject the anticholinergic path and accept an unexplained parasympathetic reflex. This is illness-script reasoning done out loud, "finding lesions" in his own logic as if presenting to himself.
- **[[Availability Heuristic|Availability]] cutting the right way.** Rabih had learned the day before, at morning report, that Cushing-reflex bradycardia from raised ICP is vagally mediated; that fresh exposure primed the reflex-bradycardia concept and made the bladder–heart connection reachable. Availability usually misleads, but here a recent case supplied the correct analogy.
- **Not blaming the abnormal number too early.** Reza's [[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive autopsy]] draws the analogy to hypoglycemia: a mildly abnormal value gets over-blamed for a presentation. Bradycardia earned the blame here only because it was unphysiologically severe — a discipline of confirming that the abnormality is bad enough to be causal before attributing symptoms to it.
- **Classify before you differentiate.** Committing to sinus versus AV block *before* generating causes prevents importing the wrong differential; the sinus classification itself down-weighted infiltrative and exotic conduction disease and pointed to the LIED schema.

## Teaching Discussion

- **Establish that the bradycardia is severe enough to be causal before attributing symptoms to it.** Reza's hypoglycemia analogy is the guardrail: mildly abnormal values get over-blamed. A rate of 27 clears that bar; a rate in the 50s might not.
- **Classify the bradycardia first, then reason about cause.** Sinus bradycardia and AV dissociation/block carry different differentials, and only a 12-lead plus rhythm strip — read by "surfing" P waves and QRS for dropped or dissociated beats — can distinguish them. Sinus bradycardia makes infiltrative and exotic conduction disease less likely.
- **Use [[LIED Mnemonic (Sinus Bradycardia)|LIED]] for sinus bradycardia, leading with drugs.** Lytes (K/Mg/Ca), Ischemia/Infiltration, Endocrinopathy (hypothyroidism), Drugs — and in the hospital the medication list is the highest-yield first pass, with beta blockers, occult ingestions, and supplements (the digoxin archetype) all in scope.
- **Add increased vagal tone as its own branch of the sinus-bradycardia differential.** The case's central update: a distended bladder can drive reflex bradycardia through the vagus, and the mechanism generalizes to Cushing-reflex bradycardia in raised ICP, insufflation bradycardia in laparoscopy, and carotid sinus hypersensitivity.
- **Know [[BRASH Syndrome]] and its threshold.** Bradycardia, Renal insufficiency, AV block, Shock, Hyperkalemia is a real and dangerous synergy, but the ingredients here were sub-threshold — a normal potassium and a stable, non-shocked patient — and the label became an anchor rather than a diagnosis.
- **Let downstream reasoning include the physiologic paradox.** Retention "should" cause pain and sympathetic tone, and anticholinergic retention "should" cause tachycardia; when the observed rate contradicts both, the contradiction itself is the clue that a non-obvious reflex is operating.
- **System factors can carry the diagnosis.** The ED nurses' reflex to obtain a urinalysis and bladder scan whenever the creatinine is up delivered the 900-cc PVR before any clinician requested it — a reminder that well-designed protocols surface the pivotal datum.
