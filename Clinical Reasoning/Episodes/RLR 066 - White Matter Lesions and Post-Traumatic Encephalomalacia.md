## Pearls

- **Confusion *with focality* ≠ disease confined to the brain.** Symmetric confusion (reduced arousal, no localizing signs) differs from confusion with a focal deficit (here, a language disturbance). In a patient with prior brain disease (MS, old stroke), a **systemic insult can unmask a latent focal vulnerability** — so pursue the neurologic and systemic workups **in parallel**, not sequentially. ([[RLR 066 - White Matter Lesions and Post-Traumatic Encephalomalacia|RLR 066]])
- **When every lead is chronic, weight each by the [[Law of Proportionality|proportionality]] to *today's* change.** Long-standing meth use, possible colonization/UTI, and a background of MS all *dilute* each finding's claim to the acute delta — and should raise the humility that **all of them may be distractors** masking something else. ([[RLR 066 - White Matter Lesions and Post-Traumatic Encephalomalacia|RLR 066]])
- **[[Non-Convulsive Status Epilepticus|Non-convulsive status epilepticus]] hides in "atypical confusion."** Perseveration on a single phrase, a subtly twitchy limb, a hyper-aroused look — consider NCSE, put on EEG, or give a **benzodiazepine trial** and watch for paradoxical clearing. NCSE usually rides on a **focal structural lesion**. ([[RLR 066 - White Matter Lesions and Post-Traumatic Encephalomalacia|RLR 066]])
- **Seizures themselves cause MRI changes.** Ictal/post-ictal **[[Seizure-Related MRI Changes|T2/FLAIR hyperintensity, diffusion restriction, and even enhancement]]** appear in regions where seizures arise or spread (classically limbic — medial temporal, insula, cingulate) — a mimic of [[HSV Encephalitis|HSV]] and autoimmune encephalitis. ([[RLR 066 - White Matter Lesions and Post-Traumatic Encephalomalacia|RLR 066]])
- **Gray-matter involvement is atypical for MS and should broaden the differential.** Cortical/limbic disease pushes toward HSV, autoimmune/limbic encephalitis, or seizure change; a symmetric pattern favors toxic-metabolic, an asymmetric/focal one favors a process *housed* in the brain. ([[RLR 066 - White Matter Lesions and Post-Traumatic Encephalomalacia|RLR 066]])
- **Old head trauma can produce [[Encephalomalacia|encephalomalacia]] *and* persistent [[Dural Enhancement|dural (pachymeningeal) enhancement]] — no external marks required.** Post-traumatic dural enhancement can persist for months to years, and encephalomalacia at the frontal pole is a classic **seizure focus** — a mundane cause of a picture that reads as PML/infection/neoplasm. ([[RLR 066 - White Matter Lesions and Post-Traumatic Encephalomalacia|RLR 066]])
- **A bland CSF does not exclude infection.** [[PML|JC virus]] and cryptococcus are classically **oligo-inflammatory**, autoimmune encephalitis often has minimal pleocytosis, and even ~1–3% of acute bacterial meningitis has no cells on day 1–2 — interpret a "normal" tap against pretest probability. ([[RLR 066 - White Matter Lesions and Post-Traumatic Encephalomalacia|RLR 066]])

## Problem Representation

A woman in her early 40s with relapsing multiple sclerosis on natalizumab (intermittently engaged in care, uncertain JC-virus surveillance) and methamphetamine use disorder is found down and confused after days unseen, awake but perseverating a single fluent phrase ("I'm funny") with a mildly weak, tremulous, twitchy right arm and leg and a flushed, hyper-aroused appearance. Workup shows methamphetamine positivity, an E. coli urinary tract infection, and a left frontal CT hypodensity; MRI reveals classic periventricular ovoid MS lesions, a distinct large juxtacortical left frontal white-matter lesion without mass effect, limbic gray-matter FLAIR hyperintensity (medial temporal lobe, insula, cingulate), and focal dural enhancement overlying the frontal lesion. She has a witnessed focal seizure with rightward version and clears on levetiracetam; CSF is unremarkable with a negative HSV PCR. The unifying diagnosis, made by neuroradiology, is post-traumatic left frontal encephalomalacia with post-traumatic dural enhancement (from an unrecalled bar fall months earlier) serving as a seizure focus that drove non-convulsive status epilepticus and seizure-related limbic MRI changes — with the MS lesions, natalizumab/PML concern, meth, and UTI as distractors.

## Case Narrative

Aaron Berkowitz presents (Reza is away), opening with a moving tribute to his late mentor **Marty Samuels**. The patient: a woman in her early 40s with MS on **natalizumab**, intermittently in care, with methamphetamine use, **found down** and confused. She is awake and alert but can only perseverate one fluent phrase — "I'm funny" — in varying intonations, looking frustrated; her right arm and leg are mildly weak with a drift, and the right arm is tremulous and twitchy.

Rabih reframes the "found down" presentation. Most found-down patients have **symmetric confusion** (reduced arousal without localizing signs); here the **language disturbance confers focality**. In a patient with MS — already prone to focal disease — the pivotal question is whether this is a **new lesion localizing to language**, or a **systemic process (toxin, UTI, metabolic) unmasking a latent vulnerability** in that region. Because *confusion with focality does not equal disease confined to the brain*, he pursues the **neurologic and systemic evaluations in parallel**: first exclude hypoglycemia and opioid effect, then run metabolic/infectious/structural/toxic workups alongside increasingly precise brain imaging.

Aaron supplies the drug teaching: MS therapeutics exploded from two injectables to over a dozen agents; **natalizumab** is an aggressive **alpha-4-integrin inhibitor** (infusion) for highly active disease, and its signature risk is **[[PML|JC-virus PML]]** — patients are screened and serially monitored for JC virus, and seroconversion usually means stopping the drug. That she's on natalizumab implies **highly active MS** and flags PML. The systemic workup then returns leads in **both** spaces: **methamphetamine positive**, an **E. coli UTI**, and a **left frontal CT hypodensity**.

Rabih weighs the contributions and finds each **diluted by chronicity**: long-standing meth use, likely colonization/UTI, and an MS background all weaken any single finding's claim on *today's* delta ([[Law of Proportionality|proportionality]]) — and, unable to "tie the thread," he keeps open that **all could be distractors** hiding something more sinister. He notes that a found-down patient has usually been down a while, so **there is time**; response to withholding meth, response to antibiotics, and clarifying the hypodensity will apportion the causes. He wants **MRI with contrast** (active demyelinating enhancement? — and given the cortical location, consider **venous thrombosis**, so possibly MRV), plus empiric **thiamine** given meth/nutrition.

The MRI is the crux. There are classic **periventricular ovoid** MS lesions, but also a **distinct large juxtacortical left frontal white-matter lesion** — different in morphology, running right up to the cortex, **without mass effect** (Aaron notes this has a *reasonable appearance for PML*, which favors juxtacortical white matter and the middle cerebellar peduncle). And there is **gray-matter FLAIR hyperintensity** in the **medial temporal lobe, insula, and cingulate** (limbic). Rabih flags the **gray-matter involvement as atypical for MS** (and against PML), broadening to **HSV encephalitis, autoimmune/limbic encephalitis, or seizure-related changes** — recalling that seizures themselves produce T2/diffusion/enhancement changes — and reasons that toxic-metabolic processes tend to be **symmetric**, so an asymmetric pattern is *housed in the brain*. Then the post-contrast sequence adds a fourth finding: **focal dural (pachymeningeal) enhancement** overlying the left frontal lesion. Drawing on the recent [[Pachymeningeal vs Leptomeningeal Enhancement|pachy-vs-lepto]] framework, Rabih runs the pachymeningitis differential (low-ICP if smooth/diffuse; dural mets if lumpy; rheumatologic — RA, EGPA, sarcoid; the *few* infections like syphilis; POEMS/hypertrophic pachymeningitis) and even wonders whether **sarcoid has been mimicking MS all along**. Four findings that will not unify.

The reveal unfolds in steps. The **next morning** she turns her head and eyes to the right, extends the right arm, and has a **~15-second focal seizure** — a **left-sided focus**. Seizures could explain the limbic changes; but *why* is she seizing (MS is a weak seizure cause, being white-matter)? She's started on **levetiracetam** and begins to **clear**, and the key learning lands: she was likely in **[[Non-Convulsive Status Epilepticus|non-convulsive status epilepticus]]** from the start — the "I'm funny" perseveration and twitchy arm were ictal/post-ictal. She's covered empirically with **acyclovir** (the MRI could be early HSV) and antibiotics. The **LP is bland** — normal protein/glucose, 3 cells, **HSV PCR negative**, JC PCR pending — prompting Rabih's CSF pearl that **oligo-inflammatory infections** (JC, cryptococcus), autoimmune encephalitis, and even early bacterial meningitis can have minimal pleocytosis, so a bland tap is interpreted against pretest probability (here lowering HSV).

Still stuck on the **dural enhancement** — too focal and real to file away — the team applies the heuristic of *provisionally ignoring one piece to see if the rest crystallize*, but it won't drawer. They take it to **neuroradiology**, whose trauma expertise (a busy county trauma center) yields an instant answer: this is **[[Encephalomalacia|post-traumatic encephalomalacia]]** at the frontal pole, and the overlying **[[Dural Enhancement|dural enhancement is post-traumatic]]** — which **can persist for months to years**, no external marks required. Collateral confirms an **unrecalled bar fall months earlier**, "never the same since." The **JC PCR** and autoimmune-encephalitis panel later return **negative**. Final diagnosis: **old head trauma → frontal encephalomalacia → a seizure focus → NCSE**, with the limbic changes being **[[Seizure-Related MRI Changes|seizure spread]]** and the dural enhancement post-traumatic; **MS, natalizumab/PML, meth, and the UTI were distractors** (meth perhaps lowering the seizure threshold). "A very common thing presenting in a somewhat uncommon way."

In the [[Cognitive Autopsy|autopsy]], Aaron is candid about **[[Anchoring Bias|anchoring]]** on PML — "even though we talk about anchoring, it doesn't mean we can't drop that anchor deep and keep it there" — and names the missed synthesis: the frontal lesion and the dural enhancement were **adjacent and should have been read as one piece**, not separated. The closing lessons: consider NCSE in atypical confusion (EEG or a benzo trial); interrogate your frame and reason without the overarching label; group adjacent findings; and **know when — and which expert — to phone**. Marty Samuels' aphorisms bookend it: *"the differential diagnosis is just the right diagnosis followed by a list of the wrong diagnoses,"* and, on hard-won experiential pattern recognition, *"we see this."*

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring on the frame]].** "MS on natalizumab" made PML almost irresistible; the autopsy's honesty — that awareness of anchoring doesn't prevent it — is the episode's spine. The antidote used was to **remove the frame** and rebuild the differential from the findings.
- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|Confusion-with-focality and parallel workup]].** The found-down schema (symmetric arousal vs focal deficit; brain lesion vs systemic unmasking) structures a simultaneous neuro + systemic evaluation and prevents premature closure on "toxic-metabolic."
- **[[Test Interpretation|Reading the MRI and CSF as interpretations]].** Gray matter atypical for MS; seizure-related changes mimicking encephalitis; a bland CSF that doesn't exclude oligo-inflammatory infection; dural enhancement that is post-traumatic, not neoplastic/infectious — each finding is read for meaning, not tallied.
- **[[Anatomical Approach|Localization]].** The rightward seizure version fixes a left-sided focus, and the juxtacortical location and adjacency of the frontal lesion to the dural enhancement are the anatomic clues the synthesis hinged on.
- **Unify adjacent findings.** The core error was separating the frontal lesion and the overlying dural enhancement; grouping them (as one traumatic process) was the key that a fresh expert supplied instantly.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Humility and phoning the right friend]].** With irreducible uncertainty, the answer came from asking a trauma-expert neuroradiologist — "know when, and which expert, to phone" — and from Marty Samuels' experiential "we see this."

## Teaching Discussion

- Separate confusion from reduced arousal (symmetric) from confusion with a focal deficit; in a patient with prior brain disease, a systemic insult can unmask a latent focal vulnerability, so run neurologic and systemic workups in parallel.
- Weight chronic findings (long-standing substance use, colonization, background disease) by their proportionality to today's change, and stay open to all of them being distractors.
- Consider non-convulsive status epilepticus in atypical confusion — perseveration, subtle twitching, hyper-arousal — and confirm with EEG or a benzodiazepine trial; NCSE usually sits on a focal structural lesion.
- Remember seizures cause MRI changes (T2/FLAIR, diffusion restriction, enhancement), classically limbic, mimicking HSV and autoimmune encephalitis; gray-matter involvement is atypical for MS and should broaden the differential.
- Know that old head trauma can cause encephalomalacia (a seizure focus) and dural enhancement persisting months to years, without external marks — a mundane explanation for an alarming scan.
- Interpret a bland CSF against pretest probability: JC virus and cryptococcus are oligo-inflammatory, autoimmune encephalitis often has minimal pleocytosis, and early bacterial meningitis occasionally has none.
- Interrogate your frame: when an anchor (MS/natalizumab → PML) resists, rebuild the differential from the findings, group adjacent findings as one process, and phone the right expert.
- (Samuels) The differential diagnosis is the right diagnosis followed by a list of wrong ones — and some things you simply learn to recognize: "we see this."
