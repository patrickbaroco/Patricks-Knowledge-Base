## Pearls

- When a brain bleed is found after a fall, the first question is always direction of causality: did the bleed cause the fall, or did the fall cause the bleed?
- Hemorrhage location predicts traumatic likelihood on a gradient: epidural is essentially always traumatic; subdural is very likely traumatic; lobar/distal intraparenchymal and subarachnoid could go either way; lacunar/deep-perforator and brainstem hemorrhage are much less likely to be trauma-related and should raise suspicion that the bleed caused the fall rather than the reverse.
- The four major causes of atraumatic intracranial hemorrhage are hypertensive small-vessel disease/arteriosclerosis (most common), cerebral amyloid angiopathy (classically lobar, not pontine), anticoagulation-related bleeding, and aneurysmal bleeding (classically subarachnoid) — rarer causes like a bleeding tumor or endocarditis-related hemorrhage are reserved for atypical presentations.
- Anticoagulation-related and aneurysmal bleeds are, by their nature, almost always symptomatic and overt; amyloid angiopathy and lacunar infarcts are frequently incidental findings — an ED physician can expect to see an incidental old lacunar stroke roughly every couple of weeks and incidental amyloid angiopathy on MRI roughly every six months.
- Don't assume "blood is blood" on CT — a radiologist can genuinely be uncertain whether an intraparenchymal hyperdensity represents a hemorrhage or a vascular malformation, and that uncertainty is a legitimate reason to pursue MRI for further characterization.
- MRI signal characteristics can favor chronic over acute blood products, and that chronicity is strong, near-definitive evidence that a hemorrhagic finding predates an acute event like a fall rather than resulting from it.
- Small-vessel hypertensive disease is a unifying diagnosis capable of producing both ischemic lacunar stroke and hemorrhagic microbleeds from the same diseased perforating vessels — an explicit brain analogue to MINOCA, where the same vessel bed's behavior differs depending on the specific event.
- With a purely lacunar ischemic stroke pattern, an embolic source workup (e.g., for atrial fibrillation) is often reasonably deprioritized, since embolism to vessels that small is inherently less likely than local small-vessel disease.
- New in-hospital hypertension has essentially two physiologic drivers: catecholamine surge (by far the more common — triggered by anxiety, urinary retention, pain, constipation, or a stimulant/withdrawal state) or aldosterone-mediated (less common — triggered by acute renal or cerebral ischemia, such as aortic/renal artery dissection or acute stroke).
- A persistently normal neurologic exam is strong evidence against hemorrhagic conversion or stroke progression as the explanation for new hypertension, and should generally be trusted over reflexively re-imaging.
- New or worsening hypertension in a patient with known cerebrovascular disease should prompt a secondary-hypertension screen (renal function/proteinuria, obstructive sleep apnea, hyperaldosteronism) before settling on primary hypertension, because the underlying cause changes the optimal antihypertensive agent — proteinuric CKD favors an ACE inhibitor/ARB, confirmed hyperaldosteronism favors spironolactone.
- Acute hemorrhagic and acute ischemic stroke management pull in opposite directions on blood pressure — aggressive lowering to limit hematoma expansion versus permissive hypertension to preserve perfusion — a genuine tension in any patient carrying evidence of both processes historically.

## Problem Representation

A previously healthy, active elderly woman on aspirin for a prior suspected TIA sustains a mechanical fall with head trauma, and is found on imaging to have both an acute-appearing pontine hyperdensity and a chronic lacunar infarct, ultimately characterized as chronic small-vessel hypertensive disease unrelated to the fall.

## Case Narrative

Reza introduced this one differently from most episodes — a real case from his own family, worked through informally over text with Robbie before making it onto the show. The patient was "Nammy," his partner Liz's grandmother: an otherwise very healthy and active elderly woman. She stood up from a restaurant table, tripped, and fell, striking the left side of her head and bruising her left eye. There was no loss of consciousness and no lightheadedness — she was fully aware throughout and described simply catching her foot.

Her relevant history was a prior suspected stroke or TIA, for which head imaging at the time had been negative for a clot; she'd been placed on a loop recorder (a Zio patch) that did not capture atrial fibrillation, and was started on aspirin for secondary prevention on that basis.

In the emergency department, her neurologic exam was normal. CT head, though, showed two things: a hyperdensity in the pons consistent with a hemorrhage, and a separate, older-appearing lacunar infarct.

Robbie's first move was the causality question that frames any post-fall intracranial finding: did the bleed cause the fall, or did the fall cause the bleed? He laid out a location-based gradient for how likely a given hemorrhage is to be traumatic — epidural bleeds are essentially always traumatic, subdural bleeds very likely traumatic, lobar or distal intraparenchymal and subarachnoid bleeds could go either way, and lacunar, deep-perforator, or brainstem hemorrhage are much less likely to be trauma-related. A pontine hemorrhage, by that logic, was much more likely to have caused the fall than to have resulted from it. He recommended examining the orbital structures for evidence of impact-related injury — fracture, retro-orbital hematoma — while not expecting to find intracranial trauma sequelae, since the location argued against a traumatic mechanism.

He then ran through the major causes of atraumatic intracranial hemorrhage: hypertensive small-vessel disease and arteriosclerosis, the most common by far; cerebral amyloid angiopathy, which characteristically produces lobar hemorrhages rather than pontine ones; anticoagulation-related bleeding; and aneurysmal bleeding, classically presenting as subarachnoid hemorrhage. Rarer causes — a bleeding tumor, an endocarditis-related hemorrhage — only enter the differential with atypical features. He distinguished which of these tend to be silent: anticoagulation-related and aneurysmal bleeds are essentially always overt by definition, while amyloid angiopathy and lacunar strokes are frequently incidental — in his own ED practice, he sees an incidental old lacunar stroke on a trauma CT roughly every couple of weeks, and incidental amyloid angiopathy on MRI roughly every six months. Hypertensive small-vessel disease, he noted, is far more common overall than amyloid angiopathy.

Robbie was initially unsure whether a truly asymptomatic pontine hemorrhage was even plausible — he'd expect a more careful specialist exam to turn up something subtle, an eye movement abnormality or a subtle sensory deficit — but said he'd trust the radiology over an insensitive bedside exam if it came to that.

The imaging story unfolded in steps. The initial CT read described a subtle small pontine finding, ambiguous between a hemorrhage and a vascular malformation, alongside the chronic lacunar infarct. An MRI was obtained for further characterization and again showed a tiny pontine focus — favored, on balance, to represent a bleed rather than a vascular malformation, but with an appearance favoring chronic rather than acute blood. The chronic lacunar infarct was reconfirmed as well.

Robbie reflected openly on two assumptions he'd carried into the case that turned out to be wrong-footed. First, he hadn't previously appreciated that a radiologist could be genuinely uncertain whether an intraparenchymal hyperdensity represents blood or a vascular malformation — his mental shortcut had always been "blood is blood." Second, he'd been too quick to dismiss the significance of her lack of symptoms. The MRI's chronicity finding, he said, provided fairly robust proof that the pontine finding predated the fall and was unrelated to it.

His synthesis: the same underlying process — small-vessel hypertensive arteriosclerosis — explained both her chronic lacunar infarct and her chronic pontine microbleed. It wasn't amyloid angiopathy (wrong location), not an aneurysmal bleed (wrong presentation), and not anticoagulation (she wasn't on any). He drew an explicit analogy to MINOCA: the small perforating vessels supplying the internal capsule, deep white matter, and brainstem can, depending on what he called "the mood of the vessel," produce either an ischemic lacunar stroke or a hemorrhagic microbleed from the same disease process — the only neurologic condition he could think of that cuts both ways like that. He added that with a purely lacunar ischemic stroke pattern, an embolic workup (chasing atrial fibrillation, for instance) is often reasonably skipped, since embolism to vessels that small is inherently improbable. Management going forward centered on aggressive risk-factor modification — blood pressure control, lipid control, and the aspirin she was already on from her prior TIA workup.

On day two, at a community hospital without on-site neurosurgery, she developed new hypertension — systolics persistently in the 170s to 180s, with no clearly documented prior history of hypertension (though her chart also didn't clearly document prior normal readings either). Robbie framed acute new hypertension as driven by essentially two mechanisms: a catecholamine surge, by far the more common — triggered by white-coat anxiety, urinary retention, pain, constipation, or a stimulant or withdrawal state such as cocaine, methamphetamine, or alcohol — or an aldosterone-mediated mechanism, less common, typically triggered by acute renal or cerebral ischemia, as in an aortic or renal artery dissection or an acute stroke. With no reason to suspect renal ischemia but with active brain findings on the table, he considered hemorrhagic conversion or a worsening ischemic process, but argued that a persistently normal neurologic exam made both unlikely, and favored a thorough neuro exam over reflexive re-imaging. He also floated simpler explanations — undertreated orbital pain, hospitalization-related anxiety.

Reza connected this back to the broader tension in her case history between hemorrhagic stroke management, which calls for aggressive blood pressure lowering to limit hematoma expansion, and ischemic stroke management, which favors permissive hypertension to preserve perfusion — a genuine conflict in a patient carrying evidence of both processes in her history. He then extended the secondary-hypertension differential himself: a mildly elevated creatinine that could reflect early chronic kidney disease or simply age-related decline in muscle mass affecting the eGFR calculation (if proteinuria were present, he'd favor an ACE inhibitor or ARB like losartan for renal protection); obstructive sleep apnea as a common and underdiagnosed contributor; and hyperaldosteronism as another secondary cause worth screening for, which would specifically favor spironolactone if confirmed. He referenced "Aaron's neuroanatomy textbook" noting that anterior pontine microbleeds are a well-described finding specifically associated with hypertension, reinforcing the anatomic fit of the whole picture.

In the end, the fall was determined to be purely mechanical — a trip, unrelated to either brain finding — and she was recovering well, with a closing comedic aside about needing a "PTE valve" that read as a joke about needing new joints rather than any real medical device.

Reza closed with the chain of reasoning the case had actually walked: why did she fall? Mechanically, unrelated to her brain findings. Why the pontine finding? Chronic small-vessel disease, the same process behind her old lacunar stroke. Why the new hypertension? That one, he stressed, has to be chased down properly — CKD, sleep apnea, hyperaldosteronism — rather than settled as "just primary hypertension," because the answer determines which antihypertensive is actually the right one for her.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Effect on Leading Hypothesis |
|---|---|
| Mechanical fall description (tripped, no LOC, no lightheadedness, fully aware) | Raises probability that the fall itself was purely mechanical rather than caused by a neurologic event |
| CT shows a pontine hyperdensity | Raises probability of an atraumatic (rather than traumatic) hemorrhage, given the deep/central location |
| Chronic-appearing lacunar infarct also present on CT | Raises probability of an underlying chronic small-vessel disease process |
| No anticoagulation, no history of aneurysm, no lobar distribution | Lowers probability of anticoagulation-related bleed, aneurysmal bleed, and amyloid angiopathy respectively |
| MRI favors chronic rather than acute blood products in the pons | Strongly raises probability that the pontine finding predates the fall and is not traumatic |
| New systolic BP 170s–180s on day 2, persistently normal neuro exam | Lowers probability of hemorrhagic conversion or stroke progression as the cause of the hypertension |
| No history of renal or cerebral ischemia symptoms preceding the BP rise | Raises probability of a catecholamine-mediated mechanism over an aldosterone-mediated one for the new hypertension |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The case is built around resisting an initial framing bias — that a bleed found after a fall must be a consequence of the fall — by applying an anatomic prior (bleed location predicts trauma likelihood) rather than defaulting to temporal association. Robbie explicitly names his own prior "worst-case, blood-is-blood" heuristic as a source of diagnostic overconfidence that the case corrected: real uncertainty about hemorrhage versus vascular malformation on CT was something he hadn't previously credited as plausible. The MINOCA analogy functions as an illness-script bridge, letting a clinician already fluent in one paradoxical same-substrate-different-outcome disease process (MINOCA) transfer that reasoning pattern to an unfamiliar one (small-vessel disease causing either ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke).

## Teaching Discussion

The core teaching point is a reusable heuristic: when a hemorrhage is found after a fall, the anatomic location — not just the temporal sequence — should drive the assessment of whether the bleed caused the fall or the fall caused the bleed, with deep/central locations (lacunar, brainstem) pointing toward the bleed as antecedent and superficial locations (epidural, subdural) pointing toward trauma as cause. The episode also reinforces that imaging modality matters: CT alone left real diagnostic uncertainty (bleed versus vascular malformation) that MRI's blood-product-aging characteristics could resolve. Finally, the new-hypertension subplot models a broader principle — a new finding in a patient with a complex neurologic history shouldn't be explained away by the most available narrative (stroke progression) without first checking that narrative against the exam, and a secondary cause should be actively sought when the answer would change management, as it does here with antihypertensive selection.
