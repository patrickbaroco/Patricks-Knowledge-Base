> [!summary] Summary
> A 27-year-old man with metastatic gastric adenocarcinoma on FOLFOX chemotherapy develops progressively slurred speech over 7–8 days, along with new gait imbalance with near-falls and difficulty with near vision. The team first works through the anatomy of dysarthria itself — distinguishing it from aphasia and from confused speech — before applying an explicit **"neurology of cancer" framework** (direct tumor involvement, treatment neurotoxicity, paraneoplastic syndromes, and cancer-associated hypercoagulability/Trousseau syndrome) to a patient whose oncologic history makes it tempting, but not automatically correct, to attribute every new finding to the cancer. Exam reveals a **scanning (cerebellar) dysarthria**, bilateral dysmetria, a wide-based unsteady gait, and a subtle right cranial nerve III adduction weakness uncovered only by cover-uncover testing — a package that localizes to the posterior fossa and points toward **leptomeningeal carcinomatosis**, ultimately confirmed by MRI leptomeningeal enhancement in the cerebellar folia and CSF cytology positive for adenocarcinoma.

## Pearls

- **Dysarthria has its own internal anatomy.** Distal articulatory weakness produces distinct patterns — labial (CN VII/lips, cannot make "p"/"b"), lingual (CN XII/tongue, cannot make "l"/"t"), and guttural (CN IX/X/larynx-pharynx, cannot make "g"/"k") dysarthria — while coordination problems (cerebellar) or upper motor neuron/corticobulbar tract lesions produce different patterns still, including a "scanning" quality with fluctuating volume, and difficulty with complex syllable sequences (e.g., "pa-ta-ka" or "Methodist Episcopal") even when the individual component sounds are intact.
- A patient's **frustration level** can be a subtle but useful discriminator between aphasia subtypes: Broca's (expressive) aphasia patients typically know what they want to say and appear frustrated by their inability to say it, while Wernicke's (receptive) aphasia patients are often unaware of their errors and unbothered by them.
- **"Neurology in the wild"** — much of clinical neurology, especially on a consult service, is not "pure" neurology (stroke, seizure, Parkinson's) in isolation but neurology embedded within another specialty's patient — obstetric neurology, oncologic neurology, and so on — and benefits from its own dedicated framework rather than a generic one.
- A useful **"neurology of cancer" framework**: (1) direct involvement — metastasis to or compression of a nervous system structure; (2) neurotoxicity of cancer treatment (chemotherapy, radiation, immunotherapy); (3) paraneoplastic (antibody-mediated) syndromes; (4) cancer-associated hypercoagulability (Trousseau syndrome) causing stroke.
- The **cover-uncover test** can unmask a subtle ocular motor weakness that the brain is actively "editing" into apparent conjugate gaze during binocular fixation — breaking fixation by covering one eye removes that compensation and lets a small deviation drift into view.
- **A single negative MRI or a single negative lumbar puncture does not rule out leptomeningeal carcinomatosis.** MRI sensitivity is highly variable (roughly 18–87% depending on series), and cytologic sensitivity on a first lumbar puncture is only about 45–70% for carcinoma (and far lower — as low as 2–32% in one large series — for CNS lymphoma), rising with repeated, high-volume sampling.

## Problem Representation

A 27-year-old man with metastatic gastric adenocarcinoma on FOLFOX chemotherapy presents with progressively worsening dysarthria over 7–8 days, accompanied by new gait imbalance with near-falls and a fall, and new difficulty with near vision, found on exam to have a scanning cerebellar dysarthria, bilateral dysmetria, a wide-based unsteady gait, and a subtle right cranial nerve III adduction weakness — a posterior fossa syndrome ultimately due to leptomeningeal carcinomatosis.

## Case Narrative

Doug presented the chief concern simply: slurred speech. Diane immediately began interrogating what "slurred speech" actually meant to the family reporting it — was this true dysarthria, or could it reflect a language problem (aphasia) or confusion — and asked about onset and associated focal deficits. Berkowitz used this to launch into the anatomy of dysarthria itself, presuming (correctly, as it turned out) that Doug, himself a neurologist, was using the term precisely.

Dysarthria — literally, failure or difficulty of articulation — can be localized by thinking about what the articulatory apparatus requires. At the most distal level, weakness of any of the relevant cranial nerves produces a characteristic pattern: facial (CN VII) weakness produces **labial dysarthria** (difficulty with "p" and "b" sounds — Berkowitz recalled a Guillain-Barré patient with bilateral facial weakness who learned to hold her lips together and substitute other phrasing to avoid needing a "p" sound); tongue (CN XII) weakness produces **lingual dysarthria** (difficulty with "l" and "t" sounds, tested with "la-la-la" or "ta-ta-ta"); and pharyngeal/laryngeal (CN IX/X) weakness produces **guttural dysarthria** (difficulty with "g" and "k" sounds). Above the level of the cranial nerves themselves, a **coordination problem** — classically cerebellar, as anyone who has observed alcohol intoxication has witnessed — produces its own pattern, and above that, the **corticobulbar tract** (the upper motor neurons descending from cortex to the brainstem — "la bulbe," the brainstem, in Berkowitz's aside on the French etymology) can be affected, producing a spastic dysarthria that is often most apparent not on single-sound testing but on complex syllable sequences ("pa-ta-ka," or a mentor's favored test phrase "Methodist Episcopal").

Doug then gave the history. A 27-year-old man with metastatic gastric adenocarcinoma (diagnosed roughly 18 months earlier, with lymph node and peritoneal metastases), currently admitted for treatment complications including generalized pain and feeding difficulty, was found to have progressively slurred speech over the preceding 7–8 days — persistent, not fluctuating, and subjectively worsening — that had not been the reason for admission but came up on review with the family. He also had new gait imbalance with multiple near-falls and one actual fall the morning of presentation, and new difficulty with near vision — trouble reading his phone, needing to squint and reposition it, something he had never experienced before. He denied frank diplopia, vertigo, headache, or neck pain, and had no focal weakness or sensory changes, though he did have generalized fatigue. His oncologic history was extensive: gastric adenocarcinoma with a recent cancer-associated pulmonary embolism (on heparin), a recent admission for ascites requiring NJ tube placement complicated by hospital-acquired pneumonia (now on home oxygen), and current treatment with FOLFOX (folinic acid, 5-fluorouracil, oxaliplatin), having previously been on FOLFIRI. He was also recently started on methadone and other opioids for pain, with multiple antiemetics. He had no tobacco, alcohol, or drug use, and was described as an unusually positive, community-engaged young man.

Faced with this dense history, Angel began with a broad localization framework: given the complexity, it was hard to know immediately whether this was intracranial or extracranial, and given the known metastatic gastric cancer, a highly relevant consideration was direct metastatic involvement of the nervous system. Angel also asked a subtle and important question — whether the patient appeared *frustrated* by his speech difficulty — reasoning toward the distinction between Broca's aphasia (expressive, patient aware and frustrated) and Wernicke's aphasia (receptive, patient often unaware and unbothered), noting Wernicke's is considerably less common in practice than in textbooks, given the vascular territories typically involved. Berkowitz acknowledged the anchoring risk explicitly: once cancer is on the table, it becomes hard not to wonder whether every new symptom is somehow related to it — a bias worth naming even when, as would later prove true here, the anchor turns out to be correct.

Pressed on where metastases would need to be to produce this syndrome, Dion suggested the cerebellum (for the gait imbalance and dysarthria) and possibly the brainstem, and specifically flagged the entity of **leptomeningeal carcinomatosis** (carcinomatous meningitis) — metastases to the meninges themselves, often producing multiple cranial neuropathies, and classically seen on MRI as contrast enhancement layering in the folia of the cerebellum.

Berkowitz then zoomed out to articulate the broader teaching frame of the episode: **"neurology in the wild."** Much of what neurologists actually do — especially on inpatient consult services — is not "pure" neurology (a patient simply presenting with a stroke, a seizure, Parkinson's disease) but neurology embedded within another specialty's patient population: the neurology of pregnancy and the postpartum period, the neurology of the ICU, and, as here, the **neurology of cancer**. He laid out a framework with four limbs: (1) **direct involvement** — metastases to a nervous system structure (brainstem, spinal cord, rarely nerve or plexus) or compression of one by tumor; (2) **neurologic complications of cancer treatment** — chemotherapy, radiation, and immunotherapy each having their own signature neurotoxicities (platinum agents and taxanes, as microtubule inhibitors that impair fast axonal transport, commonly cause a peripheral neuropathy during treatment, and oxaliplatin specifically causes a distinctive **cold-induced paresthesia**; cytarabine can cause an acute cerebellar syndrome); (3) **paraneoplastic syndromes**, in which an immune response mounted against the tumor cross-reacts against neural tissue ("friendly fire") — some antibodies (e.g., anti-NMDA receptor) cause one specific syndrome, while others (e.g., anti-Hu) can cause almost anything (neuropathy, ganglionopathy, encephalopathy, myelopathy); and (4) **cancer-associated hypercoagulability — Trousseau syndrome** — particularly with mucin-secreting adenocarcinomas, which increases stroke risk independent of any direct tumor involvement. Berkowitz recounted the eponym's history (per his mentor Marty Samuels): Armand Trousseau not only described the syndrome but later developed it himself, correctly self-diagnosed a hidden gastric cancer from his own leg clot, and died of the condition he had named — one of the few eponyms, Berkowitz noted, that genuinely "deserves the apostrophe."

Returning to the specific findings, Berkowitz asked about the difficulty with near vision. Near vision requires **convergence** — the eyes turning inward, mediated by the medial rectus muscles, innervated by **cranial nerve III**, which originates in the **midbrain**. He introduced the bedside **"Rule of Four"** for brainstem localization (cranial nerves III and IV, including all vertical gaze function, live in the midbrain; the remaining nerves distribute through the pons and medulla) as a quick way to convert a cranial nerve finding into a level of the brainstem. This raised the possibility of a dorsal midbrain process affecting the convergence pathway — though Berkowitz noted that a true structural midbrain lesion would likely produce more additional ocular findings than were present here — versus a non-lesional (paraneoplastic) process affecting the same territory, prompting discussion of **paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration** (classically anti-Yo, more common with ovarian and breast cancer) and **rhombencephalitis** (brainstem-specific paraneoplastic/autoimmune encephalitis, including a newer described antibody, Kelch-like protein 11, associated with testicular cancer).

Dion outlined the exam findings to prioritize: reflexes (to distinguish upper from lower motor neuron patterns), a careful cranial nerve exam (given cranial nerve XII involvement, checking neighboring nerves IX–XI as well), and cerebellar/coordination testing (finger-to-nose, Romberg). Doug then presented the exam: afebrile, tachycardic to 110, cachectic, on 2L nasal cannula. Neurologically, the patient was fatigued but alert and fully oriented, with **no aphasia** on detailed testing — confirming this was dysarthria, not a language disorder. His speech had a **scanning quality** — fluctuating volume and tempo — with a prominent, inconsistent difficulty on rapid alternating "pa-ta-ka" testing. Cranial nerve exam showed normal fundi and visual acuity, grossly intact extraocular movements, but a **right exotropia on cover-uncover testing**. There was **bilateral dysmetria and loss of check** on finger-to-nose testing, and a **wide-based, unsteady gait**. Motor and reflex exams were normal, with only **mildly decreased vibration sense in the toes**.

Dion raised an important caution: reflexes were normal, which was somewhat unexpected, and — critically — **not all ataxia is cerebellar**. The cerebellum is only as good as the sensory information (particularly proprioception) it receives; a patient can appear ataxic from a peripheral neuropathy or dorsal column dysfunction with a perfectly healthy cerebellum. The mild distal vibratory loss raised the possibility of **B12 deficiency** (subacute combined degeneration), which Berkowitz noted he and Dion had seen commonly working together in Haiti, often presenting without the "expected" macrocytic anemia because coexisting iron deficiency can normalize the mean corpuscular volume. However, B12 deficiency would not explain the ocular finding, leaving several possibilities on the table simultaneously: a single unifying cerebellar/brainstem lesion; a coincidental, separate oxaliplatin-related mild neuropathy; a coexisting nutritional deficiency; or multiple concurrent problems layered on top of one another in a medically complex patient.

Doug then walked through the mechanics of the **cover-uncover test** and the subtle right cranial nerve III finding it revealed. He described the underlying concept: the brain acts as an "editor," using extra innervation to keep a weak muscle in alignment during normal binocular fixation, so a small deficit may not manifest as overt diplopia or an obvious deviation on standard extraocular movement testing. Covering one eye removes the need for that compensatory fixation; when covering the *left* eye, the *right* eye was observed to drift outward — indicating the right eye's medial rectus was subtly weak in adduction, since without fixation demands it defaulted toward its unopposed lateral position. Berkowitz reinforced the mechanism from a slightly different angle: the weak muscle is doing extra work to maintain alignment during fixation, so when fixation is broken, it "relaxes" in the direction *opposite* the underlying weakness — a genuinely confusing but important piece of applied extraocular anatomy. This subtle finding was interpreted as a partial right CN III palsy (or at minimum a convergence-relevant, medial-rectus-territory deficit), consistent with — though not localizing exclusively to — the midbrain/perineural pathway already under discussion.

Asked what single test to order next, Angel chose an MRI brain with contrast, correctly reasoning that contrast enhancement would be needed both to characterize a possible mass and, critically, to detect the subtler finding of leptomeningeal enhancement that a non-contrast study could miss entirely. Berkowitz then walked through the images: on FLAIR, subtle hyperintensity was visible in the folia at the superior aspect of the cerebellum, just posterior to the midbrain; on post-contrast T1, rather than showing clean parenchymal enhancement, there was **leptomeningeal enhancement layering in the cerebellar folia**, with some pachymeningeal enhancement anterior to the temporal lobes and possible asymmetric enhancement of the third and sixth cranial nerves — with no parenchymal metastases and only a suggestion of early hydrocephalus. Doug added a key technical teaching point: the FLAIR abnormality specifically represented **FLAIR non-suppression** — since FLAIR is a T2 sequence designed to null the signal from free water (CSF), any residual brightness in a CSF space implies something else is present (protein, blood, infection, or malignant cells) that the standard nulling algorithm cannot subtract out.

Given the immunocompromised, borderline-leukopenic status of the patient, the team pursued a lumbar puncture both to evaluate for the leading diagnosis (leptomeningeal carcinomatosis) and to exclude a treatable infectious process, while also repleting thiamine empirically given his malabsorption and malnutrition risk (with Berkowitz noting Wernicke's encephalopathy as a frequently under-considered differential — its classic triad is famously insensitive, and it is not limited to alcohol use disorder but can occur with any significant malabsorptive or nutritionally depleted state, including hyperemesis gravidarum). The lumbar puncture showed a low glucose (44, with a serum glucose of ~100), an elevated protein (77), and 24 white cells, lymphocyte-predominant, with cytology returning **positive for adenocarcinoma** within about two days — a definitive diagnosis of **leptomeningeal carcinomatosis** from the patient's known gastric adenocarcinoma.

Doug closed with several teaching points on the diagnostic performance of the available tests: MRI sensitivity for leptomeningeal carcinomatosis is reported anywhere from roughly 18% to 87% depending on the series, meaning a negative scan does not rule it out; a first lumbar puncture is only about 45–70% sensitive for carcinomatous meningitis (rising to roughly 80% on a second tap and above 90% on a third high-volume tap), and sensitivity is even lower for CNS lymphoma specifically (one large 2012 series reported first-LP sensitivity of only 2–32%). The practical lesson: maintain a high index of suspicion in a cancer patient with multiple, hard-to-localize cranial neuropathies even if an initial MRI or LP is unrevealing, and be prepared to repeat imaging and to pursue repeated, high-volume lumbar punctures.

Berkowitz added a cautionary case from his own experience: a patient with gastric cancer who presented with fluctuating, difficult-to-interpret episodes of posturing and altered consciousness, with a normal EEG and an initially normal MRI, ultimately found to have gastric cancer cells in the CSF — leptomeningeal disease causing intermittent, fluctuating elevated intracranial pressure via communicating hydrocephalus (without frankly dilated ventricles), mimicking a seizure disorder until the lumbar puncture established the diagnosis. He also noted, as an aside on tumor biology, that leptomeningeal and parenchymal brain metastases tend not to co-occur as commonly as one might expect — cancers seem to have an individual "seed and soil" propensity for one compartment or the other — and that leptomeningeal carcinomatosis specifically from gastric adenocarcinoma is uncommon except very late in the disease course, with true paraneoplastic disease from gastric cancer being rare overall and more classically associated with the neuroendocrine subtype rather than adenocarcinoma, which made this an unusual but ultimately correctly-anchored presentation.

Maria closed with a comprehensive teaching-points summary, reinforcing the dysarthria localization framework, the frustration-as-aphasia-clue pearl, the convergence/Rule-of-Four discussion, the four-limbed neurology-of-cancer framework (including Trousseau syndrome as cancer-associated hypercoagulability), the MRI sequence physics (T1 vs. T2 vs. FLAIR, and FLAIR non-suppression), and the imperfect, improving-with-repetition sensitivity of both MRI and LP for leptomeningeal carcinomatosis.

| Progressive over 7–8 days, with new gait imbalance, near-falls, and a fall | Favors a subacute process (rather than an acute vascular event) — a tempo consistent with tumor growth, an evolving paraneoplastic or infectious process, or a gradually developing hypercoagulable/microvascular process, rather than a single discrete stroke |
| Known metastatic gastric adenocarcinoma on chemotherapy | Raises the "neurology of cancer" differential broadly (direct metastasis, treatment neurotoxicity, paraneoplastic syndrome, Trousseau-associated stroke) but is explicitly flagged as an anchoring risk — the presence of cancer should raise, not by itself confirm, a cancer-related explanation |
| New difficulty with near vision, localized on questioning to a convergence problem | Points to the medial rectus / CN III / midbrain convergence pathway, raising suspicion for either a structural midbrain process or a paraneoplastic/infiltrative process at that level, rather than isolating symptoms to the cerebellum alone |
| On oxaliplatin (FOLFOX) | Explains, at most, a mild peripheral neuropathy (classically cold-induced) but does not account for dysarthria, ataxia, or the convergence/near-vision problem — lowers the likelihood that chemotherapy neurotoxicity alone explains the full picture |

| Mildly decreased vibration sense in the toes | Raises the alternative or additional possibility of a sensory (dorsal column or peripheral nerve) contribution to the gait ataxia — a reminder that not all ataxia is cerebellar — and reframes B12 deficiency and oxaliplatin neuropathy as competing or coexisting explanations for this one finding specifically |
| Subtle right CN III (adduction) weakness found only on cover-uncover testing | Confirms a genuine, if subtle, cranial neuropathy rather than a purely cerebellar coordination problem, strengthening localization to the posterior fossa/brainstem-adjacent leptomeninges rather than the cerebellar parenchyma alone |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

**Leptomeningeal carcinomatosis.** Metastatic spread of solid-tumor cells to the leptomeninges, producing multifocal, often hard-to-localize findings — commonly multiple cranial neuropathies, cerebellar dysfunction, and/or fluctuating symptoms from communicating hydrocephalus. Classic MRI finding is leptomeningeal (not parenchymal) contrast enhancement layering in the cerebellar folia; CSF often shows low glucose, elevated protein, and a lymphocytic pleocytosis, with cytology as the definitive test — though sensitivity of both imaging and cytology is imperfect and improves with repeated, high-volume testing.

**Trousseau syndrome.** Cancer-associated hypercoagulability, classically with mucin-secreting adenocarcinomas, causing venous and/or arterial thromboembolism (including stroke) independent of direct tumor involvement of the nervous system — named for Armand Trousseau, who both described the syndrome and later died of it after self-diagnosing his own occult gastric cancer from a leg clot.

**Wernicke's encephalopathy — the easily-forgotten differential.** Classically taught as a triad (confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia) that is, in practice, highly insensitive — most affected patients have only one or two elements. Not limited to alcohol use disorder; occurs with any substantial malabsorptive or nutritionally depleted state, and was explicitly named here as a diagnosis the team almost overlooked in a malnourished, malabsorbing cancer patient with ataxia and an eye movement abnormality.

**Anchoring bias — "once we hear cancer."** Berkowitz explicitly names the risk of anchoring on the patient's known malignancy as the explanation for every new symptom, simply because a plausible-sounding cancer-related mechanism is always available. In this case the anchor proved correct, but the team still worked through infectious, nutritional, chemotherapy-related, and paraneoplastic alternatives before concluding it was cancer after all — modeling that anchoring risk should prompt deliberate consideration of alternatives, not an automatic switch away from the most obvious explanation.

**Reasoning tool — "neurology in the wild" / the neurology-of-cancer framework.** When a patient's neurologic presentation is embedded within a complex non-neurologic (here, oncologic) illness, an explicit sub-framework — direct involvement, treatment neurotoxicity, paraneoplastic syndrome, hypercoagulability — helps organize an otherwise unwieldy differential, rather than reasoning about the neurologic findings in isolation from the underlying disease.

**Diagnostic humility — imperfect test sensitivity.** Both MRI and first-attempt CSF cytology have meaningfully imperfect sensitivity for leptomeningeal carcinomatosis (and even more so for lymphomatous meningitis). A single negative study should not be treated as ruling out the diagnosis in a patient with a compatible clinical picture; repeat, high-volume testing is often required.

## Teaching Discussion

This episode's central organizing idea is **"neurology in the wild"** — Berkowitz's term for the reality that much of clinical neurology practice, especially inpatient consultation, involves neurologic disease embedded within another specialty's patient population rather than presenting in isolation. The explicit **neurology-of-cancer framework** built here (direct involvement / treatment neurotoxicity / paraneoplastic syndrome / Trousseau-associated hypercoagulability) is a reusable structure for any future oncology-adjacent case, much as the diplopia batch elsewhere in this vault built a reusable anatomical framework for double vision (see [[CPS 199 - Diplopia and an Isolated Microvascular Sixth Nerve Palsy|CPS 199]] and its companion episodes).

The **dysarthria localization schema** (labial/lingual/guttural articulatory weakness vs. cerebellar coordination problems vs. corticobulbar upper motor neuron dysfunction) pairs naturally with this vault's existing cranial-nerve-localization teaching, and the bedside **"Rule of Four"** for brainstem localization (CN III/IV in the midbrain, remaining nerves in pons/medulla) is a compact companion to the "neighborhood sign" reasoning used in [[CPS 199 - Diplopia and an Isolated Microvascular Sixth Nerve Palsy|CPS 199]] and the multiple-cranial-neuropathy localization framework built in [[CPS 339 - Multiple Cranial Neuropathies and a Cerebellopontine Angle Vestibular Schwannoma|CPS 339]].

This episode also offers a direct, illuminating comparison to [[CPS 269 - Headache and Leptomeningeal Lymphomatosis|CPS 269]] — the vault's other leptomeningeal-disease episode. Both share the core clinical illness script (multifocal, hard-to-localize findings; leptomeningeal enhancement on MRI; CSF cytology as the diagnostic gold standard with imperfect first-attempt sensitivity), but differ in the underlying malignancy (carcinoma here vs. lymphoma there) and in the reported diagnostic-yield numbers for CSF cytology, worth cross-referencing directly when either note is revisited.
