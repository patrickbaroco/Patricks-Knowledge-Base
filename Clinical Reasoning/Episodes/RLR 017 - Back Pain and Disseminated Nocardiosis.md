## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 77-year-old man who lives most of the year in Guatemala presents with three to four weeks of severe [[Low Back Pain|low back pain]] radiating to the legs, daily fevers with [[Rigors|rigors]], cough, dyspnea, and weight loss. He is heavily immunocompromised on two axes — a prior [[Splenectomy|splenectomy]] and chronic low-dose [[Prednisone|prednisone]] for [[Polymyalgia Rheumatica|polymyalgia rheumatica]] — with a rich exposure history (gardener, pet parakeet, US Southwest travel) and a remote [[Pseudomyxoma Peritonei|pseudomyxoma peritonei]]. Rabih works the back anatomically and reads the rigors as endovascular/deep infection, while the multi-week tempo and immunosuppression push both hosts off run-of-the-mill bacteria toward atypical, [[Granulomatous Osteomyelitis|granulomatous]] disease; Reza deliberately builds — then refuses to over-anchor on — a Central-America frame. Imaging confirms the suspicion: a destructive L4–L5 lesion with [[Vertebral Osteomyelitis|discitis-osteomyelitis]], a ventral [[Epidural Abscess|epidural abscess]], and multifocal pneumonia. The reasoning then hinges on the [[Pyogenic versus Granulomatous Osteomyelitis|pyogenic-versus-granulomatous]] branch point — sterile blood cultures and a bone biopsy showing chronic inflammation *without neutrophils* argue against pyogenic, and a serpiginous rash plus prior lung-nodule pathology reading as **necrotizing granulomas** point squarely at a granulomatous infection. A sweeping serologic and cultural workup for [[Coccidioidomycosis|cocci]], [[Histoplasmosis|histo]], [[Blastomycosis|blasto]], [[Paracoccidioidomycosis|paracocci]], [[Brucellosis|Brucella]], TB, [[Sporotrichosis|Sporothrix]], and [[Melioidosis|melioidosis]] comes back negative; the answer arrives only via [[Karius Testing|plasma microbial cell-free DNA]]: **[[Nocardiosis|disseminated Nocardia]]**. He is treated with TMP-SMX, amikacin, and meropenem for roughly a year and recovers. The episode closes on cognitive humility — both hosts missed Nocardia and reframe it as an [[Illness Script Flexibility|illness-script]] to widen.

## Pearls

- **Rigors plus [[Low Back Pain|low back pain]] should trigger an anatomical hunt for a deep or endovascular infection.** Picture the lower back layer by layer — [[Psoas Abscess|psoas muscle]], vertebrae, and the [[Epidural Abscess|epidural space]] beneath the dura — because true [[Rigors|rigors]] signal bacteremic/endovascular disease, and the three infections that hide there are [[Psoas Abscess|psoas abscess]], [[Vertebral Osteomyelitis|vertebral osteomyelitis]], and epidural abscess. Most low back pain is non-specific, so it is the red flags (neurologic compromise, cancer history) and the systemic signature that earn the deep dive.
- **Tempo plus immunosuppression rewrites the infectious differential.** Three to four weeks of illness is too indolent for garden-variety pyogenic bacteria, and this man carried *two* immune deficits — [[Asplenia|asplenia]] (encapsulated organisms, [[Babesiosis|Babesia]], malaria) and chronic [[Prednisone|steroids]] (opportunistic fungi, [[Nocardiosis|Nocardia]], reactivation) — so the prior shifts toward atypical, [[Granulomatous Osteomyelitis|granulomatous]], and fungal disease before any culture returns.
- **The pyogenic-versus-granulomatous branch point is the crux of infectious osteomyelitis, and the biopsy can move it even when it is "non-diagnostic."** [[Pyogenic versus Granulomatous Osteomyelitis|Pyogenic osteomyelitis]] is far more common, but its gap to granulomatous narrows here on two counts — *no bacteremia* (pyogenic usually seeds the blood) and a *pulmonary focus* (where granulomatous disease begins). Because neutrophils are present in over 90% of pyogenic osteomyelitis and are easy to find (whereas granulomas are easy to miss), a bone biopsy showing chronic inflammation *without* neutrophils argues meaningfully against pyogenic even without a positive stain.
- **A negative serologic and cultural workup does not exclude an atypical infection — it just exhausts the easy tests.** Serologies need an intact, unhurried immune system to turn positive, and the classic pathogens ([[Nocardiosis|Nocardia]] especially) are culture-elusive, needing special media, micro-lab communication, and up to two weeks to grow; here every conventional assay was negative and only [[Karius Testing|plasma microbial cell-free DNA]] made the diagnosis.
- **[[Sporotrichosis|Sporothrix]] and [[Nocardiosis|Nocardia]] share a footprint the hosts underweighted — soil/gardening exposure, cutaneous plus pulmonary plus osseous disease.** The serpiginous rash pulled both hosts toward disseminated sporotrichosis in an [[Asplenia|asplenic]] patient, a reasonable and well-argued guess; the actual answer, Nocardia, sits on the same [[Granulomatous Osteomyelitis|granulomatous]] list and shares the gardener-on-steroids risk profile but was under-prioritized because its illness script is remembered as indolent, not fulminant.
- **Illness scripts must flex: [[Nocardiosis|Nocardia]] can be granulomatous and can put a patient in the ICU with [[ARDS]].** The reflexive script of an indolent opportunist nearly wrote it out of contention despite textbook risk factors (chronic [[Prednisone|prednisone]], gardening); the lesson both hosts take is to widen the script's severity and histology rather than to feel defeated by the miss.

## Problem Representation

A 77-year-old Spanish-speaking man who resides most of the year in Guatemala — asplenic, on chronic low-dose prednisone for polymyalgia rheumatica, with remote appendiceal pseudomyxoma peritonei, and exposures including lifelong gardening, a pet parakeet, and recent US Southwest travel — presents with three to four weeks of severe low back pain radiating to the legs, daily fevers with rigors, dry cough, dyspnea, nausea/vomiting, and weight loss. He is febrile and hypoxemic with lumbar midline tenderness, an irregular cardiac rhythm, and an intact neurologic exam; labs show leukopenia (WBC 3.4), normocytic anemia, a mixed hepatic pattern with a bone-capable alkaline phosphatase, normal haptoglobin/LDH (against hemolysis/malaria), and a chest radiograph with bibasilar opacities plus a focal right-mid-lung lesion. Cross-sectional imaging reveals multifocal pneumonia and a destructive L4–L5 lesion with discitis-osteomyelitis, a ventral epidural abscess, and epidural phlegmon; blood cultures and a broad serologic/fungal/mycobacterial workup are negative, and a bone biopsy shows chronic inflammation without granulomas or neutrophils. After a serpiginous cutaneous eruption and re-review of a prior lung-nodule specimen showing necrotizing granulomas with microabscesses, plasma microbial cell-free DNA (Karius) returns Nocardia, establishing disseminated nocardiosis treated with TMP-SMX, amikacin, and meropenem.

## Case Narrative

The episode opens with the usual banter — a palindrome joke, Reza's pre-recording exercise to steady his nerves before a guest presenter, and Rabih confessing he does not know who Chopin is — before welcoming guest presenter **Lawrence Yuen**, a hospitalist at Northwestern and amateur Chopin pianist.

Lawrence presents a 77-year-old Spanish-speaking man who lives most of the year in Guatemala and travels to the US, where his family is, for medical care. He has three to four weeks of severe [[Low Back Pain|back pain]] radiating to the lower extremities, with daily fevers, [[Rigors|rigors]] lasting about 25 minutes once or twice a day, and a systemic cluster of dry cough, dyspnea, fatigue, weight loss, nausea, vomiting, anorexia, and bilateral leg pain.

Rabih takes the back anatomically. Most low back pain is non-specific (about 95%), so the move is to visualize the specific structures — the [[Psoas Abscess|psoas muscles]] alongside the vertebrae, the vertebral bones, and the dura beneath them — and then decide whether the presentation earns a jump to a specific cause. The red flags that predict a specific spinal diagnosis are advanced neurologic compromise ([[Myelopathy|myelopathy]] or cauda equina) and a history of cancer; here neither is yet in hand, but the constellation is worrying. He anchors on the most predictive extraspinal feature — the rigors — which he equates with systemic, usually endovascular, infection, and names the deep back infections that could unify a febrile back: [[Psoas Abscess|psoas abscess]], [[Vertebral Osteomyelitis|vertebral osteomyelitis]] (pyogenic or granulomatous), and [[Epidural Abscess|epidural abscess]]. He caveats that the common cause of fever plus back pain is influenza or COVID atop chronic back pain, but this poorly characterized multi-system illness does not fit that.

Lawrence adds the Guatemala course: a diagnosis of pneumonia (moxifloxacin, then ciprofloxacin), a diagnosis of [[Malaria|malaria]] (pyrimethamine and sulfadiazine), and lumbar CT/MRI read as a "slipped disc," after which he was sent to the US. Reza uses this to build two frames without anchoring on either — one that activates Central-American organisms ([[Histoplasmosis|histoplasmosis]], [[Paracoccidioidomycosis|paracoccidioidomycosis]]) and one that resists the geographic anchor. He treats the empiric therapy and the imaging as soft data: a response to treatment can adjust post-test probability, and an MRI can miss a spinal infection if timed poorly (he recalls a gadolinium MRI that missed an epidural abscess because it was done too early). Crucially, the three-to-four-week duration tells him this is not run-of-the-mill bacterial disease but an atypical process — mycobacterial or fungal — and he frames a multi-systemic illness leaving a signature in the lungs, spine, and legs.

The background deepens the picture. The past history includes appendiceal cancer with [[Pseudomyxoma Peritonei|pseudomyxoma peritonei]] in 2011 (complicated by PE, no recurrence, but a surveillance chest CT two months prior showed a lung nodule that was wedge-resected with benign pathology), hyperlipidemia, BPH, and [[Polymyalgia Rheumatica|polymyalgia rheumatica]] on prednisone 7.5 mg daily; surgical history includes prior [[Splenectomy|splenectomy]], cholecystectomy, and colon resection. Rabih riffs on pseudomyxoma peritonei — a benign mucinous appendiceal tumor producing gelatinous ascites and hepatic scalloping — then files the cancer history as raising, but not dominating, concern for malignant spinal disease. He fixes on the two immunologic levers: PMR is always a working diagnosis (mimicked by RA, crystalline disease, even [[Crowned Dens Syndrome|crowned dens syndrome]]), and its [[Prednisone|steroids]] threaten bone two ways — metastatic infection and osteoporotic fracture. [[Asplenia|Asplenia]] adds risk from encapsulated organisms (pneumococcus, H. influenzae, meningococcus) and intraerythrocytic pathogens ([[Babesiosis|Babesia]], malaria). His bottom line: an asplenic patient with rigors needs empiric antibiotics now and the rest sorted later.

The social history is a differential unto itself. He is a retired gardener who still gardens ([[Sporotrichosis|Sporothrix schenckii]], soil organisms), keeps a healthy pet parakeet ([[Hypersensitivity Pneumonitis|hypersensitivity pneumonitis]], [[Psittacosis|Chlamydia psittaci]], [[Cryptococcosis|Cryptococcus]], [[Histoplasmosis|Histoplasma]]), and briefly visited the US Southwest ([[Coccidioidomycosis|coccidioidomycosis]]). Reza walks each exposure but declines to anchor until imaging and labs localize the process, and Rabih supplies the pigeon-nerd callback that birds classically carry psittacosis, cryptococcosis, and histoplasmosis.

The exam shows a temperature of 102.2°F, oxygen saturation 89%, mild respiratory distress, clear lungs, an irregular random cardiac rhythm without murmur, a soft abdomen, midline lumbar tenderness, a negative straight-leg raise, and 5/5 lower-extremity strength. Rabih notes the fever confirms inflammation and that the exam lacks overt evidence of common bacterial sources — but the hypoxemia and the lumbar tenderness are the two live threads, and if the spine harbors infection the case may loop back to the chest: roughly a third of vertebral osteomyelitis carries concomitant [[Endocarditis|endocarditis]] (pyogenic route), while granulomatous osteomyelitis (TB, Histo, Brucella, Coxy) usually has a pulmonary focus.

Labs show sodium 135, creatinine 1.28, calcium 8.5, ALT 74, AST 55, alkaline phosphatase 200, normal bilirubin, WBC 3.4, hemoglobin 10.6, platelets 385, and a chest radiograph with patchy bibasilar airspace opacities plus a focal 2.2-cm right-mid-lung opacity. Reza narrates his method of [[Signal versus Noise|tracking each organ]] with pathology — prompting Rabih's line, "Reza is tracking you and he will find the answer." He reads alveolar airspace disease as most likely white cells (infection), flags the mixed hepatic pattern but warns the [[Alkaline Phosphatase|alkaline phosphatase]] may be bone in origin (a [[GGT|GGT]] would disambiguate) and that an AST>ALT pattern can be extrahepatic (hemolysis, rhabdomyolysis — worth a smear, LDH, haptoglobin given the malaria question). His plan: image the spine, start empiric antibiotics, draw blood cultures, and send a broad fungal panel ([[Beta-D-Glucan|beta-D-glucan]], [[Galactomannan|galactomannan]], coccidioidal antibodies, urine antigens).

The confirmatory data land. GGT 169, LDH 259, haptoglobin 382 (against hemolysis/malaria), and negative blood cultures; the patient is started on vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam. CT abdomen/pelvis shows multifocal basilar pneumonia and a destructive osseous lesion at L5 with epidural extension and mild thecal sac compression; CT chest shows patchy ground-glass opacities; and lumbar MRI shows [[Vertebral Osteomyelitis|discitis-osteomyelitis]] centered at L4–L5 with a ventral epidural abscess at L4 and epidural phlegmon from L4 to S2.

Rabih frames the crux as the [[Pyogenic versus Granulomatous Osteomyelitis|pyogenic-versus-granulomatous]] branch point. Pyogenic disease (staph, gram-negatives) is much more common and stays front and center, but the gap narrows here for two reasons — the *absence of bacteremia* (pyogenic hematogenous osteomyelitis usually seeds the blood) and the *pulmonary focus* (where granulomatous disease begins). Within granulomatous causes he handicaps the field: TB favors the thoracic over lumbar spine (lowering it), which nudges [[Brucellosis|Brucella]] up; the endemic mycoses are all in play, with [[Coccidioidomycosis|Coccidioides]] supported by the Southwest travel and lung/bone involvement, [[Blastomycosis|Blastomyces]] less likely without exposure, [[Paracoccidioidomycosis|Paracoccidioides]] fair game from Guatemala, and [[Histoplasmosis|Histoplasma]] less likely to cause osteomyelitis; he even flags [[Mycobacterium bovis]] (unpasteurized dairy, higher extrapulmonary dissemination). He asks for culture (lung or bone) plus a Brucella and endemic-mycosis serologic panel.

The course turns critical: the patient develops respiratory failure, [[ARDS]], and intubation. AFB smears, galactomannan, thin/thick malaria smears, blasto/histo/cocci, psittacosis, Francisella, Paracoccidioides, Coxiella, and Brucella all return negative; a dengue IgM is 3.32. Reza takes the negative serologies with a grain of salt — they require both time and an intact immune system, and this asplenic, steroid-treated host may not mount them — and pushes for tissue: culture and histopathology. Rabih, struck that most granulomatous infections are indolent yet this patient is fulminant, floats [[Melioidosis|melioidosis]] (Burkholderia pseudomallei — granulomatous but pseudomonas-like, recently reported in Central America) as a cause that could explain the severity.

A distal iliac (bone) biopsy shows chronic inflammation and *no granulomas*. Rabih reads this as genuinely useful: the absence of neutrophils — present in over 90% of pyogenic osteomyelitis and, unlike granulomas, easy to find — argues against pyogenic disease and toward granulomatous, even as the biopsy is nominally non-diagnostic. He begins to widen the aperture to include aggressive malignancy (lymphoma, aggressive lung cancer) but stays anchored in the granulomatous-infection hypothesis given the high fevers and epidemiology.

The penultimate aliquot adds two clues. The patient develops a **serpiginous** lower-extremity rash with annular erythematous thin plaques (skin pathology: perivascular dermatitis with neutrophils), and re-review of the earlier lung-nodule wedge resection — obtained when he was coughing — reveals **necrotizing granulomas with microabscesses and organizing pneumonia**. Reza builds the problem representation aloud — a Central-American man with necrotizing pulmonary granulomas and disease in spine, liver, skin, and lung — anchors in granulomas, walks through the infection/autoimmune/cancer buckets (disfavoring lymphoma and sarcoidosis), and lands on a fungal cause, betting on disseminated [[Sporotrichosis|sporotrichosis]] because of the snake-like serpiginous rash and the aggressive course in an asplenic host. Rabih agrees Sporothrix should be the top fungus, adds the [[Lupus Vulgaris|lupus vulgaris]] (cutaneous TB) association with serpiginous rash, and honestly concedes he does not know.

The reveal: cultures remained negative, and a [[Karius Testing|Karius plasma cell-free DNA]] liquid biopsy returned **[[Nocardiosis|Nocardia]] (abscessus)**. Reza calls it "TB's cousin" and both hosts reflect: Rabih under-prioritized Nocardia despite it being on the granulomatous list, and had remembered it as indolent rather than ICU-level severe; Reza had not known Nocardia could be granulomatous. Lawrence closes with the teaching: Nocardia is notoriously hard to diagnose (frequently culture-negative, requires special selective media and micro-lab communication, up to two weeks to grow), is favored by chronic prednisone and gardening exposure, and was ultimately treated with triple therapy — TMP-SMX, amikacin, and meropenem — for roughly a year, after which the patient recovered and left the ICU.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Anatomical Approach|Anatomical reasoning]] for the febrile back.** Rabih enumerates the lower-back compartments — psoas, vertebrae, epidural space/dura — and uses the rigors to promote a deep or endovascular infection, letting structure and the systemic signature (not the base rate of non-specific back pain) drive the workup.
- **[[Framing Effect|Framing]] without [[Anchoring Bias|anchoring]] on geography.** Reza consciously constructs a Central-American frame (histo, paracocci) and then explicitly refuses to let it anchor him, treating the "malaria" and "slipped disc" diagnoses from Guatemala as soft priors rather than settled facts — a model of holding a frame loosely.
- **[[Base Rate|Base rate]] disciplined by tempo and host.** Pyogenic osteomyelitis is the common default, but a three-to-four-week course in a doubly immunosuppressed host lowers the pyogenic prior and raises atypical/granulomatous disease; the base rate informs but does not dictate.
- **[[Pyogenic versus Granulomatous Osteomyelitis|The pyogenic-versus-granulomatous branch point]] as the organizing question.** Two discrete data points — sterile blood cultures and a bone biopsy without neutrophils — are each used to slide probability from pyogenic toward granulomatous, an explicit demonstration that a "non-diagnostic" biopsy still carries diagnostic weight when read for what is *absent*.
- **[[Treatment as a Diagnostic Test|Treatment and imaging as soft tests]].** Reza reasons that response to empiric antibiotics/antimalarials can adjust post-test probability and that a poorly timed gadolinium MRI can miss an epidural abscess — refusing to let prior therapy or imaging falsely exclude an atypical infection.
- **[[Signal versus Noise|Organ-by-organ tracking]].** Reza's habit of tracking which organs carry pathology (lung, spine, liver, skin) — "signal versus noise" — is what lets the necrotizing pulmonary granulomas and the spinal lesion snap together into a single multi-system granulomatous story.
- **[[Illness Script Flexibility|Illness-script rigidity]] as the miss.** Both hosts had Nocardia scripted as indolent and Reza did not have it scripted as granulomatous at all, so a textbook host (chronic prednisone, gardener) with the right histology was under-weighted; the corrective is to widen the script's severity and histology rather than to treat the miss as failure.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Cognitive humility]] as the closing theme.** Rabih explicitly reframes getting the answer wrong as fuel for improvement — practicing on cases so the reflex is right when a real patient is in front of you.

## Teaching Discussion

Rigors are a high-value symptom: they point to systemic, usually endovascular or deep, infection, and when paired with low back pain they should prompt an anatomical search of the psoas, vertebrae, and epidural space rather than reassurance about non-specific back pain. The red flags that justify escalating a back complaint are advanced neurologic compromise and a cancer history, but a poorly characterized febrile systemic illness with prominent back pain is itself a reason to image the spine.

Duration and host status reshape the infectious differential more than any single exposure. A multi-week course is too indolent for ordinary pyogenic bacteria and pushes toward atypical, granulomatous, and fungal disease, while asplenia and chronic corticosteroids each open specific doors — encapsulated and intraerythrocytic organisms for the former, opportunistic fungi and Nocardia for the latter. Exposures (gardening, birds, endemic-fungus geography) enrich the list but should be held as frames to activate rather than anchors to close on.

The pyogenic-versus-granulomatous distinction is the organizing question in infectious osteomyelitis. Pyogenic disease is more common and typically bacteremic, so negative blood cultures narrow the gap; granulomatous disease usually has a pulmonary focus, so a lung lesion widens it further. A bone biopsy is informative even when it fails to show an organism: because neutrophils appear in over 90% of pyogenic osteomyelitis and are far easier to find than granulomas, their absence is evidence against pyogenic disease.

Negative serologies and cultures must be interpreted against the host and the organism. Antibody tests require time and an intact immune system to become positive, and fastidious organisms — Nocardia above all — need selective media, direct communication with the microbiology lab, and up to two weeks of incubation, so a negative conventional workup in an immunosuppressed patient should prompt tissue sampling and, increasingly, plasma microbial cell-free DNA testing rather than reassurance. Nocardia specifically can be granulomatous and can present fulminantly with ARDS, and it is favored by chronic steroid use and soil/gardening exposure — an illness script worth widening beyond its indolent reputation.
