## Pearls

- **Acute confusion in an older adult is best opened with the [[MIST Mnemonic|MIST]] schema — Metabolic, Infectious, Structural, Toxins — with a chem-7 "fishbone" of metabolic culprits and a residual "[[MIST Mnemonic|MIST]]-negative" bucket.** The fishbone prompts sodium, uremia, glucose, calcium, phosphate, ammonia, hypoxia, and hypercarbia; when the mnemonic comes up empty, the residual bucket holds [[Seizure|seizure/postictal state]], [[Catatonia|catatonia]], [[Stroke|strategic stroke]] (thalamus, brainstem), and neurodegeneration. Hallucinations are a modifier worth characterizing — auditory versus visual can localize.
- **[[Illness Tempo|Tempo]] is the master variable in confusion, and it is only interpretable against the syndrome.** Seconds-to-minutes narrows to a vascular catastrophe, hypoglycemia, or seizure; hours-to-days is a broad acute-[[Delirium|delirium]] differential; weeks-to-months paints [[Neurodegenerative Disease|neurodegeneration]]. The same clock reads "chronic" for one complaint and "subacute" for another.
- **Antibiotics cause encephalopathy, and the beta-lactams — [[Cefepime Neurotoxicity|cefepime]] classically, but also [[Ertapenem Neurotoxicity|ertapenem]] — are prime offenders.** Cefepime neurotoxicity often brings myoclonus and a characteristic EEG (imaging usually normal); ertapenem neurotoxicity typically presents with seizures or encephalopathy and is most expected in end-stage renal disease, yet is described with **normal renal function**. Discontinuation produces dramatic improvement.
- **Reason by analogy from a known drug to an unfamiliar one, then verify — do not let the analogy stand as proof.** Reza extended what he knew about cefepime to ertapenem to generate the hypothesis, then ran a live search that returned a matched case report; the deliberate pairing of an analogic leap with a confirmatory check is the pearl, not the leap alone.
- **Do not let a patient's age truncate the differential.** Assuming an elder is not exposed to toxins, toxidromes, or STIs is a [[Representativeness Heuristic|representativeness]] trap that misses HIV, syphilis, and ingestions — everyone follows similar behavioral patterns, differing in frequency, not in kind.
- **Improvement after removing a drug (dechallenge) is powerful but confoundable, so read it as a strong clue rather than proof.** [[Treatment as a Diagnostic Test|Diagnosis by response]] must reckon with alternatives — a home toxin no longer accessible in hospital, hydration, or regression to baseline — which is why Reza still leads with the higher-morbidity metabolic, seizure, and structural hypotheses while testing the antibiotic hypothesis by withdrawal.

## Problem Representation

A previously independent 73–74-year-old man with hypertension and hyperlipidemia (on lisinopril and atorvastatin), one month out from non-operatively managed perforated appendicitis and near completion of a three-week PICC course of IV ertapenem (day ~20), presents with three to four days of confusion and hallucinations. He is afebrile and hemodynamically normal with a normal point-of-care glucose, no focal neurologic deficit, and an unremarkable first-pass metabolic and infectious workup (normal sodium, creatinine, glucose, calcium, and liver enzymes; bland urinalysis; normal chest x-ray; negative COVID). Holding all medications, hydrating, and instituting delirium precautions yields complete normalization within about 24 hours, and reflection identifies ertapenem-induced encephalopathy — a beta-lactam antibiotic-associated encephalopathy occurring here despite normal renal function.

## Case Narrative

After the signature palindrome-and-Boxing-Day banter (recorded the day after Christmas, with a newborn nephew and a puppy audible in the background), Rabih presents the case in staged fragments: a 74-year-old man brought in by his wife for three to four days of [[Delirium|confusion]] and [[Hallucinations|hallucinations]].

Reza notes that this opening already delivers the three components of a problem representation — age, tempo, and syndrome — even before any background data. Age is crucial: as we age we lose reserve across every organ, so this is by definition a vulnerable patient, and the unknown baseline is critical because it sets both how far off he is and how small an insult (constipation, a urinary tract infection) could tip him into [[Delirium|delirium]]. [[Illness Tempo|Tempo]] cannot be read without the syndrome: for confusion, seconds-to-minutes implies a vascular catastrophe, hypoglycemia, or a [[Seizure|seizure]]; a month of slow decline implies [[Neurodegenerative Disease|neurodegeneration]]; what Rabih describes — days — is acute, with a broad differential. Reza organizes that differential with the [[MIST Mnemonic|MIST]] mnemonic he attributes to Gurpreet Dhaliwal (Metabolic, Infectious, Structural, Toxins), enriched by the chem-7 "fishbone" (sodium, uremia, glucose, calcium, phosphate, ammonia, hypoxia, hypercarbia), and flags that infections may be intracranial or extracranial, that elders are prone to falls and thus to an easily missed [[Subdural Hematoma|subdural hematoma]], and that toxins include both intoxication and withdrawal (e.g., benzodiazepine withdrawal). He asks Rabih to characterize the hallucinations — auditory versus visual — since that may localize.

Rabih walks the triage course: normal vitals, a normal point-of-care glucose, and a non-focal neurologic exam. Basic labs return clean — normal sodium, creatinine, glucose, calcium, and liver enzymes, a urinalysis without pyuria, a normal chest x-ray, and a negative rapid COVID swab.

Reza is reassured that a quickly reversible cause (hypoglycemia) was excluded and that there is no obvious metabolic or infectious source, but he adds a caution: the medication list must be reviewed, because an agent like a beta-blocker could blunt a compensatory [[Sinus Tachycardia|tachycardia]] and mask an underlying insult. The non-focal exam argues against a large stroke and, since post-ictal confusion can occur without focal deficits, keeps [[Seizure|seizure]] alive; he laments that EEGs are underused. He orders a head CT to interrogate the structural causes and articulates that he is drifting toward the "[[MIST Mnemonic|MIST]]-negative" altered-mental-status bucket — seizure, [[Catatonia|catatonia]], a strategic [[Stroke|stroke]] (thalamus or brainstem, which he dislikes given the tempo), and neurodegeneration (too rapid here) — while resolving not to miss something on the mnemonic, such as an unchecked ammonia, urinary retention, or constipation.

Rabih then opens the chart and supplies the background that reframes everything. The patient's baseline is normal — fully independent in ADLs and IADLs, "a normal walking, talking human being." He carries only hypertension and hyperlipidemia (lisinopril, atorvastatin). One month earlier he had been diagnosed with **perforated [[Appendicitis|appendicitis]]**; because of how that admission unfolded he was not a surgical candidate, so he received a PICC line and three weeks of IV **[[Ertapenem Neurotoxicity|ertapenem]]**, and he was at roughly day 20, nearly finished.

Reza registers that this markedly abnormal state sits against a normal baseline, and he first chases the appendicitis itself: he recalls the recent NEJM trial (the CODA trial) showing no short-term mortality difference between antibiotic and surgical management of appendicitis — with more antibiotic-managed patients ultimately returning for appendectomy — and, noting that appendicitis usually results simply from a [[Fecalith|fecalith]]-driven obstruction, wonders whether an underlying pathology in a 70-something warrants re-reviewing the prior CT. He explicitly parks his uncertainty about the epidemiology of appendicitis at this age as an area of ignorance, and concludes the patient does not look bacteremic.

Rabih delivers the resolving move: the team held all medications in case of a drug contribution, hydrated the patient, and instituted delirium precautions — and by the next morning he was much better, corroborated by his wife, and fully normal within 24 hours. With that and some reading, a diagnosis was made.

Reza reasons that a therapeutic change (stopping something) preceded resolution, which could be coincidence or causation, and that the single agent stopped that stands out is the IV ertapenem. Working by **analogy** from the antibiotic he does know — [[Cefepime Neurotoxicity|cefepime]], which causes neurotoxicity often with myoclonus and a characteristic EEG despite normal imaging — he raises [[Antibiotic-Associated Encephalopathy|antibiotic-associated encephalopathy]] and supposes the same can occur with ertapenem, while explicitly disclaiming that he does not know every side effect of every drug. He keeps a home [[Toxidrome|toxin]]/urine tox screen on the table (a toxin no longer accessible in hospital could equally explain improvement) and warns against dismissing toxidromes or STIs in the elderly on the basis of age. Invited by Rabih to demonstrate real-time learning, Reza runs a live search and reads aloud the top hit — a case report titled *ertapenem-induced encephalopathy in a patient with normal renal function*, describing altered mental status, hallucinations, and dystonic symptoms that improved dramatically on discontinuation — noting it "gave me goosebumps" for how closely it matched the patient. Rabih confirms that is exactly the diagnosis, and adds that he had plugged the same knowledge gap afterward with Aaron Berkowitz's review of antibiotic-associated encephalopathy, which catalogues the beta-lactams (cefepime, ertapenem), the intracellular agents (fluoroquinolones, macrolides), and metronidazole, each with characteristic EEG or imaging signatures.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Analogical Reasoning|Analogical reasoning]], paired with verification, is the engine of the case.** Reza reaches an unfamiliar diagnosis (ertapenem neurotoxicity) by mapping it onto a familiar class member ([[Cefepime Neurotoxicity|cefepime]]) — same drug class, same expected phenotype — then refuses to let the analogy stand alone, running a real-time search that returns a matched case report. The teaching value is the disciplined loop: analogy to generate, literature to confirm.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Metacognitive humility]] as a named move.** Reza repeatedly marks the edges of his knowledge — "I don't know every side effect of every medication," and his admitted ignorance of appendicitis epidemiology — and converts those admissions into action (a search, a plan to read) rather than papering over them. Rabih models the same by later reading the Berkowitz review to plug the gap.
- **[[Representativeness Heuristic|Representativeness]] resisted deliberately.** Reza flags the trap of assuming an older adult is not exposed to toxins or STIs; treating age as a filter that removes whole categories would miss HIV, syphilis, and ingestions. He keeps a urine tox and a home-exposure hypothesis explicitly alive.
- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|Schema over recall]] for a high-entropy complaint.** Rather than free-associate on "confusion," Reza deploys Gurpreet Dhaliwal's [[MIST Mnemonic|MIST]] mnemonic plus the chem-7 fishbone and the "MIST-negative" residual bucket, converting an overwhelming differential into a structured, bedside-consultable checklist.
- **[[Treatment as a Diagnostic Test|Diagnosis by dechallenge]], with its confounders acknowledged.** Improvement followed removal of the drug, but Reza notes that hydration, delirium precautions, and loss of access to a home toxin are competing explanations for the same trajectory — so he treats the dechallenge as strong corroboration, not proof, and continues to lead with the higher-morbidity hypotheses.
- **[[Cognitive Autopsy|Cognitive autopsy]] on his own path.** In reflection Reza performs a cognitive autopsy, recognizing that he was "one step short" and that the single datum that clinched confidence was the dramatic improvement on drug withdrawal — an explicit accounting of which piece of evidence did the diagnostic work.

## Teaching Discussion

- For acute confusion in an older adult, open with the [[MIST Mnemonic|MIST]] schema (Metabolic, Infectious, Structural, Toxins), run the chem-7 fishbone of metabolic culprits, and when nothing fits, move deliberately into the "MIST-negative" bucket of seizure, catatonia, strategic stroke, and neurodegeneration rather than declaring the workup finished.
- Read tempo only against the syndrome: for confusion, seconds-to-minutes implies vascular, hypoglycemic, or seizure causes; hours-to-days is a broad acute-delirium differential; weeks-to-months implies neurodegeneration — and the same interval means different things for different complaints.
- Always reconcile the medication list before accepting "normal vitals," because agents such as beta-blockers can blunt the compensatory tachycardia a septic or otherwise stressed patient would mount.
- Keep the beta-lactams on the confusion differential: cefepime neurotoxicity classically brings myoclonus with a characteristic EEG, ertapenem neurotoxicity brings seizures or encephalopathy, and although both are most expected with renal impairment, ertapenem encephalopathy is reported with normal renal function.
- Use analogical reasoning to reach unfamiliar diagnoses — extend a known class member (cefepime) to a less-familiar one (ertapenem) — but discharge the uncertainty with a confirmatory literature check rather than trusting the analogy.
- Refuse to let age narrow the differential: elders are still exposed to toxins, toxidromes, and STIs, so keep a urine tox and infectious screen in play instead of filtering by demographic expectation.
- Treat improvement after stopping a drug as a strong but confoundable clue; a diagnosis by dechallenge should still account for hydration, supportive care, and removal from home exposures, and the higher-morbidity hypotheses (metabolic, seizure, structural) should be led with and excluded in parallel.
- Model naming the limits of your own knowledge and acting on them — a real-time search or a follow-up read (e.g., Berkowitz's antibiotic-associated encephalopathy review) turns an admitted gap into durable learning.
