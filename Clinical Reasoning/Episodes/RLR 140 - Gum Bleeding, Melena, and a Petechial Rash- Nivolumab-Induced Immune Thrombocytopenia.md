## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 45-year-old man on nivolumab for anal squamous cell carcinoma presents with one day of gum bleeding, melena, and a new non-blanching petechial rash — a textbook multi-site mucocutaneous bleeding picture. His platelet count comes back at 1, with a hemoglobin of 6 and completely normal coagulation studies. Robbie insists on carefully separating the anemia from the thrombocytopenia before assuming one simply causes the other, working the anemia up as its own problem before concluding it's a distractor — a straightforward consequence of acute blood loss layered on top of an isolated, severe, destructive thrombocytopenia. Using the "center of gravity" concept, he argues that isolated deep thrombocytopenia is immune-mediated in the overwhelming majority of cases, and zeroes in on the patient's PD-1 inhibitor as the specific trigger — correctly landing on nivolumab-induced immune thrombocytopenia, which responds to steroids once the drug is held.

## Pearls

- Bleeding confined to one site is usually local; bleeding appearing in multiple, unrelated sites (gums, stool, skin) should immediately raise concern for a systemic bleeding disorder rather than several coincidental local problems — though swallowed oral blood can mimic a GI source and should be considered before concluding the GI tract is truly involved.
- Mucocutaneous bleeding (gums, petechiae, easy bruising) points toward a platelet problem; deeper bleeding (large hematomas, hemarthroses, retroperitoneal bleeds) points toward a coagulation factor problem — bleeding depth and location is a fast, useful triage heuristic for which system to investigate first.
- When a patient has both thrombocytopenia and anemia, resist the reflexive assumption that the anemia is simply blood-loss-related to the low platelets — work up the anemia as its own independent problem (acute versus chronic, evidence of hemolysis, iron/B12 status) before folding it into a single explanation; if replacing all visibly lost blood wouldn't normalize the hemoglobin, a second process is in play.
- Whenever severe thrombocytopenia is identified, immediately screen for the classic life-threatening or urgent causes: recent heparin exposure (HIT), schistocytes on peripheral smear (TTP or another microangiopathic process), and blasts on smear (acute leukemia) — these must be actively excluded, not assumed absent.
- A platelet count under 10 is a strong pointer toward ITP, but only when the thrombocytopenia is truly isolated — this pearl fails in the presence of bone marrow suppression (e.g., chemotherapy) or a hemolytic process, since conditions like TTP can also produce single-digit platelet counts.
- The immature platelet fraction (IPF) is the platelet analogue of the reticulocyte count: a high IPF suggests the marrow is appropriately compensating for peripheral platelet destruction by releasing immature, but highly hemostatically "sticky," platelets — which is why patients with very low platelet counts and a high IPF often don't spontaneously bleed as severely as the number alone would suggest. IPF is most informative at the extremes (very high or very low); mid-range values are less discriminating.
- The "center of gravity" concept: characterize the full landscape of a patient's problem before estimating probabilities. A patient whose only abnormality is isolated, severe thrombocytopenia has a very high probability of primary (idiopathic) immune thrombocytopenia; broadening the "center of gravity" to include other findings (infection, autoimmune features, a new drug, cancer) should proportionally raise concern for a secondary cause.
- Roughly 80% of isolated severe thrombocytopenia is primary ITP with no identifiable secondary cause; the remaining 20% is secondary — driven by infections (EBV), autoimmune disease (lupus), immunodeficiency (CVID), drugs, pregnancy, or cancer (including CLL) — work-up for a secondary cause should start by asking what else is going on with the patient, not by further characterizing the platelet count itself.
- Bone marrow diseases rarely cause truly isolated cytopenias; when a marrow-based process does mimic isolated thrombocytopenia, the two recognized mimickers are amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia (a platelet-lineage analogue of pure red cell aplasia) and select MDS subtypes that selectively suppress platelet production — both are rare and typically only invoked once a presumed ITP case proves refractory to standard treatment.
- Anal squamous cell carcinoma is rare (roughly 1,000 US cases per year) and carries an approximately 1-in-5 association with HIV — any patient with this cancer diagnosis should prompt consideration of HIV status, given the well-documented overlap between HIV and ITP.
- Immune checkpoint inhibitors (like PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies) are a well-recognized cause of immune-related adverse events, including drug-induced ITP through the same antibody-mediated platelet destruction mechanism as primary ITP — a new cytopenia in a patient on immunotherapy should prompt early suspicion of a drug-related immune adverse event.
- ITP treatment (steroids, rituximab, plasma exchange) works by reducing antibody production or antibody-platelet binding, thereby decreasing splenic destruction of tagged platelets — platelet transfusion alone is frequently ineffective in immune-mediated thrombocytopenia because transfused platelets are destroyed by the same ongoing antibody process.
- Managing immune-related adverse events from checkpoint inhibitors requires proportionality: minor toxicity may not require intervention, but a life-threatening bleed warrants aggressive immunosuppression — and clinicians should be cautious about how aggressively and how long they suppress the immune system in response, since excessive or prolonged immunosuppression around these therapies has been associated with worse oncologic outcomes.

## Problem Representation

A 45-year-old man on nivolumab for anal squamous cell carcinoma develops acute mucocutaneous bleeding (gum bleeding, melena, non-blanching petechial rash) with a platelet count of 1 and a hemoglobin of 6, ultimately diagnosed as immune checkpoint inhibitor-induced immune thrombocytopenia responsive to steroids and drug discontinuation.

## Case Narrative

After marking the show's 140th RLR episode with some reflective banter about their combined recording history, Reza introduced what he promised would be a shorter but important case, centered specifically on the diagnostic value of color and bleeding pattern. A 45-year-old man presented with one day of gum bleeding — unprovoked, not related to brushing — followed by a bowel movement notable for completely black stool, and a new rash developing on his lower extremities.

Robbie's rapid-fire initial framing: given bleeding from the gums and rectum, it was reasonable to predict the leg rash would also turn out to be non-palpable petechiae rather than a separate dermatologic process. He laid out a general principle — bleeding from one specific site is usually a local problem, but systemic bleeding disorders, while they also start somewhere local, don't stay local. Common local causes of gum bleeding (trauma, structural dental disease) become much less likely once bleeding appears in multiple unrelated sites simultaneously. He flagged one caveat: swallowed oral blood can masquerade as a GI bleeding source, so the rectal finding needed to be interpreted with that possibility in mind — but the skin finding removed any doubt that this was systemic. Given the pattern of mucocutaneous bleeding specifically, he judged a platelet disorder highly likely, since platelet disorders characteristically cause exactly this kind of superficial, multi-site bleeding. His immediate questions: any history of a bleeding diathesis, any anticoagulant use.

Reza confirmed no personal bleeding history and no anticoagulation. Past medical history included hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and — notably — anal squamous cell carcinoma, currently being treated with nivolumab.

On exam, the rash was confirmed non-blanching, non-palpable, and petechial, exactly as predicted; a rectal exam was deferred, but the oral cavity showed diffuse bright red petechiae and drying blood at the gumline. The CBC returned a platelet count of 1, a hemoglobin of 6, and a normal white blood cell count. Chemistry showed an elevated BUN with an otherwise normal creatinine and electrolytes, normal liver chemistries, and normal PT and PTT.

Robbie called this an urgent, high-risk situation — a platelet count that low carries real risk of intracranial hemorrhage and other life-threatening bleeding. He immediately split the abnormalities into two problems, thrombocytopenia and anemia, and insisted on not automatically attributing the anemia to blood loss from the low platelets without checking. His test: if you transfused back every drop of blood this patient had visibly lost, would the hemoglobin normalize? If yes, the anemia is fully explained by bleeding and the problem can be treated as isolated thrombocytopenia; if not, a second process is contributing and the picture is a genuine bicytopenia, which changes the differential meaningfully. He outlined the immediate priorities for any severe thrombocytopenia: characterize the CBC carefully (isolated cytopenia versus bi/pancytopenia), then rule out classic urgent causes — recent heparin exposure (for HIT), schistocytes on peripheral smear (for TTP or another microangiopathic process), and blasts on smear (for acute leukemia).

Reza affirmed the approach and asked whether the severity of the thrombocytopenia itself (platelet count under 10) was informative. Robbie confirmed it can be — but only when the thrombocytopenia is truly isolated. He described having been burned by this exact pearl twice: once when a case he assumed was ITP based purely on depth of thrombocytopenia turned out to be malaria with compensated hemolysis, and once when it turned out to be TTP, which can also produce single-digit platelet counts. The rule, he said, isn't ready to "take to the bank" without confirming isolation from other cytopenias first.

Asked how to actually determine isolated versus combined cytopenia in practice, Robbie described deliberately setting aside knowledge of the platelet count and working up the anemia completely independently — acute versus chronic, evidence of hemolysis, iron and B12 status — using their standard anemia framework, and treating anything found beyond simple bleeding as a red flag requiring caution.

Reza supplied the anemia workup results: hemolysis labs all negative, no schistocytes or blasts on smear, no recent heparin exposure, a prior baseline hemoglobin around 10–11, mild tachycardia on vitals, a robust reticulocytosis, giant platelets on smear, and a markedly elevated immature platelet fraction (IPF).

Robbie recommended folding the anemia question in as settled — reticulocytosis without hemolysis strongly favors acute blood loss as the sole driver — and simplifying the problem representation to isolated thrombocytopenia causing a secondary acute blood loss anemia. He asked Reza to explain the IPF finding, which Reza described as the platelet-lineage analogue of the reticulocyte count: in peripheral platelet destruction, the marrow compensates by releasing immature platelets, and — importantly — these immature platelets are unusually "sticky" and hemostatically active, which is why even patients with extremely low platelet counts (he shared an anecdote about a patient with a platelet count of 9 who was inadvertently given aspirin at Hopkins, and a hematologist reassuring him that large immature platelets made spontaneous bleeding unlikely in that setting) often don't bleed as catastrophically as the raw number would predict. He cited a paper from a former Hopkins colleague, Brian Garibaldi, showing IPF is most informative at the extremes — a very high value strongly supports peripheral destruction with appropriate marrow compensation, while mid-range values carry little diagnostic weight.

With the anemia resolved as a distractor and the problem simplified to "X is causing isolated thrombocytopenia," Robbie introduced the "center of gravity" concept: characterizing the full landscape of everything going on with a patient, not just the platelet count in isolation. For a patient whose only abnormality is isolated severe thrombocytopenia, the probability of primary immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) approaches certainty in aggregate terms — roughly 80% of all such presentations turn out to be idiopathic/primary, with the remaining 20% secondary to a defined cause: infections like EBV, autoimmune disease like lupus, immunodeficiencies like CVID, drugs, pregnancy, or cancers like CLL. The practical workup, he explained, starts by asking what else is going on with the patient — systemic symptoms, exposures, medications — rather than further interrogating the platelet count itself. He also flagged that most ITP responds to standard treatment, and failure to respond should prompt both escalation of therapy and a search for rarer marrow-based mimickers — specifically amegakaryocytic thrombocytopenia (a platelet-lineage cousin of pure red cell aplasia) and select MDS subtypes that selectively suppress platelet production — entities generally only invoked once presumed ITP proves treatment-refractory.

Turning back to this specific patient's "center of gravity," Robbie flagged two details from the history as highly relevant: the anal squamous cell carcinoma diagnosis, a rare cancer (roughly 1,000 US cases annually) with an approximately 20% association with HIV — prompting him to want HIV status confirmed, though he assumed it had already been checked — and, more directly, the nivolumab. Immune checkpoint inhibitors are well known to cause immune-related adverse events through unchecked immune activation, including antibody-mediated platelet destruction. His working hypothesis, ready to bring to a hematology consult: nivolumab-induced immune thrombocytopenia.

Reza confirmed this was indeed the diagnosis, then posed a hypothetical: absent the HIV-associated cancer and the drug history, how would Robbie approach working up and managing ITP more generally? Robbie reiterated the center-of-gravity logic — in a patient with a truly localized problem (isolated thrombocytopenia and nothing else), primary ITP is the default, with the standard 80/20 primary-versus-secondary split guiding how hard to search for a secondary cause.

Clinically, the patient received platelet transfusion, which — as expected in immune-mediated destruction — didn't produce the expected response, since transfused platelets are subject to the same ongoing antibody-mediated destruction. Reza used this to make a broader point about terminology, preferring "immune thrombocytopenia" (primary or secondary) over "idiopathic," since all cases share the same fundamental mechanism: antibodies binding platelets and tagging them for splenic destruction. Steroids, rituximab, and plasma exchange all work by reducing antibody production or antibody-platelet binding. He described recently attending a talk on immune-related adverse event management, noting that formal guidelines exist and vary by which organ is affected and how severe the toxicity is — sometimes calling for simple steroids, sometimes for holding the causative medication, sometimes both — and that excessive or overly prolonged immunosuppression around these adverse events has been associated with worse outcomes, referencing an earlier RLR mentor's (Zalvin's) observation that immune-related adverse events can cause "any-itis" affecting virtually any organ system, each requiring its own tailored management approach. This patient responded well to prednisone with the nivolumab held.

Closing the case, Robbie offered his take-home message: focal bleeding is a focal problem, but bleeding in multiple places means the answer lives in the blood itself, and the location of bleeding (closer to the surface for platelet problems, deeper for coagulation factor problems) offers a useful first clue. His central piece of advice: once a platelet problem is identified, resist the urge to feel like the case is solved — study the CBC carefully, correctly characterize isolated versus combined cytopenias, and remember that isolated thrombocytopenia is overwhelmingly immune-mediated, with a real 20% chance of a meaningful secondary cause that changes management. His final point, specific to this case: powerful modern biologics and immunotherapies shouldn't be counteracted lightly — the response to an adverse event should be proportional to its severity, respecting both the harm being caused and the value of the underlying life-saving therapy.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Effect on Leading Hypothesis |
|---|---|
| Gum bleeding, melena, and a new non-blanching petechial rash together | Raises probability of a systemic bleeding disorder over a local process; raises probability of a platelet-mediated (mucocutaneous) bleeding pattern specifically |
| Platelet count of 1, normal PT/PTT | Confirms an isolated severe thrombocytopenia as the primary hemostatic abnormality |
| Hemolysis labs negative, no schistocytes/blasts, reticulocytosis present, prior Hgb 10–11 | Lowers probability of a combined hemolytic/marrow process; supports acute blood loss as the sole explanation for the anemia |
| No recent heparin exposure | Lowers probability of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia |
| Giant platelets, markedly elevated IPF | Raises probability of peripheral platelet destruction with an appropriately compensating marrow (supports immune thrombocytopenia over a marrow production problem) |
| No other systemic findings beyond isolated thrombocytopenia (normal WBC, no lymphadenopathy noted) | Raises probability of primary/immune thrombocytopenia per the "center of gravity" framework |
| History of anal squamous cell carcinoma | Raises probability of an HIV-ITP overlap as a secondary contributor (pending HIV status) |
| Active nivolumab (PD-1 inhibitor) therapy | Strongly raises probability of checkpoint-inhibitor-induced immune thrombocytopenia as the specific secondary cause |
| Poor response to platelet transfusion, good response to steroids + holding nivolumab | Confirms an antibody-mediated (immune) destructive mechanism, consistent with drug-induced ITP |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The case models active resistance to a subtle anchoring trap: once thrombocytopenia is found, it's tempting to attribute all downstream findings (including the anemia) to it automatically. Robbie's discipline of "unbiasing" himself from the platelet count while working up the anemia separately is a deliberate debiasing technique — treating two co-occurring abnormalities as independent hypotheses until proven linked, rather than assuming a single unifying mechanism by default. The "center of gravity" concept itself functions as an explicit anti-anchoring tool: rather than probability-estimating from the lab value alone, it forces a full inventory of the patient's context (history, exposures, medications) before settling on primary versus secondary disease.

## Teaching Discussion

The episode's throughline is a bleeding-pattern-to-mechanism heuristic (site and depth of bleeding pointing toward platelet versus coagulation factor pathology) paired with a disciplined habit of never accepting a single explanation for multiple abnormal findings without actively checking whether it truly accounts for all of them. The "center of gravity" framework is a broadly reusable tool for calibrating pretest probability of primary versus secondary disease in any presentation that could plausibly be either idiopathic or driven by an identifiable cause. Finally, the case is a clean, contemporary example of recognizing an immune-related adverse event from cancer immunotherapy — a fast-growing and easy-to-miss category of drug reaction that clinicians across specialties will increasingly need to recognize as checkpoint inhibitor use expands.
