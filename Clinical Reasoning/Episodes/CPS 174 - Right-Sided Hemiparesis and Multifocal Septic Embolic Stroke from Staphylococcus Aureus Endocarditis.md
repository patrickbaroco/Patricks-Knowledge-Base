## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 29-year-old man presents with 15 days of fever and headache, worsening confusion, and one day of sudden right-sided hemiparesis — two weeks after a dental procedure. **Aaron Berkowitz** and discussants trace the corticospinal tract to localize the hemiparesis, then use the tempo of the illness (too slow for untreated bacterial meningitis or HSV encephalitis) to reframe the fever and headache as **[[Infective Endocarditis and Septic Embolic CNS Disease|endocarditis]]**-driven rather than primary-CNS in origin. A cardiac murmur and an exam missing an expected finding — no aphasia despite right hemiparesis, facial palsy, and homonymous hemianopia — relocalizes the lesion from cortical to **deep (thalamocapsular)**, plus a separate left cerebellar lesion. MRI confirms multifocal, bilateral, supra- and infratentorial infarcts; echocardiography reveals a mitral valve vegetation; blood cultures grow *Staphylococcus aureus*. The patient undergoes valve replacement and antibiotics, improves, then relapses at 20 days with new brain abscesses at the stroke sites — septic emboli seeding despite treatment.

## Pearls

- **Hemiparesis (weakness) is not hemiplegia (complete paralysis) — the distinction matters.** Tracing the corticospinal tract (precentral gyrus → corona radiata → internal capsule → ventral brainstem → cervicomedullary decussation → lateral corticospinal tract → anterior horn cell → root/plexus/nerve → neuromuscular junction → muscle) shows why weakness confined to one whole side of the body argues for a central lesion (contralateral brain or ipsilateral spinal cord) rather than a peripheral one — a peripheral process affecting every nerve, root, and muscle on exactly one side of the body would be an odd coincidence.
- **An absent expected finding can relocalize a case.** Right hemiparesis, right central facial palsy, and right homonymous hemianopia together look like a left hemispheric syndrome — but the *absence* of aphasia (a cortical, language-dominant-hemisphere sign) argues against a cortical/large-vessel lesion and points instead to a **deep (thalamocapsular/internal capsule)** lesion, where these pathways run densely packed together without adjacent cortex.
- **Know what's actually in the cavernous sinus before invoking it.** CN III, IV, VI, V1 (and, per some sources, V2), and the oculosympathetic fibers pass through or near it — the optic nerve (CN II) and the corticospinal tract do not (mnemonic: **OTOM CAT** — Ophthalmic and Maxillary V1/V2, Trochlear IV, Oculomotor III in the wall; Carotid Artery and Abducens VI floating free in the lumen) — so an isolated hemiparesis argues strongly against cavernous sinus pathology.
- **Check the tempo of a systemic illness against the natural history of the infections you're considering.** Untreated bacterial meningitis or HSV encephalitis would make a patient critically ill or dead well before two weeks; 15 days of fever and headache is too slow for either, and should prompt reframing the systemic illness as extra-CNS (e.g. endocarditis) with a later, acute neurologic complication superimposed — rather than assuming a single, slowly-evolving primary CNS infection.
- **Multifocal, bilateral, supra- and infratentorial infarcts on diffusion-weighted imaging point strongly to a cardioembolic (or, less commonly, hematologic) source rather than a vessel problem** — Berkowitz's three-bucket framework for ischemic stroke etiology: vessels, heart, or blood.
- **Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs are only about 5% sensitive for meningitis, though highly specific**, and the classic fever/neck-stiffness/altered-mental-status triad is present in well under half of confirmed cases — a negative meningeal exam should never be used to rule meningitis out, especially in patients who present early or have taken NSAIDs.

## Problem Representation

A 29-year-old man with a dental procedure two weeks prior develops 15 days of fever and headache, then progressive confusion and somnolence, culminating in sudden right-sided hemiparesis one day before presentation. Exam shows fever, an ill appearance, a 4/6 holosystolic murmur at the cardiac apex, disorientation to time, a right homonymous hemianopia, a right central facial palsy, dysarthria, 3/5 right-sided weakness with diminished tone and reflexes and a Babinski sign, diminished right-sided pain and temperature sensation, and — on the opposite side — left-sided dysmetria and dysdiadochokinesia, without aphasia or meningeal signs. MRI shows multifocal, bilateral infarcts (left occipital, bilateral cerebellum, left basal ganglia/internal capsule, bilateral frontal regions); echocardiography shows a sizable mitral valve vegetation with moderate regurgitation; blood cultures grow *Staphylococcus aureus*. The findings localize to a deep left hemispheric lesion plus a separate left cerebellar lesion, both from septic embolic infarction secondary to *S. aureus* infective endocarditis.

## Case Narrative

The recording opens with the same standing Clinical Problem Solvers introduction and Aaron Berkowitz's **[[Neurophobia|end-neurophobia]]** framing captured in full in [[CPS 146 - Right-Sided Weakness and Numbness and Medial Medullary Syndrome from a Vertebral Artery Dissection|CPS 146]].

**Chief complaint: right-sided hemiparesis.** Discussants Sherry Chao and Kaitlyn Thomas open with the localization instinct: weakness affecting one whole side of the body suggests a lesion in the contralateral brain or ipsilateral spinal cord rather than an isolated nerve or root. Berkowitz has them trace the **[[Localization × Time Course|corticospinal tract]]** explicitly — precentral gyrus, corona radiata, internal capsule, ventral brainstem, the cervicomedullary decussation, then the lateral corticospinal tract descending to synapse on anterior horn cells, which send lower motor neurons out through roots, plexuses, and nerves to the neuromuscular junction and muscle — and draws the terminology distinction between **hemiparesis** (weakness) and **hemiplegia** (complete paralysis), since the degree of weakness itself carries information.

**History: a systemic illness with a late focal event.** Gabriela presents a 29-year-old man with 15 days of fever and headache, one day of sudden right-sided hemiparesis, and several days of worsening somnolence and confusion, with a dental procedure two weeks before symptom onset as the only positive past history. The discussants immediately raise endocarditis with septic embolization — to the meninges (meningitis), brain parenchyma (abscess), or vessel wall (mycotic aneurysm, which can rupture and cause subarachnoid or intraparenchymal hemorrhage) — alongside the possibility of direct extension from an oral/dental infection and **cavernous sinus thrombosis**. Berkowitz walks through why the cavernous sinus is an unlikely fit: it contains the oculomotor, trochlear, and abducens nerves, the ophthalmic (and, depending on the source, maxillary) branches of the trigeminal nerve, and the oculosympathetic pathway — not the optic nerve, and not the corticospinal tract — so a hemiparesis without eye-movement or facial-sensory findings argues against it. He raises meningeal infectious vasculitis (bacterial, fungal, tubercular, or meningovascular syphilis — the penetrating vessels supplying the deep brain territory can become secondarily inflamed and occluded by an overlying meningitis) as a way to unify a focal deficit with an infectious process, and recalls a case of herpes encephalitis where temporal lobe swelling compressed the midbrain enough to produce hemiparesis by mass effect on the cerebral peduncle — a reminder that "sudden" focal deficits can sometimes be **pseudo-acute**, an evolving process only noticed once weakness crosses a functional threshold.

**A tempo check reframes the whole illness.** Berkowitz presses on the 15-day duration: untreated bacterial meningitis or HSV encephalitis would make this patient critically ill or dead well before two weeks, so if this really is a bacterial or herpetic CNS infection, the fever and headache more likely reflect a *systemic* process — the presumed endocarditis itself — with the confusion and stroke representing a later, acute complication, rather than 15 days of untreated primary meningoencephalitis. As a deliberate debiasing exercise, he asks the group to imagine the case **without** the dental-procedure detail: this broadens the infectious differential to Lyme disease (whose three most common neurologic complications are meningitis, facial nerve palsy, and radiculitis — not the joint pain Sherry first suggested) and HIV (which can cause meningitis directly, or, at seroconversion, one of three recognized syndromes — aseptic meningitis with a possibly falsely negative antibody test, a Guillain-Barré-like illness distinguished from true GBS by CSF pleocytosis, or facial palsy) — though none of these typically produce hemiparesis, so they remain background knowledge rather than leading hypotheses here.

**Exam: two lesions, and a load-bearing absence.** Vitals show fever to 39.5°C; the patient is ill-appearing with a 4/6 holosystolic murmur at the apex. He is disoriented to time (not fluctuating) but oriented to person and place, with a right homonymous hemianopia (localizing to the left retrochiasmal visual pathway), a right central facial palsy (forehead spared, arguing for a central rather than peripheral CN VII lesion), and moderate dysarthria — confirming a lesion above the neck, on the left. Motor exam shows 3/5 right-sided strength with diminished tone and reflexes and a right Babinski sign — an upper motor neuron pattern, though notably without the hyperreflexia or increased tone sometimes expected, consistent with the early, evolving phase of an acute corticospinal lesion. Sensory exam shows diminished right-sided pain and temperature. Cerebellar testing shows dysmetria and dysdiadochokinesia on the **left** — since cerebellar signs are ipsilateral to their lesion, this points to a separate **left cerebellar** lesion, distinct from the right-sided (left-hemispheric) findings above. The group notices what's missing: this hemiparesis-plus-facial-palsy-plus-hemianopia package, in a presumably left-language-dominant patient, would be expected to include **aphasia** if it were a large left MCA/cortical territory stroke — its absence argues instead for a **deep** lesion (thalamocapsular/internal capsule), the same densely-packed territory classically infarcted in "lacunar" strokes, here explained by infectious vasculitis of the small penetrating arteries supplying that deep territory (classically described with tuberculous and fungal meningitis, though it occurs with bacterial meningitis too). No meningeal signs are present, but Berkowitz explicitly declines to let that lower his suspicion, citing the JAMA *Rational Clinical Examination* review showing Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs are only about 5% sensitive (though highly specific) for meningitis, and that the full fever/neck-stiffness/altered-mental-status triad appears in well under half of confirmed cases — patients in resource-rich settings often present, or take NSAIDs, before the full meningeal inflammatory response develops.

**Predicting the workup.** Before the imaging is revealed, Kaitlyn and Sherry both predict an embolic stroke from bacterial endocarditis. Berkowitz agrees, framing ischemic stroke etiology into three buckets — vessels, heart, or blood — and noting that a multifocal, bilateral, supra- *and* infratentorial pattern points strongly toward the heart. He also flags a practical point: blood cultures are roughly 80–90% sensitive for the causative organism in this setting and can secure a microbiologic diagnosis even when lumbar puncture is deferred or unsafe (as it might be with a large abscess).

**Resolution.** MRI (the patient having been transferred from an outside hospital with no CT available) shows restricted diffusion — the signature of acute infarction — in the left occipital lobe, bilateral cerebellar hemispheres, the left basal ganglia and internal capsule, and bilateral frontal regions: a multifocal, bilateral, supra- and infratentorial pattern. Echocardiography shows a sizable mitral valve vegetation with moderate mitral regurgitation. Two blood cultures grow *Staphylococcus aureus* — an organism Berkowitz notes is an uncommon primary cause of meningitis in normal circumstances (compared with *Neisseria meningitidis* and *S. pneumoniae* in adults, or *Listeria*, *E. coli*, and *Group B Streptococcus* in young infants), but a plausible seeding organism once a blood-brain or blood-tissue barrier has been violated — here, presumably through the dental procedure. HIV testing is negative and a thrombophilia workup is negative. The unifying diagnosis is *S. aureus* infective endocarditis with septic embolic multifocal cerebral and cerebellar infarction. The patient undergoes emergent mitral valve replacement and is started on ceftriaxone and vancomycin (the standard empiric regimen for presumed community-acquired bacterial meningitis in an immunocompetent adult, with ampicillin added for patients over 50 or immunocompromised, and acyclovir added if HSV encephalitis can't be excluded), improves initially with resolution of fever and mild neurologic recovery — but at 20 days develops recurrent fever and worsening neurologic exam, with repeat CT showing new brain abscesses at the sites of the prior strokes, confirming that septic emboli can seed abscesses despite adequate antibiotic therapy, requiring the team to treat through it. Berkowitz closes by naming a genuinely unresolved clinical tension in cases like this: cardiac surgeons are often reluctant to proceed with valve replacement (which requires heparinization on bypass) when there is brain involvement, for fear of an undetected mycotic aneurysm rupturing catastrophically — yet delaying surgery risks further embolic strokes from the still-infected valve, a real dilemma without a universally correct answer.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Tempo mismatch as a diagnostic tool.** Fifteen days of untreated fever and headache is incompatible with acute bacterial meningitis or HSV encephalitis, both of which are rapidly lethal untreated — used explicitly to reject an otherwise plausible primary-CNS-infection framing and correctly re-source the systemic illness to endocarditis.
- **"Remove the anchor and re-derive."** Berkowitz explicitly asks the group to imagine the case without the dental-procedure detail, testing whether the differential still makes sense independent of that one salient, temporally-proximate fact — a deliberate, named debiasing technique against over-weighting a vivid exposure story.
- **A load-bearing absence.** The missing aphasia, despite an otherwise cortical-looking triad (hemiparesis, facial palsy, hemianopia), is treated as genuinely informative negative evidence that relocalizes the lesion from cortical/large-vessel to deep/penetrator-artery territory — the same "what's missing" discipline used in [[CPS 146 - Right-Sided Weakness and Numbness and Medial Medullary Syndrome from a Vertebral Artery Dissection|CPS 146]]'s crossed-sign pivot.
- **Evidence-grounded skepticism of a negative physical sign.** Rather than let "no meningeal signs" lower suspicion for meningitis, Berkowitz cites the Kernig's/Brudzinski's sensitivity literature directly, modeling **[[Test Interpretation|test-interpretation]]** discipline for a physical exam finding, not just a lab or imaging result.
- **Ending on genuine, named clinical uncertainty.** The case closes with an explicit, unresolved tension between cardiac surgery (bleeding risk under anticoagulation with a possible undetected mycotic aneurysm) and neurology (ongoing embolic stroke risk without valve replacement) — real-world **[[Diagnostic Humility|diagnostic humility]]** where the "right answer" genuinely isn't settled.

## Teaching Discussion

- Trace the corticospinal tract explicitly when localizing any hemiparesis, and use "is this truly one whole side of the body" as an early branch point toward a central (contralateral brain, ipsilateral cord) versus peripheral process; distinguish weakness (paresis) from complete paralysis (plegia) precisely.
- A dental or oral procedure preceding a febrile illness with neurologic complications should raise endocarditis with septic embolization (to meninges, brain parenchyma as abscess, or vessel wall as mycotic aneurysm) as a unifying hypothesis — but test the exposure against real illness scripts rather than assuming causation from temporal proximity alone, and explicitly re-derive the differential as if the exposure weren't there.
- Learn the cavernous sinus's actual contents (CN III, IV, VI, V1 ± V2, oculosympathetics — not the optic nerve, not the corticospinal tract) so which structures can plausibly be affected is clear at the bedside.
- Weigh the tempo of a systemic illness against the expected natural history of the infections under consideration; untreated bacterial meningitis and HSV encephalitis both progress far too quickly for a patient to still be presenting after two weeks.
- When a clinical picture "should" include a particular finding (aphasia, in a left hemispheric syndrome) and it's absent, use that absence actively to relocalize the lesion rather than discount it.
- Never let a negative meningeal exam lower suspicion for meningitis on its own — Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs are highly specific but poorly sensitive, especially in patients who present early or have taken NSAIDs.
- Multifocal, bilateral, supra- and infratentorial infarcts point strongly to a cardioembolic source; pursue echocardiography and blood cultures promptly, since blood cultures alone can secure a diagnosis when lumbar puncture is unsafe or deferred.
- In *Staphylococcus aureus* bacteremia, pursue infectious disease consultation (associated with improved mortality), echocardiography for endocarditis, and a deliberate search for other seeded sites (joints, spine, other organs); de-escalate from vancomycin to a beta-lactam once methicillin susceptibility is confirmed.
