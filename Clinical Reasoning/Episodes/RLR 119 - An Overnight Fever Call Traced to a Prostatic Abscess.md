## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 5 AM cross-covering phone call — a 78-year-old man with advanced dementia and a G-tube, hospitalized two weeks for placement, now with a new low-grade fever and tachycardia — becomes a bread-and-butter demonstration of hospital-acquired fever reasoning. A framework built around three hospital-specific infection risks (lines, recent antibiotics, and poor PO intake) guides a careful bedside exam that finds a wrong-way condom catheter, unexplained chest mucus, and a deceptively benign abdomen. Neutrophilia and mildly elevated transaminases point the workup toward the hepatobiliary tract and a feared diagnosis of acalculous cholecystitis — until the CT scan reveals the true source sitting one organ lower: a prostatic abscess, a classic and easily missed cause of fever of unclear origin in a patient who cannot report pelvic pain.

## Pearls

- When paged for a new fever and tachycardia, first establish the patient's temperature curve and the reason for admission before deciding how urgently to act — a fever climbing for the first time in a patient's stay carries different implications than a known, already-treated, and now-resolving fever, and this single check often determines whether further workup is needed overnight at all.
- Heart rate should be interpreted in the context of fever, since fever alone predictably raises heart rate; a heart rate only modestly elevated relative to the degree of fever is reassuring, while one clearly out of proportion to the fever deserves separate explanation.
- Hospitalized patients acquiring a new infection carry three additional risk categories beyond the standard outpatient sources (respiratory, urinary, skin/soft tissue, hepatobiliary, bacteremia without a source): lines (peripheral IV-associated bacteremia, foley-associated UTI), recent antibiotic exposure (raising concern for C. diff or candidemia), and reduced oral intake (raising concern for sialolithiasis/sialoadenitis from salivary gland stasis, and acalculous cholecystitis from biliary stasis).
- A bedside exam in a patient who cannot verbally localize symptoms should specifically check catheter and line placement and function (not just their documented presence) — a condom catheter that is present on the chart but not actually correctly applied provides no diagnostic information and no therapeutic benefit, and should prompt direct inspection rather than trust in nursing documentation alone.
- Age, cognitive impairment, and immunosuppression are three variables that substantially reduce the diagnostic yield of an abdominal exam — a benign-feeling abdomen in a patient with any of these three features should not be treated as strong evidence against an intra-abdominal process, and the threshold for imaging should be adjusted accordingly.
- Empiric antibiotics are not automatically indicated for every new hospital fever; they are more clearly mandated when a patient meets one of a small set of high-acuity criteria — sepsis physiology, neutropenic fever, or decompensated cirrhosis — and short-term diagnostic clarity (e.g., results available within a couple of hours) can reasonably be awaited in a patient who does not meet these criteria and is not otherwise unstable.
- Simple (uncomplicated) cystitis is not typically expected to cause fever; when a bladder infection does produce fever, it more often reflects cystitis with obstruction (elevated intraluminal pressure driving systemic translocation) — a post-void residual should be checked when this pattern is suspected.
- Pyuria has very high predictive value for urinary tract infection in patients under roughly 50, but its predictive value plummets substantially in older adults, where asymptomatic pyuria is extremely common (present in over half of nursing home patients) due to structural and hormonal factors — prostatic disease in older men and atrophic vaginitis/estrogen loss in older women create chronic urinary tract irritation independent of true infection — making a positive urine culture in this population necessary but not sufficient for a UTI diagnosis, particularly in a patient who cannot report dysuria to help exclude other causes.
- Neutrophilia in an older adult with an unclear source of fever should raise strong concern for an intra-abdominal process; in younger patients, severe nausea and vomiting often serve as the presenting clue to intra-abdominal infection, while in older adults, neutrophilia frequently serves as the more reliable (if less specific) signal in the absence of classic symptoms.
- Prolonged hospitalization combined with poor or absent oral intake builds a coherent narrative for hepatobiliary infection along the acalculous cholecystitis spectrum — but both CT and ultrasound have meaningfully imperfect sensitivity for acalculous cholecystitis, often necessitating a HIDA scan to confirm or exclude the diagnosis when initial imaging is unrevealing.
- A prostatic abscess is an important and easily overlooked cause of fever of unclear origin in patients unable to report the classic symptoms (perineal/pelvic pain, dysuria) — it should be considered whenever pelvic or genitourinary imaging incidentally captures the prostate, and can be surprisingly easy to miss on a quick read of a CT performed for a different indication.
- Gram-positive cocci in clusters on a single positive blood culture bottle (out of two or more drawn) should raise consideration of a contaminant (commonly coagulase-negative staph) even when a more clearly pathogenic organism (e.g., E. coli in the urine) has already been identified from another source — empiric vancomycin coverage until speciation returns is a reasonable bridge while this is sorted out.
- Optimizing antibiotic penetration into an acutely inflamed prostate is a genuine pharmacologic consideration: most antibiotics penetrate an acutely inflamed prostate adequately, but Bactrim and the fluoroquinolones are specifically favored for optimized prostatic penetration — since neither is typically given intravenously, standard practice often pairs an initial broad-spectrum IV beta-lactam (e.g., ertapenem or ceftriaxone) with a prolonged oral course of one of these two agents once the patient is stable for the switch.
- Urologic drainage of a prostatic abscess is uncommonly pursued given the procedural risk and patient discomfort involved; interval imaging to confirm resolution is a more typical management strategy than primary drainage, particularly for abscesses in the low single-digit-centimeter range.

## Problem Representation

A 78-year-old man with advanced dementia, G-tube dependence, and a recent right MCA stroke, hospitalized two weeks for disposition planning, who develops a new early-morning fever and tachycardia; a bedside exam notable for a misapplied condom catheter and unexplained chest mucus, combined with neutrophilia and mild transaminase elevation, initially raises concern for hepatobiliary infection, but CT imaging reveals the true source to be a prostatic abscess with associated E. coli bacteremia/urinary infection.

## Case Narrative

Reza presented this case as an authentic, real-time account of his own overnight cross-covering shift, deliberately withholding the patient's history at first to have Rabih process the initial data cold. At 5 AM, he received a call that a 78-year-old man's temperature was 100.3°F with a heart rate of 110, with the rest of his vital signs normal.

Rabih explained that this combination of vital sign parameters is a common nursing-triggered call threshold, and that the priority on receiving such a call is establishing how sick the patient actually is — noting the reassuring fact that blood pressure remained stable and that a modestly elevated heart rate is expected alongside fever. Before pursuing any workup, he said he would want to know why the patient was hospitalized and what his fever curve looked like: a fever that had already been present and was trending down would warrant no further overnight action, while a fever that was new or trending up would prompt a fuller evaluation.

Reza confirmed he had done exactly this. The patient's one-liner: 78 years old, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, a right MCA stroke five to six months prior with residual left-sided hemiplegia and aphasia, and advanced dementia requiring G-tube feeding. He had been admitted two weeks earlier not for an acute medical problem but because his care had become too difficult for his family to manage at home, and the team was working to establish a safe discharge plan. His temperature curve had been entirely normal (98.6-98.7°F) until this call — his first fever of the admission. His medication list included as-needed Tylenol (not recently used), metoprolol (specifically for hypertension, not atrial fibrillation — a detail Reza flagged as important), aspirin, and other antihypertensive and cholesterol medications.

Rabih reasoned that since the fever was new, it likely reflected a new infection rather than a previously masked one. He organized his approach around the standard outpatient sources of fever (respiratory, urinary, and skin/soft tissue infections in essentially all patients), while noting that this patient's advanced dementia would limit how much could be learned from history, favoring an early viral swab, chest X-ray, urinalysis, and thorough skin exam; his profound aspiration risk also raised the possibility of aspiration pneumonitis rather than a true infection. He then added a hospital-specific layer to the framework: hospitalized patients acquiring new infections carry three additional risk categories beyond the standard outpatient sources — lines (a peripheral IV could seed bacteremia, or a foley catheter could seed a hospital-acquired UTI), recent antibiotic exposure (raising concern for C. diff or candidemia), and reduced oral intake (raising concern for salivary gland stasis causing sialolithiasis/sialoadenitis, and biliary stasis causing acalculous cholecystitis). Given the patient did not appear severely ill on the data so far, he recommended a bedside assessment before committing to a broader workup.

Reza then read from the note he wrote after seeing the patient. His baseline mental status (per the nurse) was nodding yes/no and knowing his name; on this encounter he was at that baseline. On exam, Reza found roughly 100-200cc of thick, non-bloody mucus pooled on the patient's chest just above the right nipple. The abdomen was non-distended, with some muscular tension but no wincing or tenderness elicited on palpation. Heart and lung auscultation were difficult given the patient's positioning and inability to cooperate, but nothing overtly abnormal was heard. The bladder was non-distended and non-tender on exam; a condom catheter was documented as in place, but on direct inspection Reza found it lying on the bed rather than actually applied to the patient. There was no peripheral IV in place (the patient was awaiting disposition, not actively receiving IV therapy), no recent stools, and — per the bedside nurse, who Reza specifically involved in the exam — no skin breakdown.

Rabih responded with specific, concrete management recommendations rather than only further diagnostic reasoning. He recommended an immediate bladder scan to assess for a large post-void residual (values over roughly 500cc being concerning), given that tachycardia and fever together generally warrant IV access regardless of the eventual diagnosis, and specifically for both fluid resuscitation and diagnostic purposes. He flagged the ambiguity of "100cc of mucus" — genuinely difficult to distinguish from vomitus at that volume — as reason enough to hold the G-tube feeds, both because of concern for aspiration and because a patient who could report nausea would very likely not be fed under similar circumstances. He emphasized that a benign-appearing abdominal exam should not be fully trusted in this patient, since age, cognitive impairment, and immunosuppression are three variables that substantially reduce the diagnostic yield of an abdominal exam. He then reasoned through the diagnostic ambiguity built into this case: pyuria could not be used to confidently diagnose a UTI in a patient unable to report dysuria (since doing so requires excluding other causes), a positive respiratory finding would still raise the question of whether the trigger was aspiration from an unaddressed abdominal process, and cellulitis had already been excluded on exam — leaving, in his view, only a clearly positive COVID or flu test as a fully explanatory, non-abdominal exit from the case. He noted that most patients with a genuinely serious intra-abdominal process will show some laboratory clue (typically neutrophilia), so there was time to proceed methodically rather than urgently.

Asked directly whether he would start empiric antibiotics, Rabih explained that empiric treatment is more clearly mandated only when a patient meets one of three high-acuity criteria — sepsis physiology, neutropenic fever, or decompensated cirrhosis — none of which this patient definitively met; given that he expected diagnostic clarity within a couple of hours at his own institution, he would not start antibiotics yet, characterizing the decision as a judgment call rather than a fixed rule. Asked whether simple cystitis alone could explain a fever, he said he had never personally seen uncomplicated cystitis cause fever, but had seen cystitis with obstruction do so with some regularity, attributing this to elevated intraluminal ("pus under pressure") pressure.

Reza's actual overnight orders were: respiratory viral panel including RSV and COVID, two sets of blood cultures, urinalysis with culture, chest X-ray, a KUB, CBC, BMP, liver chemistries, and a lipase (prompted by his abdominal exam impression), along with the bladder scan and a gastric residual check that Rabih had recommended. The bladder scan showed less than 100cc of residual urine (excluding retention), and the gastric residual was essentially nil; a peripheral IV was established, and the G-tube feeds were held. Reza's actual initial management: peripheral IV established, G-tube feeds held, a 500cc bolus of lactated Ringer's given, ceftriaxone and metronidazole started empirically, metoprolol discontinued (since it was being used for hypertension rather than rate control of atrial fibrillation), and the as-needed Tylenol discontinued, while his bowel regimen was continued given no reported loose stools.

The results: respiratory viral testing (COVID, RSV, influenza A and B) was entirely negative. The urinalysis showed a specific gravity of 1.023, more than 182 white blood cells, 81 red blood cells, and positive nitrites and leukocyte esterase. The BMP was unremarkable (creatinine 0.57, sodium 137). The liver chemistries showed an ALT of 120 and AST of roughly 45. The CBC's main abnormality was a white count of 19,000, neutrophil-predominant.

Rabih reasoned that the neutrophilia was the single most important new data point, unlocking concern for an intra-abdominal process: in his experience, younger patients with intra-abdominal infection tend to present with severe nausea and vomiting, while older adults more often present primarily with neutrophilia as the key laboratory signal. He explicitly instructed disregarding the urinalysis as diagnostic of a UTI on its own — pyuria is an expected, largely age-related finding (from BPH in older men, atrophic vaginitis in older women) present in over half of nursing home patients, meaning its predictive value, so high in patients under 50, plummets substantially in older adults; a positive urine culture in this context might simply reflect the patient's chronic baseline rather than an acute infection. Combining the neutrophilia with the mild transaminase elevation, he raised strong concern for an intra-abdominal process, specifically favoring the hepatobiliary tract given the patient's two-week hospitalization and lack of oral intake — a narrative consistent with acalculous cholecystitis. He recommended an urgent CT scan while acknowledging that both CT and ultrasound have meaningfully imperfect sensitivity for acalculous cholecystitis, often requiring a HIDA scan to confirm or exclude it.

Reza, independently worried about the ALT elevation raising concern for cholangitis, increased the ceftriaxone dose to 2 grams per UpToDate guidance for that indication. The initial imaging plan was a right-upper-quadrant ultrasound, but as the patient's temperature and white count continued rising over the weekend, the team proceeded directly to CT instead (delayed roughly five hours by scanner downtime). The CT scan, however, did not show hepatobiliary pathology — it instead revealed a well-defined abscess within the prostate.

Additional data followed: the urine culture grew E. coli, and blood cultures were positive in one of two bottles for gram-positive cocci in clusters. Rabih interpreted this combination as most consistent with E. coli as the true causative organism (given its growth in urine), with the single positive blood culture bottle for gram-positive cocci in clusters more likely, though not certainly, representing a contaminant (coagulase-negative staph) — reasonable to cover empirically with vancomycin pending further clarification given the patient's overall trajectory. He characterized a prostatic abscess as one of the more interesting causes of fever of unclear origin specifically in patients who cannot verbally localize symptoms like perineal discomfort or dysuria. On treatment, he discussed the pharmacologic nuance of prostatic antibiotic penetration: most agents adequately treat an acutely inflamed prostate, but Bactrim and the fluoroquinolones are specifically favored when optimizing prostatic penetration is the goal; since neither is commonly given intravenously, standard practice typically pairs an initial broad-spectrum IV beta-lactam (ertapenem or ceftriaxone) with a prolonged oral course of one of these agents. He noted that urologic drainage of a prostatic abscess is uncommon given its procedural risk and discomfort, with interval imaging to confirm resolution being the more typical approach, though he was uncertain of any specific size cutoff that would change that calculus.

The patient's actual course followed this pattern closely: he was initially started on vancomycin, but a rapid blood-culture identification panel (biofire) quickly identified coagulase-negative staph, prompting ID to discontinue vancomycin, increase the ceftriaxone dose to 2 grams, and involve urology. Urology elected not to drain the roughly 2 x 2 x 1.6 cm abscess, and ID recommended a full month of oral Bactrim to complete treatment. The patient subsequently developed C. diff as a consequence of his antibiotic exposure.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| New fever/tachycardia after a previously entirely normal temperature curve | Confirms an acute, new process rather than a previously masked or resolving fever |
| Metoprolol on the medication list, specifically for hypertension (not AFib) | Removes rate-controlled AFib as a confounder for the tachycardia; supports metoprolol discontinuation to allow the tachycardia to be interpreted as a vital sign |
| Condom catheter present on documentation but not actually applied on inspection | Removes any protective/diagnostic value the catheter might otherwise have provided; reinforces the need for direct inspection over chart trust |
| Benign-appearing abdominal exam in a patient with advanced age, dementia, and reduced physiologic reserve | Should not meaningfully lower suspicion for an intra-abdominal process, given known reduced exam sensitivity in this population |
| Does not clearly meet sepsis, neutropenic fever, or decompensated cirrhosis criteria | Supports awaiting rapid diagnostic workup rather than immediate empiric antibiotics |
| Marked pyuria (>182 WBC, positive nitrites/leukocyte esterase) in a 78-year-old man | Weak evidence for UTI on its own, given the high baseline prevalence of asymptomatic pyuria in this age group |
| Neutrophilia (WBC 19,000) plus mild transaminase elevation (ALT 120) | Raises strong suspicion for an intra-abdominal, specifically hepatobiliary, process |
| CT scan reveals a prostatic abscess (not hepatobiliary pathology) | Redirects the entire diagnosis away from the leading hepatobiliary hypothesis to a genitourinary source |
| Urine culture growing E. coli; 1/2 blood culture bottles with GPC in clusters | Identifies E. coli as the likely true pathogen; raises but does not confirm a contaminant explanation for the isolated GPC-in-clusters result |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Hospital-acquired fever in a patient with reduced verbal and cognitive capacity illness script: standard outpatient infection sources must be supplemented with three hospital-specific risk categories (lines, recent antibiotics, reduced oral intake), and both the history and the physical exam carry substantially reduced diagnostic yield in a patient who cannot reliably report symptoms — favoring a broader, more systematic initial workup and a lower threshold to trust laboratory signals (like neutrophilia) over the absence of localizing complaints or exam findings.

The case also illustrates a specific anchoring trap the hosts named directly: pyuria in an older adult can be misread as diagnostic of UTI, and a focal infiltrate can be misread as diagnostic of pneumonia, when either could instead be a chronic baseline finding or an aspiration event secondary to an unaddressed abdominal process — Rabih explicitly flagged this as a reasoning trap to hold onto rather than resolve prematurely, a discipline that ultimately proved correct when the true source turned out to be neither urinary nor respiratory nor hepatobiliary, but prostatic.

## Teaching Discussion

The central teaching point is the three-part hospital-acquired-infection framework (lines, antibiotics, poor oral intake) layered on top of the standard outpatient fever workup — a schema both hosts felt was underemphasized relative to how frequently it applies in practice.

A second teaching point is the markedly reduced predictive value of pyuria in older adults given the high baseline prevalence of asymptomatic bacteriuria/pyuria from BPH and atrophic vaginitis, reinforcing that a positive urinalysis in this population should not be treated as automatically diagnostic.

A third teaching point is the reduced diagnostic yield of both history and physical exam in patients with advanced age, cognitive impairment, or immunosuppression, and the corresponding need to lean more heavily on laboratory and imaging data (here, neutrophilia and eventually CT) to localize the source of infection.

The closing teaching point, articulated by both hosts, is an explicit embrace of practice variation and uncertainty in real-world clinical decision-making: rather than presenting a single "correct" algorithm, the case was framed around the discipline of taking a reasonable next step given the data available at each point in time, with the final diagnosis (a prostatic abscess) serving as a reminder that a well-reasoned leading hypothesis (hepatobiliary source) can still be wrong, and that imaging performed for one indication can reveal an entirely different, unanticipated answer.
