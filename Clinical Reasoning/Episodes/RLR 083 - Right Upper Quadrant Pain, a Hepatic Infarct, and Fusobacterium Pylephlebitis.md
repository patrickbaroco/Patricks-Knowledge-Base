## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 62-year-old man presents with five days of colicky right upper quadrant pain and fever, without the eating association or nausea that would typically anchor the case to biliary colic. A discordance between cholestatic labs and initially unremarkable imaging is resolved by trusting the labs and pursuing advanced imaging, which instead reveals a rare wedge-shaped hepatic infarct. The infarct traces to an extensive portal vein thrombosis, and the absence of portal hypertension redirects the workup toward an infectious cause — ultimately Fusobacterium-driven pylephlebitis seeded from underlying diverticulitis.

## Pearls

- Episodic right upper quadrant pain is not automatically biliary colic — the absence of a food association and the absence of nausea/vomiting are both atypical for biliary colic and should prompt deployment of the full right-upper-quadrant schema (hepatobiliary, pancreas, stomach, lung/pleura) rather than anchoring on the most common diagnosis.
- When labs and imaging disagree — cholestatic labs (AlkPhos elevation out of proportion to AST/ALT) with reassuring imaging — don't assume the imaging is right by default; a cholestatic pattern can also be caused by a source outside the liver (congestive hepatopathy, cholestasis of sepsis, Stauffer phenomenon from pyelonephritis or a distant tumor).
- The sensitivity ladder for detecting choledocholithiasis and biliary obstruction runs, in ascending order: ultrasound, CT, MRCP, EUS — a negative ultrasound or CT does not settle the question when the clinical and lab picture still points to biliary disease.
- The liver's dual blood supply (hepatic artery plus portal vein) makes true hepatic infarction rare, but a portal vein thrombosis can still cause a wedge-shaped infarct in the territory it supplies — treat an unexplained hepatic wedge lesion as a possible ischemic event even though "the liver shouldn't infarct" is the textbook teaching.
- For any newly discovered thrombus, ask two questions in sequence: where did the clot form (the pump, the pipes, or the plasma), and what is the material occluding the vessel (bland thrombus or pus)? A fever in the setting of a new thrombus should raise concern for the latter.
- Portal vein thrombosis without pre-existing portal hypertension (no ascites, no splenomegaly, no varices) points away from the classic cirrhotic-PVT pattern and toward three buckets instead: infection (pylephlebitis, usually from diverticulitis or appendicitis), local inflammation (pancreatitis, IBD), or an underlying hypercoagulable state (myeloproliferative neoplasms being the leading systemic cause).

## Problem Representation

A 62-year-old man presents with five days of colicky, spontaneous right upper quadrant pain without a food trigger, nausea, or vomiting, and a fever of 39.2°C; cholestatic liver chemistries (AlkPhos-predominant elevation, elevated bilirubin) are discordant with initially unremarkable right upper quadrant ultrasound and CT imaging (aside from an incidental finding of early diverticulitis), and empiric treatment for presumed cholangitis is started. Subsequent MRCP reveals a wedge-shaped hepatic lesion suspicious for infarct without biliary dilation, traced on Doppler and triple-phase CT to an extensive right portal vein thrombosis; with no evidence of underlying portal hypertension or a hypercoagulable condition on initial workup, one of four blood culture bottles ultimately grows Fusobacterium, localizing the diagnosis to pylephlebitis (septic portal vein thrombophlebitis) likely seeded from the patient's diverticulitis.

## Case Narrative

A 62-year-old man develops colicky right upper quadrant pain, on and off for several days, without nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea. Outside imaging (a CT, per the patient's uncertain recollection) leads to a recommendation for an MRI abdomen and transfer to another facility, where he is found to have a fever of 39.2°C, mild tachycardia, and right upper quadrant tenderness.

Before further data, the discussant reasons that episodic right upper quadrant pain in isolation would typically default to biliary colic as the operating hypothesis by base rate alone — but two atypical features (no association with eating, no nausea or vomiting) argue against that default and justify deploying the fuller right-upper-quadrant schema rather than moving straight to a cholecystitis/biliary-colic workup: hepatobiliary disease dominates the differential, with attention also owed to the pancreas and stomach (anatomically adjacent) and to the lungs and pleura. Given the high fever, the discussant reasons that hepatobiliary and pulmonary/pleural processes are more classically inflammatory than pancreatic or gastric disease, and that pulmonary or pleural disease would be expected to produce respiratory symptoms not present here — favoring hepatobiliary inflammation by base rate, organized into image-positive (cholangitis, liver abscess) versus image-negative (viral hepatitis A through E) categories.

Labs: white count 12,000 (neutrophil-predominant), hemoglobin 16.2, platelets 293,000; AST 92, ALT 61, total bilirubin 3.2 (unfractionated), alkaline phosphatase 286; sodium 132, otherwise normal chemistries. A right upper quadrant ultrasound shows cholelithiasis without biliary dilation, and incidental liver nodularity raising possible cirrhosis. CT abdomen/pelvis shows no abnormal liver morphology or biliary dilation (intra- or extrahepatic), but does show findings consistent with early left lower quadrant diverticulitis — asymptomatic on direct questioning.

The discussant frames the central tension explicitly: the alkaline phosphatase elevation is disproportionate to the AST/ALT elevation, consistent with a cholestatic pattern, and the bilirubin is inferred to be direct (since hemolysis, the major cause of indirect hyperbilirubinemia, is not supported by a normal hemoglobin) — the labs point to hepatobiliary disease, but the imaging does not confirm it, instead highlighting an unrelated diverticular finding. Rather than assuming the imaging is correct, the discussant raises the possibility that the cholestatic labs could be falsely localizing to the liver from a distant source — citing congestive hepatopathy from heart failure, cholestasis of sepsis, and Stauffer phenomenon (cholestatic liver injury from a distant process such as pyelonephritis or a paraneoplastic renal cell carcinoma) as precedents for labs pointing to the liver when the true process lies elsewhere. Because ultrasound is the least sensitive imaging modality for choledocholithiasis, followed by CT, then MRCP, then EUS, the discussant's plan is to trust the labs over the negative imaging and escalate to advanced biliary imaging (MRCP or EUS) via GI consultation, while treating empirically as cholangitis until proven otherwise: two sets of blood cultures (four bottles total), IV fluids, and broad gram-negative antibiotic coverage.

The patient improves substantially overnight — defervescing and reporting improved pain — before the MRCP is completed. The discussant raises an important caveat: improvement could reflect response to antibiotics, or could equally reflect spontaneous passage of a common bile duct stone, and cautions that a benign trajectory does not retroactively confirm the working diagnosis; if advanced imaging proves unrevealing, causes of transient hepatobiliary inflammation with spontaneous resolution (such as the cholestatic phase of viral hepatitis A through E) should still be considered, held with explicit diagnostic humility about the risk of an unexplained process recurring.

The MRCP result reframes the entire case: no biliary dilation, but a wedge-shaped, mildly T2-hyperintense lesion in the anterolateral right hepatic lobe, most consistent with an infarct or area of ischemia — an essentially unprecedented finding for both discussants. Repeat liver chemistries show AST 79, ALT 59, alkaline phosphatase 271, and bilirubin essentially unchanged at 3.1 (LDH sent and found to be 450) — trending in the right direction but not dramatically. The discussant reasons through the rarity of hepatic infarction given the liver's dual blood supply (hepatic artery and portal vein, each usually able to compensate if the other is compromised) but ultimately favors the imaging finding as real and mechanistically connected to the labs, extrapolating from other recognized causes of primary intrahepatic cholestasis with a vascular basis (ischemic cholangiopathy from COVID; hepatic vascular involvement in giant cell arteritis) to support that vascular insults can produce exactly this cholestatic laboratory pattern — reasoning that, absent a compelling alternative explanation, and because a purely extrahepatic biliary process has already been excluded by MRCP, this vascular hypothesis fits the data better than any competing explanation.

Framing this as a thrombotic or ischemic event prompts two organizing questions: what is the nature of the occluding material (bland thrombus, or pus given the fever), and where did it originate — the "pump" (cardioembolism), the "pipes" (celiac artery dissection, vasospasm, or vasculitis), the "plasma" (an inherent hypercoagulable state, e.g., cancer-associated or protein C deficiency), or the venous system. A right upper quadrant Doppler ultrasound, recommended by GI, reveals portal vein thrombosis involving the entire portal venous system; a follow-up triple-phase CT confirms complete occlusion of the right portal vein including its anterior and posterior branches — precisely correlating anatomically with the hepatic infarct's location.

The discussant clarifies a conceptual point: the portal vein functions more like a second arterial-type supply to the liver than a true systemic vein, so a portal vein thrombosis belongs in the "pipes" bucket of the schema rather than the venous bucket; a true hepatic venous infarct (from hepatic vein occlusion, as in Budd-Chiari) would be a distinct, separate process. With the site of origin necessarily in situ (the portal system drains the gut, so there is no remote embolic source), the two live questions become whether the thrombus is infected (infectious pylephlebitis, most commonly seeded from diverticulitis or appendicitis) or bland. Bland portal vein thrombosis in the absence of pre-existing portal hypertension (no ascites, splenomegaly, or varices on imaging, arguing against occult cirrhosis) points toward local inflammation (pancreatitis, inflammatory bowel disease) or a systemic hypercoagulable state — with myeloproliferative neoplasms flagged as the most common systemic cause of portal vein thrombosis specifically, prompting closer scrutiny of the CBC.

A hematology work-up for hypercoagulability is initiated (antiphospholipid antibody panel, JAK2 mutation analysis, and PNH peripheral flow cytometry) alongside continued observation; by hospital day three the patient is afebrile, pain-free, and blood cultures remain negative, prompting discontinuation of antibiotics and initiation of therapeutic anticoagulation (low molecular weight heparin transitioned to a DOAC) even before hematology results return, given clinical confidence in the diagnosis of portal vein thrombosis regardless of etiology. He is discharged.

The eventual hypercoagulability workup returns entirely negative: JAK2 negative, PNH negative, antiphospholipid antibody panel negative, and factor V Leiden/prothrombin gene mutation testing negative — collectively arguing against a myeloproliferative neoplasm (further supported by a white count and platelet count that don't fit CML or essential thrombocythemia) or another major inherited hypercoagulable state. With the "bland" hypothesis substantially weakened and no alternative occult malignancy identified as plausible (a lung cancer would likely be symptomatic; other cancers felt unlikely to have evaded detection on the imaging already obtained), attention shifts back toward an infectious cause, with the unrevisited diverticulitis finding — and consideration of a possible colonic or rectal source requiring colonoscopy — becoming the focus, alongside recognition that the relevant portal-vein-thrombophlebitis pathogens (Fusobacterium, Clostridium septicum) are often slow-growing anaerobes that can take five to six days to flag a blood culture bottle as positive.

Days after discharge, the patient's primary care physician contacts the team: one of four blood culture bottles has flagged positive for Fusobacterium, and the patient remains clinically well. Infectious disease is consulted and recommends one month of amoxicillin-clavulanate given case reports of an "abdominal Lemierre syndrome" variant (classically associated with Fusobacterium necrophorum via the internal jugular vein, but here caused by Fusobacterium nucleatum via the GI tract), even in the absence of an isolated clot-independent indication for treating a single positive bottle. The final diagnosis is framed as pylephlebitis — infectious/septic thrombophlebitis of the portal vein — almost certainly seeded from the patient's underlying diverticulitis, unifying the right upper quadrant pain, fever, cholestatic labs, hepatic infarct, and portal vein thrombosis into a single coherent process, with colonoscopy recommended given the elevated association between several of these colonic anaerobes (including Fusobacterium nucleatum) and underlying colorectal neoplasia.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The case models resisting a convenient anchor twice: first, declining to accept biliary colic as the default diagnosis despite its high base rate, because two specific features (no food association, no nausea/vomiting) don't fit; second, declining to accept reassuring-but-limited imaging as the final word once the labs suggested otherwise, instead reasoning explicitly about each test's sensitivity ceiling.

Diagnostic humility is voiced explicitly when discussing the overnight improvement: rather than treating clinical improvement as confirmation of the working hypothesis, the discussant names the competing possibility (spontaneous stone passage) and acknowledges that, absent a clear mechanistic understanding, the risk of recurrence cannot be confidently assessed either way.

The discussant borrows directly from prior pattern recognition with other organ systems — treating the hepatic infarct as "a stroke of the liver," and generalizing thrombus-analysis questions (where did it form, what is the material) from more familiar vascular events to an entity neither discussant had personally encountered — illustrating how transferable schemas substitute for direct experience with a genuinely rare finding.

The case closes on an explicit process-over-content lesson, borrowed from a colleague's teaching: what matters is not whether the diagnosis was specifically anticipated in advance, but whether a diagnostic pathway was executed that would ultimately catch it regardless — echoing the [[Bow and Arrow versus Fishing Net]] framework, where a difficult case is worked through parallel, disciplined testing rather than a single early guess.

## Teaching Discussion

A cholestatic liver chemistry pattern (alkaline phosphatase elevation disproportionate to AST/ALT) should prompt consideration not only of biliary disease but of extrahepatic causes that can produce cholestasis at a distance: congestive hepatopathy from heart failure, cholestasis of sepsis, and Stauffer phenomenon from a distant renal process (pyelonephritis or, classically, renal cell carcinoma).

The sensitivity of imaging for common bile duct disease increases in a predictable order: ultrasound (least sensitive), then CT, then MRCP, then EUS (most sensitive) — a negative study earlier in this sequence should not be treated as definitively excluding biliary obstruction when the clinical and laboratory picture remains suggestive.

Because the liver has dual blood supply (hepatic artery and portal vein), true hepatic infarction is rare, but portal vein thrombosis can still produce a wedge-shaped territorial infarct when compensatory arterial flow is insufficient — a recognized, if uncommon, phenomenon supported by prior case literature.

For any newly identified thrombus, two questions organize the workup: where did the clot originate (cardioembolic "pump," arterial "pipes," an inherent "plasma" hypercoagulable state, or venous), and what is the occluding material (bland thrombus versus infected/purulent) — a febrile patient with a new thrombus should always raise concern for the latter.

Portal vein thrombosis without pre-existing portal hypertension should prompt a search across three buckets: infection (pylephlebitis, most often from diverticulitis or appendicitis), local inflammation (pancreatitis, inflammatory bowel disease), and systemic hypercoagulability, with myeloproliferative neoplasms as the leading systemic cause to screen for.

Certain colonic anaerobes recovered from blood cultures — Clostridium septicum, Gemella, and Fusobacterium nucleatum among them — carry meaningfully elevated likelihood ratios for underlying colorectal neoplasia (as high as roughly 17 for Clostridium septicum in cited literature), warranting colonoscopy even when the acute infectious process resolves uneventfully; these organisms are frequently slow-growing anaerobes that may take five to six days to flag a positive blood culture, so a delayed single-bottle positive should not be dismissed as a contaminant without consideration.

Pylephlebitis (septic thrombophlebitis of the portal vein) is sometimes termed an "abdominal Lemierre syndrome," analogous to the classic internal-jugular-vein Lemierre syndrome from Fusobacterium necrophorum, but more often caused by Fusobacterium nucleatum arising from a GI source; treatment typically involves a prolonged course of targeted antibiotics (here, one month of amoxicillin-clavulanate) informed by infectious disease consultation.
