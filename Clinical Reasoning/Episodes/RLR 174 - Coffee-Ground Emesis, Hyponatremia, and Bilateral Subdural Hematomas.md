## Pearls

- **Vomited blood means the stomach was overwhelmed by a brisk bleed — or the patient is vomiting for another reason and the blood is incidental.** Blood isn't inherently nauseating (most **[[Upper GI Bleeding|GI bleeds]]** descend as **[[Melena|melena]]**), so for the stomach to send it *up*, it is either functionally **full** from a torrential bleed (the sick minority) or the patient is **vomiting for a separate cause** (the **[[Mallory-Weiss Tear|Mallory-Weiss]]** paradigm: vomit → tear → then blood) — and a **[[Coffee-Ground Emesis|coffee-ground]]** picture tolerated for **3 days** is a disconnect that argues against a large bleed.
- **GI-bleed presentation is set by velocity — and the "not-melena" presentations are a rare, much sicker subset.** Roughly **80–90%** of GI bleeds present as **[[Melena|melena]]** (slow), while brisk bleeds present as **[[Hematochezia|hematochezia]]** or **[[Hematemesis|hematemesis]]/coffee grounds** — so a hemodynamically stable patient with a near-baseline hemoglobin and days of coffee grounds likely has a **proximal/[[Swallowed Blood|swallowed source]]** (a lip laceration, epistaxis, esophagitis) and is vomiting for another reason entirely.
- **Hyponatremia is a free-water-versus-solute imbalance — read the urine osmolality first.** Too much water relative to what the kidney can excrete (**GFR**, **ADH**) and what **solute** is ingested; a **low [[Urine Osmolality|urine osm]]** means the kidney is excreting well, so the driver is **intake** — water without food (**[[Low Solute Hyponatremia|low-solute]]**, "tea-and-toast"/potomania physiology), here a partner hydrating but not *feeding* a nauseated patient — with **urine (Na + K) × 2** a better free-water-clearance proxy than urine osm (confounded by ineffective osmoles like urea).
- **Don't let a sodium of 120 close the case.** A 120 is rarely the *sole* cause of profound confusion, especially at 90 (a lower tolerance threshold makes symptoms *appear* at higher sodiums but does **not** make correction more urgent); when a safe correction (up ~10 with 500 cc, comfortably under the **[[Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome|<12/day]]** limit) leaves the **confusion unchanged**, another process is at work — so ask *why is he vomiting?*
- **Correlate clinically — don't explain the whole presentation with the first abnormality you find.** "Coffee grounds → reflex endoscopy" and "Na 120 → reflex cause-of-encephalopathy" are the same trap as "consolidation → reflex pneumonia"; the **unexplained thread** (the nausea/vomiting) is the actual case, and resisting **[[Premature Closure|premature closure]]** on a plausible-but-partial finding is the skill.
- **The bilateral subdural hematoma is medicine's sneakiest diagnosis — get the head CT.** **[[Subdural Hematoma|Subdurals]]** often have **no witnessed trauma and no external bruising**, a **bilateral** pattern hints at a spontaneous or **[[Bridging Veins|retching/bridging-vein]]** mechanism, and there is a chicken-and-egg with vomiting (falls/violent emesis cause it; it then causes nausea and confusion) — so in a frail elder, a contributory subdural hides behind a "dementia/placement" or "hyponatremia" label until imaged.

## Problem Representation

A frail 90-year-old man with metastatic prostate cancer presents with three days of vomiting (reported by his wife as coffee-ground in appearance), poor intake, progressive weakness, and multiple falls; he is hemodynamically stable with a lip laceration and a hemoglobin only 1 point below baseline (9 vs 10), suggesting the coffee-ground material is swallowed blood rather than a significant gastrointestinal hemorrhage — and therefore that he is vomiting for another reason. Discharged with capacity, he returns the next day with worsening confusion and unsteadiness and a serum sodium of 120 (low serum osmolality, normal glucose, urine sodium <10, urine osmolality 125 before fluids), indicating a low-solute (water-versus-solute imbalance) hyponatremia from water intake without adequate food in a nauseated patient with well-functioning kidneys; a 500 cc saline bolus raises the sodium to 130 without improving his confusion, and a head CT reveals bilateral subdural hematomas without mass effect (attributable to falls and/or violent retching, off anticoagulation, without external signs of trauma). Over a day he becomes non-nauseated, lucid, and stable, the unifying story being bilateral subdural hematomas producing nausea/vomiting and confusion, with the coffee-ground emesis from swallowed blood and the hyponatremia from consequent low-solute water intake — three threads each requiring clinical correlation rather than closure on the first abnormality.

## Case Narrative

**The coffee grounds.** A **90-year-old** man with **metastatic prostate cancer** is brought in by his wife for **3 days of vomiting**, some episodes appearing as **[[Coffee-Ground Emesis|coffee grounds]]**, with poor intake, progressive weakness, and **multiple falls**. Rabih's priority when a fluid comes *out* of a patient is to exclude **blood** — but he argues the probability of a significant bleed is **low**. He teaches the physiology: an **[[Upper GI Bleeding|upper GI bleed]]** presents by **[[Velocity of Bleeding|velocity]]** — most (~**80–90%**) as **[[Melena|melena]]** (slow trickle), the brisk minority as **[[Hematochezia|hematochezia]]** or **[[Hematemesis|hematemesis]]/coffee grounds** — and crucially, **blood isn't inherently nauseating** (people pass it down without vomiting), so for the stomach to send it *up* it must be **functionally full/overwhelmed** by a torrential bleed. Such patients are **very sick, very fast**; **surviving 3 days** of coffee grounds while hemodynamically stable is a **disconnect** that lowers the pretest probability of a large bleed without shutting the door on it.

The exam resolves the thread: the only finding is a **lip laceration**, and the **hemoglobin is 9** (baseline 10 — only 1 point down). The coffee grounds are almost certainly **[[Swallowed Blood|swallowed blood]]** from the lip (a proximal source — like esophagitis or epistaxis — can produce coffee grounds without a big bleed). Rabih reframes: this is not a GI bleed, so the patient is **vomiting for a still-unknown reason** — the **[[Mallory-Weiss Tear|Mallory-Weiss]]** paradigm inverted (**vomiting first**, blood second). "**You have work to do.**"

**Leaving against advice.** In the ED the patient wants to leave. Rabih would "**walk him to the door**": if a patient can **[[Against Medical Advice|walk out of an ED]]** (rise, perfuse, reach the exit without vomiting), they're likely fine; a hospital is **not a jail** (you cannot hold someone with **[[Capacity|capacity]]**); and walking alongside — rather than blocking the exit — both makes the point that the patient is in charge and creates the "elevator pitch" to invite return. He is deemed to have capacity and is discharged.

**The sodium.** The next day his wife brings him back **more confused**, unsteady, still nauseated and eating little, with a stable hemoglobin but a **serum sodium of 120**. Rabih first excludes **hyperglycemia** (normal glucose → true **[[Hyponatremia|hypoosmolar hyponatremia]]**), then teaches it "simply": hyponatremia is **too much free water relative to solute**, bounded by **renal free-water excretion** (**GFR**, **ADH**) and **solute intake**. He would want the **GFR** and a **[[Free Water Clearance|free-water-clearance]]** proxy — **[[Urine Osmolality|urine osm]]**, better yet **urine (Na + K) × 2** (since urine osm is confounded by ineffective osmoles like urea). On acuity, he cautions that a **120** is **unlikely to be the sole cause** of this much confusion — at 90 the *threshold* for symptoms is lower, but that makes it **more sensitive, not more emergent** — so he'd give **500 cc saline** (he's been vomiting), make him **NPO**, and collect the data. The results: **low serum osm, normal glucose, urine Na <10, urine osm 125** (pre-fluid) → the kidney's **excretory capacity is excellent**, so this is a pure **[[Low Solute Hyponatremia|water-versus-solute imbalance]]** — a devoted partner **hydrating but not feeding** a nauseated man. The bolus raises the sodium **120 → 130** (a safe correction, well under the **[[Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome|<12/day]]** limit) — but the **confusion and nausea do not improve**, proving the **120 is not the whole story**, and re-centering the real question: **why is he nauseated?**

**The subdural.** A **head CT** shows **[[Bilateral Subdural Hematoma|bilateral subdural hematomas]] without mass shift**. Rabih — "humbled by subdurals more than by PEs" — notes the **bilateral** pattern suggests a **spontaneous or [[Bridging Veins|retching/bridging-vein]]** mechanism (an unwitnessed severe trauma would leave external marks), and raises a **chicken-and-egg**: did **falls/violent vomiting** cause the subdurals (which then cause nausea and confusion), or did a separate cause of nausea provoke the **retching** that tore the bridging veins? He'd review the **medication list** for anticoagulants (none here), consult **neurosurgery**, expect an **interval scan** and often **anti-seizure** coverage (itself nauseating), and hold that the case "may look tucked but is an open thread." The follow-up favors the subdurals as the driver of a transient nausea/confusion syndrome: within a day he is **non-nauseated, lucid, eating, labs improved**, the subdurals **stable** on the 6-hour scan (so subtle Reza could barely find them), the wife confirming **frailty and unwitnessed falls** with **no external bruising**, and **no blood thinners**. Rabih shares a parallel humbling case — a 90-year-old billed as "**dementia/placement**" who turned out to have a **massive right subdural with mass effect** — to make the point that a contributory subdural **slips under the radar** behind a high **[[Base Rate|base-rate]]** label (≈50% of 90-year-olds have a dementia syndrome).

**The lessons.** Reza commends the two "sniffs": refusing to reflex to **endoscopy** on "coffee grounds," and refusing to blame the **encephalopathy on a sodium of 120** — both instances of **[[Correlate Clinically|correlating clinically]]** rather than closing on the first abnormality ("**correlate rawly**"). Rabih's closing, on how one gets there: you are **never fully "ready"** at the end of training, everyone (himself included — a peer-review finding of care below standard 10+ years out) makes serious mistakes, and the only path is **lifelong learning** through exactly this format — see cases, present them, reflect, and grow.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Velocity of Bleeding|Velocity]] and the "why vomit blood" logic.** Blood descends unless the stomach is **overwhelmed**; a tolerated multi-day **[[Coffee-Ground Emesis|coffee-ground]]** course is a disconnect, redirecting to a **[[Swallowed Blood|proximal/swallowed source]]** and a **separate cause of vomiting**.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Reading the urine in hyponatremia]].** A **low [[Urine Osmolality|urine osm]]** localizes the problem to **intake** (**[[Low Solute Hyponatremia|water vs solute]]**), not the kidney; **urine (Na + K) × 2** beats urine osm as a free-water proxy.
- **Acuity vs sensitivity ([[Symptomatic Hyponatremia|symptomatic hyponatremia]]).** A low threshold in the elderly makes symptoms appear "early" but does **not** raise the urgency of correction; a safe rise that fails to clear the confusion **falsifies** the sodium as the cause.
- **[[Correlate Clinically|Correlate clinically]] / resist [[Premature Closure|premature closure]].** The first abnormality (coffee grounds, Na 120) is a plausible-but-partial explanation — like consolidation-equals-pneumonia — and closing on it hides the **unexplained thread** (the nausea).
- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring]] and the base-rate trap.** A frail 90-year-old invites a "**dementia/placement**" or "**hyponatremia**" label; the **[[Subdural Hematoma|subdural]]** is the sneakiest diagnosis precisely because it hides behind those high **[[Base Rate|base rates]]**.
- **Chicken-and-egg reasoning.** **[[Bilateral Subdural Hematoma|Bilateral subdurals]]** and vomiting are causally entangled (falls/retching → **[[Bridging Veins|bridging-vein]]** tear → nausea/confusion → more retching); the direction is held open pending history.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Humility]] and process.** Capacity/"walk-to-the-door," the admission of ongoing serious errors even a decade out, and lifelong learning as the only remedy — the meta-frame of the episode.

## Teaching Discussion

- Reason from the physiology of vomited blood: because blood is not inherently nauseating, hematemesis or coffee-ground emesis means either a bleed brisk enough to overwhelm the stomach (a rare, very sick subset) or vomiting from another cause with incidental blood, and a multi-day, hemodynamically stable "coffee-ground" course argues strongly against a large hemorrhage.
- Localize a gastrointestinal bleed by velocity — most present as melena, the brisk minority as hematochezia or hematemesis — and, in a stable patient with a near-baseline hemoglobin, look for a proximal or swallowed source (lip laceration, epistaxis, esophagitis) and a separate reason for vomiting.
- Frame hyponatremia as an imbalance between free water and solute, bounded by renal free-water excretion (GFR, ADH) and solute intake, and use a low urine osmolality (better, urine sodium plus potassium times two) to localize the problem to intake — water without adequate food — rather than to the kidney.
- Resist attributing profound confusion to a sodium of 120, recognizing that a lower symptomatic threshold in the elderly does not increase the urgency of correction, and treat a safe correction that fails to improve the confusion as evidence of a second, unexplained process.
- Correlate clinically rather than closing on the first abnormality — coffee grounds do not mandate endoscopy and a sodium of 120 does not equal the cause of encephalopathy, just as a consolidation does not equal pneumonia — and keep the unexplained thread (here, the nausea) at the center of the workup.
- Keep the bilateral subdural hematoma high on the list in a frail elder with nausea, confusion, or falls, since subdurals frequently occur without witnessed trauma or external bruising, hide behind dementia or hyponatremia labels, and can be both cause and consequence of vomiting through bridging-vein injury.
- Manage a patient who wishes to leave by assessing capacity and walking with them rather than blocking the exit, using the interaction to reinforce that the patient is in charge and to deliver return precautions.
