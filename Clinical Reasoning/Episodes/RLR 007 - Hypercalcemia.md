## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 67-year-old man with recurrent strokes, contractures, advanced dementia, and diabetic [[End-Stage Renal Disease|ESRD]] on hemodialysis is admitted only to swap a leaking [[PEG Tube Dysfunction|G-tube]] — a pure disposition admission the intern wants to move past. The incidental abnormality that hijacks the case is an asymptomatic total calcium of 11.5. Rabih first refuses to localize the G-tube leak to the tube itself, then reframes the [[Hypercalcemia|hypercalcemia]] as *unexpectedly high for an ESRD patient*, who should be hypocalcemic — a delta that both amplifies the finding and raises [[Tertiary Hyperparathyroidism|tertiary hyperparathyroidism]]. A suppressed [[Parathyroid Hormone|PTH]] moves the case to the non-PTH arm; an elevated [[Bone-Specific Alkaline Phosphatase|bone alk phos]] points to a true chronic bone-involving disorder; normal [[Serum Protein Electrophoresis|SPEP]] and a benign [[Kappa–Lambda Free Light Chain Ratio|kappa–lambda ratio]] push [[Multiple Myeloma|myeloma]] down. An elevated [[PTHrP]] then drags the workup toward occult malignancy — but a negative [[Skeletal Survey|skeletal survey]] and negative [[PET-CT]] leave it unexplained. Rabih lands on [[Hypercalcemia of Immobilization|immobilization]] as the most coherent cause; Reza, unable to shake the PTHrP, sits at 70% agreement. Either way it changes nothing for a patient whose prognosis is poor, and the calcium normalizes once the dialysate bath is adjusted.

## Pearls

- **Read a lab against the baseline the patient *should* have, not against the population range.** An ESRD patient is expected to be hypocalcemic — from phosphate retention binding calcium, impaired renal [[1-Alpha-Hydroxylation|1-α-hydroxylation]] of vitamin D reducing gut absorption, and often [[Cinacalcet|cinacalcet]] suppressing PTH. A "high-normal" calcium in that host is a large delta and should raise the alarm earlier, and it puts [[Tertiary Hyperparathyroidism|tertiary hyperparathyroidism]] on the list.
- **In silent, mild, chronic hypercalcemia with a non-suppressed picture ruled out, prioritize the two I's — [[Milk-Alkali Syndrome|ingestion]] and [[Hypercalcemia of Immobilization|immobilization]].** Malignancy and granulomatous disease usually announce themselves; an asymptomatic patient "minding his own business" more often has milk-alkali (check the bicarb and the TUMS list) or immobilization hypercalcemia.
- **Interpret free light chains by the ratio, and correct the ratio for renal function.** Both light chains elevated equals inflammation; a markedly *asymmetric* elevation suggests a monoclonal process. Because [[End-Stage Renal Disease|ESRD]] clears kappa more poorly than lambda, the ratio normally skews toward kappa (up to ~3:1) — so even a mild *lambda*-predominant skew in a dialysis patient is alarming despite being under the usual 4:1 threshold.
- **[[Multiple Myeloma|Myeloma]] is purely lytic, so choose the imaging that sees lysis.** A [[Bone Scan|bone scan]] measures osteoblastic activity and can miss myeloma entirely unless a lytic lesion has fractured and is repairing; a [[Skeletal Survey|skeletal survey]] is better but still misses ~10%, and CT is the first-tier test when concern is high.
- **An elevated [[PTHrP]] is a high-morbidity lead worth chasing even in a patient who can't report symptoms — but weigh its positive predictive value against contradicting data.** PTHrP points to a solid tumor ([[Squamous Cell Carcinoma|squamous cell]] anywhere, renal, breast), yet it can be falsely elevated in renal failure, and a negative [[PET-CT]] plus a stable weight plus a chronic course argue hard against occult cancer.
- **[[Hypercalcemia of Malignancy|Hypercalcemia of malignancy]] is usually an end-stage manifestation** — median survival about six months — so a PET-negative, weight-stable, years-long hypercalcemia fits the malignancy [[Illness Script|illness script]] poorly.

## Problem Representation

A 67-year-old man with recurrent ischemic strokes, advanced dementia with four-limb contractures, and diabetic ESRD on hemodialysis, admitted for mechanical G-tube replacement, is incidentally found to have asymptomatic, chronic, mild hypercalcemia (total 11.5, ionized 1.5) that is notable precisely because ESRD should render him hypocalcemic; workup shows a suppressed PTH, elevated bone-specific alkaline phosphatase, normal SPEP with a non-monoclonal light-chain ratio, and an elevated PTHrP without any identifiable tumor on skeletal survey or PET-CT — leaving immobilization hypercalcemia as the leading, if unproven, explanation.

## Case Narrative

The intern presents what is meant to be a throwaway admission: a 67-year-old man, bedbound and non-communicative with contractures in all four extremities from recurrent ischemic strokes, diabetic [[End-Stage Renal Disease|ESRD]] on hemodialysis three times weekly, hypertension, and [[PEG Tube Dysfunction|PEG-tube]] dependence. He was sent from his nursing facility because the G-tube was leaking; GI wanted their attending present to replace it, so he was admitted overnight with the plan to swap the tube and transfer him back. The only abnormality in an otherwise unchanged exam and near-baseline labs is a total calcium of 11.5 (with sodium 130, potassium 4.3, bicarbonate 23). The intern asks to move on to the next patient.

Rabih first declines to accept the framing of the G-tube itself. Leaking fluid does not localize the problem to the tube — a loosened connection is common, but higher intragastric fluid from ileus or obstruction is a rarer, important alternative, made unlikely here by the absence of nausea, vomiting, or distention. He adds a bedside point: a patient not on a PPI is pouring pH-2 gastric acid onto peri-tube skin, which is corrosive. Then he turns to the calcium as the finding worth leaving the algorithm for. Rather than reflexively sending a [[Parathyroid Hormone|PTH]], he asks what can be inferred from the data in hand. Newly discovered [[Primary Hyperparathyroidism|primary hyperparathyroidism]] would be demographically odd in an older man (classically middle-aged women); metastatic malignancy is unlikely against an unchanged baseline; [[Granulomatous Disease|granulomatous]] causes seem remote. He reframes the real question as *what causes hypercalcemia in an otherwise asymptomatic patient*, and prioritizes the two I's — [[Milk-Alkali Syndrome|ingestion]] (milk-alkali from TUMS, though the non-elevated bicarbonate is mild evidence against it) and [[Hypercalcemia of Immobilization|immobilization]] — while still measuring PTH to begin.

Reza then supplies the variable Rabih had set aside: the ESRD. Rabih immediately recognizes its weight — these patients are almost always *hypocalcemic*, from phosphate retention binding calcium and impaired renal [[1-Alpha-Hydroxylation|1-α-hydroxylation]] of vitamin D, often compounded by [[Cinacalcet|cinacalcet]]. A patient who is hypercalcemic *despite* ESRD represents a delta from a low baseline, which both amplifies the finding and introduces [[Tertiary Hyperparathyroidism|tertiary hyperparathyroidism]] as a mechanism.

The data then arrive in deliberate increments. PTH comes back at 9.4 against a lower limit of 10–12 — suppressed, moving the case firmly to the non-PTH arm and excluding primary and tertiary hyperparathyroidism. Alkaline phosphatase is elevated at 190 and had been elevated previously, with a calcium of 10.9 months earlier; with normal liver enzymes this is a bony rather than hepatic source, later confirmed by a [[Bone-Specific Alkaline Phosphatase|bone-specific alk phos]] of 23 (ULN 15). Rabih is careful that this only reinforces a genuine, subacute-to-chronic bone-involving disorder without discriminating among causes, since hyperparathyroidism, malignancy, granulomatous disease, and immobilization can all raise it — though it argues against isolated milk-alkali. An ionized calcium of 1.5 (ULN 1.35) confirms true hypercalcemia, which matters especially because the patient cannot report symptoms; vitamin D is normal at 36.

Reza relays his nephrologist Manny's synthesis: pure immobilization causes hypercalcemia readily in children and adolescents with healthy, active bone, but in adults usually requires a *two-hit* setup — for example [[Paget Disease of Bone|Paget disease]] plus immobilization — with the elevated bone alk phos as a possible clue to underlying Paget. The team then chases [[Multiple Myeloma|myeloma]], reasoning that diabetic ESRD is often erroneously attributed and that myeloma could explain both the renal disease and the calcium. The [[Serum Protein Electrophoresis|SPEP]] is normal. Free light chains show kappa 383 and lambda 188 (both far above the ~20s ULN) with a [[Kappa–Lambda Free Light Chain Ratio|kappa–lambda ratio]] of 2. Rabih reads this as inflammation rather than malignancy — the ratio, not the absolute values, is the discriminating test, and a ratio under 3–4 is reassuring. He adds the renal refinement: ESRD clears kappa more poorly than lambda, so the ratio normally skews toward kappa; a ratio *this* modest would even make him wonder about the creatinine, and a lambda-predominant skew in an ESRD patient would be alarming even under the usual cutoff.

The intern, anticipating the workup, had also sent a [[PTHrP]], which returns elevated at 50 (ULN 20). This drags the case toward [[Hypercalcemia of Malignancy|paraneoplastic hypercalcemia]] from a solid tumor — [[Squamous Cell Carcinoma|squamous cell carcinoma]] anywhere, renal cell, or breast being the common associations. A [[Skeletal Survey|skeletal survey]] (chosen over a [[Bone Scan|bone scan]] because myeloma is purely lytic and a bone scan measures osteoblastic activity) shows no lytic lesions, no fractures, and nothing suggesting Paget. The tension is explicit: an elevated PTHrP against a patient with no reported change in baseline, no weight loss, no cough — an [[Illness Script|illness script]] that does not match hypercalcemia of malignancy, except that the patient cannot voice symptoms. Given the morbidity of missing a tumor, the team pursues it: heme/onc e-consult recommends a [[PET-CT]], which returns negative for any hypermetabolic focus. The calcium normalizes after the dialysate bath is lowered to 2.5 to pull calcium off, with the PTH rising in response.

The hosts close on genuine disagreement. Rabih walks his five-cause framework — PTH/endocrine, cancer, ingestion, granulomatous, immobilization — and eliminates each: PTH suppressed; cancer improbable when hypercalcemia (usually end-stage, median survival ~6 months) coexists with a negative PET, stable dialysis weights, and a years-long course; ingestion unlikely in a controlled facility; granulomatous disease against a negative PET; leaving immobilization, whose pretest probability he judges high in a chronically contracted, demented patient, and which he has now seen cause several similar cases. He would call it immobilization, document due diligence, and remain ready to be wrong. Reza, examining trends (PTH 115 with low calcium in early 2021, then 65, now suppressed), cannot shake the elevated PTHrP and sits at 70% agreement, wondering aloud whether a tumor simply has not surfaced on imaging yet. Both agree it does not change management for a patient whose prognosis is poor, and that the next step is a conversation with the surrogate decision-maker.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring]] and [[Premature Closure|premature closure]] on the disposition.** The admission was pre-labeled a mechanical G-tube swap, and the intern explicitly wants to "move on to the next patient." The pull is to treat the labs as noise around a solved problem; Rabih's whole first move is to refuse that frame and ask what the calcium is doing there.
- **[[Framing Effect|Framing]] by host physiology.** The same calcium of 11.5 means something different in an ESRD patient, who should be hypocalcemic. Reading the value against the *expected* baseline rather than the population range is what converts a mild abnormality into a meaningful delta — the inverse of blaming a trivially abnormal number.
- **[[Base-Rate Neglect|Base rates]] steering the differential without a single test.** Rabih down-weights primary hyperparathyroidism purely on demographics (older man vs the classic middle-aged woman) and elevates the two I's because they are the causes that fit an asymptomatic, indolent presentation — using prior probability, not new data, to reorder the list.
- **A standout lab that "takes you somewhere you weren't expecting."** The PTHrP is the case's cognitive fork: an [[Illness Script|illness script]] mismatch (no weight loss, no symptoms, chronic course) collides with the morbidity of missing a tumor. Reza and Rabih model holding a lead as *possible* while explicitly weighing its [[Positive Predictive Value|positive predictive value]] against contradicting evidence, rather than treating one abnormal value as truth.
- **Test interpretation as reasoning, not lookup.** The free light chains are read through the ratio and then re-corrected for renal clearance; the imaging choice (skeletal survey over bone scan) is driven by the *biology* of a purely lytic tumor. Both are examples of interpreting a test against the specific host rather than its generic reference range.
- **Comfort with an unresolved diagnosis.** The hosts end in explicit disagreement (Rabih favoring immobilization, Reza at 70%, unable to discharge the PTHrP) and agree the uncertainty does not change management — a model of separating diagnostic closure from the decision to act.

## Teaching Discussion

- The organizing move of the case is Rabih's five-bucket framework for hypercalcemia — PTH/endocrine, malignancy, ingestion, granulomatous, immobilization — worked as an elimination once PTH is suppressed. It pairs with the two-I heuristic (ingestion, immobilization) for the specifically *silent, mild, chronic* presentation.
- Manny's rule sharpens the immobilization hypothesis: healthy young bone can become hypercalcemic from immobilization alone, but in adults immobilization is uncommon as the sole cause, so before attributing it entirely to immobility you should investigate for a second contributing cause (e.g., [[Paget Disease of Bone|Paget]]) — which reframes the elevated bone alk phos as a possible clue rather than noise.
- The free-light-chain teaching is the most transferable technical point: elevation of both chains signals inflammation, asymmetry signals monoclonality, and in ESRD the baseline ratio is kappa-skewed — so a lambda-predominant ratio is worrisome even below the standard 4:1 cutoff.
- The stated, unresolved knowledge gaps are worth carrying forward as thoughts, not just questions: the positive predictive value of an elevated PTHrP, how a negative PET-CT should modify it, and whether paraneoplastic hypercalcemia can precede a radiographically detectable tumor (as paraneoplastic encephalitis can). The hosts do not know these answers and say so.
- The management point stands independent of the diagnosis: the calcium is dialyzable and normalizes with a lower dialysate bath, and the genuine "next step" is a goals-of-care conversation with the surrogate for a patient whose prognosis is already poor.
