> A man with poorly controlled insulin-dependent diabetes (A1C 13) presents to the VA with two days of nausea and vomiting, tachycardia, and a white count of 16,000 with a glucose of 400 but no significant acidosis or ketosis. The discussion opens with a general framework for approaching nausea (intensity and tempo as a function of time, "what's in the blood," and the reflexive workup for tachycardic, poorly-tolerating-PO patients), then follows the case through gastroparesis-versus-obstruction reasoning to a CT scan revealing a massively distended stomach — and, tucked beside the liver, a subtle finding of air in the gallbladder. The reveal is Bouveret syndrome: chronic, painless (diabetes-blunted) cholecystitis leading to a cholecystoduodenal fistula, through which a large gallstone migrated into the duodenum and lodged in the pylorus, causing gastric outlet obstruction while simultaneously allowing air to reflux into the normally sterile biliary tree.

## Pearls

- The CP Solvers "nausea thought train" approach uses the intensity and tempo of nausea as a function of time to prioritize the differential: constant, severe nausea points toward intracranial, cardiac, or obstructive causes; waxing-and-waning nausea points toward a partially obstructing process.
- When no localizing symptom accompanies nausea, think about "what's in the blood" — endocrine causes (adrenal insufficiency, thyroid disease), toxins (cannabis hyperemesis syndrome), and metabolic derangements become the next tier of the differential.
- Zofran (ondansetron) and other QT-prolonging antiemetics are safe to give as a single dose in nearly all patients regardless of baseline QTc; the real risk of torsades emerges with scheduled, around-the-clock dosing, not one-time administration.
- For initial fluid resuscitation, the choice between normal saline and lactated Ringer's does not matter for the first one to two liters in most patients; LR becomes preferable once a patient is developing or at risk for metabolic acidosis, and should be used cautiously in cirrhotic patients with elevated lactate who cannot metabolize the added lactate load.
- A negative urine ketone panel does NOT exclude ketosis — urine testing detects acetoacetate and acetone but misses beta-hydroxybutyrate, the dominant ketone body in true starvation/DKA states; a serum beta-hydroxybutyrate is the test that actually excludes significant ketosis.
- Poorly controlled diabetes should broaden, not narrow, the differential for an elevated white count and for atypical symptom presentation — autonomic neuropathy can blunt pain (as with silent MI or painless cholecystitis) and cause gastroparesis, and hemoconcentration from polyuria/vomiting can inflate a white count that would otherwise be unremarkable.
- Proximal obstructions (near the pylorus) may not produce visible abdominal distension on exam despite dramatic radiographic distension, whereas more distal small bowel obstructions are more likely to visibly distend the abdomen — this can make a real gastric outlet obstruction easy to underestimate on inspection alone.
- Pneumobilia (air within the biliary tree) in a patient without prior biliary intervention requires an anatomic explanation, because an intact sphincter of Oddi normally prevents air from refluxing backward into the bile ducts — its presence should prompt a search for a fistulous communication between the biliary tree and the GI tract.
- Bouveret syndrome (a proximal variant of gallstone ileus) occurs when a large gallstone erodes through a cholecystoduodenal fistula and lodges in the pylorus or proximal duodenum, causing gastric outlet obstruction; the combination of gastric outlet obstruction plus pneumobilia on imaging is the classic, if easy-to-miss, radiographic signature.

## Problem Representation

An insulin-dependent diabetic man with acute nausea, vomiting, tachycardia, and a mildly elevated white count and glucose, found on CT to have gastric outlet obstruction and pneumobilia, ultimately diagnosed with Bouveret syndrome from a cholecystoduodenal fistula.

## Case Narrative

The patient, a man with poorly controlled insulin-dependent diabetes (A1C 13, poor medication adherence, limited engagement with care), presented with two days of nausea and vomiting, entirely without an accompanying localizing symptom — no chest pain, no abdominal pain, no urinary symptoms, no neurologic complaints. Vitals showed tachycardia with a normal blood pressure and normal oxygen saturation, and an initial screening exam was unremarkable: clear lungs, soft and non-tender abdomen, normal neurologic exam.

The discussion of an approach to isolated nausea and vomiting began with the recommendation to look hard for any accompanying symptom, since nausea by itself is highly nonspecific. Absent that, the intensity and tempo of the nausea as a function of time becomes a useful organizing tool: severe, constant nausea raises concern for an intracranial process, an acute coronary syndrome, or a significant obstruction, while waxing-and-waning nausea suggests a partially, intermittently obstructing process. Because the patient's population (older, at cardiovascular risk) warrants a low threshold for cardiac causes, an EKG was recommended as a near-universal first step, along with basic labs (BMP, CBC, LFTs, lipase) to assess electrolyte derangements and to screen for less obvious drivers such as adrenal insufficiency, thyroid disease, or a toxin like marijuana (cannabis hyperemesis syndrome). Practical management questions were also addressed along the way: a single dose of ondansetron can be given without first checking a QTc, since the meaningful arrhythmia risk from QT-prolonging antiemetics arises with scheduled, repeated dosing rather than a one-time dose; and the choice between normal saline and lactated Ringer's does not matter for the first one to two liters of resuscitation, with LR becoming the preferred fluid mainly when a patient has or is developing a metabolic acidosis (and should be used cautiously in cirrhotic patients who cannot clear the added lactate).

With this framework in place, the patient's actual data began to complicate the picture. His white count was 16,000 and his glucose was 400 (consistent with his known A1C of 13), but his basic metabolic panel showed no anion gap and no evidence of significant ketosis on urine studies, and his renal and liver panels, lactate, and lipase were all normal. He felt somewhat better after a liter of fluid and a dose of ondansetron — enough to attempt oral intake — but vomited again about ten minutes after trying a small amount of food.

The elevated white count and glucose were interpreted cautiously: in a poorly controlled diabetic who has been vomiting and likely polyuric, some of the leukocytosis could reflect hemoconcentration rather than a true inflammatory process, but in an immunocompromised-by-hyperglycemia host, an elevated white count still deserved to be taken seriously and prompted specific questioning about cough, dysuria, flank pain, and careful skin/foot examination for occult infection. On the metabolic side, the normal urine ketones were explicitly flagged as insufficient to exclude diabetic ketoacidosis, since urine testing does not detect beta-hydroxybutyrate, the primary ketone produced in a starvation-like state; a serum beta-hydroxybutyrate was sent and was also negative, along with a normal anion gap, together making significant DKA unlikely.

With cardiac and neurologic causes essentially excluded and DKA effectively ruled out, attention turned to whether the patient's poorly controlled diabetes itself was altering how his underlying problem presented. Diabetic autonomic neuropathy, specifically gastroparesis, was raised as a leading consideration — patients with gastroparesis often lack the abdominal pain one might expect, presenting instead with bloating, early satiety, and vomiting, which fit this patient's history of taking in a small amount of food and vomiting shortly after. It was also noted that the more proximal an obstruction is (closer to the pylorus), the less likely it is to produce visible abdominal distension on exam, even though it may be dramatic on imaging — meaning a soft, non-distended-appearing abdomen on inspection does not rule out a real, high-grade obstruction.

Because the patient continued to look unwell, a CT of the abdomen and pelvis with contrast was obtained, and it showed a massively distended stomach with a "popped" appearance at the pylorus, along with a completely decompressed distal bowel — a pattern consistent with gastric outlet obstruction rather than a more distal small bowel process. An NG tube was placed and immediately decompressed roughly 600cc of gastric contents.

Radiology's formal read then added a second, easily overlooked finding: a lucency adjacent to the liver, ultimately identified as air within the gallbladder itself — pneumobilia. Reasoning through the anatomy, air reaching the gallbladder without any gallbladder wall perforation implied that air (and, by the same route, potentially a gallstone in the opposite direction) must be traveling through the cystic duct and common bile duct, or through a direct fistulous connection, since an intact sphincter of Oddi should normally prevent air from refluxing backward into the biliary tree from the duodenum.

Putting gastric outlet obstruction together with pneumobilia pointed toward Bouveret syndrome: a large gallstone, too big to pass through the cystic duct, instead erodes directly through the wall of a chronically inflamed gallbladder into an adjacent segment of bowel (typically the duodenum), creating a cholecystoduodenal fistula. Once that fistula exists, the gallstone can migrate through it into the GI tract and lodge in the pylorus or proximal duodenum, causing gastric outlet obstruction — while the same fistula allows air (and other GI contents) to reflux backward into the gallbladder and biliary tree, producing the pneumobilia. Upper endoscopy confirmed a gallstone wedged in the pylorus, with no malignant lesion seen. The mechanism was explicitly linked to the patient's diabetes: Bouveret syndrome classically arises in patients — often those with advanced, poorly controlled diabetes — whose autonomic/sensory neuropathy allows chronic, even necrotizing cholecystitis to progress silently, without the abdominal pain that would normally prompt earlier evaluation and intervention, giving the gallbladder time to erode a fistula rather than simply become acutely, painfully inflamed. The patient's right upper quadrant was non-tender even in retrospect. He was left pending cholecystectomy as an inpatient.

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| Isolated nausea/vomiting with tachycardia, no other localizing symptom | Broad differential; prioritize hypovolemia as immediate driver, screen cardiac/metabolic/endocrine causes |
| WBC 16,000, glucose 400 in a poorly controlled diabetic | Raises concern for infection but tempered by expected hemoconcentration from vomiting/polyuria |
| Normal anion gap, negative urine and serum beta-hydroxybutyrate | Effectively excludes significant DKA as the cause |
| Soft, non-distended abdomen on initial exam | Does not exclude a high-grade obstruction, especially if proximal (near pylorus) |
| CT: massively distended stomach with decompressed distal bowel | Confirms gastric outlet obstruction over a more distal small bowel process |
| Air adjacent to/within the gallbladder (pneumobilia) without gallbladder perforation | Implies an abnormal communication (fistula) between the biliary tree and GI tract |
| Gastric outlet obstruction + pneumobilia together | Classic combination for Bouveret syndrome |
| History of ~5 UTIs/year, chronic poorly controlled diabetes | Supports a chronic, indolent, pain-blunted process (consistent with diabetic autonomic/sensory neuropathy enabling silent chronic cholecystitis) |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Bouveret syndrome illness script: an elderly or diabetic patient with blunted visceral pain sensation, a history of chronic or silent cholecystitis, presenting with nausea, vomiting, and gastric outlet obstruction from a gallstone lodged in the pylorus/duodenum, with pneumobilia on imaging as the key corroborating clue pointing to a cholecystoduodenal fistula.

The case is a lesson in not letting the most common explanation (viral gastroenteritis, medication effect, or "diabetic gastroparesis" as a diagnosis of exclusion) foreclose a search for a mechanical cause when the clinical picture — tachycardia, persistent vomiting, failure to tolerate even small oral intake — does not fully fit. It is also a lesson in reading imaging comprehensively: the massively distended stomach was the dramatic, attention-grabbing finding, but the small, subtle lucency next to the liver carried the key to the actual mechanism.

## Teaching Discussion

The episode's structural teaching point is the CP Solvers nausea thought train: first exhaust the search for an accompanying localizing symptom, then use the tempo and intensity of the nausea over time to prioritize intracranial, cardiac, and obstructive causes versus a "what's in the blood" metabolic/endocrine/toxin search when no localizing symptom is found. This gives a generalizable structure to what is otherwise one of the most nonspecific presenting complaints in medicine.

A second teaching point is the limitation of urine ketone testing: because it does not detect beta-hydroxybutyrate, a negative urine ketone panel can falsely reassure a clinician that ketosis has been excluded, when in fact the dominant ketone in a true starvation or DKA-like state may still be present and undetected — a serum beta-hydroxybutyrate is required to truly exclude it.

The central diagnostic teaching point is the pairing of gastric outlet obstruction with pneumobilia as the radiographic signature of Bouveret syndrome, and the broader principle that free air or gas in an anatomic compartment where it does not belong (the biliary tree, in this case) always demands an anatomic explanation — it does not occur spontaneously, and reasoning backward through the relevant anatomy (cystic duct, common bile duct, ampulla of Vater, duodenum) is what allows a clinician to infer the existence of a fistula before it is directly visualized.
