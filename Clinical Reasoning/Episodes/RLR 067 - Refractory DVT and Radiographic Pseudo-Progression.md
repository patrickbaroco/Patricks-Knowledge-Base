## Pearls

- **Radiographic "progression" is not the same as true progression — merge imaging with a [[D-dimer|biomarker]].** Organizing/chronic clot can *look* like it's growing; a rising **D-dimer** in parallel with clot size supports real progression, while a flat D-dimer should make you re-read the study before acting. The general principle recurs everywhere: radiation necrosis vs tumor progression, osteomyelitis MRI vs healing (trust the ESR). [[Radiology Is Not Pathology|Radiology is not pathology]]. ([[RLR 067 - Refractory DVT and Radiographic Pseudo-Progression|RLR 067]])
- **Work "worsening clot" from the mundane, not the exotic.** First ask: is the clot *really* worse? Is the patient **therapeutic** (subtherapeutic dosing and DOAC pharmacology — renal/hepatic function, BMI, drug interactions; check an **[[Anti-Xa|anti-Xa]]** for adherence/exposure)? Are there drug-specific traps ([[Warfarin|warfarin]]-induced protein C/S dip, [[Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia|HIT]])? Only then reach for cancer or [[Antiphospholipid Syndrome|APLS]]. ([[RLR 067 - Refractory DVT and Radiographic Pseudo-Progression|RLR 067]])
- **Sensory symptoms don't always localize to nerve.** Keep [[Sensory Symptom Mimics|ortho and vascular mimics]] (claudication/PAD, tendon/joint problems) and an anticoagulation **retroperitoneal hematoma** on the lumbosacral plexus in mind; decouple a symptom that doesn't map cleanly onto the presumed lesion. ([[RLR 067 - Refractory DVT and Radiographic Pseudo-Progression|RLR 067]])
- **Sort [[Pulmonary Nodules|many pulmonary nodules]] (>10) into four buckets — and don't forget exposures.** Infection (septic emboli; granulomatous TB/NTM/fungi), cancer (hematogenous mets; lymphomatoid granulomatosis), autoimmune (GPA, sarcoid), and **exposure** (silica, beryllium, talc, cement, metal — inhaled, injected, or implanted). Many-nodules-but-only-mild-dyspnea should specifically raise an **inhaled/vascular exposure**. ([[RLR 067 - Refractory DVT and Radiographic Pseudo-Progression|RLR 067]])
- **The CBC is a hypercoagulability and cancer gauge.** Anemia hints at metastatic burden; MPN, MAHA, HIT, and APLS all leave [[CBC|CBC]] fingerprints (cytopenias/abnormal counts). A normal CBC and stable weight argue against the occult cancer that a scary nodule read invites. ([[RLR 067 - Refractory DVT and Radiographic Pseudo-Progression|RLR 067]])
- **Unilateral, fixed, non-resorbing clot should raise a local compression syndrome.** Why is he clotting *only there* and failing to resorb, rather than clotting everywhere? **[[May-Thurner Syndrome|May-Thurner]]** (left common iliac vein compressed by the right iliac artery) is the sneaky "true hypercoagulable" — classically young women on OCPs, but it can be an **incidental** >50% compression in the general population, so it must be tied to the clinical picture before it's treated. ([[RLR 067 - Refractory DVT and Radiographic Pseudo-Progression|RLR 067]])
- **When a consultant strongly disagrees, you're usually not on the same page about the problem.** Get on the phone, be generous (~20 minutes), and stay open to being corrected — here the radiologist re-read the *original* ultrasound and found the clot had never progressed, dissolving the case. It also prevented harm: stenting/thrombolysing an **incidental** May-Thurner. ([[RLR 067 - Refractory DVT and Radiographic Pseudo-Progression|RLR 067]])

## Problem Representation

A 75-year-old man with hypertension, prior multiple total knee and hip arthroplasties (remote, uncomplicated) and a left hip revision five years ago complicated by a chronic left superficial-femoral-vein DVT — treated with subtherapeutic apixaban (2.5 mg BID) and never fully resorbed — presents with left-leg tingling and exertional dyspnea, convinced his clot has worsened again after a recent hip revision. Ultrasound is read as clot progression and CT chest (no PE) shows numerous bilateral pulmonary micronodules read as suspicious for metastatic disease; CBC is normal, weight is stable, PET-CT shows no FDG avidity, and a CT venogram shows >50% compression of the left common iliac vein (May-Thurner-type). On expert re-review, the original ultrasound shows the clot never actually progressed (radiographic pseudo-progression), the iliac vein compression is incidental, the pulmonary nodules are non-avid and indeterminate (followed over time), and the leg numbness is a separate, non-recurring problem — a case of radiographic findings mistaken for active pathology, corrected by merging imaging with the clinical picture and biomarkers.

## Case Narrative

Reza opens by invoking Gurpreet Dhaliwal (via a Bob Centor interview): published clinical unknowns are **"sanitized"** cases, whereas RLR presents **raw, unsanitized** medicine — wrong imaging reads and all. The patient: a 75-year-old man with tingling and an "odd sensation" in the left leg and exertional dyspnea, who announces he has another blood clot. Background: remote uncomplicated arthroplasties; then a hip revision five years ago complicated by a **left superficial-femoral-vein DVT** (Rabih notes "superficial femoral" is a **deep** vein — a naming trap) treated with **subtherapeutic apixaban**; a recent revision with radiographic **clot worsening**; hematology restarted therapeutic dosing; and now he presents certain the clot is worse, with a repeat ultrasound reading as **progression** and a chest CT (no PE) showing **numerous pulmonary micronodules**.

Rabih first **decouples the symptoms**. Sensory complaints don't always map to nerve — [[Sensory Symptom Mimics|ortho (tendon/joint) and vascular (claudication/PAD) mimics]] exist, and an anticoagulation-related **retroperitoneal hematoma** could compress the lumbosacral plexus — so the numbness, being less urgent and not cleanly mapping onto the DVT, is **parked** (kept, not tucked in). He holds the dyspnea and the "worsening clot," and — because the CT already excludes edema/effusion/tumor as dyspnea causes — deems the **nodules likely contributory**, but withholds deep analysis pending characterization: like nephrologists parsing the nephron, pulmonologists parse the lung unit into **airway / vascular / lymphatic**, and the nodule differential depends on which.

Then the case's spine. Rabih teaches that **radiographic clot "progression" can be pseudo-progression** — organizing chronic clot transitioning to chronic clot fools the eye — and the corrective is to **pair imaging with a biomarker**: anticoagulation experts track the **[[D-dimer|D-dimer]]** alongside clot size, and a parallel D-dimer rise supports *real* progression. He generalizes: radiology is not pathology — radiation necrosis vs tumor progression, and osteomyelitis where ID trusts the **ESR** over the MRI. His **[[Worsening Clot|worsening-clot schema]]** leads with the mundane: is the clot truly worse; is the patient **therapeutic** (subtherapeutic dosing here, plus DOAC pharmacology — renal/hepatic function, BMI, interactions — checkable via **[[Anti-Xa|anti-Xa]]** for exposure/adherence); and are there drug-specific traps ([[Warfarin|warfarin]] protein C/S transient hypercoagulability, [[Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia|HIT]]) — *before* invoking cancer or [[Antiphospholipid Syndrome|APLS]].

The radiologist's read escalates the nodules to **"metastatic until proven otherwise."** Rabih simplifies: forget airway/vascular/lymphatic if you can't get it — first ask **few vs many** nodules. For **many (>10)** the buckets are **infection** (septic emboli — staph, fusobacterium; granulomatous — TB, NTM, fungi), **cancer** (hematogenous mets; **lymphomatoid granulomatosis**), **autoimmune** (GPA, sarcoid), and — often forgotten — **exposure** (silica, beryllium, talc, cement, metal; inhaled, injected, or implanted). The lack of inflammatory symptoms lowers infection and autoimmune; cancer looms given the "worsening clot," but **many-nodules-with-only-mild-dyspnea** should specifically prompt an inhaled/vascular exposure. He then leans on the **[[CBC|CBC]]** (normal — no anemia to suggest metastatic burden; no MPN/MAHA/HIT/APLS cytopenia) and **stable weight** to argue against occult cancer, and raises the sneaky true cause: a **local compression syndrome**, asking the key question — *why is he clotting only there and failing to resorb, not clotting everywhere?* — pointing to **[[May-Thurner Syndrome|iliac-vein compression]]** (classically young women on OCPs, but perhaps unmasked here by cumulative surgical triggers). The team, meanwhile, had gone straight to a **PET-CT** — which shows **no FDG avidity**; hematology recommends age-appropriate screening and a DOAC switch. The patient, strikingly health-literate ("I'm more likely to die *with* my cancer than *from* it"), declines colonoscopy and grows frustrated that no cause has been found, threatening to leave.

Reza, prompted by the patient's conviction, orders a **CT venogram** for May-Thurner; the first study captures only the (clear) arterial phase, but the repeat shows **>50% compression of the left common iliac vein** by the right common iliac artery — with the radiologist thoughtfully noting it **can be a normal variant**, and flagging the prior femur fracture/ORIF/arthroplasty as predisposing. Rabih frames it correctly: **radiology does not equal pathology** — in most people this is incidental — but in a patient with genuinely worsening thrombosis it could be significant, warranting **IR for thrombolysis and venous stenting**. IR, however, declines and signs off. Rabih's counsel on **consultant disagreement**: a markedly different opinion usually means you're **not on the same page about the problem**, so get on the phone, be generous (~20 minutes), and stay open to being corrected. On the call, the same radiologist reveals two things: the clot is **distal** to the compression (not the proximal location expected if compression-related), and — decisively — on re-reading the **original ultrasound**, the **clot had never progressed**. It was **pseudo-progression** all along, vindicating Rabih's very first instinct; the D-dimer was never obtained (the acknowledged miss). The patient could safely resume apixaban.

The case resolves as an **absence of scary disease**: the numbness never recurred (a separate problem, perhaps a slipped disc), the clot was never worsening, the **May-Thurner is incidental**, and the **PET-negative nodules** are indeterminate and simply followed over time. The patient, hospitalized five days with a PET, multiple CTs, and no clear answer, leaves frustrated and distrustful. In the [[Cognitive Autopsy|autopsy]], Reza names the fix: get the **D-dimer at time zero** and **re-read the ultrasound with the radiologist**, which would have short-circuited the whole odyssey — and both close on the core lesson that **radiology must be merged with the clinical picture and a biomarker**, and that vigilance prevented real harm (thrombolysing/stenting an incidental finding), echoing the analogy that endocarditis vegetations, like treated osteomyelitis, often persist sterile on imaging so **biomarkers, not pictures alone, adjudicate**.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Data point | Hypothesis raised/adjusted | Direction of update |
|---|---|---|
| Left-leg tingling with a "worsening" DVT | DVT-caused numbness | Decoupled (ortho/vascular/hematoma mimics) |
| Ultrasound read as clot progression | True clot progression | Held, but pseudo-progression suspected |
| Subtherapeutic apixaban history | Inadequate anticoagulation exposure | Raised (most common cause) |
| CT chest: numerous micronodules, no PE | Metastatic cancer | Raised by read; tempered by context |
| Normal CBC, stable weight | Occult metastatic cancer | Lowered |
| PET-CT: no FDG avidity | Active malignancy | Lowered (indeterminate nodules) |
| Only mild dyspnea despite many nodules | Inhaled/vascular exposure (silica/talc/etc.) | Raised (often forgotten) |
| Unilateral, non-resorbing left clot | Local compression (May-Thurner) | Raised |
| CT venogram: >50% left common iliac vein compression | May-Thurner as the cause | Raised, then reframed as possibly incidental |
| Clot distal to compression; original US re-read flat | True clot progression / May-Thurner causation | Lowered → pseudo-progression, incidental |
| Numbness never recurred | Neurologic cause needing workup | Lowered (separate, benign) |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Radiology Is Not Pathology|Radiology is not pathology]].** The organizing thesis: a "progressing" clot, "metastatic" nodules, and a ">50% compression" are all *radiographic* statements that must be merged with symptoms and biomarkers before they become diagnoses — and re-reading the primary image (not the report) broke the case.
- **[[Law of Proportionality|Proportionality]] and the biomarker check.** A flat [[D-dimer|D-dimer]] against a "worse" clot, and only mild dyspnea against a florid nodule burden, are disproportions that should trigger re-reading rather than escalation.
- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|Mundane-first schemas]].** The worsening-clot schema (real? therapeutic? drug complication?) and the many-nodule schema (infection/cancer/autoimmune/exposure) keep the differential ordered and resist jumping to cancer/APLS.
- **[[Base Rate|Base rate and incidental findings]].** The commonest cause of worsening clot is inadequate anticoagulation, and >50% iliac compression is frequently an **incidental** normal variant — both temper the exotic reads.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Humility, consultation, and de-escalation]].** Knowing when to phone an expert (and that a consultant's disagreement signals a shared-understanding gap), and the restraint *not* to stent an incidental finding, are the case's quiet wins — vigilance as harm-avoidance.
- **Decoupling and parking a symptom.** Separating the numbness (which didn't map to the clot) prevented a false unifier and, correctly, it proved unrelated and self-limited.

## Teaching Discussion

- Treat radiographic progression as a hypothesis, not a fact: organizing clot can mimic growth, so pair imaging with a biomarker (D-dimer for clot, ESR for osteomyelitis) and re-read the primary study before acting.
- Work worsening clot from the mundane outward — is it really worse, is anticoagulation therapeutic (dosing, DOAC pharmacology, anti-Xa), and are there drug-specific complications (warfarin protein C/S, HIT) — before invoking cancer or antiphospholipid syndrome.
- Decouple symptoms that don't map cleanly onto the presumed lesion; sensory complaints have ortho and vascular mimics, and anticoagulation can cause a plexus-compressing hematoma.
- Sort many pulmonary nodules into infection, cancer, autoimmune, and exposure, and remember that a heavy nodule burden with mild symptoms should raise inhaled or vascular exposures.
- Use the CBC and weight trend as cancer and hypercoagulability gauges, and interpret a scary radiology read against them and against a PET.
- Consider local compression syndromes (May-Thurner) for unilateral, non-resorbing clot, but recognize >50% iliac compression is often an incidental variant that must be tied to the clinical picture before treatment.
- When a consultant disagrees strongly, assume a shared-understanding gap: call, spend the time, and stay open to being corrected — it can dissolve the case and prevent treating an incidental finding.
- Recognize the human cost of diagnostic uncertainty; restraint (not stenting an incidental May-Thurner) is sometimes the highest-value action.
