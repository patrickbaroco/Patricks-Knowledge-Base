## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A soft-spoken 54-year-old man has two weeks of progressive, isolated **left** [[Unilateral Lower Extremity Edema|lower-extremity swelling]] — 2+ pitting to the mid-shin, tense, no skin changes, right leg pristine, normal vitals, no systemic symptoms. Reza fixes the [[Illness Tempo|tempo]] (subacute) and works the [[Anatomical Approach|anatomy]]: a modest left-over-right predominance is *expected* because the left iliac vein runs under the right iliac artery ([[May-Thurner Syndrome|May-Thurner]] compression), but overt asymmetry means a **local** process, not [[Bilateral Lower Extremity Edema|systemic]] volume overload. By [[Base Rate|base rate]] the first move is a [[Deep Vein Thrombosis|DVT]] ultrasound (with [[Cellulitis|cellulitis]] a distant second, argued down by two weeks without redness or fever). The ultrasound is **negative** and he is discharged as [[Venous Stasis|venous stasis]] — but Reza flags this as a [[Search Satisficing|premature]] landing: a negative study must be weighed against pretest probability and study quality, and *unilateral* venous stasis is itself a red flag ("why would the venous valves fail in only one leg?"). He would pursue **CT of the abdomen/pelvis/leg** for a compressing mass. Months later the man returns with edema to the mid-thigh and new **pain on weight-bearing**; the only abnormal lab is an **isolated [[Alkaline Phosphatase|alkaline phosphatase]] of 376**. Reza reframes to atraumatic [[Vascular Insufficiency|vascular insufficiency]] (venous over arterial — it worsens on standing; [[Compartment Syndrome|compartment]] and neurogenic causes argued down by tempo) and splits the ALP by origin — a [[GGT|GGT]] to separate **bone from liver** — reasoning toward osteoblastic bone disease. CT shows **osteosclerotic and mixed lytic-sclerotic lesions in femur and pelvis with a pathologic hip fracture**; a [[Return to the Bedside|repeat exam]] finds a previously-missed **2–3 cm left inguinal lymph node**. Reza runs his [[Lytic Bone Lesion|bone-lesion]] schema (age >40 / cancer history, imaging, symptomatic-with-fracture, labs) to **malignancy**, then focal GU-region adenopathy + osteoblastic mets → **[[Prostate Cancer|prostate cancer]]**. The **[[PSA|PSA is 1500]]**; he starts androgen-deprivation therapy and is transferred for fracture repair, the edema most likely **[[Lymphedema|lymphedema]]** from nodal obstruction. The teaching spine: **bilateral edema reasons systemic→local, but unilateral edema reasons local→systemic — and venous stasis for a *unilateral* leg must be a diagnosis of exclusion reached only after considering nodes and central venous obstruction**.

## Pearls

- **Unilateral and bilateral leg edema are reasoned in *opposite directions*.** [[Bilateral Lower Extremity Edema|Bilateral edema]] is worked systemic-first (heart, liver, kidney, meds, endocrine) and often lands on [[Venous Stasis|venous stasis]] as the local endpoint; [[Unilateral Lower Extremity Edema|unilateral edema]] starts *local* ([[Deep Vein Thrombosis|DVT]], [[Cellulitis|cellulitis]], stasis) and, when it won't resolve, must **radiate proximally to the retroperitoneum and pelvis** — nodes and central venous compression. Calling unilateral swelling "venous stasis" is only legitimate *after* that systemic pass.
- **A negative DVT ultrasound is only as good as the pretest probability and the study.** When [[Deep Vein Thrombosis|DVT]] risk is high, a negative scan doesn't close the question: confirm the study visualized all segments, talk to radiology, and consider repeating it ([[Test Interpretation|test interpretation]]). This patient had three or four negative scans — each true, none of them the answer.
- **Unilateral venous stasis is a contradiction that should trigger a CT.** [[Venous Stasis|Venous stasis]] is the commonest cause of leg edema over 50, but its pathophysiology (age-degraded venous valves) is *bilateral* — so a tense, 2+, one-legged edema is a red flag for a mass compressing the [[May-Thurner Syndrome|iliac vein]] or [[Lymphedema|lymphatics]]. Red flags that earn imaging: an IVC filter, prior vascular surgery, pelvic fullness, or [[Inguinal Lymphadenopathy|lymphadenopathy]].
- **Anatomy predicts a *little* left-sided edema — but not this much.** The left common iliac vein passes beneath the right common iliac artery, so almost any volume state swells the left leg slightly more ([[May-Thurner Syndrome|May-Thurner]] compression); when the asymmetry is obvious to the eye, stop attributing it to a systemic cause and take an [[Anatomical Approach|anatomical arrow]] through the leg — skin, subcutaneous space, muscle, fascia, bone.
- **An isolated alkaline phosphatase forces the bone-versus-liver fork — and a [[GGT|GGT]] settles it.** With normal bilirubin and transaminases, an [[Alkaline Phosphatase|ALP]] of 376 is either infiltrative liver disease or bone turnover; a GGT rising in proportion means liver, a normal GGT means **bone** — osteoblast activity from a healing fracture or **osteoblastic metastasis** (classically [[Prostate Cancer|prostate cancer]]).
- **New pain in a chronically swollen leg reframes the whole problem — and re-examining finds what was missed.** Pain on weight-bearing shifts "big heavy leg" to [[Vascular Insufficiency|vascular insufficiency]] (venous origin worsens on standing, arterial improves), and going [[Return to the Bedside|back to the bedside]] uncovered the [[Inguinal Lymphadenopathy|inguinal node]] that had been there all along — the exam finding that could have caught this before the pathologic fracture.

## Problem Representation

A 54-year-old man with no known history presents with two weeks of progressive, isolated left lower-extremity edema — 2+ pitting to the mid-shin, tense, without skin changes, erythema, fever, or systemic symptoms, and a normal contralateral leg and vitals. The asymmetry marks a local rather than systemic process; a DVT ultrasound is negative and routine labs (CBC, renal panel, LFTs) are normal, and he is discharged as venous stasis. Over the next two to three months the edema ascends to the mid-thigh and develops pain on weight-bearing, with a normal-colored, very tense leg and a new isolated alkaline phosphatase elevation to 376 (normal bilirubin and transaminases). Reframed as atraumatic vascular insufficiency of probable venous origin with a bone-versus-liver ALP question, CT reveals osteosclerotic and mixed lytic-sclerotic lesions in the femur and pelvis with a pathologic hip fracture, and a repeat examination discovers a previously missed 2–3 cm left inguinal lymph node. A bone-lesion schema (age, imaging, symptomatic pathologic fracture, labs) points to malignancy; focal GU-region adenopathy with osteoblastic metastases indicates prostate cancer, confirmed by a PSA of 1500 — with the unilateral edema most likely lymphedema from nodal/pelvic obstruction. He is started on androgen-deprivation therapy and transferred for complex orthopedic repair.

## Case Narrative

Rabih presents, with a storyteller's disclosure: he met the patient partway through the journey but will narrate as if present throughout. A 54-year-old man — soft-spoken, gentle, and worried — has, out of the blue, developed progressively worsening swelling of the **left** leg. He first shrugged it off, but it grew bad enough that he could barely walk and struggled to put on his shoes. In the ED his vitals are normal, he has no other complaints and no systemic symptoms, and the only finding is **2+ pitting edema to the mid-shin, tense, without skin changes**. Reza's first question is the one Rabih later calls the hinge of the case: *symmetric, or left greater than right?* The answer — **left only, the right pristine** — is where the reasoning begins.

Reza sets the [[Illness Tempo|tempo]] first (hyperacute, acute, subacute, or chronic); at two weeks this is acute-to-subacute. The problem is left [[Unilateral Lower Extremity Edema|lower-extremity swelling]] in a middle-aged man without background history yet. He offers an anatomy pearl: almost *any* cause of volume overload should swell the **left** leg slightly more than the right, because the left common iliac vein passes beneath the right common iliac artery ([[May-Thurner Syndrome|May-Thurner]] physiology) and is mildly compressed — but if the asymmetry is *obvious*, one should **not** attribute it to a bilateral, systemic cause ([[Bilateral Lower Extremity Edema|heart, liver, kidney, medication, or venous stasis]]). So this is unilateral, asymmetric edema, and by [[Base Rate|base rate]] the number-one consideration is a **clot — a [[Deep Vein Thrombosis|DVT]]** — meaning the reflex is a lower-extremity ultrasound. [[Cellulitis|Cellulitis]] is the other common asymmetric cause, but without a systemic inflammatory response and with two weeks of non-improvement it fits poorly. He then models the [[Anatomical Approach|analytical alternative]] to a pure base-rate reflex: take an arrow through the leg — skin and soft tissue, subcutaneous space, muscle, fascia, bone. His plan: a lower-extremity ultrasound, and — absent redness or fever — he might not even draw labs first.

Rabih praises the pairing of base-rate and [[Analogical Reasoning|analogical]] reasoning, then springs the first turn: the **DVT ultrasound is negative**, the CBC, renal panel, and LFTs are normal, and the man is **discharged as [[Venous Stasis|venous stasis]]**. Reza's response is a lesson in [[Test Interpretation|test interpretation]] and in resisting [[Search Satisficing|a premature landing]]. A negative ultrasound is not automatically a true negative: one must weigh the **pretest probability**, confirm the **study quality**, and check with radiology whether every venous segment was actually visualized. Venous stasis *is* the most common cause of leg edema over 50 (aging degrades the venous valves), and an atypical presentation of a common disease is always conceivable — but here he flags a **red flag**: the pathophysiology of venous stasis is *bilateral*, so why would the valves fail in only the left leg? A tense 2+ edema with that stark left-right contrast makes the label suspect. If the edema is as impressive as described, he would pursue a **CT of the abdomen, pelvis, and left leg** to look for a mass compressing the venous or lymphatic system.

The ultrasound quality, Rabih confirms, was superb. Two to three months later the man returns — the edema now ascends to the **mid-thigh**, and for the first time there is **pain on weight-bearing**: it hurts to stand, where before the leg was merely heavy. The exam shows very impressive, tense edema; the CBC and renal panel are normal, and the liver enzymes are normal **except an alkaline phosphatase of 376**. Reza asks about color (normal) and reframes. New pain in a swollen extremity raises **trauma/fracture** first — but fractures usually present quickly and with a story of injury, so he frames this as *atraumatic*, progressive, painful, tense left-leg edema. Removing fracture, the worry becomes **[[Vascular Insufficiency|vascular insufficiency]]**: at the capillary level the tissue isn't perfusing. He walks the categories. **Arterial** disease (claudication, embolus) doesn't fit — no arterial clues, and it wouldn't produce this swelling illness script. **Venous** insufficiency does: a chronic clot (or a compressing mass on the left iliac vein) can raise venous pressure so high that arterial inflow can't oxygenate the tissue, producing pain — and (the reason he asked about color) severe cases show **[[Phlegmasia Cerulea Dolens|phlegmasia cerulea dolens]]** (blue) or **[[Phlegmasia Alba Dolens|alba dolens]]** (white). A tell: venous-origin pain **worsens on standing** (blood pools) while arterial improves (gravity assists inflow). He rules out a **neurogenic** cause (wouldn't explain the swelling) and weighs **[[Compartment Syndrome|compartment syndrome]]** (the leg is very tense) but argues the chronic tempo against it — an acute compartment wouldn't smolder for months; a slow one would adapt. Plan: a **CK**, and a **CT** for vessel compression or an occult fracture — invoking, by [[Analogical Reasoning|analogy]], a diabetic patient mislabeled DVT then cellulitis who ultimately had [[Charcot Arthropathy|Charcot arthropathy]] from unrecognized fractures.

He then dissects the **[[Alkaline Phosphatase|ALP]]**. With normal bilirubin, AST, and ALT there are no liver clues; an isolated ALP is either **liver** (an isolated ALP of hepatic origin usually signals an infiltrative process) or **bone**. He would send a **[[GGT|GGT]]**: elevated in proportion → liver; normal → bone. Bone ALP is an **osteoblast** enzyme, so it rises with **osteoblastic metastatic disease** (classically [[Prostate Cancer|prostate cancer]]) or with a **healing fracture** (osteoblasts rebuilding). The GGT thus frames the edema as liver infiltration versus bone metastasis/fracture.

Rabih, admiring how far ahead the discussion has run, adds an extra "aliquot." The **CT** shows **multiple osteosclerotic and mixed lytic-sclerotic lesions in the femur with a pathologic fracture of the hip**, and similar mixed lesions in the **pelvis**. Surprised, he goes **[[Return to the Bedside|back to examine the patient]]** and now feels a large, previously-missed **left inguinal lymph node, 2–3 cm**, with nothing on the right.

Reza pulls up his whiteboard **[[Lytic Bone Lesion|bone-lesion]] schema** (with a plug for the reflection videos he and Rabih make after each case). Four questions organize it: (1) **epidemiology** — age >40 and/or a cancer history, because widespread bone lesions in that group are usually malignant; (2) **imaging characteristics** — the radiologist's read and differential; (3) **symptomatic?** — here a pathologic fracture; (4) **labs** — the anticipated ALP. His main concern is **malignancy** over infection or endocrine disease: the localized, extremity-associated adenopathy argues against a metabolic cause like [[Hyperparathyroidism|hyperparathyroidism]], and the absence of fever, leukocytosis, or travel argues against infection. Malignancy then splits liquid versus solid; the **focal** (not diffuse) adenopathy plus age favor a **solid** cancer (though [[Lymphoma|lymphoma]] can go to bone), and focal GU-region nodes point to a **GU malignancy — [[Prostate Cancer|prostate cancer]]**, notorious for osteoblastic bone metastasis. Rabih, laughing that he can't hold it any longer, reveals the **[[PSA|PSA of 1500]]**: the man is started on **[[Androgen Deprivation Therapy|androgen-deprivation therapy]]** and transferred to a specialized center for complex orthopedic repair of the fragility fracture. The edema, they agree, is most plausibly **[[Lymphedema|lymphedema]]** from malignant nodal/pelvic obstruction rather than the fracture.

The reflection is the episode's gift. Rabih reframes the whole problem by *direction of reasoning*. **[[Bilateral Lower Extremity Edema|Bilateral]] leg edema** is first analyzed as a **systemic** disease (heart, liver, kidney, meds, endocrine) and, after a decent evaluation, often *arrives* at the local diagnosis of [[Venous Stasis|venous stasis]] — the journey runs systemic → local. **[[Unilateral Lower Extremity Edema|Unilateral]] leg edema** is the exact opposite: it starts **local** (venous stasis, DVT, cellulitis) and, when the leg won't declare itself, must **radiate proximally** to the retroperitoneum and pelvis where nodes and venous compression live — local → systemic. His hard-won rule: **rendering "venous stasis" for a unilateral leg requires first excluding a systemic/central cause**, exactly as one would for bilateral edema. The chance to catch it early was the **lymph-node exam**; given how prominent the edema was, palpable pathological nodes were likely present at the first visit. Red flags that mandate a CT for unilateral edema: an **IVC filter**, prior **vascular surgery**, **pelvic fullness**, or **[[Inguinal Lymphadenopathy|lymphadenopathy]]**.

Reza closes on the meta-lesson of *building schemas in real time and then codifying them* — he will create a "unilateral edema" page in his digital notebook, linked to this very case, so the on-the-fly reasoning is anchored to an encounter rather than read at random. Rabih notes that Reza reconstructed, live, the schema Rabih had labored over for two days — and that the reflection is what moves an on-the-fly approach into durable long-term memory, so it isn't lost a month later. ### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Direction of reasoning: unilateral vs bilateral edema.** The episode's spine is that [[Bilateral Lower Extremity Edema|bilateral]] edema is worked *systemic → local* (and often ends at [[Venous Stasis|venous stasis]]), while [[Unilateral Lower Extremity Edema|unilateral]] edema is worked *local → systemic* — so "venous stasis" for one leg is a **diagnosis of exclusion** that requires a proximal (nodal, central-venous) pass first.
- **[[Anatomical Approach|Anatomical approach]] as the analytical backup to base rate.** When the [[Base Rate|base-rate]] reflex (clot? infection?) doesn't pan out, an arrow through the leg (skin → subcutaneous → muscle → fascia → bone) keeps the differential complete — and the [[May-Thurner Syndrome|May-Thurner]] anatomy calibrates how much left-sided asymmetry is "allowed" before it becomes pathologic.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Test interpretation]] of a negative study.** A negative DVT ultrasound is conditioned on pretest probability and study quality; three or four true-negative scans never displaced the need to look proximally. The same discipline splits an isolated **[[Alkaline Phosphatase|ALP]]** by origin with a [[GGT|GGT]] (liver vs osteoblastic bone).
- **[[Search Satisficing|Search satisficing]] / [[Premature Closure|premature closure]] / [[Diagnostic Momentum|momentum]].** The first disposition — negative clot study → "venous stasis" → discharge — is the failure mode the case is built to expose; the label persisted across visits despite the internal contradiction of *unilateral* stasis.
- **[[Return to the Bedside|Return to the bedside]].** The malignant inguinal node was found only on *re-examination* after the CT; the reflection's honest question — how to detect this before its devastation — answers itself as "the lymph-node exam."
- **[[Vascular Insufficiency|Vascular-insufficiency framework]].** New pain reorganizes the problem into arterial vs venous vs neurogenic vs compartment, discriminated by tempo and by the standing test (venous worsens, arterial improves) — with [[Phlegmasia Cerulea Dolens|phlegmasia]] as the severe venous endpoint.
- **[[Lytic Bone Lesion|Bone-lesion schema]] and [[Analogical Reasoning|schema-building on the fly]].** Four questions (epidemiology, imaging, symptomatic/fracture, labs) route widespread mixed lesions to malignancy and, via osteoblastic pattern + GU nodes, to [[Prostate Cancer|prostate cancer]]; Reza reconstructs the unilateral-edema schema live by analogy, and the *reflection/codification* step (linking the schema to this case) is what makes it durable.

## Teaching Discussion

- Ask "one leg or two?" first, and let the answer set the direction of the workup: bilateral edema is analyzed systemic-first and often lands on venous stasis, while unilateral edema is analyzed local-first and must then radiate to the pelvis and retroperitoneum — nodes and central venous obstruction — before venous stasis is accepted.
- Treat "venous stasis" as a diagnosis of exclusion for a unilateral leg. Its pathophysiology is bilateral valve degeneration, so a tense, markedly asymmetric edema is a red flag; get cross-sectional imaging when there is an IVC filter, prior vascular surgery, pelvic fullness, or lymphadenopathy.
- Don't over-trust a negative DVT ultrasound. Weigh pretest probability, confirm study quality, ask whether all venous segments were visualized, and repeat or extend imaging when suspicion stays high — multiple true-negative scans can still miss the real, more proximal problem.
- Use the May-Thurner anatomy to calibrate asymmetry: a small left-over-right predominance is physiologic (left iliac vein under right iliac artery), but obvious asymmetry should not be blamed on a systemic cause — take an anatomical arrow through skin, subcutaneous tissue, muscle, fascia, and bone.
- Let new pain in a chronically swollen leg reframe the problem to vascular insufficiency: distinguish venous (worsens on standing, can cause phlegmasia) from arterial (improves on standing), and argue compartment syndrome and neurogenic pain up or down by tempo.
- Split an isolated alkaline phosphatase by origin. With normal bilirubin and transaminases, send a GGT: proportional elevation means liver (often infiltrative), a normal GGT means bone — osteoblast activity from a healing fracture or an osteoblastic metastasis such as prostate cancer.
- Work widespread bone lesions with a fixed schema — epidemiology (age >40, cancer history), imaging characteristics (partner with the radiologist), symptomatic status (pathologic fracture), and labs — and remember that osteoblastic/sclerotic metastases with focal GU-region adenopathy point to prostate cancer; confirm with PSA.
- Go back to the bedside when imaging surprises you. The malignant inguinal node was palpable but missed; re-examination is often the cheapest test that reframes the case, and reflecting/codifying an on-the-fly schema afterward is what makes the reasoning durable.
