> *(A tribute episode — dedicated to **Rafael "Rafa" Medina**, a beloved CP Solvers team member who died far too young; his line "**one life, so many dreams**" gives the episode its name. It also **resolves the cliffhanger from RLR 054's Case 2** — the woman who deteriorated after jaw surgery — which RLR 055 did not.)* A **middle-aged woman** with **depression** (on **sertraline**) undergoes **elective TMJ repair** with her **jaw wired shut**, receiving **propofol as the sole primary sedative**, and shortly after emerges **agitated, confused, rigid, hyperthermic, tachycardic, and hypertensive** — transferred to the ED still intubated. Labs: **neutrophilic leukocytosis (14)**, **normal renal/hepatic function**, **CK 3,500**. Reza refuses to make "time-point-zero" anything but **what happened in the OR**, reframes the picture as a **hyperactive skeletal-and-sympathetic state**, and — pointedly saying **"elevated temperature," not "fever"** — builds the classic **rigidity-plus-hyperthermia** differential: **[[neuroleptic malignant syndrome|NMS]]** (dopamine-deficient), **[[serotonin syndrome]]** (serotonin excess), **[[malignant hyperthermia]]** (RYR1/genetic, volatile anesthetics/succinylcholine), plus **malignant catatonia** and even **[[stiff person syndrome]]** (GABA-deficient). She is **Ativan-responsive** (which doesn't discriminate), extubated, then **re-agitates → aspirates → ICU on high-flow**. The workup **excludes the usual suspects**: **UTox negative, TSH normal**, pharmacy confirms **only sertraline** — yet **no hyperreflexia/clonus** (against serotonin syndrome), **no "screaming" antipsychotic** (against NMS), and **onset well after the OR with no volatile anesthetic** (against MH). Neurology and psychiatry are **"befuddled" — the syndrome is there but no trigger fits**, and they refuse to settle for "atypical NMS." A **literature search** yields the answer: **[[propofol-induced neuroexcitatory syndrome]]** — a rare, under-recognized **NMS/serotonin-like reaction to propofol itself** (distinct from **propofol infusion syndrome**, a mitochondrial toxicity). With **withdrawal of propofol and benzodiazepines**, she recovers over 2–3 days, is extubated, **CK normalizes**, **propofol is added to her allergy list**, and she later **tolerates sertraline without recurrence**. The teaching spine: **sequence of events is time-zero**; **you can treat a hyperactive-muscle syndrome without a precise diagnosis** (cool, benzodiazepines, ± dantrolene, protect the kidneys from rhabdomyolysis); and — the case's soul — **pursue what doesn't fit** rather than accept a comfortable "atypical" label, because *an atypical presentation of something you know may be something you don't know that mimics it*.

**Pearls**

- **Sequence of events *is* time-point-zero.** When a patient deteriorates after a procedure, clinical reasoning starts **in the operating room**, not at ED arrival. Resist the urge to treat the OR course as irrelevant background — the **exposure** there is usually the key.
- **Say "elevated temperature," not "fever."** Naming it "fever" prematurely **anchors on infection**. For a **rigid, hyperthermic** post-op patient, frame the problem as a **hyperactive skeletal-and-sympathetic state** and reason toward the **drug/neuromuscular** causes first.
- **The rigidity-plus-hyperthermia differential, by biochemistry:** **dopamine-deficient → [[neuroleptic malignant syndrome|NMS]]**; **serotonin excess → [[serotonin syndrome]]**; **anticholinergic toxidrome**; **GABA-deficient → [[stiff person syndrome]]/tetanus**; and **[[malignant hyperthermia]]** (RYR1-mediated, triggered by **volatile anesthetics/succinylcholine**, often with **masseter spasm**). Add **malignant catatonia**, which overlaps NMS.
- **Distinguish NMS from serotonin syndrome by *reflexes* and *tempo*, not benzo response.** **Serotonin syndrome**: **abrupt** onset/offset, **hyperreflexia, clonus, myoclonus**. **NMS**: **slower** onset/offset, **"lead-pipe" rigidity with hyporeflexia**. **Malignant hyperthermia** strikes **intra-operatively** with a volatile/succinylcholine trigger. **Benzodiazepine responsiveness doesn't discriminate** — all of them improve somewhat.
- **You can manage a hyperactive-muscle syndrome without pinning the diagnosis.** Regardless of label: **stop the offending agent**, **cool the patient**, **benzodiazepines** (± **dantrolene** for severe rigidity/hyperthermia), and **protect the kidneys** — trend **CK, K, Ca**, and give **fluids** for evolving **rhabdomyolysis** (a CK of 3,500 isn't yet dangerous, but the *next* one could be 20,000 with myoglobinuric AKI).
- **[[Propofol-induced neuroexcitatory syndrome]] is a real, rare entity.** A small fraction of patients develop an **NMS/serotonin-like syndrome from propofol itself**, suspected precisely **because the usual triggers are absent**. It is **distinct from propofol infusion syndrome** (a mitochondrial toxicity, classically in children on prolonged high-dose infusions). Management is **withdrawal + supportive care**; **label propofol an allergy** to prevent a recurrence on re-exposure.
- **Pursue what doesn't fit.** The experts recognized the *syndrome* but honestly flagged the **missing trigger** and refused to accept "atypical serotonin syndrome/NMS." That discipline mattered: a wrong "atypical" label would have let the patient get **propofol again** and relapse. **An atypical presentation of something you know may actually be something you don't know that mimics it.**
- **Therapy can be the diagnostic test.** Improvement upon **withdrawing propofol** (and weaning benzodiazepines) both **treated** and **confirmed** the drug-induced etiology.

**Problem Representation**
A middle-aged woman on sertraline who developed agitation, rigidity, hyperthermia, autonomic instability, and CK elevation immediately after propofol-based anesthesia for TMJ surgery, in whom NMS, serotonin syndrome, and malignant hyperthermia were each excluded by the absence of a fitting trigger — diagnosed with propofol-induced neuroexcitatory syndrome and resolving with drug withdrawal and supportive care.

## Case Narrative

The episode opens not with a case but with grief: Rabih and Reza **dedicate it to Rafael "Rafa" Medina**, a young, charismatic CP Solvers team member who had passed away suddenly. Rabih recalls Rafa's signature line — *"I only have one life to live, and I have so many dreams"* — and his obsessive love of medicine, and both hosts frame continuing to teach as the tribute Rafa would have wanted (a GoFundMe for his family is mentioned). Then the palindrome intro, and the case — which **picks up RLR 054's Case 2**, the patient Rabih had "signed out" from the ED.

Rabih re-presents: a **middle-aged woman**, little history yet, transferred **from the OR to the ED still intubated** on minimal sedation after **elective jaw (TMJ) surgery**, now **very agitated, febrile, tachycardic, hypertensive, and rigid**. Labs show a **mild neutrophilic leukocytosis (14)**, **normal creatinine/renal function**, **normal liver enzymes**, and a **CK of 3,500**. Reza's first principle: **you cannot remove the OR from the story** — *"time-point-zero should be what happened in that operating room."* He reframes her state as **hyperactive** in two systems — **skeletal muscle tone** and **sympathetic tone** — and, drawing on his **pre-operative risk-stratification** habit of scrutinizing the med list (because home medications shape anesthetic choice and can precipitate **NMS** or **QT-related arrhythmia**), sets up the syndrome. Deliberately, he says **"hyperthermic," not "febrile,"** to avoid **premature closure on infection**. The **contending diagnoses**: **NMS**, **malignant hyperthermia** (rare, genetic susceptibility), and **serotonin syndrome** — with **management** the same regardless: **calm the hyperactive state** with **benzodiazepines ± dantrolene**, and prevent the temperature and CK from climbing into **myoglobinuric kidney injury**. He offers the **biochemical scaffold**: NMS = a **dopamine-deficient** state → dysautonomia + Parkinsonian rigidity; serotonin/MH = other biochemical routes to the same **hyperactive** picture.

The next "aliquot": she was **Ativan-responsive** (2 mg calmed her), got **extubated** by anesthesia, then **~10 minutes later re-agitated**, pulled at lines, and had a **severe aspiration event → hypoxemia → ICU on high-flow** (not re-intubated — the acute management covered in the prior episodes). The **background** now fills in: **depression** on **sertraline** (an SSRI), possible **alcohol use**, **TMJ dysfunction** driving the elective repair, and — following the med trail — **propofol as the sole primary peri-procedural sedative** (no other anesthetic, no vasopressors). Reza notes that **therapy can serve as the diagnostic test**, but **benzo-responsiveness doesn't prioritize** among NMS, serotonin syndrome, MH, or **malignant catatonia** (all respond). The **sertraline** raises **serotonin syndrome** — which he distinguishes from NMS by **tempo** (serotonin syndrome **abrupt on/offset**, like the way one separates **bradykinin- from histamine-mediated angioedema**; NMS **slower**) and by **hyperreflexia/myoclonus** (serotonin) — and he flags that **MH classically involves masseter spasm** (unassessable here, jaw wired shut) and is triggered by **succinylcholine/volatile anesthetics**. His bottom line is **management over label**: **hold the SSRI**, **continue benzodiazepines**, **cool**, **trend CK/K/Ca and give fluids** for possible rhabdomyolysis, **send a UTox** to be safe (stimulant/anticholinergic), and **involve neurology** — while gently resisting the reflex to blame occult infection ("she came in normal; something happened in that OR").

The **workup narrows the field by subtraction**: **UTox negative**, **TSH normal**, pharmacy confirms **only sertraline**. She has a **persistent temperature ~38–38.5 despite acetaminophen**, improves in rigidity with benzodiazepines, and needs heavy sedation. **Neurology and psychiatry** are consulted and are **"befuddled"**: **serotonin syndrome doesn't fit** (no hyperreflexia; no plausible medication narrative), **NMS doesn't fit** (no "screaming" antipsychotic), and **MH doesn't fit** (onset well after the OR, no inhaled trigger). They are certain of the **syndrome class** — a muscle that is **leaking, rigid, and driving the hyperthermia** — but not the **trigger**, and they **refuse to settle for "atypical."** Reza, riffing on the differential for **stiffness** (tetanus, myotonic disorders, **stiff person syndrome** with anti-GAD antibodies — recalling his own *Journal of Hospital Medicine* "EGAD" case), directs a **literature search** on **post-propofol rigidity and hyperthermia**.

The answer is **[[propofol-induced neuroexcitatory syndrome]]** — an **exceedingly rare** entity in which propofol precipitates an **NMS/serotonin-like reaction** in a small fraction of recipients, **distinct from propofol infusion syndrome** (a **mitochondrial toxicity**, classically in children on prolonged infusions). It becomes the working diagnosis **because nothing else fits and the usual triggers are absent**. As predicted by the "**drug-withdrawal**" logic, **stopping propofol** (and weaning benzodiazepines) leads to **recovery over 2–3 days**: the muscles calm, the fever breaks, she is **successfully extubated**, her **CK normalizes** (no persistent myopathy), **propofol is added to her allergy list**, and two weeks later she **tolerates sertraline without recurrence**. Reza closes on the meta-lesson — the value of **recognizing a phenotype, noticing it doesn't fit, and pursuing the misfit** rather than accepting a comfortable "atypical" label — and Rabih adds that *an atypical presentation of something you know may be something you don't yet know that mimics it.*

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The case is a clinic in **resisting the comfortable label**. Every experienced clinician "recognized the syndrome," and the **anchoring** temptation — to call it **atypical NMS** or **atypical serotonin syndrome** and move on — was strong precisely because **management wouldn't change**. What the team did instead is the episode's moral: they treated the **missing trigger** as a **signal, not noise**, because the *identity* of the trigger had a real consequence — **propofol re-exposure would relapse her**. Reza's framing hygiene is threaded throughout: **"elevated temperature, not fever"** (to avoid **premature closure** on infection), **sequence-of-events as time-zero** (to keep the OR exposure central), and **benzodiazepine response as non-discriminating** (a therapeutic win that is *not* diagnostic information). The distinction he draws — **serotonin syndrome's abrupt on/offset and hyperreflexia** vs **NMS's slower tempo and lead-pipe hyporeflexia** vs **MH's intra-operative volatile trigger** — is the practical core, and the **"EGAD"/stiff-person** tangent models **broadening** the stiffness differential when the common causes fail. The generalizable heuristic Rabih names — *an atypical presentation of something you know may be something you don't know that mimics it* — is the antidote to the **representativeness** shortcut that stops at "close enough." And the whole arc reprises the series theme that **you can act decisively on management while the diagnosis is still open** (cool, benzos, protect the kidneys) without letting that comfort **foreclose the inquiry**.

## Teaching Discussion

**Sequence of events and the framing of a post-procedure crisis.** When a patient decompensates around a procedure, the **operating room is time-zero**. Review the **anesthetic record and home medications** first: succinylcholine or volatile agents raise **malignant hyperthermia**; a home antipsychotic (or abrupt dopaminergic withdrawal) raises **NMS**; serotonergic agents raise **serotonin syndrome**. Framing the presentation as **"hyperthermia + rigidity + dysautonomia"** — rather than "post-op fever" — points reasoning toward the **neuromuscular/drug** causes and away from a reflexive infection workup.

**The hyperthermia-with-rigidity quartet.** **[[Neuroleptic malignant syndrome]]** — dopamine blockade/withdrawal; **days**-long onset; **lead-pipe rigidity, hyporeflexia**, autonomic instability, high CK. **[[Serotonin syndrome]]** — serotonergic excess; **abrupt** (hours); **clonus, hyperreflexia, myoclonus** (lower-extremity predominant), agitation. **[[Malignant hyperthermia]]** — RYR1-mediated; **intra-operative** with **volatile anesthetics/succinylcholine**; **masseter spasm**, rapidly rising ETCO₂ and temperature; treat with **dantrolene**. **Malignant catatonia** — clinically overlaps NMS (rigidity, autonomic instability, hyperthermia) and responds to **benzodiazepines/ECT**. Add **anticholinergic toxidrome** (dry, flushed, mydriasis, *no* rigidity) and **GABA-deficient** stiffness (**tetanus**, **[[stiff person syndrome]]**) to the broader net.

**Distinguishing them at the bedside.** **Reflexes and clonus** are the most useful discriminator — **prominent in serotonin syndrome, blunted in NMS**. **Tempo** helps — **serotonin syndrome is fast on and fast off; NMS is slow on and slow off** — but timing overlaps, so it cannot stand alone. **Trigger and setting** matter — an **intra-operative** onset with a **volatile/succinylcholine** exposure and **masseter spasm** flags MH. **Benzodiazepine response is shared** and therefore **not discriminating**.

**Management is largely diagnosis-agnostic.** **Stop the offending agent**; provide **aggressive supportive care** — **cooling**, **benzodiazepines** for agitation/rigidity, **IV fluids**, and vigilance for **rhabdomyolysis** (trend **CK**; watch **potassium** and **calcium**; protect renal function against **myoglobinuric AKI**). Add **dantrolene** for MH and for severe NMS-spectrum rigidity/hyperthermia; **bromocriptine** is an NMS option and **cyproheptadine** a serotonin-syndrome option. A **CK of ~3,500** is not yet ominous, but the trajectory is what counts.

**Propofol: two distinct muscle syndromes.** **[[Propofol infusion syndrome]] (PRIS)** is a **mitochondrial toxicity** — metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalemia, cardiac failure — classically in **children** (and adults) on **prolonged, high-dose** infusions; it is dose/duration-related and often fatal. **[[Propofol-induced neuroexcitatory syndrome]]** is different: an **NMS/serotonin-like neuroexcitatory reaction** (agitation, rigidity, hyperthermia, autonomic instability) attributed to **propofol itself**, described in case reports, and diagnosed largely by **exclusion of the usual triggers** plus a **temporal link to propofol and resolution on withdrawal**. The practical endpoint is to **document propofol as an allergy** so it is avoided at future anesthetics.

**"Pursue what doesn't fit."** The highest-value move in this case was **epistemic honesty**: naming the **discordance** (a classic syndrome with **no classic trigger**) instead of smoothing it into "atypical." The corollary heuristic — *an atypical presentation of a familiar disease may in fact be a distinct entity that imitates it* — is how the literature-defined rarities enter a clinician's script. It is also how you avoid the **consequential error** here: mislabeling would have permitted **propofol re-exposure** and a preventable relapse.
