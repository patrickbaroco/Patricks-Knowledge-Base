## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A doubleheader plus a "rapid fire" third case. Case 1 is a name-that-bug morning-report presentation: a young woman with a week of fever, reported (non-reproducible) neck stiffness, belly pain, and a strikingly neutrophil-predominant leukocytosis that argues against a viral process — prompting empiric bacterial meningitis coverage even after ID recommends stopping antibiotics. The eventual answer is Coxsackievirus B meningitis with a benign course, and the discussion turns to whether a good outcome retroactively indicts an aggressive workup. Case 2 is a pre-operative cardiac clearance for total hip arthroplasty that uncovers severe aortic insufficiency from myxomatous valve degeneration — not aortopathy, not a bicuspid valve, not rheumatic disease — with a teaching detour into the intrinsic-valve-versus-aortic-root framework and the counterintuitive AI management mantra of raising the heart rate while lowering the blood pressure. Case 3 is a rapid-fire anemia case that reapplies the "substance or stem cell" framework (first introduced in RLR 135) to a five-year progressive microcytic anemia, landing on idiopathic GI blood loss and severe iron deficiency treated with high-dose IV iron rather than transfusion.

## Pearls

- Neutrophil-predominant (as opposed to lymphocytic) leukocytosis argues strongly against most classic viral syndromes (mononucleosis, CMV, influenza) and should reframe the differential toward a pyogenic bacterial process until proven otherwise.
- Most pyogenic bacterial infections arise from mucocutaneous-lined structures that have contact with the outside world — skin, the respiratory/oral tract, the GU/GI/GYN/perineal region — rather than from rare deep, normally sterile spaces like the liver, meninges, or heart; base rates favor the common source even when the presentation is dramatic.
- A reported symptom (neck stiffness) doesn't need to be reproducible on exam to be taken seriously; in the right clinical context (fever, systemic toxicity) it can still justify an LP.
- "Don't let the sun set on a neutrophilic leukocytosis" — in one clinician's experience, a WBC of 27,000 with neutrophil predominance has essentially never gone unexplained, with C. diff colitis (a toxin-driven, almost paraneoplastic-like process) being the closest thing to a truly benign explanation.
- A consultant's recommendation (including "stop antibiotics") is data, not a verdict — clinicians should feel empowered to disagree with sub-specialty input when their own bedside gestalt says otherwise, and should notice when they're reflexively deferring to authority rather than reasoning independently.
- The correct standard for auditing a diagnostic decision isn't "was the final diagnosis benign" but "would I have wanted this same workup done for the person I love most, given what was known at the time" — outcome bias should not be allowed to retroactively relabel a sound process as an overreaction.
- Enteroviral (including Coxsackievirus) meningitis classically produces a lymphocytic-predominant CSF pleocytosis in an otherwise well, non-encephalopathic patient with gentle meningismus — but why some enteroviral infections drive a striking peripheral neutrophilic leukocytosis is not fully settled and shouldn't be dismissed as an obviously benign combination on peripheral labs alone.
- For incidentally discovered aortic insufficiency, the first branch point is intrinsic valve disease versus a consequence of aortopathy/aortic root dilation — the same logical structure used to separate primary from functional mitral regurgitation.
- Bicuspid aortic valve is unusual in that it straddles both categories — a congenital anomaly of neural crest cell migration that can cause both intrinsic valve dysfunction and an associated aortopathy — and is probably the single most common cause of incidentally found AI with no other clues.
- The differential for intrinsic valvular AI includes endocarditis (the most important cause not to miss, including indolent organisms like Bartonella for chronic presentations), rheumatic heart disease (common worldwide, less so in the US), myxomatous degeneration, radiation-induced valve disease, and post-TAVR AI.
- Leaflet count matters: a trileaflet valve essentially rules out bicuspid disease as the cause of AI, and normal other-valve morphology argues against a rheumatic process.
- Heart failure is a clinical diagnosis, not an echocardiographic one — a patient can have severe AI with a reassuring resting echo (normal LV size/wall thickness) and still decompensate once activity demands rise, which matters specifically when a sedentary pre-op patient is about to be mobilized aggressively in rehab.
- Aortic insufficiency management mantra: keep the heart rate up, bring the blood pressure down. Higher afterload increases the regurgitant fraction and LVEDP (raising pulmonary congestion risk), while a slower heart rate lengthens diastole — the exact phase during which AI regurgitant flow occurs — worsening the regurgitant volume.
- Beyond the well-known contraindications (cardiogenic shock, HFpEF without another indication), beta blockers are specifically counterproductive in two other settings: advanced restrictive cardiomyopathy (where the already-stiff, over-full ventricle needs preserved chronotropy, not prolonged diastole) and chronic aortic insufficiency (where prolonged diastole simply extends the regurgitation window).
- Isolated anemia — no other cytopenias, no evidence of overt hemorrhage or hemolysis — narrows cleanly to two buckets: a "substance" problem (deficiency of iron, B12, or a hormone like EPO/TSH; or excess of a toxic/suppressive substance like methotrexate or alcohol) or a "stem cell" problem (myelodysplastic syndrome, rarely pure red cell aplasia).
- A markedly low MCV in this setting narrows further: acquired microcytosis is iron deficiency until proven otherwise, with thalassemia as the main stem-cell-adjacent mimic.
- Ferritin and percent iron saturation are often the only two labs needed to confirm iron deficiency anemia when the pretest probability is already high from the substance-or-stem-cell/microcytosis framework.
- In an older patient with iron deficiency anemia and no localizing GI symptoms (no epigastric pain, no weight loss, no stool changes) after a thorough history and physical, idiopathic/unfindable GI blood loss is more likely than colon cancer or peptic ulcer disease, which tend to declare themselves with other symptoms or complications over a multi-year timeframe; AVMs are another common, often unfindable, cause.
- Standard of care still calls for EGD/colonoscopy in this setting, but if a patient declines, they will often do fine on iron repletion alone — a caution against over-medicalizing with escalating scope or capsule endoscopy workups when the source may never be found and doesn't need to be.
- The Ganzoni equation can be used to calculate total body iron deficit for dosing high-dose IV iron formulations (e.g., ferric carboxymaltose, deliverable up to 1 gram per infusion), which is often preferable to blood transfusion when feasible and time allows.
- Real-time large language model use (e.g., ChatGPT/GPT) is increasingly functioning as an informal "curbside" sounding board during live clinical reasoning — not a replacement for real consultants, but a way to rapidly generate and stress-test a differential when a colleague isn't available to bounce ideas off of.

## Case Narrative

### Case 1: Name That Bug — Coxsackievirus B Meningitis

Reza brought this case from morning report as a "name that bug" exercise. A woman in her 30s, who works at a camp and is regularly around children, presented after about a week of fevers, a sensation of neck stiffness that she reported but that wasn't reproducible on exam, diffuse abdominal pain, muscle soreness, and loose stools. She was tachycardic and febrile (T 39°C, HR 110) with a borderline low blood pressure (94/66), but mentating clearly with no encephalopathy. Her abdomen was diffusely, mildly tender without focal peritoneal signs. Labs showed a markedly neutrophil-predominant leukocytosis at 27,000, normal platelets, a mildly low hemoglobin, and modestly elevated transaminases (ALT 100, AST 80), with an otherwise unremarkable chemistry panel.

Robbie's first instinct, symptomatically, was something like mononucleosis — fever, malaise, hepatitis-range transaminase elevation — but the neutrophil-predominant (rather than lymphocytic) differential argued hard against that and against the other usual viral suspects like CMV or influenza. He reframed the case as a pyogenic bacterial infection until proven otherwise, invoking the principle that most pyogenic bacterial infections come from mucocutaneous-lined structures with access to the outside world (skin, the respiratory or oral tract, the GU/GI/GYN/perineal region) rather than rare deep, normally sterile compartments like the liver, heart, or meninges. The diarrhea alone wouldn't usually produce a leukocytosis this dramatic, so despite the neck stiffness not being reproducible on exam, he took it seriously enough to want a lumbar puncture. His plan was blood cultures and an HIV test first, then empiric antibiotics, then steroids, skipping a pre-LP CT, then proceeding to the LP itself. For empiric coverage he reasoned that ceftriaxone plus vancomycin would cover the majority of pyogenic bacterial meningitis pathogens (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis), and added ampicillin for Listeria coverage — not because of classic Listeria risk factors, but because of the prominent GI/abdominal symptoms pointing toward that tropism. He considered acyclovir low-risk to add even though he thought HSV meningitis was less likely given the neutrophilic (rather than lymphocytic) pattern, and explicitly flagged that the overall picture — symptoms present for a full week in a presumably vaccinated, immunocompetent patient — was atypical for straightforward bacterial meningitis, but said his personal morbidity threshold had already been crossed.

In the actual ED course, blood cultures and an HIV test were drawn and the patient was started on vancomycin and ceftriaxone. Infectious disease was consulted given the severe sepsis picture; they recommended a broader outpatient-adjacent workup (a tick-borne panel, leptospirosis, Coxiella, and an additional serologic/viral test) and recommended stopping the antibiotics. Robbie pushed back hard on that recommendation. In his experience, the only truly "benign" explanation he could think of for fever with a neutrophil-predominant leukocytosis of this magnitude was C. diff colitis — a toxin-driven process that behaves almost like a paraneoplastic phenomenon rather than a straightforward pyogenic infection — and otherwise, a WBC of 27,000 with neutrophil predominance has essentially never gone unexplained in his experience. He said he would not have stopped antibiotics without first identifying a source. Reza used this moment to reflect on his own trajectory: earlier in training and early faculty life he had over-indexed on matching whatever a consultant recommended, and has since learned that isn't automatically the right move — some of the other faculty in that same morning report also questioned the decision to stop antibiotics.

In the real clinical course, the LP was never actually performed at that point — the patient was instead started empirically on doxycycline, which didn't help and was discontinued. Reza then paused the group to ask Robbie to predict what a hypothetical LP would show before revealing the real result: when an LP was eventually performed, it showed a lymphocytic pleocytosis, and the patient went on to recover fully over the following days. Robbie's read on that result was that it fit a viral, likely enteroviral, meningitis in an otherwise well, non-encephalopathic young patient with only gentle meningismus — and he explicitly recalled his own prior RLR case of rotavirus/enterovirus meningitis (RLR 116). He flagged, though, that the striking neutrophilic leukocytosis remained "too serious to ignore," and admitted he didn't know of a well-established mechanism linking enteroviral meningitis to a peripheral neutrophilic leukocytosis this pronounced — he caveated his answer as reflecting only his current knowledge. When Reza pushed for a specific enterovirus subtype that would fit the muscle soreness, transaminitis, and meningitis together, Robbie admitted he didn't know specific subtypes off the top of his head.

Reza then described how he'd used ChatGPT/GPT in real time while working through the case: his own top initial differential, even before knowing the actual serology, was Coxsackievirus B — a strong fit for the camp/childcare exposure, fecal-oral transmission, and the virus's known tropism for the liver, muscle, and meninges. GPT flagged, correctly as it turned out, that Coxsackievirus wouldn't typically be expected to produce this degree of neutrophilic leukocytosis — a mismatch it called out on its own. The eventual serologic/viral panel came back positive for Coxsackievirus B, confirming the diagnosis despite that unresolved mismatch. Robbie closed with a meta-reflection on whether "the outcome justifies the process" — whether a benign final diagnosis retroactively means the empiric vancomycin and ceftriaxone were an overreaction. He argued the correct audit standard isn't the eventual diagnosis but what you would want done for the most cherished person in your life presenting with an identical picture: he'd still want the LP performed regardless of how it turned out, and framed the real test of whether the process was sound as the counterfactual of a fatal, missed bacterial meningitis with the same initial presentation — independent of how this particular case actually resolved.

### Case 2: Aortic Insufficiency — Myxomatous Valve Degeneration

Reza presented as the medicine consult attending, called for routine pre-operative cardiac clearance ahead of a total hip arthroplasty in an older man with severe hip osteoarthritis and poor mobility. A couple of weeks before the consult, the patient had had an episode of chest pain that led to an ED workup: a normal EKG, a negative troponin, and an echocardiogram that unexpectedly showed severe aortic insufficiency without overt heart failure symptoms.

Robbie's framework for AI started with the same branch point used for mitral regurgitation: is the regurgitation intrinsic to the valve leaflets themselves, or is it secondary to aortopathy and aortic root dilation? Echocardiography helps but isn't always definitive — a TEE or CTA gives a better look at the aortic root, though an astute echocardiographer can sometimes infer intrinsic-versus-aortopathy purely from leaflet motion. He flagged bicuspid aortic valve as an unusual entity that straddles both categories at once: a congenital anomaly of neural crest cell migration that can cause both intrinsic valve dysfunction and an associated aortopathy. For intrinsic valvular AI, his differential included endocarditis (the most important diagnosis not to miss, including indolent organisms like Bartonella in more chronic presentations), rheumatic heart disease (a major cause worldwide, less common in the US), myxomatous degeneration, radiation-induced valve disease, and post-TAVR AI. Absent other clues pointing to aortopathy, he suggested bicuspid valve was probably the single most likely explanation for incidentally discovered AI — and asked how many leaflets had actually been seen on imaging.

Reza then revealed the actual echo findings: a normal-sized aortic root (arguing against aortopathy), a trileaflet valve (ruling out bicuspid disease), normal morphology of the other valves including the mitral valve (arguing against a rheumatic process), and a single dysfunctional leaflet producing eccentric regurgitant flow — a picture most consistent with myxomatous degeneration of the aortic valve, an age-related process with hypertension, smoking, genetics, and environmental exposure as contributing risk factors (reasoning Reza again worked through with GPT's help). Reza described using GPT extensively throughout this case, describing it as functionally replacing the informal curbside conversation he'd otherwise have with a colleague — not a replacement for an actual specialist, but a tool he called "greater than, greater than, greater than" his own baseline knowledge in this particular domain.

On management, the patient's LV size and wall thickness were normal, which was reassuring against a significantly elevated resting LVEDP, but the group discussed how chronic severe AI that's asymptomatic at rest may not be well tolerated once physiologic demand rises — a real concern here given the patient's sedentary baseline from his hip disease and the fact that surgery and rehab would both increase demand on the heart. Heart failure is a clinical diagnosis, not purely an echocardiographic one, so post-operative mobilization carried a real risk of unmasking previously subclinical decompensation. Cardiology's pre-operative plan emphasized afterload reduction as the critical lever — higher afterload increases the regurgitant fraction, raising LVEDP and pulmonary congestion risk. The patient was on a combination hydrochlorothiazide/lisinopril pill (25/40mg), and, per standard (if debatable) practice, this was held the morning of surgery. Working through the perioperative blood pressure plan with GPT, Reza's team decided to reinitiate lisinopril alone (not the hydrochlorothiazide component, given NPO status and uncertain volume/renal status) once systolic BP rose above 140, deliberately avoiding both an overly tight target around 130 (risk of hypotension) and the higher tolerance of up to 160 sometimes cited in general ACC guidance, which felt too permissive specifically for severe AI. In the event, post-op systolic BP rose to about 160, lisinopril was given, and by the next morning systolic pressure was down to about 120; the patient tolerated the procedure well and was doing well in rehab, though Reza flagged ongoing concern that increasing post-op mobility could still unmask previously subclinical heart failure.

Robbie added a pearl on heart rate management specific to AI. Beyond the well-known scenarios where beta blockers are contraindicated (cardiogenic shock, and HFpEF without another indication for the drug), there are two further situations where beta blockade is specifically counterproductive: advanced restrictive cardiomyopathy, where prolonging diastole doesn't help because the ventricle is already pathologically stiff and full and instead needs preserved chronotropy, and chronic aortic insufficiency, where regurgitant flow happens specifically during diastole, so slowing the heart rate lengthens the time available for regurgitation. His summary mantra for AI: keep the heart rate up, bring the blood pressure down.

### Case 3: Progressive Iron Deficiency Anemia (Rapid Fire)

The final case was framed by Reza as a "rapid fire" — something that, in his words, "can be done in five minutes." An older man with multivessel coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation/flutter, and a prior stroke was hospitalized for a new CVA. He was on apixaban and aspirin, both held because of a progressive anemia: hemoglobin had fallen from 14 four years earlier, to 12 two years earlier, to 10 a year earlier, to 9 six months earlier, and now to 6.7, with an MCV of 66 — markedly microcytic. Neurology, managing the acute stroke, consulted medicine specifically for the anemia workup.

Robbie applied the same "substance or stem cell" framework he'd used in RLR 135. With isolated anemia — no other cytopenias, and nothing suggesting overt hemorrhage (which usually presents with hypotension and acute blood loss) or hemolysis (which usually comes with indirect hyperbilirubinemia and an elevated LDH) — the differential narrows to exactly two buckets: a substance problem (deficiency of iron, B12, or a hormone like EPO or TSH; or excess of a toxic or suppressive substance or medication, such as methotrexate or alcohol) or a stem cell problem (myelodysplastic syndrome, or more rarely pure red cell aplasia). Adding the markedly low MCV narrowed the differential further, to either a stem cell process like thalassemia or, far more commonly, a substance process — acquired microcytosis being, essentially by rule, iron deficiency until proven otherwise. Robbie asked for exactly two labs: ferritin and percent iron saturation. The results — ferritin 7, iron saturation essentially undetectable — confirmed severe iron deficiency anemia.

Robbie credited a GI colleague, Jay Ryan (distinguished, in a joking aside from Reza, from "Trilo"/Caleb — the brilliant but interpersonally difficult mentor both of them recall meeting simultaneously early in training, hovering over Robbie in the ED insisting he "read the EKG, child") for a teaching point: in an older man with iron deficiency anemia and no other localizing GI symptoms — no epigastric pain, no weight loss, no change in stool pattern — after a reasonably thorough history and physical, the most likely explanation is idiopathic, unfindable GI blood loss rather than colon cancer or peptic ulcer disease, both of which tend to declare themselves with other symptoms or complications over a timeframe this long (five years of slowly progressive anemia here). Arteriovenous malformations were flagged as another possible, similarly often-unfindable, cause. The practical conclusion: EGD and colonoscopy remain standard of care and should still be offered, but if the patient declines them, he is likely to do fine regardless — a caution against escalating to more invasive workups like capsule endoscopy when the more likely reality is idiopathic blood loss that responds to iron repletion alone. Reza added a treatment-dosing detail, referencing the Ganzoni equation to calculate the patient's total iron deficit (roughly 2 grams in this case) and noting that a high-dose IV iron formulation like ferric carboxymaltose — deliverable up to 1 gram per infusion and prescribable by GI or hematology at their institution — was preferred over transfusion when feasible. The added complexity here was that the patient needed apixaban restarted for both his CVA/AFib and his coronary disease, currently held because of the anemia, so a definitive endoscopic evaluation was planned specifically to identify (and potentially treat) any bleeding source before anticoagulation could be safely resumed.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Case 1 is built around a base-rate argument (pyogenic infections favor mucocutaneous-accessible sites over rare deep spaces) colliding with a striking outlier lab value (neutrophilic leukocytosis at 27,000) that resists easy dismissal even after the diagnosis turns out to be viral. The case is also a direct study in authority bias and outcome bias: Reza's own reflection on historically over-deferring to consultant recommendations, and Robbie's explicit argument that a benign final diagnosis should not retroactively be used to judge an appropriately aggressive initial workup as wrong.

Case 2 illustrates a structured branching illness script (intrinsic valve versus aortopathy, further branching by leaflet count and other-valve morphology) used to move an initially broad differential for AI down to a single most-likely etiology using only a few echo findings. It also carries an implicit anchoring caution: chronic AI with a reassuring resting echo can still mask decompensation risk under future physiologic stress, so a normal resting study shouldn't be read as reassurance against future risk.

Case 3 reuses the "substance or stem cell" framework verbatim from RLR 135, reinforcing it as a durable heuristic for isolated anemia, and adds a secondary teaching point about resisting the urge to keep escalating workup (capsule endoscopy, etc.) once the pretest probability has already shifted heavily toward a self-limited, treatable explanation — a caution against sunk-cost-driven overinvestigation.

## Teaching Discussion

This episode's connective tissue across all three cases is the tension between algorithmic pattern-matching and knowing when to stop trusting the algorithm. In Case 1, the "name that bug" framing initially invites viral-versus-bacterial pattern matching by cell differential alone, but the discussion explicitly resists letting the final (benign) diagnosis relabel the initial (aggressive, reasonable) workup as a mistake — Robbie's "cherished person" heuristic is offered as a general-purpose antidote to outcome bias in retrospective case review. Reza's parallel reflection on having previously over-indexed on consultant recommendations adds a second layer: expertise elsewhere (ID's recommendation) is valuable input, not an automatic override of one's own bedside reasoning.

Case 2 is a clean demonstration of using structural imaging findings (leaflet count, root size, other-valve morphology) to prune a differential efficiently, but it also carries a subtler teaching point about the limits of resting hemodynamic measurements — a structurally reassuring echo doesn't guarantee tolerance of increased physiologic demand, which matters directly for a patient about to undergo surgery and rehabilitation.

Case 3, despite its "rapid fire" framing, reinforces one of the series' most durable frameworks (substance or stem cell) and pairs it with a valuable de-escalation teaching point: once a highly probable, treatable diagnosis has been reached, resisting further workup escalation is itself a form of good clinical judgment, not negligence.

A theme running quietly under all three cases — flagged explicitly by Reza in Cases 1 and 2 — is his real-time use of GPT as an informal reasoning aid during live clinical decision-making, explicitly framed as supplementing rather than replacing actual consultants and colleagues.
