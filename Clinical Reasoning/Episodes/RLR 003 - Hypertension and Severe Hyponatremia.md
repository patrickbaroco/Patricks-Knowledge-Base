## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> An elderly, highly functional woman visiting the US from France — limited English, no overnight interpreter — is brought in by family after three days of nausea and non-bloody vomiting, mild confusion, and a home systolic of ~180. The triage picture of nausea + vomiting + confusion + hypertension pulls the team toward an intracranial emergency, but a [[Non-Contrast CT Head|CT head]] is negative and the story reframes: nausea came first, and she had stopped her antihypertensives ([[Lisinopril]], [[Amlodipine]]) because she couldn't keep them down. Rabih uses the case to argue that [[Essential Hypertension|primary hypertension]] alone does not cause acute end-organ symptoms — symptomatic hypertension means an acute insult or out-of-control [[Secondary Hypertension|secondary hypertension]] — so the blood pressure is a consequence here, not the cause. Basic labs reveal the real problem: sodium 110, with ketonuria (starvation ketosis from poor intake). Working the [[Hyponatremia|hyponatremia]] as hypotonic, a specific gravity of 1.017 (≈ urine osm 600 via the [[Specific Gravity Rule|×30 rule]]) and a urine sodium of 65 point toward [[SIADH]], reinforced by a low uric acid of 1.8. But a liter or two of fluid overcorrects her sodium 110 → 124 in ~4 hours — behavior more consistent with hypovolemic, aldosterone-driven ADH than fixed SIADH. The overcorrection itself becomes the emergency, and the team deliberately re-lowers the sodium with [[DDAVP]] and [[D5W]] to prevent [[Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome|osmotic demyelination]]. The withheld twist: she was also on [[Hydrochlorothiazide|hydrochlorothiazide]], and the final read is multifactorial hyponatremia — thiazide + hypovolemia + SIADH-like physiology — with adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism excluded and a faint chest-imaging lymph node left for outpatient follow-up.

## Pearls

- **Primary hypertension alone does not produce acute end-organ symptoms.** Rabih's core claim: a patient with essential [[Essential Hypertension|hypertension]] who simply stops their pills does not land in the hospital *from the hypertension itself* — analogous to a primary headache syndrome not causing persistent neurologic damage. Symptomatic/very high pressure should trigger a search for either an **acute** driver ([[Aortic Dissection|dissection]], pain, withdrawal, [[Hypertensive Encephalopathy|encephalopathy]]) or **[[Secondary Hypertension|secondary hypertension]] gone out of control** ([[Obstructive Sleep Apnea|OSA]], [[Renal Artery Stenosis|renal artery stenosis]], [[Pheochromocytoma|pheo]], Cushing).
- **Classify hypertension in three buckets that can coexist:** acute (never hypertensive before, reversible trigger), chronic primary (idiopathic), and chronic secondary (identifiable lesion). A single patient can carry any combination, which is what makes symptomatic hypertension confusing at the bedside.
- **Don't let the sodium hijack the whole case.** [[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring]] on a low sodium is a classic internist trap — Rabih cites a morning-report patient whose real diagnosis was osteomyelitis and Reza one whose sodium was incidental to a fungal vasculitis. Confirm the number is actually worth the 20-minute detour before taking it.
- **Ketonuria without hyperglycemia = starvation or alcoholic ketosis** (with euglycemic DKA on [[SGLT2 Inhibitors|SGLT2 inhibitors]]/recent insulin as the caveat). Here it simply flags days of poor intake — a supporting clue to hypovolemia, not the headline.
- **Work hyponatremia in order: tonicity → ADH on/off → appropriate vs. inappropriate.** Assume [[Hypotonic Hyponatremia|hypotonic]] absent alcohol/other clues; an elevated urine osm says ADH is active; the [[Specific Gravity Rule|specific gravity ×30 rule]] (last two digits × 30) estimates urine osm at the bedside (1.017 ≈ 600) when contrast and hyperglycemia are absent. Then ask whether ADH is riding with aldosterone (appropriate) or alone ([[SIADH|inappropriate]]).
- **Use the urine sodium as an aldosterone proxy — but respect its false positives.** A high urine sodium (here 65) suggests SIADH, yet is artificially elevated by recent fluids, [[Diuretics|diuretics]], kidney dysfunction/[[Acute Tubular Necrosis|ATN]], or bladder irrigation. A **low uric acid** (1.8) and a **[[Fractional Excretion of Uric Acid|FEUA]] > 12%** further support SIADH over thiazide.
- **Rapid correction with plain fluids points to hypovolemic, aldosterone-driven ADH, not fixed SIADH.** The body prioritizes volume over osmolality; restoring volume shuts off baroreceptor-mediated ADH, a dilute [[Water Diuresis|water diuresis]] follows, and the sodium leaps — exactly the 110 → 124 seen here.
- **Overcorrection is its own emergency.** A rise > ~6 mEq/L in 24 h risks [[Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome|osmotic demyelination]] — real, delayed by days, and litigated. Re-lower proactively with [[DDAVP]] + [[D5W]] and nephrology; don't treat "the number is better now" as success.
- **When the urine sodium is high and it isn't ATN, five causes remain:** [[SIADH]], [[Hydrochlorothiazide|thiazides]], [[Hypothyroidism|hypothyroidism]], [[Adrenal Insufficiency|adrenal insufficiency]], and [[Cerebral Salt Wasting|cerebral salt wasting]]. [[Thiazide-Induced Hyponatremia|Thiazide-induced hyponatremia]] is the great mimic — it can look hypovolemic *or* SIADH-like.
- **Hyponatremia is usually multifactorial — think "and," not "or."** Re-test and re-evaluate after your intervention; the response to therapy is itself a diagnostic that can unmask a second or third contributor.

## Problem Representation

An elderly, previously highly functional woman visiting from France — with limited English and no available interpreter, itself a barrier to history — presents with three days of nausea, non-bloody vomiting, and mild confusion, a home systolic of ~180 after stopping her antihypertensives for intolerance, and is found to have severe hyponatremia (sodium 110) with ketonuria, an estimated urine osmolality of ~600, a urine sodium of 65, and a low uric acid (1.8) — a picture ultimately read as multifactorial hyponatremia (thiazide effect plus hypovolemia from poor intake plus SIADH-like physiology) complicated by iatrogenic overcorrection to 124 within hours, mandating deliberate re-lowering to prevent osmotic demyelination.

## Case Narrative

Reza frames the encounter honestly before any data: an elderly woman on vacation from France, limited English, no in-person interpreter available in the middle of the night, and confused — so the history is genuinely hard to acquire, a fact he insists belongs *inside* the clinical reasoning rather than as an aside. She is a baseline highly functional executive, brought in by family after three days of nausea and non-bloody emesis and an inability to keep anything down, capped by a home systolic she measured herself at 180.

Handed nausea + vomiting + mild confusion + hypertension, Rabih deliberately separates the syndrome from the one abnormality that might explain it, and interrogates the link before accepting it. He lays out that hypertension comes in three forms that can coexist: acute (a normotensive person made hypertensive by a reversible trigger like a fracture's pain or alcohol withdrawal), chronic primary/idiopathic, and chronic [[Secondary Hypertension|secondary]] (a specific lesion — [[Obstructive Sleep Apnea|OSA]], [[Renal Artery Stenosis|renal artery stenosis]], Cushing). His central conviction is that [[Essential Hypertension|primary hypertension]] alone does not cause acute complications — he has never seen chronic essential hypertension, even at a systolic of 200 after stopping meds, produce an acute event by itself, drawing an analogy to primary headache syndromes not causing persistent neurologic injury. So if hypertension is to be blamed for these symptoms, one must find an acute process ([[Aortic Dissection|dissection]] → [[Hypertensive Encephalopathy|hypertensive encephalopathy]], or a [[Pheochromocytoma|pheo]]) — otherwise the safer posture is to treat the blood pressure as a *clue or a consequence*, prioritize a problem in the brain producing nausea/vomiting/confusion (including [[Intracranial Hypertension|raised intracranial pressure]]), and hold the pressure to one side. When Reza probes the point directly — someone with essential hypertension who stops their meds — Rabih holds firm: the real culprits in "hypertensive emergency" are usually **underdiagnosed** OSA or renal artery stenosis (his two memorable cases: occult OSA, and a renal-artery-stenosis patient tipped over by an NSAID).

The workup follows exactly that instinct: the team zooms on an intracranial focus, gets a [[Non-Contrast CT Head|CT head]] — negative — and only then gathers collateral. She is on [[Lisinopril]] and [[Amlodipine]] and had stopped them over the preceding days because the nausea prevented her from swallowing them. That reordering matters: the **nausea preceded** the hypertension, so the blood pressure is downstream. Basic labs drawn before imaging show the real story — sodium **110**, creatinine 0.76, anion gap 17, urine specific gravity 1.017 with **2+ ketones**.

Rabih pauses to resist a reflex. Before diving into the hyponatremia tunnel he asks whether any other clue deserves attention, warning — with a fresh morning-report example of a sodium of 126 that hijacked twenty minutes while the true diagnosis was osteomyelitis (and Reza's own of a 126 that distracted from a fungal vasculitis) — that a sodium can distract the whole team. The one collateral clue is the **ketonuria**: without hyperglycemia it means starvation or alcoholic ketosis (euglycemic DKA on [[SGLT2 Inhibitors|SGLT2 inhibitors]]/recent insulin being the caveat), most likely here just poor intake, though occult alcohol use would fit a nausea/vomiting/confusion syndrome.

He then works the [[Hyponatremia|hyponatremia]] as a schema. First tonicity: absent significant alcohol (serum alcohol here undetectable), assume [[Hypotonic Hyponatremia|hypotonic]] — too much water. The first, most important question is whether ADH is the culprit, gauged by an inappropriately elevated urine osm; while awaiting the lab he uses Reza's [[Specific Gravity Rule|specific gravity rule]] (last two digits × 30, valid when no contrast and no hyperglycemia), turning 1.017 into an estimated **~600 mOsm** — a whopping value signaling high ADH activity. Is that ADH physiologic (riding with aldosterone) or not? The urine sodium is the aldosterone proxy: low means appropriate; high means [[SIADH|inappropriate]] — or a false positive. The urine sodium comes back **65** — technically elevated, supporting SIADH, but Rabih enumerates the artifacts that inflate it (recent fluids, [[Diuretics|diuretics]], kidney dysfunction/[[Acute Tubular Necrosis|ATN]], or [[Bladder Irrigation|bladder irrigation]] sampling saline), none of which obviously apply, leaving SIADH as the leading read.

Then the case turns. She receives a liter or two of fluid and her sodium jumps **110 → 124 in about four hours** — faster than desired. A serum uric acid returns **low at 1.8**. Rabih summarizes and confronts the tension: the urine sodium says SIADH, but such a brisk response to fluid argues against fixed SIADH (in which continued ADH concentrates urine at ~600 while infused fluid at ~300 mOsm is *more dilute than the urine*, diluting plasma and worsening hyponatremia). The reconciliation is that SIADH need not be continuous — a stimulus (nausea/vomiting, or a medication) can switch off — so either she had appropriate, hypovolemic ADH with a misleading urine sodium, or a transient SIADH that resolved with cessation of its stimulus. Reza sharpens it: the rapid rise is classic for **hypovolemia**, the strongest ADH stimulus of all — the body picks volume over osmolality, so replenishing volume silences baroreceptor-driven ADH, unleashes a dilute [[Water Diuresis|water diuresis]], and the sodium leaps.

That leap is now the emergency. A rise of 14 points over hours far exceeds the ~6 mEq/24 h ceiling and threatens [[Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome|osmotic demyelination]] — which Reza stresses is real, delayed, and a recurring source of litigation. The team reverses course, using [[DDAVP]] and [[D5W]] (with nephrology) to bring the sodium **back down**.

The withheld twist lands in the debrief. On deeper history she was also taking **[[Hydrochlorothiazide|hydrochlorothiazide]]** — only lisinopril had surfaced initially — and her creatinine fell from 0.76 to ~0.5, confirming a hypovolemic/AKI component in a frail elderly patient. Reza offers the schema for a high urine sodium that isn't ATN: **SIADH, thiazide, hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, cerebral salt wasting**; adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism were tested and negative. The low uric acid (and the concept that a [[Fractional Excretion of Uric Acid|FEUA]] > 12% is nearly slam-dunk for SIADH over thiazide) had pushed everyone toward SIADH — yet Reza flags his own unease reconciling a *low* uric acid with thiazide, which usually causes hyperuricemia. Rabih answers with a just-read JAMA 2022 review: [[Thiazide-Induced Hyponatremia|thiazide-induced hyponatremia]] affects ~7% of hospitalized hyponatremic patients, presents as hypovolemic *or* SIADH-like euvolemic disease, may involve a genetic reduction in a prostaglandin transporter in the distal nephron increasing water absorption, and is worsened by potassium depletion — and, per a downloaded thiazide review, normal-or-low uric acid/creatinine can accompany it by mechanisms that remain "unclear despite numerous studies." Reza adds the volume-status physiology: low effective arterial blood volume lowers uric acid via reduced filtration/excretion, unlike the hypervolemic SIADH state that excretes it. The synthesis is deliberately multifactorial — thiazide + hypovolemia + SIADH-like features — with a faint chest-imaging lymph node kept in reserve for outpatient re-imaging to exclude an occult malignancy. She corrected safely, showed **no** osmotic demyelination, and the episode closes on the meta-point Reza names outright: he "got" Rabih only because he **withheld the thiazide** — the clues (especially the rapid fluid response) were there to be caught.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Framing / de-anchoring the hypertension.** Rabih explicitly refuses to let the eye-catching systolic anchor the case, dissecting hypertension into acute/primary/secondary and arguing primary HTN can't *acutely* injure — a [[Framing Effect|reframe]] that converts the blood pressure from suspected cause to downstream consequence.
- **Anchoring on the sodium.** Both hosts name the internist's temptation to let a low sodium consume the encounter; the osteomyelitis and fungal-vasculitis anecdotes are cautionary [[Anchoring Bias|anchoring]] cases where the number was a distraction from the real diagnosis.
- **Schema-driven, sequential Bayesian reasoning.** The hyponatremia is worked as an ordered algorithm (tonicity → ADH on/off via urine osm → appropriate vs. inappropriate via urine sodium/uric acid), each datum nudging the estimate rather than snapping to a label — a model of transparent [[Bayesian Reasoning|updating]].
- **Treating the therapeutic response as a diagnostic test.** The overcorrection to 124 is read as *evidence*, not just a complication — it re-opens the SIADH-vs-hypovolemia question, illustrating that response to intervention updates the differential.
- **Resisting [[Search Satisficing|search satisficing]] / single-cause bias.** Reza's "and, not or" insistence — hyponatremia is usually multifactorial — keeps the team from stopping at SIADH and lets thiazide + hypovolemia + SIADH-like physiology all remain in play.
- **Recognizing a manufactured blind spot.** The final diagnosis was gettable; Reza deliberately **withheld the thiazide**, and both note the clue (rapid fluid response) was present — a clean demonstration of how missing history, not missing reasoning, drives error, and why [[Premature Closure|premature closure]] on SIADH was the risk.
- **Holding a discrepant datum honestly.** Rather than force the low uric acid to fit, Reza flags it as not-fully-reconciled with thiazide and both go to the literature — modeling comfort with residual uncertainty over false coherence.

## Teaching Discussion

- **Symptomatic or severe hypertension demands a driver beyond "essential HTN off meds."** Look for an acute reversible insult or out-of-control secondary hypertension ([[Obstructive Sleep Apnea|OSA]], [[Renal Artery Stenosis|renal artery stenosis]], [[Pheochromocytoma|pheo]], Cushing); the two classically underdiagnosed culprits behind "hypertensive emergency" are OSA and renal artery stenosis (the latter easily tipped by an NSAID).
- **Order the hypertension question at the bedside:** is this acute, chronic primary, chronic secondary, or a combination? Naming the bucket disciplines the workup and prevents blaming a chronic number for an acute presentation.
- **A low sodium earns your time only after you confirm it's the real problem.** Guard against letting it eclipse the rest of the case; ketonuria, imaging findings, and the actual syndrome may matter more.
- **Estimate urine osmolality instantly with the specific gravity ×30 rule** (last two digits × 30), valid when the patient hasn't received contrast and isn't hyperglycemic — a fast read on whether ADH is active while the formal osm is pending.
- **The urine sodium is an aldosterone proxy, not an oracle.** It is falsely elevated by recent fluids, diuretics, kidney dysfunction/ATN, and bladder irrigation — interpret it against timing and context before calling SIADH.
- **Four adjuncts refine "appropriate vs. inappropriate ADH":** response to fluids, clinical context, urine (Na + K) as a better urine-osm surrogate, and uric acid — low supports SIADH (FEUA > 12% nearly slam-dunk), high supports appropriate ADH.
- **Volume beats osmolality every time.** Hypovolemia is a stronger ADH stimulus than hyponatremia is an off-switch; that hierarchy explains why volume repletion can abruptly correct the sodium and why a brisk response argues for hypovolemic physiology.
- **Overcorrection is the danger you create.** Keep the rise ≤ ~6–8 mEq/L/24 h; when you overshoot, actively re-lower with DDAVP and D5W and involve nephrology — osmotic demyelination is real and typically presents days later, after apparent improvement or even discharge.
- **[[Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome|Osmotic demyelination]] behaves like other demyelinating diseases:** it requires a rapid osmolar shift (classically overcorrected hyponatremia, but also settings like liver transplant) and, like MS, manifests days after the insult; the old term "central pontine myelinolysis" undersells the ~50% with extrapontine white-matter involvement.
- **Thiazides are the great hyponatremia mimic.** [[Thiazide-Induced Hyponatremia|Thiazide-induced hyponatremia]] (~7% of hospitalized hyponatremia) can look hypovolemic or SIADH-like, may reflect a genetic prostaglandin-transporter reduction increasing distal water absorption, is aggravated by potassium depletion, and — counterintuitively — can run with a normal or low uric acid, a still-unexplained finding. Always confirm the full medication list.
- **Hyponatremia is usually multifactorial.** Expect contributors to add ("and," not "or"), and re-test/re-evaluate after each intervention; the safest posture treats the first plausible cause as partial until the response confirms or refutes it.
