## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> An older man with diabetes and hypertension presents with two days of severe first-MTP toe pain, diagnosed clinically as podagra (gout) and treated with a steroid taper after an NSAID allergy and a too-late window for colchicine. Two and a half weeks later he returns by ambulance with a foul-smelling, gangrenous, purulent toe — wet gangrene requiring emergent antibiotics and amputation — despite normal, easily palpable distal pulses on both visits. Post-amputation ankle-brachial and toe-brachial indices reveal the culprit: isolated severe microvascular disease with entirely normal macrovascular flow, a diagnosis the presenting physician had no way to suspect from pulses alone. The episode closes with an unusually candid personal reflection on diagnostic guilt, the limits of a single-encounter time-course history, and the relationship-building that carried the physician-patient relationship through the outcome.

## Pearls

- In an older patient with diabetes and hypertension presenting with acute first-MTP pain, gout is the dominant pretest diagnosis — roughly 80% of such presentations can be diagnosed clinically without arthrocentesis, driven by the joint's relative coolness (favoring urate crystal precipitation) and repetitive weight-bearing trauma at that specific joint.
- Serum uric acid is an unreliable single-timepoint test during an acute gout flare: it can transiently fall below the usual diagnostic threshold (roughly 6-7 mg/dL) as crystals actively precipitate out of solution during the attack itself — a "normal" uric acid does not exclude gout, though a level consistently below 6 makes an ongoing flare physiologically implausible.
- Acute gout treatment options span NSAIDs, colchicine, oral or intra-articular steroids, and (typically rheumatology-driven) IL-1 blockade with anakinra; colchicine's efficacy is time-sensitive and concentrated in the first 24 hours of a flare, making it a poor choice once a patient presents several days into symptoms.
- Adequate steroid dosing for an acute gout flare requires real doses (e.g., prednisone 40mg, dosed multiple times daily) and an appropriate taper over 7-10 days — under-dosing is a common practical failure mode.
- Wet gangrene (necrotic, malodorous tissue with purulent drainage and systemic leukocytosis) is a surgical and infectious emergency distinct from dry gangrene, which is not itself an emergency and often autoinfarcts/autoamputates without operative intervention.
- Gas identified on plain film or CT within infected soft tissue raises concern for a gas-forming organism (classically Clostridium) but does not confirm it; empiric coverage for wet gangrene should be broad, spanning Clostridium, Group A Streptococcus, and polymicrobial flora (particularly in diabetic patients), alongside urgent surgical source control — antibiotics alone are not sufficient.
- Palpable, even Doppler-confirmed, distal pulses do not exclude severe distal ischemia when the underlying process is isolated microvascular disease: macrovascular flow can be entirely preserved while microvascular (digital) perfusion is critically impaired, producing frank gangrene in a foot with normal-feeling pulses — analogous to how a clean coronary angiogram does not exclude MINOCA.
- Ankle-brachial index interrogates macrovascular flow and can be entirely normal in isolated microvascular disease; toe-brachial index is the correct test to detect microvascular ischemia missed by ABI and pulse exam alone.
- The tempo of tissue change is itself diagnostic information: gangrene evolving acutely over days favors an acutely obstructing process (thrombus or embolus), while gangrene developing over weeks to months favors chronic microvascular ischemia — an ambiguous or unverified symptom tempo directly weakens confidence in either explanation.
- Diagnosing acute gout without crystal-proven arthrocentesis requires either a documented history of prior, similar episodes or a confidently anchored, verified short symptom time course; when the reported tempo is vague, unwitnessed, or not independently corroborated, the diagnosis is more fragile than it feels in the moment, and other processes (isolated microvascular ischemia, an evolving infection) can present similarly underneath a presumptive "first gout flare" label.
- The same metabolic risk-factor profile (diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia) that predisposes to gout also predisposes to microvascular disease, so the two processes share overlapping pretest probability drivers and can convincingly masquerade as one another in the same patient.
- Separating "what were the sequence of events and what do the data actually show" from "what did I do wrong" supports a more accurate, less self-punishing case review after an adverse outcome, without requiring self-blame to drive genuine practice change.

## Problem Representation

An older man with diabetes and hypertension presenting with brief, unwitnessed first-MTP toe pain treated presumptively as podagra, who returns weeks later with wet gangrene of the same toe and, despite robust and Doppler-confirmed pedal pulses on both visits, is found on toe-brachial index testing to have isolated severe microvascular ischemia rather than a macrovascular occlusive event.

## Case Narrative

Rabih presented this case as one of his own — a patient he had personally treated, and one whose course he described, from the outset, as "a very, very educational experience... where I experienced many highs and many lows, mostly lows." A man arrived in the emergency department reporting two days of severe pain over his right first metatarsophalangeal joint, with no prior episodes like it, no trauma, and a past medical history of moderately controlled diabetes (A1c around 7.5), hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. His vital signs and systemic exam were entirely normal; he was comfortable except when directing attention to his toe, at which point he winced.

Reza worked through the pretest reasoning before the exam findings were revealed: in a population with diabetes, hypertension, and age as risk factors, toe pain at the first MTP is overwhelmingly most likely gout, driven by the joint's relative coolness (favoring urate crystal precipitation) and by repetitive weight-bearing trauma at that specific site — the same aging-related risk profile that predisposes to atrial fibrillation also predisposes to gout. He organized his broader differential around a musculoskeletal-pain framework: trauma versus no trauma, evidence of edema or erythema (raising gout probability), a comparison to the contralateral foot for subtle asymmetry, confirmation of pulses to exclude a vascular process, and — if the exam was unrevealing — a low threshold for an X-ray, with diabetic neuropathic ulceration and underlying osteomyelitis flagged as another possibility, particularly since neuropathic patients often present with painless, febrile-only infections rather than florid local pain.

The exam showed exquisite tenderness over the first MTP with mild diffuse swelling extending to the dorsum of the foot, no skin breakdown, no erythema, and 2+ dorsalis pedis pulses. Labs were unremarkable apart from a uric acid of 8, and the X-ray showed no acute findings. Rabih was ready to discharge the patient with a treatment plan for acute podagra when he discovered a documented anaphylactic reaction to NSAIDs, prompting him to ask Reza how he would treat the flare.

Reza reviewed the treatment options — NSAIDs, colchicine, oral or intra-articular steroids, and, typically via rheumatology, IL-1 blockade with anakinra — noting his own preference for colchicine given its favorable comorbidity profile relative to NSAIDs, dosed as an initial loading dose followed by a lower maintenance dose over roughly a seven-day course, and most effective when given within the first 24 hours of a flare. Because the patient was already three to four days into his symptoms by the time this discussion took place, colchicine's window had passed; anakinra was off the table as a first presentation requiring an impractical same-day rheumatology consult; and NSAIDs were contraindicated by his allergy. Rabih instead treated with high-dose prednisone (40mg) with a 7-10 day taper, and the patient was discharged.

Two and a half weeks later, Rabih recognized the same patient's name on the department board — this time arriving by ambulance. A colleague warned him about the smell before he even entered the room. The patient reported feeling better for the first couple of days after treatment, then progressively worse each day since, but had not returned to care despite the escalating symptoms, citing competing life stressors. On exam, his sock came off to reveal a gangrenous first toe oozing purulent material, a low-grade fever, tachycardia, a white count of 25,000 with normal hemoglobin and platelets, mild acute kidney injury, and plain film gas in the subcutaneous soft tissue of the toe, confirmed on CT and not extending beyond the midfoot.

Reza reasoned through the new presentation: leukocytosis, dark necrotic tissue, and purulent drainage together define wet gangrene, a medical emergency distinct from dry gangrene (which typically autoinfarcts and does not require urgent intervention), because dying, purulent tissue creates ongoing risk of bloodstream seeding and endovascular infection, and because the process can continue propagating proximally, threatening more of the limb. Subcutaneous gas raised concern for a gas-forming organism like Clostridium, though it did not confirm one; he reasoned through the same organism list invoked for necrotizing fasciitis (Clostridium, Group A Streptococcus) plus polymicrobial coverage given the patient's diabetes, recommending vancomycin plus piperacillin-tazobactam, urgent surgical or podiatric involvement for source control, and anticipating that source control would likely mean amputation of the digit given how far the process had progressed.

At this point Reza and Rabih both grappled with the same question: was the original presentation actually podagra, or was this infection already brewing underneath a presumptive gout diagnosis? Reza reasoned that a short prednisone course is unlikely to be meaningfully immunosuppressive (the same dosing is routinely used for COPD exacerbations without concern), favoring the interpretation that the second presentation represented an occult diabetic foot process — an infection or ulcer without an alarming surface appearance at the first visit — rather than an infection caused by the steroid treatment itself.

Rabih then revealed the piece of data that reframed the entire case: on both the first and second visits, the patient had had 2+, easily Doppler-confirmed dorsalis pedis pulses — no vascular surgery consult had even been considered on the return visit, given how robust those pulses felt. Podiatry, consulted for the antibiotic complexity of the admission, recommended a transmetatarsal amputation the following morning.

Reza connected this to a prior case Rabih had presented him — dialysis access-related steal syndrome — recalling that microvascular ischemia can cause chronic distal digital gangrene that coexists with normal proximal pulses, but flagged that the tempo here was the piece that didn't fit: this patient did not have gangrene weeks earlier, and its appearance now suggested something acutely obstructing the vessel rather than a purely chronic microvascular process. He reasoned that ankle-brachial index and even palpable distal pulses do not rule out chronic microvascular ischemia — analogous to how a clean coronary angiogram does not exclude MINOCA (myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries) — and that more advanced imaging of the digital vessels, or a toe-brachial index specifically, could resolve the question, while wondering aloud whether an atheroembolic or microembolic phenomenon too small to affect proximal vessels but large enough to occlude digital microvasculature might explain the discrepancy.

Podiatry took the patient to the operating room the next day after antibiotic stabilization. Intraoperatively, the foot was grossly ischemic — the amputation site did not bleed at all — prompting a vascular surgery consult and formal ABI/toe-brachial index testing. On the unaffected left foot, the ankle-brachial index was normal at 1.0, but the toe-brachial index was markedly abnormal at 0.3; on the amputated right foot, the ankle-brachial index was also normal at 1.0 (a toe index could no longer be obtained). Vascular surgery concluded the patient had isolated severe microvascular disease, with entirely preserved macrovascular flow.

Rabih visited the patient in the hospital afterward and learned that, similar to how he minimized reporting his condition, the man's foot had actually been bothering him for weeks to months longer than he had initially conveyed, competing with other life stressors he was managing. Rabih described the moment he saw the returning, gangrenous foot as one where he "collapsed" clinically and emotionally, replaying the initial encounter and searching for what he had missed, unable to sleep that night. Over the following days he came to two conclusions. Medically: confidently diagnosing acute gout without crystal proof requires either a history of prior, recurrent episodes, or complete certainty about the symptom time course — a two-day story he had accepted without independent verification. Personally: the deeper lesson was about the power of relationship-building with patients, and his gratitude that the patient, rather than blaming him, met the outcome with generosity and understanding — an experience Rabih described as reshaping how he thinks about what it means to be a physician, beyond the purely diagnostic dimension of the case.

The episode closed with a brief practical addendum: Reza noted that with the benefit of a rheumatology colleague's input, colchicine can sometimes be started even a few days into a flare — a detail that, had it been known at the first visit, might have changed the treatment choice, though neither host framed this as something that would have prevented the ultimate outcome.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| Diabetes, hypertension, age, acute first-MTP pain | Strongly favors gout (podagra) as the leading diagnosis on pretest probability alone |
| Exquisite first-MTP tenderness, mild swelling, no erythema/ulcer/skin breakdown, 2+ DP pulses | Consistent with uncomplicated podagra; reassures against vascular or infectious process at initial presentation |
| Uric acid 8, normal X-ray | Supports gout; does not exclude it if it had been lower (uric acid can transiently drop during an active flare) |
| Return visit: malodorous, gangrenous, purulent toe with leukocytosis (WBC 25,000), low-grade fever, tachycardia | Establishes wet gangrene — a surgical/infectious emergency, distinct from dry gangrene |
| Subcutaneous gas on imaging, not extending beyond midfoot | Raises (does not confirm) a gas-forming organism; widens empiric coverage rather than narrowing it |
| 2+, Doppler-confirmed pulses present on both visits, including at the gangrenous presentation | Argues against a proximal macrovascular occlusive event despite frank distal gangrene |
| Intraoperative finding: no bleeding at the amputation site | Confirms severe distal ischemia despite normal-feeling proximal pulses |
| Normal ankle-brachial index bilaterally; toe-brachial index 0.3 on the unaffected foot | Localizes the disease process to the microvasculature, with macrovascular flow preserved |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Isolated microvascular digital ischemia illness script: a patient with diabetes/vascular risk factors develops distal digital gangrene despite normal, even Doppler-confirmed, proximal pulses; ankle-brachial index is falsely reassuring because it interrogates macrovascular flow, while toe-brachial index reveals the true microvascular deficit — the same diagnostic gap illustrated by MINOCA on coronary angiography.

The case illustrates anchoring risk in the opposite direction from most illness scripts discussed on the podcast: rather than anchoring on a single alarming finding, the treating physician anchored on a strongly reassuring one (robust distal pulses) that, in isolated microvascular disease, does not carry the ruling-out power it usually does. The episode is also a candid, first-person illustration of hindsight bias and diagnostic guilt — Rabih explicitly models separating "what were the sequence of events" from "what did I do wrong" as a way to conduct honest case review without collapsing into self-blame.

## Teaching Discussion

The central clinical teaching point is that distal pulses — even when robust and Doppler-confirmed — test macrovascular flow and can be entirely normal in isolated microvascular disease severe enough to cause frank gangrene; toe-brachial index, not ankle-brachial index, is the test that reveals this gap, and the MINOCA analogy (a clean angiogram does not exclude a myocardial infarction caused by microvascular or non-obstructive disease) gives clinicians a transferable mental model for the same phenomenon in different vascular beds.

A second teaching point concerns the fragility of a purely clinical gout diagnosis: an acute presentation without crystal-proven arthrocentesis is only as strong as its supporting time course, and a diagnosis built on an unwitnessed, unverified "two days" of symptoms is more vulnerable to being wrong — or to obscuring a coexisting process — than it feels at the bedside. Rabih's explicit takeaway, that confident non-arthrocentesis gout diagnosis requires either recurrent prior episodes or a locked-in time course, is a directly actionable rule for future practice.

A third, more personal teaching point is the episode's unusually candid treatment of diagnostic guilt and physician-patient relationship repair after an adverse outcome. Rather than framing the case purely as a diagnostic puzzle, both hosts spent significant time on Rabih's emotional process — his initial self-blame, his deliberate shift toward objective case reconstruction, and his gratitude for the patient's generosity — modeling a form of reflective practice that complements, rather than replaces, the clinical reasoning work.
