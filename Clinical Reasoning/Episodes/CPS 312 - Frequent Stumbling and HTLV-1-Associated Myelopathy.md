## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A neurology Virtual Morning Report with neurologist **Aaron Berkowitz** on **frequent stumbling** — a chief concern that is really a problem of **[[Gait Disorder|gait]]**, which Berkowitz frames as the nervous system's ultimate stress test (vision, frontal "gait app," proprioception, cerebellum, motor output, and vestibular input all in concert). A **47-year-old man from the Peruvian Andes (now Lima)** has **three years of progressive stumbling and bilateral lower-limb weakness**, then **six months of constipation, nocturia, and — most recently — erectile dysfunction** (the symptom that brought him in). Using the **[[Anatomical Approach|localization-first]]** method, the discussants place bilateral leg weakness *without cognitive change* below the brain and brainstem, and the **S2–S4 sphincter/autonomic** features narrow it to **[[Myelopathy|spinal cord or cauda equina]]** ([[Onuf's Nucleus|Onuf's nucleus]] is why long-nerve neuropathies spare the sphincters). Exam confirms a pure **[[Upper Motor Neuron vs Lower Motor Neuron|upper-motor-neuron]]** picture — spasticity, hyperreflexia, clonus, bilateral Babinski, and a left **[[Hoffman Sign|Hoffman sign]]** — with normal sensation. Over a **three-year tempo**, the chronic-myelopathy schema runs degenerative, neoplastic, infectious ([[Tuberculous Myelopathy|TB]], schistosomiasis, HIV, **[[HTLV-1 Associated Myelopathy|HTLV-1]]**), hereditary ([[Hereditary Spastic Paraplegia|HSP]], adrenomyeloneuropathy), and metabolic ([[Subacute Combined Degeneration|B12/copper]]) causes. MRI shows only **thoracic cord atrophy** (no compression, no mass, mild CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis with slightly high protein); a negative infectious/syphilis work-up leaves a chronic, mildly inflammatory, non-compressive myelopathy — and **HTLV-1 serology is positive**, diagnosing **tropical spastic paraparesis (HAM/TSP)**, endemic in Peru. The clincher: the "family history of **[[Multiple Sclerosis|MS]]** in his wife" was itself HTLV-1 — this patient was part of a reported **family cluster of ten** infected by shared vertical/sexual transmission.

## Pearls

- **[[Gait Disorder|Gait]] is the nervous system's ultimate stress test, which is why it is examined last.** Normal walking requires vision and visuospatial perception, a frontal "gait app" for motor planning and rhythm (impaired in NPH, sometimes called gait apraxia), proprioceptive input to parietal cortex and cerebellum, coordinated cerebellar and motor output, and intact vestibular function — so stumbling can localize almost anywhere, and non-neurologic (orthopedic, foot-drop, visual) causes must be excluded first.
- **Bilateral lower-limb weakness *without* cognitive change points below the brain; add S2–S4 sphincter dysfunction and you are in the cord or cauda equina.** Bilateral cerebral leg weakness is "gourmet neurology" (parasagittal meningioma, bilateral ACA infarct); the brainstem is unlikely without cranial-nerve signs. Long peripheral nerves and even ALS tend to **spare the sphincters** ([[Onuf's Nucleus|Onuf's nucleus]]), so early bowel/bladder/sexual involvement with leg weakness localizes centrally.
- **Reflexes separate [[Upper Motor Neuron vs Lower Motor Neuron|upper- from lower-motor-neuron]] disease — "everything goes up" in UMN.** UMN: hyperreflexia, increased tone/spasticity, up-going toe (Babinski), clonus; LMN: hyporeflexia, decreased tone, fasciculations. The **[[Hoffman Sign|Hoffman sign]]** is the upper-limb analogue of Babinski, and the UMN pattern of weakness (leg extensors stronger than flexors, arm flexors stronger than extensors) mirrors the chronic post-stroke posture.
- **Read a chronic myelopathy by tempo and by MRI compartment.** A three-year, slowly progressive, non-traumatic myelopathy runs a schema of **degenerative** (spondylosis), **neoplastic** ([[Spinal Meningioma|meningioma]], myxopapillary ependymoma), **infectious** ([[Tuberculous Myelopathy|TB/Pott]], schistosomiasis, HIV vacuolar myelopathy, [[HTLV-1 Associated Myelopathy|HTLV-1]]), **hereditary** ([[Hereditary Spastic Paraplegia|HSP]], adrenomyeloneuropathy), and **metabolic/toxic** ([[Subacute Combined Degeneration|B12/copper]]) causes; imaging is triaged as **extradural vs intradural-extramedullary vs intramedullary**, then as **mass vs signal change vs atrophy**.
- **[[HTLV-1 Associated Myelopathy|HTLV-1-associated myelopathy / tropical spastic paraparesis]] (HAM/TSP) is a chronic, mildly inflammatory, non-compressive myelopathy that causes cord atrophy.** Endemic in Peru, the Caribbean, and Japan; transmitted by breastfeeding, sex, and blood; only a small fraction of those infected develop myelopathy. It travels with **[[Strongyloides|strongyloides]]** co-infection and **[[Adult T-cell Leukemia-Lymphoma|adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma]]**, can mimic ALS (but with bladder involvement), and has **no proven disease-modifying treatment**.
- **A "family history" can be the diagnosis in disguise.** The wife's reported **[[Multiple Sclerosis|multiple sclerosis]]** was actually HTLV-1 — shared exposure gave shared disease, and this patient belonged to a reported cluster of ten infected family members. When two people in a household carry the same neurologic label, ask whether one diagnosis (and one exposure) explains both.

## Problem Representation

A 47-year-old man from the Peruvian Andes, now in Lima, with early and advanced cardiovascular disease (CABG at 46, hypertension) and a remote treated parasitic infection, presents with three years of slowly progressive frequent stumbling and bilateral lower-extremity weakness, followed over the last six months by constipation, nocturia, and erectile dysfunction. Examination shows a pure upper-motor-neuron syndrome confined to the legs — mild spastic paraparesis (4/5), increased tone, hyperreflexia, clonus, bilateral Babinski signs, and a left Hoffman sign — with normal cognition, normal sensation, and no cranial-nerve findings, localizing to the spinal cord. MRI shows thoracic cord atrophy without compression or a mass, and CSF is mildly lymphocytic with slightly elevated protein and normal glucose; a broad infectious and syphilis work-up is negative. The chronic, non-compressive, mildly inflammatory myelopathy in a patient from an endemic region — whose wife carries a diagnosis of "multiple sclerosis" — is HTLV-1-associated myelopathy (tropical spastic paraparesis), confirmed by positive HTLV-1 serology.

## Case Narrative

A CP Solvers Neurology VMR with **Aaron Berkowitz** teaching, **Valeria** presenting the case (from Peru), and **Shubangi** and **Nalayan** as discussants. The chief concern: **frequent stumbling**.

**Framing a gait complaint.** Berkowitz opens on the deceptive simplicity of "stumbling": [[Gait Disorder|gait]] is an acquired, un-taught function that draws on essentially the entire nervous system, which is why it is tested *last* — it is the stress test that reveals subtle failures in vision, sensation, coordination, and motor control acting in concert. He sketches the pieces: visual and visuospatial input; a frontal-lobe "gait app" coordinating rhythmic movement (its disruption in normal-pressure hydrocephalus sometimes called gait apraxia); proprioception traveling up the peripheral nerves and cord to parietal cortex and, subconsciously, to the cerebellum; and coordinated cerebellar and motor output — plus the vestibular system. He notes non-neurologic mimics (orthopedic hip/knee disease, ankle contracture, foot drop) and uses a vivid analogy — his infant niece's wide-based, arms-flexed "magnetic" first steps look like the gait of an elderly person with failing proprioception or NPH, because her frontal lobes and nerves are not yet myelinated.

**HPI.** A **47-year-old man** from the **Andes, now living in Lima**, has **three years of progressive frequent stumbling with worsening bilateral lower-limb weakness**. **Six months** before presentation he developed **constipation and nocturia**, and in the **last few weeks erectile dysfunction** — the symptom that finally brought him to care. No weight loss, no fever. PMH is notable for a **coronary bypass one year ago** and **hypertension diagnosed at 40** (on aspirin, atorvastatin, enalapril), and a **treated parasitic infection four years ago** (organism unremembered). Family history: **his wife was diagnosed with multiple sclerosis five years ago**.

**Localizing with the [[Anatomical Approach|localization-first method]].** Nalayan invokes the neurology mantra — **time course into localization** — and the group works the anatomy. Bilateral leg weakness with **no cognitive features** makes the brain unlikely (bilateral cerebral leg weakness is rare "gourmet neurology": parasagittal meningioma or bilateral ACA infarct); the brainstem is unlikely without cranial-nerve signs. That places the lesion at the **thoracic cord and below** — cord, roots, nerves, neuromuscular junction, or muscle. The **autonomic features** (constipation, nocturia, erectile dysfunction) map to **S2–S4**, and Berkowitz uses them to narrow further: peripheral neuropathies (even severe Guillain–Barré) and ALS characteristically **spare the sphincters** ([[Onuf's Nucleus|Onuf's nucleus]] is preserved), because the nerves to the sphincters are very short. Sphincter dysfunction *with* leg weakness therefore points to **[[Myelopathy|cord or cauda equina]]** — and since S2–S4 roots alone would not cause this much leg weakness (the legs are L2–S1), Berkowitz favors the **spinal canal / cord**. The presence of both motor and sensory potential in the cord and nerves (versus a pure motor NMJ/muscle problem) is flagged for the exam to sort out.

**Loose ends in the history.** Berkowitz reasons about the incidental data. The **advanced cardiovascular disease** at 47 raises aortic atherosclerosis and rare **[[Spinal TIA|spinal TIAs/infarction]]** (usually acute, not a three-year course). The **parasitic infection** prompts a tour of parasites that reach the cord — **schistosomiasis** (freshwater exposure, usually acute myelopathy/cauda equina), spinal **[[Neurocysticercosis|neurocysticercosis]]** (almost always with brain lesions), and rare parasitic abscesses.

**The chronic-myelopathy schema over a three-year [[Illness Tempo|tempo]].** For a slowly progressive, non-traumatic myelopathy the group builds the differential: **degenerative** spondylosis (possible even at 47 with heavy physical work; cervical and lumbar levels move most); **neoplastic** — a slow benign tumor such as a **[[Spinal Meningioma|meningioma]]** or a conus **myxopapillary ependymoma**; **infectious** — **[[Tuberculous Myelopathy|tuberculosis]]** (Pott disease, arachnoiditis, tuberculoma; the leading non-traumatic myelopathy cause under 50 in TB-endemic areas), HTLV-1, and **HIV vacuolar myelopathy** (usually in advanced AIDS); and **hereditary/metabolic** — **[[Hereditary Spastic Paraplegia|hereditary spastic paraplegia]]**, adrenomyeloneuropathy, and **[[Subacute Combined Degeneration|B12 deficiency]]** (subacute combined degeneration, typically with prominent dorsal-column signs and little weakness). Berkowitz also wonders whether the wife's "MS" is a **shared myelopathy from a common cause** rather than two coincidental diagnoses.

**Exam.** T 37 °C, BP 140/90, HR 90, RR 18. General exam near-normal except **gingival bleeding**. Neuro: **oriented ×3**, **mild spasticity predominantly in the legs**, **increased tone**, **4/5 power in both legs**, **hyperreflexia, clonus, bilateral Babinski signs, and a left [[Hoffman Sign|Hoffman sign]]**; he could **walk unaided** with no single characteristic gait. Sensation — including vibration and proprioception — was **normal**. This is a clean **[[Upper Motor Neuron vs Lower Motor Neuron|upper-motor-neuron]]** syndrome ("everything goes up"), confirming a **spinal cord** localization; the left Hoffman raises the possibility the lesion extends to the cervical level.

**Work-up.** Hgb 13.5, normal WBC, hepatic and renal panels. **LP**: opening pressure 14 cm H₂O, clear fluid, **10 lymphocytes (100%)**, **protein 65**, **normal glucose ratio (60/80)**; India-ink and KOH negative, AFB negative, cryptococcal India ink negative, **VDRL/FTA negative**. Chest X-ray normal. **MRI spine**: **atrophy of the thoracic cord**, read as "marked loss of motor axons" — **no compression, no mass, no meningeal enhancement**. Berkowitz frames the imaging by compartment (extradural bone, intradural-extramedullary meninges, intramedullary cord): the bones and meninges are clean, and the cord shows **atrophy rather than a mass or signal change** — fitting a degenerative process, HIV vacuolar myelopathy, or **HTLV-1**. The mildly inflammatory CSF (10 lymphocytes, protein 65) argues against a purely degenerative or acute-inflammatory (MS/NMO) process and back toward a **chronic low-grade inflammatory myelopathy** — i.e., HTLV-1.

**Resolution.** The final test was an **enzyme immunoassay for HTLV-1 (positive)** with **positive serum serology**, diagnosing **HTLV-1-associated myelopathy / tropical spastic paraparesis (HAM/TSP)** — "really common here in Peru." The family-history clue resolved beautifully: the wife's "**[[Multiple Sclerosis|multiple sclerosis]]**" was itself **HTLV-1**, and this patient was part of a **reported family cluster of ten** infected members — transmission in Peru occurring chiefly by **breastfeeding**, then sexually and (less often) by blood. Berkowitz and Valeria review the associations: co-infection with **[[Strongyloides|strongyloides]]** (and higher risk of strongyloides hyperinfection and of **[[Adult T-cell Leukemia-Lymphoma|adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma]]**), and HTLV-1 as a cause of an **ALS-like motor neuron disease** distinguished by bladder involvement. Only a small percentage of infected people develop the myelopathy, and there is **no proven treatment** (antiretrovirals and steroids both lack good evidence); this patient, still ambulatory, was not treated, whereas his more affected wife had received steroids.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Anatomical Approach|Localization before differential]].** The engine of the case was Berkowitz's "time course into localization": strip bilateral leg weakness of a brain cause (no cognition), use S2–S4 autonomic features and the [[Onuf's Nucleus|Onuf's-nucleus]] principle to land in the cord, and let the pure-UMN exam confirm it — only *then* apply the etiologic schema. Localization narrows the differential far more efficiently than pattern-matching to a disease.
- **[[Illness Tempo|Tempo-driven differential]].** A three-year course pruned the schema — acute causes (spinal TIA, transverse myelitis, epidural hematoma) fell away, and chronic, indolent processes (degenerative, slow tumor, TB, HTLV-1, hereditary, metabolic) rose. The mildly inflammatory CSF then argued against a purely degenerative or acute-inflammatory course, favoring chronic low-grade inflammation.
- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring / fixation]], named at the bedside.** Shubangi twice caught herself — "I think I am fixating" — anchored on a compressive/canal-stenosis lesion. Berkowitz reframed it as *correctly* fixating (the localization was right even if the etiology was not), modeling that naming your anchor lets you keep the useful part and discard the premature closure.
- **[[Availability Heuristic|Availability]] from prior cases.** Nalayan volunteered "I have a bias there as well" toward tuberculosis, primed by Valeria's two previous TB presentations. Berkowitz validated TB on its merits (base rates in a TB-endemic region) rather than on its availability — "even if Valeria hadn't presented two TB cases, we would have to think about this."
- **[[Base Rate|Base rates]] and geography.** Endemic exposure did real diagnostic work: TB and HTLV-1 are common where this patient lives, and the cited epidemiology (non-traumatic myelopathy under 50 in TB-endemic areas is most often TB; over 50, metastasis) shows how local prevalence should reshape the ranking.
- **The family history as signal, not noise.** The wife's "MS" looked like an unrelated familial factoid but was the diagnosis — a reminder that a shared household diagnosis can reflect a shared exposure and a single unifying explanation.

## Teaching Discussion

- Treat a gait complaint as a whole-nervous-system problem: because normal walking integrates vision, frontal motor planning, proprioception, cerebellar coordination, motor output, and vestibular input, examine gait last as a stress test, and exclude non-neurologic (orthopedic, foot-drop, visual) contributors before localizing within the neuroaxis.
- Localize bilateral lower-extremity weakness by first removing the brain (unlikely without cognitive features) and brainstem (unlikely without cranial-nerve signs), leaving cord, roots, nerves, neuromuscular junction, and muscle; then use accompanying features to narrow further.
- Use S2–S4 sphincter and sexual dysfunction to localize: because long peripheral nerves and ALS tend to spare the sphincters (Onuf's nucleus), early bowel, bladder, or erectile dysfunction accompanying leg weakness points to the spinal cord or cauda equina rather than a peripheral neuropathy.
- Distinguish upper- from lower-motor-neuron disease with the reflex exam ("everything goes up" in UMN — hyperreflexia, increased tone, up-going toe, clonus; the Hoffman sign as the upper-limb Babinski), and recognize the UMN pattern of weakness (leg extensors stronger than flexors, arm flexors stronger than extensors) that mirrors chronic post-stroke posture.
- Build the chronic-myelopathy differential by tempo (degenerative, neoplastic, indolent infection, hereditary, metabolic/toxic) and read the MRI by compartment — extradural (bone) versus intradural-extramedullary (meninges) versus intramedullary (cord) — and then by whether the cord shows a mass, signal change, or atrophy.
- Recognize HTLV-1-associated myelopathy (tropical spastic paraparesis) as a chronic, mildly inflammatory, non-compressive myelopathy that produces cord atrophy in patients from endemic regions (Peru, the Caribbean, Japan), transmitted by breastfeeding, sex, and blood, associated with strongyloides co-infection and adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma, sometimes mimicking ALS (with bladder involvement), and lacking a proven disease-modifying therapy.
- Let epidemiology and base rates weight the differential (TB and HTLV-1 where they are endemic), name anchoring and availability bias when they surface so localization is preserved while premature etiologic closure is avoided, and treat a shared household diagnosis as a possible clue to a single unifying exposure.
