## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 32-year-old man on [[HIV Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis|HIV PrEP]] and sexually active with men presents with a week of severe rectal pain, 7/10 at baseline and 12/10 with bowel movements. Vitals, abdominal exam, and external anal inspection are unremarkable — but the chief concern of *rectal* pain, worsened by defecation, points hard at [[Infectious Proctitis|proctitis]], and a lubricated digital exam so painful the patient expels the finger drives the posterior probability toward rectal mucosal inflammation. He is swabbed for gonorrhea/chlamydia at three sites, sent for [[Syphilis Serology|syphilis serology]] and HIV, and given empiric [[Ceftriaxone]] plus [[Doxycycline]]. Two days later he returns with a multifocal vesicular-and-pustular rash — during an active [[Mpox]] outbreak. The team isolates, swabs, and deliberately keeps [[HSV]]/[[VZV]] and [[Secondary Syphilis]] in play rather than anchoring on mpox. Results land sequentially: HIV negative, then RPR 1:64 with a positive treponemal antibody (secondary syphilis, treated with [[Penicillin]] with a [[Jarisch-Herxheimer Reaction]] warning), then a positive mpox swab. The case is a study in [[Correlated Risk|correlated STI risk]] ("if you're at risk of one, you're at risk of another") and in not letting the first positive test — or the outbreak everyone is primed for — close the search.

## Pearls

- **Rectal pain is a self-localizing chief concern.** Unlike "abdominal pain" (many organs, extra-abdominal mimics), rectal pain — especially with tenesmus and pain worsened by defecation — puts the spotlight directly on the rectum and [[Infectious Proctitis|proctitis]], and lets you drop cardiac/pulmonary considerations from the outset.
- **A normal-appearing anus does not exclude proctitis.** Inspecting the anus is not inspecting the rectum above it. You can have florid rectal inflammation with a bland external exam — and sometimes spreading the anus reveals purulence tracking out.
- **Expose the anus properly or you will miss the common causes.** The highest-yield anorectal-pain diagnoses are small: [[Anal Fissure|anal fissures]] (often under 1 cm), anal warts, and hemorrhoids. A shy, microscopic peek misses them; the skin has to be actively spread.
- **Pain that escalates with bowel movements localizes to inflamed mucosa.** Stool passing over an inflamed rectum reproducing/amplifying pain (here 7/10 → 12/10) is itself a diagnostic signal for proctitis, not an incidental detail.
- **Normal vital signs don't exclude proctitis** — infectious proctitis can run without any systemic inflammatory signature, and immunosuppression (e.g., undiagnosed HIV/AIDS) can further blunt the response.
- **Swab, don't just palpate.** Rather than repeat a painful digital exam, use a rectal swab for gonorrhea and chlamydia, and test multiple sites (rectal, pharyngeal, urine); send syphilis serology in parallel.
- **[[HIV Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis|PrEP]] prevents HIV, and only HIV.** Highly effective PrEP does nothing for gonorrhea, chlamydia, syphilis, HSV, or mpox — a PrEP-adherent patient can still acquire and transmit all of them, which is exactly why the broader STI workup still applies.
- **Current empiric STI regimen:** [[Ceftriaxone]] 500 mg IM (single dose) for gonorrhea plus [[Doxycycline]] 100 mg PO BID × 7 days for chlamydia. (Related 2024 CDC prevention strategy: [[Doxy-PEP]] 200 mg within 72 hours of sex for MSM/transgender women with a bacterial STI in the prior year.)
- **Syphilis serology has two arms answering two different questions.** The treponemal-specific antibody, once positive, stays positive for life and only says "ever infected." The non-treponemal test (RPR/VDRL) is quantitative, tracks active disease, and is followed to gauge treatment response. RPR 1:64 is a high, meaningful titer; 1:4 is low (likened to a weakly positive ANA — present, not alarming on its own).
- **Respect syphilis enough to expect any morphology.** Classic secondary syphilis is macular/papular and not typically pustular — but syphilis can produce nearly any rash. At any stage, screen for neuro/ocular/otic involvement (headache, meningismus, hearing/vision change), which would prompt LP and change treatment duration.
- **Anticipate the [[Jarisch-Herxheimer Reaction]].** After penicillin for syphilis, treponemal killing can trigger fever, chills, and hypotension within hours — prime the patient and team so it isn't misread as treatment failure or anaphylaxis.
- **[[Mpox]] belongs on the MSM proctitis differential** and can present without the classic febrile prodrome. Its rash may be vesicular/pustular and multifocal rather than dermatomal. Operationally: isolation, appropriate PPE, lesion swab, ID involvement, contact tracing, and post-exposure smallpox/mpox vaccination for close contacts (orthopoxvirus cross-protection).
- **A vesicular rash has a narrow differential.** In contrast to the ubiquitous macular/papular rash, vesicles pull toward [[HSV]] and [[VZV]] (including disseminated varicella), both of which can also cause proctitis. Unroof a lesion to test for the herpesviruses; mpox testing does not require unroofing.

## Problem Representation

A 32-year-old man on HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (negative antibody one month prior), sexually active with men, with no prior STIs, presents with one week of severe anorectal pain (7/10 baseline, 12/10 with defecation) and no fever or systemic symptoms — found to have normal vitals, a benign abdomen, an unremarkable external anal exam but exquisitely painful digital rectal exam, and no fissure, wart, or hemorrhoid — a picture of [[Infectious Proctitis]] that, after empiric gonorrhea/chlamydia therapy, evolved with a new multifocal vesicular-and-pustular rash into a dual diagnosis of [[Secondary Syphilis]] (RPR 1:64) and [[Mpox]] proctitis.

## Case Narrative

The case opens on a striking chief concern: severe rectal pain in a 32-year-old man, present for a week. Reza immediately contrasts this with abdominal pain — where many intra- and extra-abdominal organs must be entertained — noting that *rectal* pain, particularly if paired with tenesmus, shines the spotlight on rectal pathology and [[Infectious Proctitis|proctitis]]. In a young patient, sexual history becomes the pivotal branch point: a history of receptive anal intercourse would place him at high risk for STIs and gram-negative infection.

More history sharpens the picture. The pain is anchored in the anus and rectum, 7/10 at baseline and 12/10 with bowel movements, without fevers or systemic symptoms. Rabih then "freezes" the exam deliberately: vitals normal, patient well-appearing, abdomen normal, and the anus — on inspection — unremarkable. Reza uses the pause to make an anatomic point: viewing the anus does not evaluate the rectum, and proctitis can exist with a bland external exam. He would gently spread the anus looking for purulence or blood, cautious about a finger given the pain. Rabih adds the practical teaching: expose widely, because the commonly missed lesions — [[Anal Fissure|anal fissures]] under a centimeter, anal warts, hemorrhoids — are small and are by far the most frequent causes of anorectal pain in the ED. This patient had none of them, and a lubricated digital exam was so painful he immediately expelled the finger.

The revealed history fits the syndrome: he takes HIV PrEP with a negative antibody a month prior, no prior STIs, no tobacco or alcohol, occasional marijuana, and is sexually active with men only. Reza narrates his probability of proctitis jumping from roughly 40% to ~90% on the strength of the finger-expulsion finding. He builds the infectious differential for proctitis in MSM — gonorrhea, chlamydia (including the [[Lymphogranuloma Venereum|LGV]] serovars), syphilis, and enteric organisms such as E. coli — prioritizing infection over non-infectious causes like ulcerative colitis, and invoking the principle that risk for one STI implies risk for another. He also raises the ongoing mpox outbreak and the importance of a genital exam for ulcers. The plan: rectal swab rather than repeat palpation, testing for gonorrhea/chlamydia, plus syphilis serology.

The team sends rectal, pharyngeal, and urine gonorrhea/chlamydia, along with HIV and RPR. HIV returns negative same-day; RPR takes ~48 hours. He is discharged with pain control and, after shared decision-making, accepts empiric therapy — [[Ceftriaxone]] 500 mg IM plus a 7-day course of [[Doxycycline]], the current regimen.

Two days later, tests still pending, he returns with a new rash and unchanged rectal pain. The distribution is multifocal: vesicles on the buttock, macules and papules on the chest, and pustules on the right elbow and left forearm. The team, already primed for [[Mpox]], moves to isolate him (negative-pressure room, N95), acknowledging both the need to protect contacts and the risk of making a marginalized patient feel stigmatized. Reza notes mpox can cause proctitis and can present without the classic febrile prodrome, and reviews the operational steps — swab, ID notification, contact tracing, and post-exposure smallpox/mpox vaccination for meaningful contacts. Crucially, he then slows himself down: MSM + rash does not equal mpox. He uses the rash's *features* — its multifocal, non-dermatomal distribution and, above all, its vesicular component — to build a narrow vesicular differential centered on [[HSV]] and [[VZV]] (including disseminated varicella), which can themselves cause proctitis. He would unroof a lesion for the herpesviruses (mpox does not require unroofing) and would re-send HIV serology. Pustules are unroofed and sent, isolation guidelines given, and the patient referred to ID.

The rest evolves by chart. The syphilis test returns first: positive treponemal antibody with an RPR of 1:64. Reza works the serology carefully — the treponemal antibody stays positive for life and only marks prior infection, while the quantitative RPR reflects and follows active disease; a titer of 1:64 is impressive, and a false positive is not a realistic concern given the patient's history. With the rash, this is [[Secondary Syphilis]]; he screens conceptually for neuro/ocular/otic involvement, treats with IM [[Penicillin]], and primes for a [[Jarisch-Herxheimer Reaction]]. He explicitly refuses to close the case on syphilis alone — "I'm not done with mpox." The mpox swab then returns positive as well, confirming co-infection. The patient receives mpox vaccination and, one month later, is back to baseline health, still on PrEP. In the debrief, Rabih is candid that the dominant real-world experience was not elegant reasoning but logistical friction — wrong fax numbers at the health department, understaffed labs, unfamiliar order sets — which nearly crowded out any reflection on the case itself, and both discussants sit with the stigma such a diagnosis carries for an already marginalized community.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Data point | Hypothesis raised/adjusted | Direction of update |
|---|---|---|
| Chief concern: *rectal* pain, one week | Proctitis / anorectal pathology; drops cardiac/pulmonary | Localizes search space tightly to the rectum |
| Pain 12/10 with bowel movements | Rectal mucosal inflammation ([[Infectious Proctitis|proctitis]]) | Raises proctitis (stool over inflamed mucosa) |
| Normal vitals, no fever, well-appearing | Systemic inflammatory process | Modest decrease — but explicitly *not* exclusionary for proctitis |
| Normal external anal inspection | Fissure / wart / hemorrhoid; and rectal disease | Lowers external lesions; does **not** lower rectal inflammation |
| No fissure, wart, or hemorrhoid on proper exposure | Common benign anorectal causes | Substantial decrease — removes the usual top of the ED differential |
| Digital exam so painful the finger is expelled | Proctitis | Large increase (Reza: ~40% → ~90%) |
| MSM, receptive intercourse, on PrEP, negative HIV 1 month ago | Infectious proctitis (GC, CT/LGV, syphilis, enteric); HIV | Raises STI causes; HIV prior lowered but re-checked |
| New multifocal vesicular + pustular rash, no prodrome | [[Mpox]] (outbreak, MSM, vesicular) | Raised strongly as leading unifier |
| Rash is non-dermatomal with a vesicular component | Narrow vesicular differential: [[HSV]], [[VZV]] | Raised deliberately alongside mpox — resists premature closure |
| HIV negative (repeat) | Immunosuppression blunting response | Down-weighted |
| Treponemal Ab positive + RPR 1:64 | [[Secondary Syphilis]] | Confirmed active syphilis; false positive judged implausible |
| Rash present with positive syphilis serology | Staging: secondary syphilis | Sets secondary stage; prompts neuro/ocular/otic screen |
| Mpox swab positive | Mpox proctitis | Confirmed — a *second*, independent diagnosis |
| Gonorrhea/chlamydia negative (already empirically treated) | GC/CT proctitis | Down-weighted, but coverage already given |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Anatomic localization as the first move.** Reza's opening contrast between "abdominal pain" (wide) and "rectal pain" (narrow) is a clean example of letting the chief concern define the relevant anatomy before generating a differential — which immediately prunes whole organ systems and focuses the workup.
- **Explicit pretest→posttest updating.** Naming the proctitis probability out loud (~40% → ~90%) after the finger-expulsion finding models disciplined Bayesian reasoning and makes visible *which* datum actually moved the estimate.
- **Resisting [[Availability Heuristic|availability]] and the outbreak anchor.** Despite being primed for mpox, Reza explicitly refuses "MSM + rash = mpox" and forces the differential open using rash morphology. This is the counter-move to [[Premature Closure]] driven by a salient, top-of-mind epidemic.
- **[[Correlated Risk|Correlated-risk reasoning]].** "If you're at risk of one STI, you're at risk of another" (paired with the vitamin-deficiency analogy) is why syphilis and HIV were sent alongside gonorrhea/chlamydia — and why a positive syphilis test did not end the search. This principle directly predicted the mpox co-infection.
- **One positive test ≠ case closed.** The positive RPR was treated as necessary but not sufficient; holding the differential open past the first hit is exactly what caught the second, independent diagnosis.
- **Test interpretation against pretest probability.** The two-arm syphilis serology is interpreted structurally (lifelong treponemal marker vs. quantitative, disease-tracking RPR), and the possibility of a false-positive RPR is weighed against — and dismissed by — a compatible sexual history and a high 1:64 titer.
- **Morphology and distribution do the discriminating work.** Vesicular-versus-macular/papular and dermatomal-versus-scattered are used as genuine discriminators, narrowing an enormous rash differential to a short, actionable list.
- **Systems thinking beyond the diagnosis.** The reasoning folds in isolation logistics, PPE, contact tracing, contact prophylaxis, and the patient's experience of stigma — and the debrief is honest that bureaucratic friction can dominate and even overshadow the clinical reasoning in real time.

## Teaching Discussion

- **The chief concern's anatomy should set the search space before the differential is built.** Rectal pain, with tenesmus and defecation-worsened pain, is close to self-localizing; leaning on that early prevents an over-broad, unfocused workup and justifies dropping cardiopulmonary causes up front.
- **Inspecting the anus is not evaluating the rectum, and the external exam must be done properly.** The commonly missed anorectal lesions are small (sub-centimeter fissures, warts, hemorrhoids) and require active exposure; a normal external exam should never be taken as evidence against proctitis of the rectum above.
- **Normal vitals are weak evidence against proctitis.** Infectious proctitis frequently lacks any systemic inflammatory signature, so a reassuring temperature and white count should not deflate the hypothesis — and immunosuppression can further mask it.
- **Prefer a swab to a repeat painful exam, and test multiple sites.** Rectal, pharyngeal, and urine gonorrhea/chlamydia plus syphilis serology is the efficient, low-harm way to interrogate an infectious proctitis differential.
- **PrEP is HIV-specific.** Effective HIV prophylaxis provides no protection against other STIs; the broader STI workup and empiric coverage remain fully indicated in a PrEP-adherent patient.
- **Know the current empiric regimen and its rationale.** Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM (single dose) for gonorrhea plus doxycycline 100 mg BID × 7 days for chlamydia is current CDC-aligned therapy; doxy-PEP (200 mg within 72 hours of sex) is the related 2024 prevention strategy for high-risk MSM/transgender women.
- **Two-arm syphilis serology answers two questions.** The treponemal-specific antibody is a lifelong "ever-infected" marker; the non-treponemal RPR/VDRL is quantitative, tracks active disease, and is followed for treatment response. Titer magnitude matters (1:64 high, 1:4 low), and false positives should be weighed against pretest probability rather than assumed.
- **Syphilis can imitate almost any rash and can involve the CNS/eye/ear at any stage.** Even when the morphology is atypical (e.g., pustular), keep syphilis in the frame, and always screen for neuro/ocular/otic symptoms that would mandate LP and alter treatment duration.
- **Warn before you treat: the Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction.** Fever, chills, and hypotension within hours of penicillin reflect treponemal killing, not treatment failure or allergy — anticipating it prevents mismanagement.
- **Mpox is on the MSM proctitis differential and can lack the classic prodrome.** Its rash can be vesicular/pustular and multifocal; management is as much operational (isolation, PPE, swab, ID, contact tracing, post-exposure vaccination) as diagnostic, and orthopoxvirus cross-protection underlies smallpox-vaccine use.
- **Vesicles narrow the differential; use them.** A vesicular component pulls toward HSV and VZV (including disseminated varicella), which can also cause proctitis — unroof a lesion for the herpesviruses (mpox needs no unroofing) rather than pattern-matching on the outbreak alone.
- **A shared risk factor is a portal to correlated risks, and one positive test doesn't close a multi-pathogen story.** Finding syphilis did not end the case; deliberately holding the differential open is what caught the mpox co-infection.
- **The reasoning doesn't end at the diagnosis.** Isolation logistics, contact tracing, prophylaxis for contacts, and the patient's experience of stigma are part of the case — and, as the debrief candidly notes, real-world bureaucratic friction can dominate the encounter and deserves its own attention.
