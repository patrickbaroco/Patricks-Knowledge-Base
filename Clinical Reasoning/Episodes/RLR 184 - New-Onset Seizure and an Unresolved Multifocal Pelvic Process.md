## Pearls

- **"Unresponsive" presumes breathing — which presumes a pulse — so quantity of blood to the brain is adequate, and the question becomes quality versus an intrinsic brain problem.** Someone with enough brainstem function to breathe but not to wake is unlikely to be globally hypoperfused; investigate **[[Quantity vs Quality of Blood|blood quality]]** (BUN, alcohol level, ammonia/liver failure) with basic tests, but a **deep** encephalopathy — and any **focality** — should raise a **primary intracranial process** through the **[[Altered Mental Status|intrinsic-vs-extrinsic]]** model.
- **The environment dates the illness.** A room strewn with feces in multiple places signals a **[[Illness Tempo|subacute]]**, serious decline rather than an acute apoplectic event — the scene is data. (Prehospital, "found down" management is glucose, naloxone for pinpoint pupils/low respiratory rate, and airway protection; arriving without a trauma/high-level code activation is a reassuring prognostic sign that the airway was being protected.)
- **A focal neurologic sign that is an *excess* (rhythmic movement), in an encephalopathic patient, is a seizure until proven otherwise — film it and get an EEG.** Metabolic derangements (hypo-/hyperglycemia) can be focal, but the combination of **underwhelming labs and focality** points intrinsic; capturing the event on video and confirming with **[[EEG|EEG]]** matters when the bedside read (here, "psychomotor agitation") disagrees. Every **[[New-Onset Seizure|new adult seizure]]** needs an MRI, and many need an LP.
- **Seizure versus altered mental status is a question of the *nature of the vulnerability* — and a new adult focal seizure means "find the focal lesion."** Most systemic insults (a UTI, a pneumonia) only confuse or seize a brain that is already vulnerable — diffuse neurodegeneration underlies most AMS, a **focal structural lesion** underlies most adult seizures — and only a septic-shock-level insult seizes a truly healthy brain. The most common cause of an acquired seizure in an older adult is **[[Stroke|stroke]]**; the **no-miss** with fever and a temporal-lobe seizure is **[[HSV Encephalitis|HSV encephalitis]]** in an otherwise immunocompetent older person.
- **The pelvis, mediastinum, and retroperitoneum are danger zones — they hold enormous disease silently because of their reserve.** A dulled systemic fingerprint (blunted fever/vitals) can hide a **[[Prostate Abscess|prostate abscess]]**, a **6 cm [[Bladder Stone|bladder stone]]**, and a **[[Rectal Mass|rectal mass]]** all at once; a CT with weak *a priori* justification here proved decisive, a reminder to image the **[[Danger Zones|deep compartments]]** when the syndrome is systemic and unexplained.
- **When you don't yet know the problem, don't try to solve it — remain open rather than commit to a hunch.** It is too soon for pretest probabilities before the data are in; the multifocal pelvic findings may be **[[Hickam's Dictum|true-true-and-unrelated]]** or contiguously linked (a **[[Colovesical Fistula|recto-prostatic fistula]]**, hematogenous seeding), and the honest move is to **[[Diagnostic Restraint|gather every piece]]** — MRI brain and pelvis, biopsy, cultures, endoscopy — and tie it together in retrospect rather than forcing a unifying story early.

## Problem Representation

A 70-year-old socially isolated, frail man with a presumed history of alcohol use (later found to drink little; alcohol level nil) was found unresponsive in a feces-strewn room on a welfare check, indicating a subacute decline; he arrived protecting his airway with a normal glucose and vitals. In the emergency department he developed rhythmic left-sided movements with impaired awareness, and — against underwhelming laboratory studies (normal CBC and electrolytes, creatinine 1.2, alcohol nil, urine tox positive only for cannabinoids) — an EEG confirmed right temporal epileptiform activity corresponding to the movements, establishing a new-onset focal (temporal-lobe) seizure treated with levetiracetam and fosphenytoin. His course was complicated by a probable left-base aspiration pneumonia and a multidrug-resistant (Bactrim-sensitive) *E. coli* urinary tract infection, both of which improved with antibiotics, and by transient focal groin pain. A CT of the pelvis revealed three findings — a 2×3 cm prostate abscess, a chronic 6 cm bladder stone, and a new 3×4 cm rectal mass concerning for malignancy — raising the possibility of contiguous recto-prostatic disease (fistula) versus independent processes. As of this episode the diagnosis is unresolved: MRI of the brain and pelvis, prostate abscess aspiration, and rectal-mass biopsy are pending, and the unifying explanation (if any) for the seizure and the pelvic pathology remains to be determined.

## Case Narrative

*This is an actively unfolding case that Reza's colleague (Eric) brought to him; the episode ends before the diagnosis is known, with a promise to share the MRI and biopsy results on a later episode. The value here is the reasoning, not a resolution.*

The scenario is stark. A **70-year-old** man, socially isolated and monitored by a social worker because he is vulnerable, cannot be reached; a welfare check by VA police finds him **unresponsive in a room filled with feces**. Rabih begins with the physiology of the word *unresponsive*: because bystanders called him unresponsive rather than not breathing, he is presumably **breathing — and therefore has a pulse** — so **[[Quantity vs Quality of Blood|quantity of blood]] to the brain is adequate** (a person with enough brain function to breathe but not to wake is unlikely to be globally hypoperfused). That reframes the problem toward **quality of blood** (elevated BUN, a high alcohol level, liver failure) versus a **primary intracranial** process, and the **depth** of the encephalopathy heightens concern for the latter. He notes the prehospital essentials (glucose, naloxone for pinpoint pupils and a low respiratory rate, airway protection) and reads two reassuring signs: the patient **arrived protecting his airway** without a trauma or high-level code activation. Finally, he uses the **scene to date the illness** — feces in multiple places implies a **[[Illness Tempo|subacute, serious decline]]**, not an acute apoplectic event.

In the ED, the **point-of-care glucose, vitals, and airway are fine**. Shortly after arrival he develops **rhythmic movements of the left side of the body** with his eyes open but no communication. Rabih synthesizes two threads — **underwhelming bloodwork** and **focality** — into concern for something **intrinsically wrong with the brain**. Working the **[[Altered Mental Status|AMS intrinsic-vs-extrinsic]]** model, extrinsic causes (insufficient blood, impossible while breathing; poor-quality blood, so far unrevealing) recede, and the **focal neurologic sign** — importantly an **excess** (a movement disorder) rather than a deficit — makes him **[[New-Onset Seizure|worry most about seizure]]**. He would treat it as a seizure and exonerate that before entertaining alternatives (a movement disorder localizing elsewhere; metabolic focality from hypo-/hyperglycemia). The team gives **Ativan** and the movement breaks; the labs are reassuring (normal CBC and electrolytes, **creatinine 1.2**, **alcohol nil**, urine tox positive only for **cannabinoids**), and — crucially — the event was **filmed**. Neurology, viewing the video, initially favors **psychomotor agitation**, but the **[[EEG|EEG]]** shows **right temporal epileptiform activity** corresponding to the movements. He is loaded on **levetiracetam and fosphenytoin**.

Two infections accrue. A **urinalysis** with pyuria and nitrites prompts **ceftriaxone**; then, on the floor, he becomes **hypoxemic** with a **left-base consolidation** (possible **[[Aspiration Pneumonia|aspiration pneumonia]]**), **spikes a fever**, and his white count rises 11→14k, prompting **vancomycin/piperacillin-tazobactam**, after which he defervesces and begins to wake. Urine cultures grow a **[[Multidrug-Resistant E. coli|multidrug-resistant *E. coli*]]** (resistant to fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins, penicillins; **sensitive to Bactrim**). Awake, he reports **focal groin pain**, but a CT is delayed when he refuses, and by the next day the pain has resolved; he also declines a brain MRI over sentimental earrings.

Rabih steps back to reason about **causation**. The seizure is the one thread that remains genuinely open: an aspiration event and pyuria he can explain, but **why a new-onset adult seizure?** He teaches the discriminator: **most systemic insults only confuse or seize a *vulnerable* brain** — the vast majority of patients with a UTI or pneumonia don't seize, and those who get confused from a UTI do so because it is "attacking a vulnerable brain." **Seizure versus AMS is a difference in the nature of the vulnerability**: diffuse neurodegeneration underlies most AMS, a **focal structural lesion** underlies most adult seizures (in the young, a subradiological or genetic focus), and only a **septic-shock-level** insult seizes a healthy brain. Therefore a new adult focal seizure means **find the focal lesion**: the most common cause is **[[Stroke|stroke]]**, and the **[[HSV Encephalitis|HSV encephalitis]]** possibility — fever plus a **temporal-lobe** seizure in an immunocompetent older person — is the **no-miss emergency**; everything else can wait. On the focality question, he notes that **all seizures begin somewhere and then generalize** (we're just not always privy to the onset), and that the sick **temporal lobe** here is "waiting for the wrong thing to happen."

The CT — pushed by the colleague's worry about the groin pain and a possible structural hip or vascular lesion, and obtained after an episode of **hematochezia** — is read out one finding at a time. First, a **[[Prostate Abscess|2×3 cm prostate abscess]]**: Rabih reasons that most prostatic abscesses arise **contiguously** (prostatitis in the setting of UTIs, plausible here given the MDR organism and under-treated infection), but the **widespread systemic syndrome** keeps **hematogenous** seeding open, prompting **[[Blood Cultures|blood cultures]]** and a full survey **including the spine**. Second, a **6 cm [[Bladder Stone|bladder stone]]** (5 cm a year earlier): chronic, from **stasis**, most often due to **[[Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia|BPH]]**/bladder outlet obstruction, large enough to require internal lithotripsy. Third, a new **3×4 cm [[Rectal Mass|rectal mass]]**: **[[Rectal Cancer|rectal cancer]]** is the leading concern, but with so much contiguous pelvic pathology he keeps open a malignant *or* infectious rectal process and a **[[Colovesical Fistula|recto-prostatic/colovesical fistula]]** — and, given the brain seizure, even a **[[Brain Abscess|brain abscess]]** or **[[Brain Metastasis|metastasis]]**.

Rabih's honest gestalt closes the episode. The **statistical unifier** might be "**infection NOS**," but the patient's **dulled systemic fingerprint** makes him wonder about **[[Hickam's Dictum|true-true-and-unrelated]]** processes. His strongest teaching is one of **[[Diagnostic Restraint|restraint]]**: *"If you're tempted to solve this problem, you're making a mistake — you don't know what the problem is yet. It's too soon to have pretest possibilities."* He would **remain open to all possibilities**, gather everything (MRI brain and pelvis, prostate aspiration, rectal biopsy, endoscopy, chest imaging, cultures), and let time exonerate the dangerous dynamic diagnoses — noting that a **week** of a non-progressive neurologic course already argues against untreated HSV encephalitis. He and Reza also reflect on the **value of the CT** (without it the prostate abscess would have gone unknown) and on the **[[Danger Zones|pelvis and mediastinum]]** as compartments that "hold so much disease without it being obvious." The case is left explicitly **unresolved**, to be continued.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Quantity vs Quality of Blood|Quantity vs quality of blood]] to the brain.** The opening move — breathing implies a pulse implies adequate perfusion, so shift to blood quality vs intrinsic disease — is a clean **[[Altered Mental Status|AMS]]** organizing principle that immediately narrows the field.
- **[[Illness Tempo|Tempo]] read from the scene.** The feces-strewn room dates a subacute decline; environment is diagnostic data, not just background.
- **Focal *excess* → seizure, confirmed against a discordant bedside read ([[Test Interpretation|test interpretation]]).** A movement is an excess, not a deficit; video plus **[[EEG|EEG]]** overturned the "psychomotor agitation" impression and localized to the right temporal lobe.
- **The vulnerability model of seizure vs AMS ([[Schema-Based Reasoning|schema]]).** Systemic insults exploit a vulnerable brain; the *kind* of vulnerability differs (diffuse neurodegeneration → AMS; focal structural lesion → adult seizure). Hence a new adult focal seizure mandates **finding the lesion** — **[[Stroke|stroke]]** (most common) and the no-miss **[[HSV Encephalitis|HSV encephalitis]]**.
- **[[Anatomical Approach|Anatomical]] "danger zones."** The **[[Danger Zones|pelvis, mediastinum, and retroperitoneum]]** hold large disease silently; a low-*a priori* CT was decisive, illustrating the value of imaging the deep compartments in an unexplained systemic syndrome.
- **[[Hickam's Dictum|Occam vs Hickam]] under a dulled fingerprint.** "Infection NOS" is the tidy unifier, but blunted fever/vitals across prostate, lung, urine, rectum, and brain make **true-true-and-unrelated** a live possibility; the case refuses a premature single story.
- **[[Diagnostic Restraint|Diagnostic restraint]] / resisting [[Premature Closure|premature closure]].** The signature teaching: when the problem is not yet defined, do not try to solve it, do not assign pretest probabilities prematurely — gather data and synthesize in retrospect. **[[Diagnostic Humility|Humility]] and time** (a non-progressive week) become diagnostic tools.

## Teaching Discussion

- Start "found down" reasoning from the word unresponsive: because the patient is breathing he has a pulse and adequate cerebral perfusion, so weigh blood quality (BUN, alcohol, ammonia/liver) against a primary intracranial process, and let the depth of encephalopathy and any focality push you intrinsic.
- Use the environment to establish tempo — a soiled, disordered scene indicates a subacute, serious decline rather than an acute event — and recall that prehospital management of the undifferentiated obtunded patient is glucose, naloxone when opioid signs are present, and airway protection.
- Treat a focal neurologic excess (rhythmic movement) in an encephalopathic patient as a seizure until proven otherwise: capture it on video, obtain an EEG when the bedside impression is uncertain, and remember that every new adult seizure warrants MRI and often an LP.
- Distinguish seizure from altered mental status by the nature of the underlying vulnerability — diffuse neurodegeneration for most AMS, a focal structural lesion for most adult seizures — and understand that systemic insults usually only unmask a vulnerable brain, so a new adult focal seizure obligates a search for the focal lesion.
- Keep stroke (the most common acquired cause of seizure in older adults) and HSV encephalitis (the no-miss emergency with fever and temporal-lobe seizures in an immunocompetent host) at the front of the differential, and use time as a test: a non-progressive neurologic course over a week argues strongly against untreated HSV encephalitis.
- Respect the deep body compartments — pelvis, mediastinum, and retroperitoneum — as danger zones that harbor large disease with a blunted systemic signature, and image them when a systemic syndrome remains unexplained.
- Interpret a prostate abscess as usually contiguous (prostatitis with UTI, especially with resistant organisms), but keep hematogenous seeding open when the illness is systemic, prompting blood cultures and a broader survey including the spine; read a giant bladder stone as chronic stasis (often from BPH), and a rectal mass as malignancy until proven otherwise while considering an infectious mimic and a recto-prostatic/colovesical fistula.
- Practice diagnostic restraint when the problem is not yet defined: resist assigning pretest probabilities or forcing a unifying diagnosis prematurely, remain open to true-true-and-unrelated processes, gather the definitive data (MRI brain and pelvis, biopsy, cultures, endoscopy), and synthesize in retrospect.
