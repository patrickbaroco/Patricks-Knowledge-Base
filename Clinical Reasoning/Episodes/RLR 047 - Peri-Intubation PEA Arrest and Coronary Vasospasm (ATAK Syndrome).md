> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 67-year-old woman presenting for elective rhinoplasty loses her pulse moments after sedation and intubation, arresting in **[[pulseless electrical activity|PEA]]**; two rounds of CPR and 2 mg of epinephrine achieve return of spontaneous circulation, and she arrives post-ROSC. Rabih reframes the arrest through a schema in which **the code-blue differential is just an extreme form of the shock differential** — PEA maps onto abrupt distributive, hypovolemic, or obstructive shock (plus [[hyperkalemia]]/acidosis), while VF/VT is the "cardiogenic" branch — and works the peri-intubation causes (hypoxemia, [[pneumothorax]], [[anaphylaxis]], [[air embolism]], succinylcholine [[hyperkalemia]]). The field EKG shows an **inferior [[STEMI]] with [[complete heart block]]** (ST-elevation II/III/aVF with III>II implicating the [[right coronary artery|RCA]], reciprocal depression in I/aVL, and puzzling V3–V4 elevation), yet the **coronary angiogram is completely clean**. An echo reveals **[[Takotsubo cardiomyopathy]]** (apical ballooning, EF ~40%), and the ST changes recur, resolve with nitroglycerin, and normalize entirely between episodes. The unifying diagnosis is **[[coronary vasospasm]] plus Takotsubo — the [[ATAK complex]]** (Adrenaline, Takotsubo, Anaphylaxis, Kounis) — triggered by the autonomic shifts of sedation/intubation and amplified by epinephrine, and treated empirically with **[[calcium channel blocker|calcium channel blockers]]** and nitrates. The reasoning spine is the **interepisodic EKG** (abnormal → normal → abnormal) that captures a coming-and-going process.

**Pearls**

- **The [[code blue]] schema *is* the [[shock]] schema, extreme.** PEA = abrupt distributive / hypovolemic / obstructive shock (plus [[hyperkalemia]] and acidosis); VF/VT is the cardiogenic branch, identified instantly by rhythm. Translating "PEA" this way turns the H's and T's into a coherent hypotension differential.
- **A heart attack can kill through PEA, not only VF/VT** — via profound bradycardia/heart block or a mechanical complication (e.g., ruptured VSD) — so ST-elevation in a PEA arrest is not a contradiction.
- **ST-elevation alone has a broad differential; ST-elevation in a vascular territory *with reciprocal depression* is ~95% specific for transmural occlusion.** III>II elevation points to [[right coronary artery|RCA]]/RV involvement (get right-sided leads).
- **A clean cath does not exclude an occlusive-looking ACS.** The differential for [[cath-negative ACS]] is recanalization (a lysed thrombus/embolus), [[microvascular dysfunction]], and — most commonly — **[[coronary vasospasm]]**; mimics include [[Takotsubo cardiomyopathy|Takotsubo]], [[pulmonary embolism|PE]], and tamponade.
- **[[Takotsubo cardiomyopathy]] is, in essence, catecholamine-driven vasospasm of the whole left ventricle** — apex stunned, base hyperdynamic (± LVOT obstruction) — which is why it shares triggers (emotional/physical stress, **epinephrine**) with focal [[coronary vasospasm]].
- **Interepisodic testing clinches episodic disease.** The alternating **abnormal → normal → abnormal** EKGs, nitro-responsiveness, and complete normalization between attacks are the signature of vasospastic ([[Prinzmetal angina|Prinzmetal/variant]]) angina; provocative [[acetylcholine]] testing is confirmatory but unnecessary when the pretest probability is high.

**Problem Representation**
A 67-year-old woman with peri-intubation PEA arrest and a transient inferior STEMI-pattern with complete heart block but angiographically normal coronaries, found to have Takotsubo cardiomyopathy with recurrent, nitro-responsive coronary vasospasm — the catecholamine-mediated ATAK complex.

## Case Narrative

Reza presents the case as it unfolded. A 67-year-old woman arrives in the OR suite for an **elective rhinoplasty**; anesthesia sedates and intubates her, and moments later she has **no pulse** — **[[pulseless electrical activity|PEA]] arrest**. Two rounds of CPR and 1–2 mg of epinephrine restore spontaneous circulation, and she is brought to the emergency department.

Rabih opens on the meta-question: **is this really unexpected?** The critical retrospective task is to decide whether a *hidden preoperative vulnerability* (say, silent coronary disease unmasked by surgical stress) drove the arrest, or whether it arose purely from the peri-procedural seconds-to-minutes. He makes his starting assumption explicit — that she is otherwise healthy — while flagging it as an assumption, not a fact.

He then delivers the episode's organizing schema. **Shock and pulselessness are the same differential at different extremes.** Shock branches into cardiogenic, distributive/septic, obstructive, and hypovolemic; pulselessness branches into VF/VT versus PEA. The mapping: VF/VT *is* the cardiogenic branch (identified instantly by rhythm), and PEA absorbs the distributive, hypovolemic, and obstructive branches — **plus** the crucial additions of **[[hyperkalemia]] and acidosis**. So "PEA arrest" translates into *abrupt-onset distributive, hypovolemic, or obstructive shock, or a metabolic/electrolyte catastrophe.* Applied to a peri-intubation arrest, the live possibilities are succinylcholine-induced **[[hyperkalemia]]**, respiratory **acidosis**, **hypoxemia** (right-mainstem intubation with lung collapse), **[[pneumothorax]]** from mechanical ventilation, **[[anaphylaxis]]** to induction medications, and **[[air embolism]]** from IV placement.

For the post-ROSC evaluation he sequences: **neurologic exam** (to guide cooling and prognosis) → **update the family** → **EKG** → **point-of-care labs** (hemoglobin, potassium, calcium, magnesium) → **[[point-of-care ultrasound|POCUS]]** for the obstructive triad ([[pneumothorax]], [[pulmonary embolism|PE]], tamponade). What evades ultrasound, CT, and basic labs — a **toxin/medication** effect — is the tier-two consideration.

**The field EKG** (rate 37) is where the case pivots. Rabih models a **reflex read** first: a very low rate with atrial activity of uncertain origin, and immediately eye-catching **ST elevation in II/III/aVF**. Slowing down, he finds **reciprocal ST depression in I/aVL** and the confirmatory but confusing **ST elevation in V3–V4**. Two nuances: the PR interval keeps changing while the ventricular rate stays fixed — **[[complete heart block]]** (to be confirmed with calipers) — and **ST elevation in III greater than in II**, which localizes to the **[[right coronary artery|RCA]] with RV involvement** (prompting right-sided leads). He reasons that the V3 elevation could reflect a **right-dominant [[posterior descending artery|PDA]]** wrapping around the apex to reach the precordial leads. His verdict: **ST elevation with reciprocal depression is diagnostic of an acute transmural MI (~95%)** — ST elevation *alone* has a differential, but ST elevation in a vascular distribution *with reciprocal change* has almost none — here an **inferior STEMI complicated by complete heart block**. The cath lab is activated.

Then the twist: the coronary angiogram is **completely clean** — no plaque rupture, thrombus, dissection, or stenosis. Rabih first calibrates the base rate: death from MI is *usually* via VF/VT, but MI can cause **PEA** through **bradycardia/heart block** or a **mechanical complication** (ruptured VSD), so the PEA-with-STEMI story is internally consistent. Confronted with a clean cath against findings this specific for occlusion, he refuses to discard the ischemia: **"I don't buy the cath."** The differential for **[[cath-negative ACS]]** becomes recanalization (a thrombus/embolus that lysed before angiography), **[[microvascular dysfunction]]**, and — most commonly — **[[coronary vasospasm]]**, with **[[Takotsubo cardiomyopathy|Takotsubo]]**, **[[pulmonary embolism|PE]]**, and tamponade as the principal mimics. He notes the well-described association of **vasospasm with epinephrine**, and reaches for a formal echocardiogram to address Takotsubo, effusion, and a large PE.

The subsequent data confirm the trajectory. A repeat EKG **10 hours later is normal sinus rhythm with no abnormalities**. The **echo shows Takotsubo features** — apical ballooning, EF ~40%. On hospital day 3 she has chest pain and **"EKG number one is back"** — the inferior STEMI pattern recurs — then **resolves with nitroglycerin** and normalizes shortly after. Rabih synthesizes toward a *single* process: the echo is hard to argue with for [[Takotsubo cardiomyopathy|Takotsubo]], but the full syndrome — **cath-negative, nitro-responsive, EKG-normalizing-between-attacks** chest pain — is dominated by **[[coronary vasospasm]]** (likely RCA), with Takotsubo understood as vasospasm writ large across the ventricle. He names the unifying entity from his own notes: the **[[ATAK complex]]** — **A**drenaline, **T**akotsubo, **A**naphylaxis, **K**ounis — the co-occurrence of Takotsubo and **[[Kounis syndrome]]** (allergic/anaphylaxis-associated coronary vasospasm), all **bound by catecholamines**, where epinephrine given for one can precipitate the other. He flags the spasm triggers to hunt on the medication and social history: **ergots/triptans, cocaine, and especially smoking**.

Reza fills in the team's reconstruction and the meta-lessons. Crucially, **she arrested *before* receiving any epinephrine** — during sedation itself — so the initiating trigger was the **autonomic shift of peri-intubation** (vasospasm classically occurs at night with high vagal tone; **hyperventilation** worsens it, and she was hyperventilated while intubated). The **epinephrine ×2** then layered on the **stress (Takotsubo) cardiomyopathy**. Management is empiric **[[calcium channel blocker|calcium channel blockers]]** ± nitrates; **provocative [[acetylcholine]] testing** can confirm vasospasm but is unnecessary when the pretest probability is high, and repeated catheterization is avoided (dissection risk). A durable pearl: in **[[Prinzmetal angina]]**, arteries with *some* fixed obstruction are at *increased* risk of spasm. Both hosts land on the reasoning signature they return to often — **interepisodic capture**: it is the *between-episode* EKGs (abnormal → normal → abnormal) that make this diagnosis. Reza is gently skeptical of the acronym itself ("like BRASH — naming the findings and calling it a syndrome"), but the physiologic unification stands. The patient — like Rabih's near-identical peripartum residency case — recovers within a day of calcium channel blockade and is discharged well.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The dominant discipline is **refusing to let a single "normal" test overturn a high-probability syndrome** — "I don't buy the cath." A negative angiogram is treated as a test with real false-negative modes (recanalization, spasm, microvascular disease) rather than as proof against ischemia, echoing the broader lesson that **absence of a finding rarely excludes a diagnosis when the pretest probability is high**. The case guards against **anchoring on a fixed atherosclerotic STEMI** by keeping the functional/dynamic causes (vasospasm, Takotsubo) live, and against **premature closure on Takotsubo alone** by noticing that the *syndrome* (nitro-responsive, EKG-normalizing) exceeds what stress cardiomyopathy explains. **Sequence-of-events reasoning** — she arrested *before* epinephrine — reassigns the initiating trigger from the drug to the autonomic perturbation of intubation. The **interepisodic** move (diagnose the process by what the EKG does *between* attacks) is the reusable reasoning tool, the same one used for other paroxysmal syndromes. Reza's skepticism toward the ATAK acronym is a healthy check against **mistaking a named cluster for a mechanism** — while conceding the catecholamine physiology that binds the parts.

## Teaching Discussion

**One schema for shock and for the code.** The highest-yield teaching is the unification: a code blue is severe, spectrum-end hypotension, so the **PEA differential is the non-cardiogenic shock differential** (distributive, hypovolemic, obstructive) plus **[[hyperkalemia]] and acidosis**, while VF/VT is the cardiogenic branch made visible by the monitor. This reframes the ACLS "H's and T's" from a memorized list into a physiologic hypotension schema, and it directs the post-ROSC work-up: neuro exam and family first, then EKG, point-of-care electrolytes/hemoglobin, and **[[point-of-care ultrasound|POCUS]]** for the obstructive trio, with toxins/medications as the elusive tier-two cause.

**Peri-intubation arrest has its own differential.** The minutes around induction generate specific mechanisms: **succinylcholine [[hyperkalemia]]**, respiratory **acidosis**, **hypoxemia** (right-mainstem intubation, lobar collapse), **[[pneumothorax]]** from positive-pressure ventilation, **[[anaphylaxis]]** to induction agents, and **[[air embolism]]** from line placement. Enumerating these is what lets the clinician later ask *which* peri-procedural insult — or which pre-existing vulnerability — actually fired.

**Reading the inferior STEMI.** The EKG teaching is a compact master class: **ST elevation with reciprocal depression** (here II/III/aVF up, I/aVL down) is far more specific for occlusion than ST elevation in isolation; **III>II** localizes to the **[[right coronary artery|RCA]]** with likely **RV involvement** (obtain right-sided leads, e.g., V4R); and a **right-dominant [[posterior descending artery|PDA]]** wrapping the apex can explain otherwise-puzzling precordial (V3–V4) elevation. Recognizing an accompanying **[[complete heart block]]** (dissociated, changing PR with fixed ventricular rate) both confirms the inferior territory (AV nodal supply from the RCA) and explains a bradycardic PEA.

**Cath-negative ACS is a diagnosis, not a dead end.** When the angiogram is clean but the electrocardiographic story screams occlusion, the differential is **recanalization** (a thrombus or embolus that lysed spontaneously before imaging), **[[microvascular dysfunction]]**, and above all **[[coronary vasospasm]]** — with **[[Takotsubo cardiomyopathy|Takotsubo]]**, **[[pulmonary embolism|PE]]**, pericarditis, and tamponade as mimics to actively exclude. The correct instinct is to trust a highly specific pattern over a single imperfect test.

**Takotsubo and vasospasm are catecholamine cousins.** [[Takotsubo cardiomyopathy]] can be conceptualized as **catecholamine-driven vasospasm of the entire left ventricle** — apical stunning with basal hyperkinesis and possible **LVOT obstruction** — which is why emotional/physical stress and **exogenous epinephrine** trigger both it and focal **[[coronary vasospasm]]**. Their co-occurrence, especially in the setting of anaphylaxis or adrenaline, is packaged as the **[[ATAK complex]]** (Adrenaline, Takotsubo, Anaphylaxis, **[[Kounis syndrome]]**), where **Kounis** is allergic/mast-cell-mediated coronary vasospasm and where treating one component with epinephrine can unmask another.

**Diagnosing and treating vasospasm.** [[Prinzmetal angina|Vasospastic (Prinzmetal/variant) angina]] favors **rest/nocturnal** onset (high vagal tone), is worsened by **hyperventilation**, and is triggered by **smoking, cocaine, and ergot/triptan** agents; paradoxically, vessels with *some* fixed plaque spasm more readily. It is **nitro-responsive**, and the diagnosis is often made by **interepisodic capture** — normal EKGs between abnormal ones. **Provocative [[acetylcholine]] testing** confirms it but is optional when pretest probability is high (young-ish patient, nitro-responsive, fully normalizing EKG), and empiric **[[calcium channel blocker|calcium channel blockers]]** ± long-acting nitrates are both therapeutic and diagnostic; repeat catheterization is minimized to avoid iatrogenic dissection.
