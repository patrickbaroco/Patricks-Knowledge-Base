## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Reza presents his own hospital team's case: a diabetic man with a single patch of left leg cellulitis whose story becomes genuinely strange when an unexpectedly ordered repeat CRP jumps from 30 to 300 and the previously unaffected right leg starts to hurt without any visible findings. A CT scan reasonably excludes necrotizing fasciitis, but the real twist arrives with the blood cultures: gram-negative rods, ultimately Pasteurella multocida from the patient's four cats, causing hematogenous multifocal cellulitis rather than a single localized skin infection. The episode closes with a decades-old Lawrence Tierney pearl — that cat-scratch-family infections don't require an actual scratch or bite — vindicated in real time.

## Pearls

- The first branch point in unilateral lower extremity swelling is symmetric versus asymmetric, but with a caveat: a truly symmetric process can still look worse on one side than the other, so "asymmetric" should really be understood as "one-side-dominant" rather than an assurance that the other side is completely uninvolved.
- At base rate, the two leading causes of unilateral leg erythema and edema are cellulitis (far more common) and DVT (which, in a clinician's own experience, less often produces overt erythema); a diagnostic threshold for cellulitis can reasonably be reached on history and exam alone (a vulnerable host, redness, warmth, fever) without waiting for labs.
- Shiny, non-uniform, warm redness in a patient with a clear predisposing vulnerability (diabetes, immunocompromise) is a strong enough clinical picture to favor cellulitis over DVT as the explanation for the redness itself, even though the same patient may separately warrant a DVT workup for other reasons (e.g., a recent major orthopedic surgery).
- DVT is not a medical emergency; because most emergency-department-diagnosed DVTs have already been present for several days by the time of diagnosis, it is often reasonable to treat presumed cellulitis first and use the clinical response over roughly 48 hours as a substitute for an immediate ultrasound, reserving imaging for cases that don't improve as expected.
- Elevating an edematous extremity being treated for cellulitis is not just comfort care — reducing tissue edema improves antibiotic penetration into the subcutaneous space, and marking the leading edge of erythema with a pen provides an objective way to track whether the process is truly worsening, since some initial worsening of visible redness is expected even with effective treatment.
- Outpatient oral antibiotics (e.g., amoxicillin-clavulanate or cephalexin) with clear safety-netting instructions are a reasonable option for uncomplicated cellulitis in a hemodynamically stable, non-toxic-appearing patient who prefers to avoid admission and further workup — a fever alone does not mandate hospitalization or IV therapy.
- An unexpectedly or reflexively ordered lab value (one not sent to test a specific hypothesis) should be interpreted cautiously, since an isolated abnormal result outside its original clinical context can send a workup down an unintended path; the corrective is to actively look for a plausible clinical explanation for the abnormality rather than either dismissing or over-interpreting it in isolation.
- A markedly rising CRP in a patient already being treated for a presumed diagnosis, especially combined with a new symptom in a previously uninvolved area, should prompt reconsideration of the working diagnosis rather than simply being attributed to the known process — particularly toward a more dangerous process like necrotizing fasciitis in a diabetic patient.
- The components of the LRINEC (necrotizing fasciitis risk) score — including glucose and sodium — require extra caution in diabetic patients, since hyperglycemia itself falsely lowers the measured sodium and needs correction before it can be meaningfully applied.
- A negative modern CT scan for necrotizing fasciitis carries a very high negative predictive value in published surgical-cohort data, making it a reasonable and reassuring test to obtain when clinical suspicion for necrotizing fasciitis is present but not overwhelming.
- Blood cultures have a low yield in uncomplicated cellulitis (well under 5% in published estimates) and should not be sent reflexively for every febrile skin infection; the decision to send them should be driven by how far along the SIRS/sepsis-severity spectrum the patient is (hypotension, a markedly elevated white count), not fever alone.
- When asked to guess the likely organism behind an uncomplicated skin and soft-tissue infection sight-unseen, "staph and strep" (gram-positive organisms in clusters or chains) is correct the vast majority of the time — a useful default that should only be abandoned when specific clues (an unusual exposure, an atypical culture result) argue otherwise.
- A useful mental model for typical bacterial flora: picture the body as a donut, with gram-positive organisms colonizing the outer ring (skin) and gram-negative organisms populating the inner ring (the GI and GU tracts) — infections tend to reflect whichever compartment was breached.
- Not every environmental ("exogenous") organism is an atypical, intracellular pathogen (like Rickettsia, Leptospira, or Histoplasma) — plenty of ordinary gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria live in the environment or on animals without normally colonizing human skin or gut, and a specific environmental exposure (e.g., cat contact) should be actively elicited rather than assumed away.
- Certain organisms with a particular tropism for skin and the ability to disseminate hematogenously (Pasteurella multocida from cat exposure; also described with group A Streptococcus) can cause genuinely separate, sequential foci of cellulitis at different body sites in the same bacteremic episode — true bilateral or multifocal cellulitis from a single hematogenous source is rare but real, and a new symptom at a second site during an active bacteremic cellulitis should be taken seriously as a possible new focus rather than dismissed as coincidental.
- Cat-associated skin and soft tissue infections (Pasteurella, Bartonella, and related organisms) do not require an obvious bite or scratch mark to occur — transmission via a simple lick onto broken or intact skin has long been described, and a clean exam without visible bite/scratch marks should not be used to rule out a cat-associated exposure.

## Problem Representation

An older man with diabetes and chronic venous stasis presented with one day of left lower extremity erythema, edema, warmth, and tenderness consistent with cellulitis, treated empirically with IV antibiotics, whose course was complicated the following day by an unexpectedly re-ordered CRP that jumped from mildly elevated to markedly elevated alongside new, exam-negative tenderness in the previously unaffected right leg — reasonably worked up for necrotizing fasciitis (excluded by CT) before blood cultures revealed gram-negative rods, ultimately Pasteurella multocida acquired from the patient's four cats, causing a true hematogenous multifocal cellulitis in which the new right leg symptom was an early second focus rather than an unrelated finding.

## Case Narrative

Reza presents a case from his own hospital medicine team: an older man with a history of diabetes (on metformin and a DPP-4 inhibitor) and long-standing venous stasis presents to the emergency department with one day of left lower extremity pain and redness.

Robbie's initial approach starts with the classic first branch point for lower extremity edema — symmetric versus asymmetric — while flagging the caveat that a genuinely symmetric process can still appear worse on one side, so the label "asymmetric" really just means one-side-dominant rather than a guarantee the other side is entirely spared. Applying base rate reasoning, he frames this as most likely one of two processes: cellulitis (by far the more common cause of unilateral erythema and edema, especially with a predisposing vulnerability like diabetes or a portal of entry such as tinea pedis or onychomycosis) or DVT (which, in his own clinical experience, less often produces true erythema, though venous hypertension can occasionally progress to phlegmasia cerulea dolens or, with superimposed arterial insufficiency, phlegmasia alba dolens). He also flags an anatomic differential worth keeping in mind — an occult fracture, a deeper infection like necrotizing fasciitis, or diabetic Charcot arthropathy from repetitive microtrauma in a patient who lacks protective sensation.

On evaluation, the patient is afebrile-adjacent (temperature 100.6°F) with otherwise normal vitals. His right leg shows only chronic venous stasis changes with no acute findings; his left leg has a non-circumferential patch of erythema and edema with irregular borders, extending from mid-ankle nearly to the knee, warm and tender to palpation.

Robbie states he has reached a diagnostic threshold for cellulitis based on the vulnerable host, redness, warmth, and fever alone. He organizes his thinking by categorizing it as suppurative versus non-suppurative (which affects antibiotic choice) and by severity (mild, moderate, or severe based on SIRS criteria and hemodynamics). His working plan: non-suppurative, mild-to-moderate cellulitis, most likely staphylococcal or streptococcal in origin, treated with at least ceftriaxone; elevate the leg to improve antibiotic penetration into edematous subcutaneous tissue; mark the leading edge of the erythema with a pen to objectively track progression, since some visible worsening is expected even with effective treatment before it starts to reverse; and, given the fever and immunocompromised status, send blood cultures. He notes the patient's normal blood pressure doesn't yet mandate pseudomonal coverage, though adding it (given the diabetes) wouldn't be unreasonable, while MSSA and streptococcal coverage are clearly indicated regardless.

Reza poses a hypothetical aside: if this same patient refused any workup and asked only to be treated empirically, what would Robbie do? Robbie reframes the question around why antibiotics are given at all — to prevent a subset of patients from progressing to severe sepsis, to shorten the duration of illness, and to improve symptoms — and concludes that, absent hypotension or other red flags, outpatient oral antibiotics (cephalexin more likely than amoxicillin-clavulanate) with clear return-precaution safety-netting would be entirely reasonable; most such patients do well.

Reza reveals this is a real inpatient from his own hospital team, and that he's relieved the patient wasn't simply discharged from the emergency department, given what follows. He asks whether Robbie would order a DVT ultrasound. Robbie initially says yes if the patient is being admitted, but reconsiders: given the strong clinical picture for cellulitis (vulnerability, shiny non-uniform redness, warmth), he wouldn't feel compelled to pursue DVT imaging purely for the leg findings themselves — noting that his own recent real patient with a similar presentation did get an ultrasound, but specifically because of a recent total hip arthroplasty raising independent DVT risk, not because the redness itself suggested DVT. Reza shares his own practice pattern: since DVT is not a medical emergency and most ED-diagnosed clots have already been present for days, watching the response to cellulitis treatment over about 48 hours can substitute for an immediate ultrasound in ambiguous cases.

The patient is admitted (mostly for social reasons, since nothing in the initial labs was alarming — white count 11, CRP 30, with a normal reference range under 5) and started on IV vancomycin with his leg elevated. The next day, in what Reza describes as an honest scheduling mistake, an intern accidentally re-orders another CRP, which comes back at 300 — a dramatic rise. The cellulitis exam is essentially unchanged, but the patient now reports new discomfort in his previously unaffected right leg, tender just above the ankle in a way that echoes how his left leg felt just before the redness first appeared there, though the right leg shows no visible abnormality on exam.

Robbie reflects on the general difficulty of interpreting a lab that wasn't sent to test a specific hypothesis, recalling a tangential case of his own — a patient with C. diff who had an unexpectedly elevated ESR and CRP that turned out to reflect an incidentally discovered glomerulonephritis (flagged by hematuria and proteinuria on a routine urinalysis) rather than the C. diff itself. Applying that same caution here, he takes the markedly elevated CRP together with the new, symptomatic-but-exam-negative right leg seriously: in a diabetic patient, this combination reflexively raises concern for necrotizing fasciitis, even though bilateral necrotizing fasciitis at two separate sites would be unusual, and even though diabetic patients with necrotizing fasciitis often present with fewer of the classic "hard signs" than immunocompetent patients with the more typical monomicrobial form (which usually starts internally and works outward). He recommends checking the sodium and calculating a LRINEC score, while flagging that both the glucose and (uncorrected) sodium components of that score are unreliable in a diabetic patient, since hyperglycemia itself artificially lowers the measured sodium.

The sodium comes back normal. Before Reza has formally staffed the case with his team, his resident — showing good independent judgment — orders a CT scan. Reza credits a locally cited San Francisco General Hospital study of roughly 30-35 surgically confirmed necrotizing fasciitis cases stratified by CT findings, showing that a negative modern CT carries essentially 100% negative predictive value for the diagnosis. The CT is reassuringly negative for necrotizing fasciitis, though it does show soft tissue stranding consistent with and reinforcing the cellulitis diagnosis.

The blood culture result becomes the case's real pivot point. Reza asks Robbie to predict, based on base rates, what the cultures are likely to show. Robbie explains that blood culture yield in simple urinary tract infection, community-acquired pneumonia, or uncomplicated cellulitis is quite low, and that the decision to send cultures at all should be driven by how far along the SIRS/sepsis spectrum a patient sits (hypotension, a markedly elevated white count) rather than fever alone — in this case, the fever and white count together likely tipped the ordering clinician's threshold. Asked to guess the organism, Robbie offers his standard teaching heuristic to trainees — "staph and strep," gram-positive organisms in clusters or chains — as correct roughly 99% of the time.

Reza reveals the actual result: two of two blood culture bottles grow gram-negative rods, with no abdominal symptoms, a normal urinalysis, and no abdominal history to suggest an obvious GI or GU source. Robbie reasons through the possibilities: gram-negative rod bacteremia most commonly reflects Enterobacteriaceae (E. coli, Klebsiella, Proteus) arising from a urinary source, but E. coli as a primary cause of skin and soft tissue infection would be unusual absent fecal contamination or a second, separate concurrent infection. Given the diabetes and the overall cellulitis picture, he leans toward Pseudomonas as his leading guess, while noting he'd need to reconsider if told the organism was specifically Klebsiella or E. coli.

Reza then offers a teaching frame to guide Robbie the rest of the way: picture the bugs and the body as a donut, with gram-positive organisms colonizing the outer ring (the skin) and gram-negative organisms populating the inner ring (the GI and GU tracts) — but cautions against the common assumption that any environmental ("exogenous") organism must be an atypical, intracellular pathogen (Rickettsia, Leptospira, Histoplasma, tuberculosis, COVID); plenty of ordinary environmental bacteria, gram-positive or gram-negative, simply don't normally live on human skin or in the human gut. Prompted toward his own cats (Sydney and Gino), Robbie arrives at the answer: Pasteurella multocida.

Reza confirms: the patient has four cats at home that scratch and bite him. The blood cultures grew Pasteurella multocida, an organism recognized for its ability to disseminate hematogenously and cause multifocal cellulitis — separate cellulitis foci at more than one site — rather than remaining confined to a single localized wound. True bilateral or multifocal cellulitis from a single infectious source is rare, since most cellulitis is assumed to represent one localized inoculation, but a small subset of organisms with a particular skin tropism and capacity for dissemination (Pasteurella here; Reza also recalls a prior group A streptococcal bacteremia case with sequential right leg and left arm cellulitis) can genuinely produce this pattern. In retrospect, the new right leg tenderness wasn't an incidental finding or diagnostic noise — it was an early warning sign of an emerging second cellulitis focus. The patient was treated with ceftriaxone, narrowed to amoxicillin-clavulanate, and did well; the exact inoculation site for the second leg was never determined (whether from a separate bite or scratch, or purely hematogenous spread from the first site), and the exact treatment duration was debated between the hosts (seven days versus the six days actually chosen).

Reza closes with a personal story from his third year of residency at the San Francisco VA, presenting a cat-exposure case at a morbidity-and-mortality-style teaching conference in front of Lawrence Tierney ("LT"), a legendary and by-then semi-retired mentor known for dozing during long conference sessions. Citing a pearl from LT's own published "Book of Pearls" — that cat-scratch-family infections don't require an actual scratch or bite, since transmission via a simple lick is well described — Reza offered that explanation for the case at hand, only for a freshly awakened LT to groggily contradict his own written teaching in the moment ("that doesn't sound right"), to Reza's mortification. Years later, this Pasteurella patient — who denies ever having been bitten or scratched by his cats — serves as a real-world vindication of LT's original written pearl, closing the loop on a formative, humbling teaching memory Reza still carries with him.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Data Point | Effect on Differential |
|---|---|
| Diabetic patient, unilateral erythema/edema/warmth/fever, non-circumferential irregular patch | Raises probability of cellulitis to a diagnostic threshold; lowers probability of DVT as the cause of the visible redness |
| Recent total hip arthroplasty (in the real clinical analog case referenced) | Independently raises probability of DVT, justifying ultrasound even when the redness itself favors cellulitis |
| CRP rising sharply from 30 to 300 with unchanged local exam | Raises concern that the initial diagnosis (uncomplicated cellulitis) is incomplete; raises suspicion for a deeper or disseminated process such as necrotizing fasciitis or bacteremia |
| New tenderness in the contralateral leg, exam otherwise normal | Raises concern for either bilateral necrotizing fasciitis or an emerging second infectious focus, rather than being dismissed as unrelated |
| Normal sodium, negative CT for necrotizing fasciitis (with stranding consistent with cellulitis) | Substantially lowers probability of necrotizing fasciitis given the high negative predictive value of a negative modern CT |
| Blood cultures positive for gram-negative rods, no GI/GU symptoms or history | Lowers probability of a typical Enterobacteriaceae/urinary source; raises probability of an atypical, non-enteric gram-negative organism and an alternative (non-GI/GU) exposure |
| History of 4 pet cats with scratching/biting behavior | Raises probability of Pasteurella multocida specifically; explains both the bacteremia and its capacity for hematogenous multifocal cellulitis |
| Absence of visible bite/scratch marks on exam | Does not lower probability of a cat-associated infection, since lick-only transmission is well described |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Symmetric-versus-asymmetric edema framework, with a nuance caveat**: using laterality as a first branch point while explicitly guarding against over-interpreting "asymmetric" as "unilateral only."
- **Reflexively-ordered-lab caution**: treating an unexpectedly obtained lab (the accidentally re-ordered CRP) as needing an actively sought clinical explanation rather than either dismissal or uncritical acceptance, illustrated by a tangential recalled case (C. diff patient with an incidental GN).
- **LRINEC-in-diabetics caveat**: recognizing that a validated risk score's individual components (glucose, sodium) can be systematically distorted by a patient's baseline comorbidity, requiring correction before the score is meaningfully applied.
- **Blood-culture-yield-by-syndrome base rate**: calibrating the decision to send cultures to the SIRS/sepsis severity spectrum rather than fever alone, reinforced by a directly researched statistic (<5% yield in uncomplicated cellulitis).
- **"Donut" flora model and the exogenous-does-not-equal-atypical correction**: two paired teaching devices used together to correctly localize an unusual gram-negative skin/soft-tissue pathogen without defaulting to a rare intracellular organism.
- **Multifocal-hematogenous-cellulitis pattern recognition**: recognizing that a new symptom at a second body site during an active bacteremic infection can represent a genuine second focus (a "signal, not noise" framing) rather than reflexively attributing it to an unrelated or benign cause — reinforced by a directly analogous recalled case (bacteremic group A strep with sequential leg-then-arm cellulitis).
- **Intergenerational pearl transmission and vindication**: a specific, written mentor teaching (cat licks can transmit infection without a bite/scratch) recalled, initially contradicted by the mentor himself in the moment, and ultimately validated by a real patient years later — reinforcing the show's recurring theme of case-based/mentor-sourced recall as a diagnostic trigger.

## Teaching Discussion

This case is built around resisting diagnostic closure at exactly the moment closure feels most natural: a straightforward cellulitis diagnosis, already reasonably reached and being appropriately treated, is not allowed to absorb two subsequent surprising data points (a dramatically rising CRP and a new contralateral symptom) without genuine re-examination. The team's response — working through necrotizing fasciitis as the more dangerous possibility, using an appropriately caveated LRINEC calculation and a well-evidenced CT negative predictive value to reasonably exclude it — models calibrated escalation rather than either premature reassurance or unwarranted alarm.

The blood culture result functions as the episode's central teaching pivot: rather than accepting a surprising gram-negative result as an isolated microbiologic curiosity, the reasoning process (the donut model, the exogenous-is-not-always-atypical correction, and directly asking about animal exposure) leads cleanly to the actual explanation and, retrospectively, reframes the contralateral leg symptom from an ambiguous finding into a meaningful early clue.

The closing personal anecdote reinforces a broader, recurring show theme: a specific, sometimes years-old piece of clinical teaching — even one initially doubted by its own source in the moment — can be exactly the piece of knowledge that resolves a real case much later, underscoring the durable value of collecting and remembering individual clinical pearls over time.
