> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 60-year-old man with a bewildering history — [[thrombotic microangiopathy]] of unknown cause, an undiagnosed [[lymphoproliferative disorder]], and a **splenectomy one week ago** for refractory thrombocytopenia — arrives with **sudden-onset severe epigastric pain radiating to the back**, BP 212/tachycardic. Rabih works acute abdominal pain through **[[VIPO framework|VIPO]]** (Vascular, Inflammation, Perforation, Obstruction) after first excluding the "neighbors" (chest — [[myocardial infarction|MI]]/[[pulmonary embolism|PE]]; pelvis), emphasizes the **vascular** branch given the abrupt onset and hypertension ([[aortic dissection]]), and — once the recent surgery reframes the case — pivots to **post-splenectomy complications** (OPSI, and especially **[[splenic vein thrombosis|splenic/mesenteric vein thrombosis]]**), ordering a CT with a **delayed venous phase**. The scan shows **no aortic pathology but a large splenic vein thrombosis and a golf-ball gallstone**; the first lipase and pancreas are **normal**. Surgery calls the thrombosis "expected" and not the pain source. The next day the **lipase returns at 800** — the diagnosis is **[[gallstone pancreatitis]]**, the one cause of pancreatitis so **hyperacute** that a patient can reach the ED *before the lipase rises* (which lags pain by hours), with an early **normal lipase and normal CT**. The reasoning spine: **hyperacute presentations are half-told stories** ("read half the book"), **expert opinion has a predictive value below 100%**, and the value of the **interval test** (repeat lipase / repeat scan).

**Pearls**

- **[[VIPO framework|VIPO]] for acute abdominal pain** — Vascular, Inflammation, Perforation, Obstruction — but only after excluding an *extra*-abdominal source: the **chest "attic"** ([[myocardial infarction|MI]], [[pulmonary embolism|PE]] with diaphragmatic irritation) and, occasionally, the pelvis (a distended bladder can reach the LUQ).
- **Abrupt pain + hypertension flags the vascular branch** — [[aortic dissection]] (e.g., descending, with renal-artery involvement) is the can't-miss — though hypertension and tachycardia are often just **pain**.
- **Exam below the belt line matters**: femoral pulses, **inguinal/femoral hernias**, testicular pathology, and a bladder scan — cheap maneuvers that catch missed causes.
- **The background rewrites the foreground.** A **[[splenectomy]] one week ago** converts "abdominal pain" into "post-laparotomy / post-splenectomy complication" — **[[overwhelming post-splenectomy infection|OPSI]]** and **[[splenic vein thrombosis|splenic/mesenteric vein thrombosis]]** (order a **delayed venous-phase** CT, which is not routine).
- **[[Gallstone pancreatitis]] has a unique hyperacute signature**: pain peaks within minutes (vs the hours-to-days onset of alcohol/hypertriglyceridemia/hypercalcemia), yet **lipase rises only hours after onset** — so a patient arriving early can have a **normal lipase and normal CT**. If pancreatitis is missed early, it is almost always gallstone. (Lipase is preferred over amylase partly for its longer half-life.)
- **"Read half the book."** Hyperacute syndromes may be **incomplete stories** — like a sentinel [[subarachnoid hemorrhage]] bleed, early-normal reflexes in [[Guillain-Barré syndrome]], or [[zoster sine herpete]] before the rash — so the **interval test** (repeat lipase, repeat scan) can complete the narrative.
- **Expert opinion is a test with predictive value < 100%.** "Trust the expertise of those who care for these patients — but not blindly"; keep asking whether they are *right and there's another cause*, or *wrong about this one*.

**Problem Representation**
A 60-year-old man one week post-splenectomy (for TMA/lymphoproliferative-associated refractory thrombocytopenia) with sudden severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, an incidental splenic vein thrombosis and large gallstone on an early CT with a normal lipase, ultimately diagnosed with hyperacute gallstone pancreatitis (plus anticoagulation for the thrombosis).

## Case Narrative

Reza presents the case "as you'd see it in the ED," withholding the past history at first. A 60-year-old man develops **sudden severe epigastric pain radiating to the back** at 8 p.m. and is in the ED within an hour; his systolic BP is **212**, heart rate ~105, and he looks comfortable upright with an otherwise unremarkable exam.

Rabih builds the frame for **abrupt abdominal pain**: first confirm it is truly intra-abdominal, because the belly has **neighbors** — the **chest "attic"** ([[myocardial infarction|acute MI]]; [[pulmonary embolism|PE]] with diaphragmatic irritation referring downward) and, less often, the **pelvis** (he has seen a bladder reach the LUQ). Then he runs **[[VIPO framework|VIPO]]** — **V**ascular (e.g., [[mesenteric ischemia]]), **I**nflammation (pancreatitis), **P**erforation, **O**bstruction (biliary, luminal, ureteral). Given the **abrupt onset and hypertension**, he foregrounds the **vascular** branch and the can't-miss **[[aortic dissection]]** (descending, with renal-artery involvement as a hypertension mechanism) — while noting hypertension/tachycardia are readily explained by pain alone. Immediately actionable: an **EKG**, a **bladder scan**, and exam **below the belt line** (femoral pulses, **inguinal/femoral hernias**, testicular pathology). His one five-second chart move would be to check for **known aortic pathology** — which, if present, sends the patient straight to CT before labs. Asked for his list of abdominal-pain diagnoses **makeable without imaging**, Rabih recites the (now eight-item, ever-growing) set: **zoster, rectus-sheath hematoma, pancreatitis, DKA, SBP, UTI, pregnancy, inferior MI, and urinary retention**.

The data arrive: RUQ palpation elicits **LUQ pain**; femoral pulses intact, no mass; bladder <200 cc; EKG **sinus tachycardia**, troponin negative. Labs show a **mild leukocytosis/neutrophilia, mild anemia, platelets 800,000**, baseline stage-3 CKD, **normal liver chemistries, normal lipase**, and reported nausea without vomiting. Then the loaded past history: a renal biopsy a year ago showed **[[thrombotic microangiopathy]]** of unclear cause; he later developed an **undiagnosed [[lymphoproliferative disorder]]** with splenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, and severe thrombocytopenia refractory to steroids and rituximab — leading to a **[[splenectomy]] one week before this presentation**.

Rabih makes the pivotal move: **the background rewrites the foreground.** Rather than force a chronic lymphoproliferative disease to explain a hyperacute event, he reframes around the **recent surgery** — any post-laparotomy abdominal pain earns a CT A/P (for occult bowel/vascular injury) — and, specifically, **post-[[splenectomy]] complications**. He organizes those into **infection** (**[[overwhelming post-splenectomy infection|OPSI]]** — encapsulated *Strep pneumo/Neisseria/H. influenzae*, mitigated by pre-op vaccination, but not protective against *Staph aureus*, gram-negatives like *Klebsiella*, or **[[Babesia]]** if transfused) and **vascular** (chronic post-splenectomy **[[pulmonary hypertension]]**; acutely, **[[splenic vein thrombosis|splenic/mesenteric vein thrombosis]]**, splenic arterial/venous injury, and bleeding). The practical consequence: he orders **not** a routine CT but a protocol with **arterial, portal, and a delayed venous phase** — the last precisely because it is *not* routine and is needed to catch **mesenteric/splenic vein thrombosis**.

The scan (and smear) return: a peripheral smear shows **schistocytes**; blood cultures no growth; and the multiphase CT shows **no aortic pathology, a large splenic vein thrombosis, and a large (golf-ball) gallstone** — but **no pancreatitis**. The overnight resident, unsure whether the thrombosis is expected or the pain source, appropriately calls surgery, who reply that a splenic vein thrombosis is **expected post-splenectomy, not treated, and not causing the pain** — "look for another cause."

Rabih first disposes of the **schistocytes**: with **normal-to-high platelets and no hemolysis**, this does *not* look like an active [[microangiopathic hemolytic anemia|MAHA]], and TMA (whose vascular injury is typically small-vessel — kidney, brain, heart — presenting as ischemic colitis when it hits the gut) is unlikely the dominant process; he'd loop in hematology but keep his enthusiasm low. On the thrombosis, he articulates the meta-lesson: an **expert's read has a predictive value below 100%** — "trust the expertise of those who care for these patients, but not blindly." The schema becomes: are they **right, and there's another cause**, or **wrong about this one**? He works the **[[CT-negative abdominal pain]]** differential ([[adrenal insufficiency]] — excluded by the hypertension; intermittent processes like torsion/volvulus/intussusception — and he'd ask whether the patient had pain *during* the scan), and would escalate to heme-onc/GI.

The reveal: the **next day the lipase returns at 800**. Rabih delivers the teaching that unlocks the case — the **"read half the book"** principle: hyperacute presentations can be **incomplete stories** (his examples: EBV cerebellitis that starts unilateral, [[Guillain-Barré syndrome]] with initially normal reflexes, [[zoster sine herpete]] before the rash, and a **sentinel [[subarachnoid hemorrhage]] bleed** preceding catastrophe). A lipase of 800 now explains the sudden epigastric-to-back pain, and the **golf-ball gallstone** re-enters — either causative or a marker of other stones — as **[[gallstone pancreatitis]]**. Reza closes with the crux he found in UpToDate: **most causes of acute pancreatitis (alcohol, hypertriglyceridemia, hypercalcemia) present over hours to days, but gallstone pancreatitis is uniquely hyperacute** — peak pain within ~20 minutes — while **lipase does not begin rising until hours after onset**. So the *only* acute pancreatitis you tend to miss early is **gallstone**, and only because the patient beat the enzyme to the ED; an early **normal lipase and normal CT** do not exclude it. (Lipase is favored over amylase in part for its **longer half-life**; an early trypsinogen-activation-peptide can flag pancreatitis before the lipase, if available.) The correct move was the one Reza admits he would *not* have made — a **repeat (interval) lipase** — the enzymatic parallel to the **interval CT** that reveals an evolving mesenteric thrombosis or early ischemia.

The disposition: hematology recommends **anticoagulation** for the splenic vein thrombosis after all — not because it usually needs treatment post-splenectomy, but because of the **large clot burden plus the hypercoagulable TMA history** — and the pain is treated as **gallstone pancreatitis**. As a coda, Rabih offers the **[[splenic vein thrombosis|mesenteric/splenic vein thrombosis]] schema**: first ask **is there portal hypertension?** (cirrhosis carries ~30% portal-vein-thrombosis risk, higher with HCC); if not, look for **local inflammation** (pancreatitis, IBD, appendicitis/diverticulitis → septic **pylephlebitis**); and if neither, suspect **malignancy or a hypercoagulable state** ([[polycythemia vera]], [[antiphospholipid syndrome|APLS]]). Usually the scan shows the thrombus *and* its cause in one snapshot — so an **image-negative mesenteric vein thrombosis** should push you toward an occult cancer, a hypercoagulable state, or (as here) a cause the scan was simply **too early** to show.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Two anchoring traps are named and defused. First, **"once negative, always negative"**: Reza confesses he would never have repeated a normal lipase — the enzyme was filed as settled — which is exactly why the **interval test** (repeat lipase, like a repeat troponin/UA, or an interval CT) is the corrective for **hyperacute, still-evolving** disease. Second, **deference to expertise**: the surgery team's (reasonable) "expected, not the cause" is treated as a **test with a predictive value below 100%**, prompting an explicit fork — *right with another cause* vs *wrong about this one* — rather than blind acceptance or reflexive doubt. The **background-rewrites-foreground** move prevents the opposite error of forcing a chronic lymphoproliferative narrative onto a hyperacute event. And Rabih models **calibrated dismissal of a red herring** (schistocytes without thrombocytopenia or hemolysis) — noting it should "take a lot" to set aside a finding, but that the absent MAHA features and the mismatched vascular bed justify it here. The unifying discipline is epistemic humility about **timing**: the story was only half-told at hour one.

## Teaching Discussion

**A schema for abrupt abdominal pain.** Before **[[VIPO framework|VIPO]]** (Vascular, Inflammation, Perforation, Obstruction), confirm the pain is intra-abdominal — the **chest** ([[myocardial infarction|MI]], [[pulmonary embolism|PE]]) and occasionally the **pelvis** can masquerade. Abruptness plus hypertension pushes the **vascular** branch and the **[[aortic dissection]]** rule-out to the front, and the **below-the-belt-line exam** (femoral pulses, hernias, testes) and bladder scan are high-value, low-cost maneuvers. The "diagnosable without imaging" list (zoster, rectus-sheath hematoma, pancreatitis, DKA, SBP, UTI, pregnancy, inferior MI, urinary retention) is a useful reminder that a CT is not the only path.

**The background is not context — it is data.** A recent **[[splenectomy]]** transforms the differential: any post-laparotomy abdominal pain warrants imaging for occult injury, and splenectomy specifically raises **[[overwhelming post-splenectomy infection|OPSI]]** (encapsulated organisms — vaccinate; but not fully protective, so consider *Staph*, gram-negatives, and **[[Babesia]]** if transfused) and a **vascular** cluster (chronic **[[pulmonary hypertension]]**; acute **[[splenic vein thrombosis|splenic/mesenteric vein thrombosis]]**, vascular injury, bleeding). The concrete downstream is a **CT protocol with a delayed venous phase**, which is not ordered by default and is what actually visualizes venous thrombosis.

**Gallstone pancreatitis is the hyperacute outlier — and lipase lags.** The high-yield pearl: whereas alcohol-, hypertriglyceridemia-, and hypercalcemia-related pancreatitis build over **hours to days**, **[[gallstone pancreatitis]]** is **hyperacute** (peak pain within minutes) because a stone acutely obstructs the pancreatic outflow; yet **serum lipase does not rise until several hours after onset** and CT can be normal that early. The practical corollaries: (1) the pancreatitis you miss early is almost always **gallstone**; (2) an early **normal lipase/CT** does not exclude it; and (3) the fix is the **interval lipase**. Lipase is preferred to amylase partly because of its **longer half-life** (it stays up when a late-presenting amylase may have normalized), and an early **trypsinogen-activation peptide**, where available, can precede the lipase.

**"Read half the book": hyperacute syndromes are incomplete stories.** A recurring RLR/VMR theme — the **sentinel bleed** before a devastating [[subarachnoid hemorrhage]], **normal early reflexes** in [[Guillain-Barré syndrome]], **[[zoster sine herpete]]** before the vesicles, unilateral-before-bilateral post-viral cerebellitis — is that a patient who **presents too soon** may show only the first chapter. The countermeasure is deliberate **serial evaluation**: the **interval lipase** here is the enzymatic analog of the **interval CT** that later reveals an evolving mesenteric thrombosis or early ischemia.

**Expert opinion is evidence, not proof.** A consultant's judgment ("expected, not the cause") carries genuine predictive weight but **not 100%**. The disciplined stance — *trust, but not blindly* — reframes the decision as a two-branch question (are they **right and something else is going on**, or **wrong about this finding**?) and keeps the search alive without disrespecting expertise. It applies symmetrically to the ED physician, too.

**A schema for splenic/mesenteric vein thrombosis.** First branch: **portal hypertension present?** (cirrhosis ≈ 30% PVT, higher with HCC). If not: **local inflammation/infection** (pancreatitis, IBD, appendicitis/diverticulitis → septic **pylephlebitis**). If neither: **malignancy or hypercoagulable state** ([[polycythemia vera]], [[antiphospholipid syndrome|APLS]]). Because imaging usually reveals the thrombus **and** its cause together, an **image-negative** mesenteric vein thrombosis is the head-scratcher that should trigger a hunt for occult cancer or thrombophilia — or the recognition, as here, that the scan was simply **too early** to show the pancreatitis that produced it. Management individualizes: routine post-splenectomy splenic vein thrombosis is often left alone, but a **large clot in a hypercoagulable host** earns **anticoagulation**.
