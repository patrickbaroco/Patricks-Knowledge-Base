## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Two short cases. An elderly, fully pacemaker-dependent man develops transient shaking and cold sensations with exertion two days after his electrophysiologist enables rate-responsive pacing — a change meant to finally let his heart rate rise with activity. Turning the feature off eliminates the symptoms, but the real answer is that his previously rate-limited, diseased conduction system had been silently protecting him from angina; letting his heart rate climb for the first time unmasked a 70% mid-LAD lesion, with cold, shaky "angina" as the atypical presenting symptom. Separately, a man with prostate adenocarcinoma on androgen deprivation therapy develops a growing peri-prostatic mass despite an undetectable PSA — not neuroendocrine dedifferentiation as feared, but malakoplakia, a mass-forming inflammatory pseudotumor from incompletely cleared recurrent UTIs, cured with three months of levofloxacin.

## Pearls

- When a symptom is described vaguely ("I don't feel right when I move"), analyze the chief concern before layering on the patient's known history — an episodic, activity-triggered syndrome should prompt an attempt to reproduce the symptom under direct observation before further reasoning, since watching it happen often yields more diagnostic information than any single test.
- When a temporal association exists between a trigger (like a medication or device change) and a new symptom, explicitly categorize the relationship into one of three buckets — the trigger is causal (100% responsible), contributory (responsible for only part of the picture, with something else present), or coincidental (0% responsible) — rather than defaulting to full causation or dismissing the association entirely.
- A pacemaker or conduction system that has been chronically limiting a patient's heart rate can inadvertently function as protection against exertional angina, by preventing myocardial oxygen demand from ever rising high enough to outstrip a stenotic coronary supply; restoring a normal rate response can "unmask" previously silent coronary artery disease, sometimes presenting with atypical anginal-equivalent symptoms (such as cold sensations and shakiness) rather than classic chest pain.
- In a patient with known prostate adenocarcinoma whose disease appears to be radiographically progressing despite a suppressed or undetectable PSA, consider neuroendocrine dedifferentiation of the tumor, since neuroendocrine cells do not secrete PSA the way glandular prostate tissue does — but confirm with biopsy before assuming the worst, since indolent neuroendocrine tumors (not just aggressive small cell transformation) can also occur.
- Malakoplakia is a mass-forming, inflammatory pseudotumor that can mimic malignancy on imaging, classically arising in the setting of chronic or recurrent urinary tract infection (most often E. coli); it results from incompletely cleared intracellular bacteria within histiocytes and is diagnosed by pathognomonic Michaelis-Gutmann bodies on special staining — a mass that looks ominous enough to prompt a hospice conversation may in fact be fully reversible with a prolonged, intracellularly active antibiotic course.
- Malakoplakia is not confined to the kidney or urinary tract — it can involve essentially any organ, and its pathophysiology sits between a classic pyogenic abscess (extracellular organisms) and granulomatous disease (organisms sequestered within macrophages), which likely explains why affected patients often lack the classic fevers, chills, and pain of a typical abscess despite harboring ongoing infection.

**Case 1:** An elderly, fully pacemaker-dependent man with a history of symptomatic bradycardia and a prior pacemaker (previously complicated by SVC syndrome) develops purely exertional shaking and cold sensations two days after his electrophysiologist enables rate-responsive pacing; a normal cardiopulmonary workup and complete symptom resolution when rate responsiveness is turned off initially suggest a device-related cause, but the true mechanism is unmasking of a previously silent 70% mid-LAD coronary stenosis, with atypical anginal-equivalent symptoms triggered by his newly restored ability to raise his heart rate with exertion.

## Case Narrative

### Case 1: Exertional Cold Sensations After a Pacemaker Adjustment

An elderly man with a history of symptomatic bradycardia (possibly age-related conduction system disease) had received a permanent pacemaker years earlier, an implant course previously complicated by SVC syndrome. He had been living an active, improving life — losing weight, wanting to take walks with his partner — but was limited because his heart rate would not rise with exertion, since he was fully pacemaker-dependent and the device was not programmed to respond to activity. His electrophysiologist adjusts the device to enable rate-responsive pacing — a feature that uses physiologic cues (such as respiratory rate or other surrogate markers of sympathetic activity) to increase the paced rate during activity. Two days after this adjustment, he presents to the ED reporting that every time he moves, he feels terrible: shaky and cold, though without any discomfort at rest.

The responding discussant frames this using a taught principle: analyze the chief concern (feeling unwell with movement) before incorporating the associated history, since "not feeling right when moving" could reflect cardiopulmonary strain under exertional demand, but could equally reflect a musculoskeletal trigger (recalling a prior RLR case of pacemaker-induced diaphragmatic stimulation) or another movement-triggered process such as BPPV. Because this is a clearly episodic, triggered syndrome, the single most useful next step is to have the patient reproduce the symptom under direct observation rather than reasoning further from history alone.

On further questioning, the patient clarifies: he feels off, cannot exert himself, gets shaky, and feels cold — without losing consciousness. Chart review reveals a prior thyroidectomy, with the patient maintained on levothyroxine. Physical exam is completely normal, without jugular venous distension; vitals are normal, and he is paced at 65 beats per minute. EKG shows every beat as sensed and paced, each with a corresponding pacer spike. Labs show a normal troponin and pro-BNP; chest X-ray shows only the pacemaker device, without pulmonary edema.

Clarifying further, the discussant confirms the symptoms are purely exertional — completely absent at rest, present only with movement and exertion — and, with no overt musculoskeletal findings on exam, reasons that the cardiopulmonary apparatus (rather than an isolated MSK trigger) is the more likely site of the problem, particularly given the temporal link to the pacemaker adjustment. He wonders whether the device might be inadvertently stimulating an unintended structure (echoing the earlier diaphragmatic-stimulation case) or whether there is some abnormal interaction between the device's activity-sensing mechanism and the patient's physiology. He recommends blood cultures, given that "feeling cold" in a patient with implanted cardiac hardware should always prompt consideration of device infection — while acknowledging this is unlikely given the purely exertional pattern — and proposes directly observing the patient walking while simultaneously having electrophysiology interrogate the device.

The presenting discussant reveals that the symptoms began only after the rate-responsiveness setting was changed, and introduces a general framework for reasoning about a trigger-symptom relationship: when a trigger (here, exertion or the device change) precedes a symptom, the relationship can be categorized as causal (the trigger is 100% responsible), contributory (the trigger is only partially responsible, with something else contributing), or coincidental (the trigger is unrelated). Applying this, the responding discussant wonders whether the pacemaker itself, the heart's response to exertion, or some combination of the two is driving the symptom.

Electrophysiology is consulted; they identify some EKG artifact noise, interrogate the device, and find it functioning normally, but turn off rate responsiveness as a trial — after which the patient becomes completely symptom-free. This establishes that rate responsiveness is causal or contributory to the syndrome, though EP concludes the device itself is not malfunctioning. The responding discussant reasons analogically that because rate-responsive pacing is used broadly without this complication, something must be unique about this particular patient. He recalls an analogous personal case in which pacemaker leads caused poor tricuspid valve coaptation and pacemaker-induced AV synchrony worsened exertional hypotension via worsening tricuspid regurgitation — though he notes this patient's symptoms don't clearly suggest a drop in cardiac output, and he cannot account for the specific sensation of transient coldness, acknowledging the limits of his reasoning at this point.

The presenting discussant reveals the answer: this patient has coronary artery disease that had been effectively masked by his own diseased conduction system. Because his native rate had been chronically limited, his heart rate — and therefore his myocardial oxygen demand — had never been able to rise high enough during exertion to outstrip his coronary supply and provoke angina. Once rate-responsive pacing let his heart rate climb appropriately with activity for the first time, the increased myocardial oxygen demand exceeded what his diseased coronary arteries could supply, functioning as an inadvertent "free stress test." His anginal equivalent was atypical — cold sensations and shakiness rather than classic chest pain. A coronary angiogram reveals a 70% mid-LAD lesion, which is stented, resolving his symptoms.

### Case 2: A Growing Prostatic Mass with an Undetectable PSA

A man in his 60s with known prostate adenocarcinoma (previously node-positive) on androgen deprivation therapy presents with a peri-prostatic cystic mass on imaging; his PSA, previously elevated, is now undetectable. The presenting discussant asks for a differential diagnosis of radiographic disease progression in a prostate adenocarcinoma patient with a suppressed PSA.

The responding discussant draws an analogy to antibiotic resistance developing under selection pressure, noting that cancers under treatment pressure can similarly evolve — citing an extreme example of case-reported spontaneous cures of metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma via an acquired mutation (including a case seen locally), and a more common, less extreme phenomenon: neuroendocrine dedifferentiation. Prostate adenocarcinomas nearly always contain some admixed neuroendocrine features; when disease appears to progress without a corresponding rise in PSA, neuroendocrine dedifferentiation should be suspected, since neuroendocrine tumor cells do not secrete PSA the way glandular prostate tissue does. This dedifferentiation is usually a sinister transformation into small cell carcinoma, though occasionally a more indolent neuroendocrine tumor (such as a VIPoma, referenced from a prior morning report case) can result instead.

A biopsy is performed: there is no evidence of small cell transformation, and the adenocarcinoma present is stable, unchanged from prior — refuting the neuroendocrine dedifferentiation hypothesis. Additional history is then revealed: the patient has had roughly five UTIs in the past year, involving E. coli, Pseudomonas, and other organisms, each requiring antibiotics.

The responding discussant now raises concern for a prostatic abscess as the source of recurrent infection, especially given the mass's anatomic relationship to the prostate — it appears to be growing from, and attached to, the prostate itself — while acknowledging uncertainty about whether a prostatic diverticulum-type structure could predispose to this.

The presenting discussant reveals the diagnosis: malakoplakia. Initial biopsy findings (histiocytic infiltration) had raised concern for small cell carcinoma and prompted early hospice discussions, but a specific special stain identified pathognomonic Michaelis-Gutmann bodies (intracellular calcium-containing inclusions), confirming malakoplakia — inflammation, typically in the context of recurrent UTI, that produces a mass mimicking cancer. He explains this reflects incompletely eradicated intracellular bacteria (the causative organism, often E. coli, persisting within histiocytes) rather than a true granuloma, and notes this diagnosis is being seen with increasing frequency in the era of antibiotic stewardship, when infections may be undertreated. Malakoplakia can coexist with prostate adenocarcinoma (the biopsy process itself increases UTI risk, compounding the problem) and, contrary to older teaching that it is primarily a renal entity, can affect virtually any organ. Because the causative organisms hide intracellularly, treatment requires an antibiotic with good intracellular penetration — here, levofloxacin for three months — after which a repeat MRI shows the previously six-centimeter peri-prostatic mass has completely resolved.

The responding discussant closes by characterizing malakoplakia's pathophysiology as occupying a middle ground between a classic pyogenic abscess (extracellular organisms) and granulomatous disease (organisms sequestered inside macrophages) — a pyogenic organism effectively hiding inside histiocytes — which may explain why the patient lacked the classic fevers, chills, and pain typically expected of an abscess despite harboring ongoing infection.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Case 1 models the explicit "three C's" (causal, contributory, coincidental) framework for interpreting a temporal association between a trigger and a symptom, preventing premature closure on either extreme (assuming the device caused everything, or dismissing the temporal link as coincidence) before the underlying mechanism was actually understood.

Both discussants explicitly acknowledge, in real time, not knowing the answer to Case 1 and reasoning analogically from prior, structurally similar cases (diaphragmatic stimulation, pacemaker-induced tricuspid regurgitation) — modeling that partial pattern-matching from adjacent cases is a legitimate diagnostic strategy even when it doesn't immediately yield the correct answer.

Case 2 illustrates how an ominous-appearing radiographic finding in a cancer patient can trigger premature closure toward disease progression or a new malignant process (nearly leading to a hospice conversation) before a specific pathologic stain reveals a fully treatable inflammatory mimic — a caution against assuming the worst-fitting narrative (cancer progression) over a less familiar but ultimately more likely benign explanation once the data (stable biopsy, recurrent UTI history) pointed elsewhere.

## Teaching Discussion

When a vague, activity-triggered symptom is reported, analyze the chief concern on its own terms before layering in the patient's known history, and prioritize direct observation of the reproduced symptom over further inference from history alone.

Explicitly categorizing a trigger-symptom relationship as causal, contributory, or coincidental (the "three C's") provides a disciplined way to avoid over- or under-crediting a temporally associated event, and should be revisited as new data arrives rather than settled prematurely.

A chronically rate-limited heart (from conduction system disease or under-programmed pacing) can inadvertently mask coronary artery disease by preventing myocardial oxygen demand from ever exceeding a stenotic supply; restoring normal rate responsiveness can unmask this disease, and the resulting anginal equivalent may present atypically (cold sensations, shakiness) rather than as classic chest pain.

In prostate cancer patients, radiographic progression with a suppressed or undetectable PSA should raise concern for neuroendocrine dedifferentiation, but this requires biopsy confirmation — a chronic-UTI-driven inflammatory pseudotumor (malakoplakia) is an important alternative that can closely mimic malignancy and should specifically be considered when a patient has a history of recurrent, especially E. coli, urinary tract infections.

Malakoplakia is diagnosed by pathognomonic Michaelis-Gutmann bodies on special staining and, unlike malignancy, is fully treatable with a prolonged, intracellularly active antibiotic course (such as a fluoroquinolone); it is not confined to the kidney and can occur wherever chronic, incompletely cleared intracellular infection persists.
