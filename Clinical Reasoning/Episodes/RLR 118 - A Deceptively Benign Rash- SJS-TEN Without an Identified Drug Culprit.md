## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 39-year-old, otherwise healthy man presents with four featureless days of fever and is sent home reassured — a reasonable decision, reframed here as managing "inflammation of unknown origin" rather than reflexively invoking the classic three-week fever-of-unknown-origin definition. Two days later he returns with a new triad: oral blistering, red discharging eyes, and a mild chest rash. What looks at first like a plausible mucocutaneous viral process is escalated immediately once the fever-plus-rash life-threatening-causes schema is activated, with Stevens-Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis (SJS/TEN) flagged specifically because these conditions classically start deceptively mild before exploding into necrosis and sloughing. No culprit medication is ever found — an important twist, since roughly 20% of SJS/TEN cases have no identifiable drug trigger — and the case becomes not just an infectious-disease and dermatology puzzle but a demonstration of updating one's own illness script in real time rather than discarding a diagnosis because it doesn't fit prior teaching.

## Pearls

- For an undifferentiated fever without localizing symptoms in an otherwise stable patient, "inflammation of unknown origin" (with hyperthermia as a lesser mimicker category) is often a more useful working frame than reflexively invoking the classic three-week fever-of-unknown-origin definition — that timeline exists mainly because most self-limited viral illnesses resolve within three weeks, so at day four, an undiagnosed self-limited virus remains the leading working hypothesis if the patient is not otherwise deteriorating.
- A practical "Infection 1.0" approach to an undifferentiated fever of a few days' duration in a stable patient: examine the most common sites of infection (respiratory, urinary, skin/soft tissue, GI, hepatobiliary, and bacteremia without a source), obtain a chest X-ray and basic labs (CBC, LFTs, BMP), and deliberately omit tests unlikely to be informative given the history (e.g., skip a urinalysis in a patient with no urinary symptoms, since an incidental abnormal finding could be misleading rather than clarifying).
- Never fully close the diagnostic door on a patient discharged with a presumed self-limited illness — an initial hypothesis (likely viral, will resolve) should come with an explicit safety net (return precautions) rather than an assumption that the story is over, since a minority of patients will deviate from the expected course and reveal something more serious.
- A single small, easily overlooked finding (a lip ulcer, a subtle mucosal lesion) can be the entire explanation for a persistent, unexplained fever — a reminder to escalate to a more thorough "infection 2.0" search (valves, bone, mucosa) in a patient whose fever doesn't resolve as expected, rather than assuming a benign or occult process.
- Viral reactivation (e.g., recurrent HSV) occurring during a separate systemic illness can be either the primary driver of a presentation or an incidental bystander triggered by physiologic stress from an unrelated process — distinguishing between the two is often difficult in real time and requires ongoing reassessment as more data accumulates.
- For red eye with discharge, the discharge character and laterality help distinguish viral (usually unilateral, clear/watery, contagious), bacterial (often bilateral, thick/mucopurulent), and allergic causes — but any red eye accompanied by systemic fever should be treated as a red flag, since most benign causes of conjunctivitis do not themselves produce systemic inflammation.
- Blurred vision that persists even after ocular discharge has been wiped away (rather than resolving once the discharge is cleared), and pain specifically with eye movement, are red-flag findings that should escalate a seemingly simple red eye into concern for a more serious underlying process.
- When approaching fever with a new rash, prioritizing life-threatening causes first — infection and drug reaction, ahead of autoimmune and malignant causes — is a safer initial organizing framework than working through the full differential in order of likelihood, given the speed at which some of these processes can become catastrophic.
- Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis (SJS/TEN) classically begin with a deceptively mild-appearing rash before rapidly progressing to mucosal blistering, skin necrosis, and sloughing — recognizing this trajectory early, before frank necrosis appears, is what allows the diagnosis to be considered and acted on in time.
- In any patient with a rash and suspected SJS/TEN, examine the genital and perianal mucosa specifically to assess the full extent of mucosal involvement — SJS/TEN is functionally a burn-equivalent emergency requiring aggressive supportive care for infection risk, volume depletion, and electrolyte derangement, regardless of whether a specific causative agent or antidote can be identified.
- Roughly 20% of SJS/TEN cases have no identifiable causative drug — an important, underappreciated statistic, since the absence of a drug history should not be used to argue against the diagnosis.
- The Nikolsky sign — gentle lateral pressure on intact skin near a bulla causing the epidermis to shear off and denude — supports a diagnosis involving epidermal-dermal separation (SJS/TEN or an autoimmune blistering disease) and can be anticipated and predicted before it is formally elicited if the rash's behavior is being tracked closely.
- Diseases with mucosal tropism causing a fever/rash/mucosal syndrome include the herpesviruses (HSV, CMV, EBV, HHV-6/7 — typically producing a dermatomal or vesicular, non-blanching rash), measles (worth considering in any patient with ocular, cutaneous, and mucosal findings given current variability in vaccination rates, even without a strong exposure history), and less classically, syphilis and Mycoplasma-induced mucositis (a specific entity worth keeping in one's back pocket for predominantly mucosal presentations).
- First-episode presentation of a bullous disease with prominent oral/mucosal involvement is atypical for classic bullous pemphigoid (usually more purely cutaneous) and should raise concern for an underlying paraneoplastic trigger, prompting consideration of biopsy with immunofluorescence to distinguish pemphigus vulgaris from bullous pemphigoid by the depth and pattern of immunoglobulin deposition.
- Recognizing when a case doesn't fit one's own prior illness script — and being willing to ask whether the script itself needs updating, rather than dismissing the case as atypical — is a distinct, higher-order clinical reasoning skill from simply having broad factual knowledge; explicitly voicing uncertainty about whether a well-known syndrome can occur without its "textbook" trigger is a demonstration of that skill, not a gap in it.

## Problem Representation

A 39-year-old otherwise healthy man with four days of unexplained fever, discharged after a reassuring initial workup, who returns two days later with a new triad of oral mucosal blistering, bilateral conjunctival injection with discharge, and a blanching truncal rash that rapidly progresses to skin sloughing, erythroderma, and a positive Nikolsky sign — ultimately confirmed by skin biopsy as Stevens-Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis despite an extensive workup never identifying a causative drug.

## Case Narrative

Rabih presented a 39-year-old man, otherwise healthy aside from infrequent, well-controlled mucocutaneous HSV recurrences (not frequent enough to warrant prophylaxis), who presented to a local emergency department after four days of persistent fever with no localizing symptoms whatsoever — no cough, shortness of breath, rash, urinary symptoms, diarrhea, or headache, just subjective warmth and mild body aches. His vitals showed a temperature of 39.3°C and heart rate of 110, with an otherwise normal exam and vital signs.

Reza reframed the case away from the classic three-week fever-of-unknown-origin definition, arguing that this timeline exists mainly because most self-limited viral illnesses resolve within three weeks — at day four, in a stable patient, an undiagnosed self-limited virus remained the leading working hypothesis, best labeled "inflammation of unknown origin" (with hyperthermia as a lesser mimicker category) rather than prematurely invoking a formal FUO workup. He described his own practical approach for a patient like this presenting to clinic: a focused exam of the most common sites of infection (respiratory, urinary, skin/soft tissue, GI, hepatobiliary, bacteremia without a source), a chest X-ray, and basic labs (CBC, LFTs, BMP) — deliberately skipping a urinalysis given the absence of urinary symptoms, reasoning that an incidental finding there would be more likely to mislead than clarify. He predicted this would most likely represent a self-limited viral illness that would resolve on its own.

This matched what actually happened: the patient's CBC, renal panel, LFTs, urinalysis, chest X-ray, and a comprehensive respiratory viral swab were all negative, and he was discharged with reassurance. Two days later, however, he returned — this time to his home institution, the San Francisco VA — with a new symptom triad: oral pain and blistering that he initially attributed to an HSV recurrence, new red and discharging eyes, and a mildly painful rash on his chest. His vital signs remained notable for persistent fever and low-grade tachycardia.

Reza used the deviation from the expected course to reinforce a broader teaching point: never fully close the diagnostic door on a patient discharged with a presumed self-limited illness. He recalled a personal case from training at Johns Hopkins in which a patient's unexplained fever on hospital day seven was ultimately traced to a single overlooked HSV ulcer under the lip, found only once the workup escalated to an "infection 2.0" search of valves, bone, and mucosa — illustrating how a small, easily missed lesion can be the entire explanation for a fever. He recommended an HIV test regardless of reported risk behavior, and reasoned through the tension between two possibilities: that the apparent HSV recurrence was itself driving a mucocutaneous process, versus that it was a reactivation triggered as a bystander by an unrelated, more systemic process — a distinction he acknowledged was difficult to resolve without more data.

Turning to the new findings, Reza worked through red eye with discharge (the differential of bacterial, viral, and allergic conjunctivitis distinguished largely by discharge character and laterality), while flagging that any red eye accompanied by systemic fever should itself be treated as a red flag, since most benign causes of conjunctivitis do not produce systemic inflammation; he added that blurred vision persisting after discharge is wiped away, and pain with eye movement, would further escalate concern. On seeing photographs of the exam (not directly reproducible here), the findings included a single bulla on the left buccal mucosa with severe oral pain limiting the patient's ability to swallow, bilateral conjunctival injection with yellow crusted discharge at the medial canthi, and an erythematous, blanching papular rash across the back.

With this combination in view, Reza activated the RLR schema for life-threatening causes of fever and rash, prioritizing infection and drug reaction ahead of autoimmune and malignant causes. Within the drug-reaction category, he set aside DRESS and AGEP as inconsistent with the mucosal involvement pattern, and flagged SJS/TEN as the diagnosis that had to be strongly considered — specifically because these conditions classically begin with a deceptively benign-looking rash before rapidly progressing to necrosis, sloughing, and bullae. He recommended examining the genital and perianal mucosa to assess the full extent of involvement, since SJS/TEN is functionally a burn-equivalent emergency requiring aggressive supportive care (infection, volume, and electrolyte vigilance) regardless of whether a specific culprit medication or antidote could be identified. Within the infectious, mucosal-tropism category, he worked through the herpesviruses (HSV, CMV, EBV, HHV-6/7 — typically producing a dermatomal or vesicular, non-blanching rash, judged inconsistent with this patient's blanching papular rash), herpes eczema herpeticum (dismissed on the same non-vesicular grounds), measles (flagged as worth considering given current vaccination-rate variability, even without a strong exposure history), and syphilis (acknowledged as less familiar to him as a cause of severe painful oral lesions specifically). His proposed next steps were to ask directly about any medication exposure, swab for HSV, and watch the rash closely, closing with the explicit instinct that "things are about to blow up."

Dermatology was consulted immediately. Extensive bloodwork looking for a systemic autoimmune mucocutaneous signature returned completely negative, and dermatology specifically raised and tested for Mycoplasma-induced mucositis (negative) along with comprehensive viral testing (also negative). Despite an exhaustive search — including reviewing pharmacy records — no culprit medication was ever identified, and HIV testing was negative. Shortly afterward, the patient developed severe, extremely painful genital and perianal lesions.

The rash then progressed dramatically overnight: a quarter-sized area of denuded, sloughed skin below the right eye, an edematous and ptotic right eyelid, diffuse erythroderma-pattern erythema across the anterior chest and back, sloughing on the left shoulder, and a tense bulla below the right ear. Ophthalmology diagnosed bilateral corneal abrasions, and a positive Nikolsky sign was elicited — gentle lateral pressure on a bulla causing complete denudation — exactly as Reza had predicted before it was formally tested.

With no culprit medication ever identified, Reza broadened his differential beyond SJS/TEN to include the autoimmune bullous disease category — pemphigus vulgaris versus bullous pemphigoid, distinguished by the depth and pattern of immunoglobulin deposition on immunofluorescence — reasoning that a first-episode presentation with such prominent oral and mucosal involvement would be atypical for classic bullous pemphigoid (usually a more purely cutaneous disease) and should raise concern for an underlying paraneoplastic trigger. He recommended dermatology proceed with a biopsy to distinguish between these possibilities.

Dermatology's final assessment cited that approximately 20% of SJS/TEN cases have no identifiable causative drug, which had been their leading suspicion throughout, though the autoimmune blistering possibilities Reza raised remained under consideration until biopsy. Skin biopsy ultimately showed extensive epidermal necrolysis, confirming SJS/TEN rather than an autoimmune bullous disease. Immunosuppressive therapy has only weak, marginal evidence in SJS/TEN, but the patient was nonetheless given steroids, IVIG, and etanercept; he continued to worsen and was transferred to a burn center, with no further clinical update available as of recording.

Reza closed by drawing a parallel to a real case he had witnessed at a prior morning report — an identical, deceptively benign-then-explosive rash course, though that case did have an identified culprit medication — and reiterated the 20% drug-culprit-negative SJS/TEN statistic as an important, underappreciated teaching point given how strongly a "no drug exposure" history can otherwise seem to argue against the diagnosis. Rabih's closing reflection specifically praised Reza's willingness, mid-case, to question and potentially update his own illness script for SJS/TEN — voicing genuine uncertainty about whether the syndrome could occur without any drug trigger at all — as itself a high-level clinical reasoning skill, distinct from and arguably more valuable than simply possessing broad factual knowledge.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

SJS/TEN illness script: a rash that appears mild or benign at onset, especially with mucosal (oral, ocular, genital) involvement and systemic fever, should be treated as a potential harbinger of rapid progression to necrosis and sloughing — early recognition, before frank denudation appears, is what allows timely escalation to burn-level supportive care.

The case's central bias lesson is about illness-script rigidity versus illness-script updating: rather than downweighting SJS/TEN because no drug culprit could be found (a plausible but ultimately incorrect basis for dismissal, given the well-described drug-negative subset), the reasoning stayed open to the diagnosis while simultaneously and appropriately broadening to consider autoimmune bullous alternatives — modeling comfort with genuine uncertainty ("I don't know if this can happen without a drug") as a feature of expert reasoning rather than a gap in it.

## Teaching Discussion

The central teaching point is the deceptive early trajectory of SJS/TEN — a seemingly mild, blanching rash with mucosal involvement that can explode into necrosis and sloughing within a day — and the corresponding need to examine the full mucosal surface area (oral, ocular, genital/perianal) and manage the patient with burn-level vigilance regardless of whether a specific trigger is identified.

A second teaching point is the reframing of undifferentiated short-duration fever as "inflammation of unknown origin" rather than reflexively invoking the classic three-week fever-of-unknown-origin definition, paired with a practical, appropriately limited "Infection 1.0" initial workup for a stable patient.

A third teaching point is the ~20% statistic for drug-culprit-negative SJS/TEN — an important corrective to the intuitive but flawed reasoning that a negative medication history substantially lowers the probability of the diagnosis.

The most conceptually significant teaching point, emphasized explicitly by both hosts in the closing discussion, is the distinction between having broad factual knowledge and having the flexibility to recognize when a specific case doesn't fit one's existing illness script — and to treat that mismatch as a prompt to update the script rather than a reason to abandon an otherwise well-supported diagnosis.
