## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> GUEST Aaron Berkowitz presents a case from the emergency department: a healthy woman in her mid-50s with three episodes of what sounds like ordinary nocturnal leg cramps after overexerting herself carrying her sick dog up and down the stairs. Reza and Robbie both initially frame this as almost certainly benign, and Robbie explicitly names the unease of a case that "shouldn't" have prompted a neurology consult at all. A single detail — the spasms weren't painful — reframes the case from muscle to nervous system, and an otherwise pristine neuro exam turns out to hide one abnormal finding: asymmetric hyperreflexia with an upgoing toe. The case ultimately reveals a large but minimally symptomatic meningioma causing focal motor seizures with post-ictal (Todd's) paralysis, caught almost entirely on the strength of a single remembered prior case and a complete, unhurried reflex exam.

## Pearls

- Frame any complaint of involuntary muscle movement along the neuron–neuromuscular-junction–muscle axis, distinguishing tremor, fasciculation, myoclonus, and muscle spasm/cramp before generating a differential.
- Base rates matter: the overwhelming majority of muscle twitches and spasms are benign, but a short list of reversible metabolic causes (electrolyte disturbances, stimulant substances) and one "can't miss" diagnosis (ALS) should always be screened for.
- A gut sense that a case "shouldn't" have generated this level of concern (three ED visits or a subspecialty consult for a seemingly mundane complaint) is itself useful data — it usually means either more history is available than has been elicited, or the treating team already noticed something worth taking seriously.
- Being led astray in a case is often not a failure to know a diagnosis, but a failure to correctly frame the underlying problem.
- As a consultant, most calls for a given specialty turn out not to be that specialty's disease (most altered mental status isn't a stroke, most weakness or dizziness isn't neurologic) — this creates a "signal-to-noise bias" where developing appropriate skepticism as a heuristic simultaneously creates real risk of dismissing the rare true positive.
- Classic muscle cramps (like a nocturnal calf "Charlie horse") are painful — a reported muscle spasm that is NOT painful is atypical and should prompt consideration of a different level of the nervous system (peripheral nerve, or higher) rather than the muscle itself.
- Difficulty bearing weight immediately after an episode, especially without pain to explain it, suggests a genuine transient sensory-motor phenomenon rather than simple guarding.
- If a movement disorder is diagnostically ambiguous, ask the patient (or capture on video) exactly what they see and feel — subtle distinctions like a muscle "ripple" (myokymia) versus true cramping versus stiffening can meaningfully redirect the differential.
- "ER rule number one" — if a patient can eat, walk, and talk, the test of time is a legitimate diagnostic tool; not every undifferentiated complaint needs to be fully resolved on the first visit.
- A complete neurologic exam — including inspection for atrophy/fasciculations under the patient's clothing, and reflexes — should never be skipped even when the story sounds mundane; abnormal findings are easy to miss with a rushed or partial exam.
- Patients who present with fasciculations but no associated weakness are unlikely to have ALS; in typical ALS presentations, weakness is the driving complaint, and fasciculations are usually noticed only incidentally on exam.
- Hyperreflexia strongly localizes to an upper motor neuron lesion in the central nervous system.
- When central nervous system disease is asymmetric, the brain is a more statistically likely localization than the spinal cord, simply because the brain is a much larger structure — a lesion large enough to be symptomatic while sparing the contralateral side is far easier to have in the brain than in the narrow spinal cord (though exceptions exist, such as a focal demyelinating plaque affecting one side of the cervical cord).
- A useful medicine-wide analogy for asymmetric disease and organ size: it's much easier to get a unilateral pneumonia than a unilateral aortic dissection.
- Pure motor deficits without any sensory involvement, in a small structure like the spinal cord, require an implausibly small and precisely localized lesion — making a proportionally larger structure like the brain the more likely site when sensory exam is entirely normal.
- The classic bedside test for spasticity: with the patient supine and legs straight, lift and release the area under the knee — normally the heel drags smoothly along the bed; in spasticity, the whole leg kicks up.
- An "episodic neurologic problem" framework (migraine, seizure, TIA) is a fast, effective way to structure an otherwise vague complaint and decide whether further workup is warranted — absence of headache argues against migraine, a positive/gain-of-function symptom argues against TIA (typically a negative/loss-of-function phenomenon), often leaving seizure as the best-fitting category.
- Focal (partial) seizures can be purely motor, presenting as stiffening or posturing without classic generalized convulsive jerking, and can be followed by a Todd's paralysis — a transient, post-ictal focal weakness in the affected limb that can be mistaken for ongoing muscle dysfunction.
- Seizure semiology can be extraordinarily varied and strange (ictal spitting, ictal crying, post-ictal nose-wiping, complex automatisms like somersaulting or pelvic thrusting in frontal lobe seizures) — even experienced epileptologists sometimes disagree on ambiguous video footage.
- A career-long "U-shaped" calibration curve for a specific diagnosis is common and worth naming explicitly: early in training, fear of missing a diagnosis causes overcalling; with more experience (including negative test results), confidence in ruling it out grows, sometimes into undercalling; with further seasoning, accumulated pattern recognition of atypical presentations can swing suspicion back upward again.
- Meningiomas can grow quite large, particularly away from the skull base where there is more anatomic "real estate," before producing any symptoms — a sizable lesion with minimal clinical findings is a recognized pattern, not a contradiction.
- The classic imaging signature of meningioma: an extra-axial (not intraparenchymal) mass with homogeneous contrast enhancement and a "dural tail," where enhancement extends along the adjacent dura.
- Most meningiomas are low-grade (WHO grade 1) and benign; many people harbor small, incidental meningiomas found on unrelated imaging that need nothing more than periodic surveillance for growth.
- Treatment for a symptomatic meningioma ranges from single-session stereotactic radiosurgery for smaller lesions to surgical excision for larger or more mass-effect-heavy ones.
- Deep, sustained experience with common ("bread and butter") presentations — rather than exposure to rare zebra diagnoses — is often what builds the pattern recognition needed to catch an atypical presentation of a common thing; a self-described "master of diverticulosis" can out-diagnose a zebra-hunter precisely because they've seen so many subtle variations on the common stuff.

## Problem Representation

A healthy woman in her mid-50s develops three self-limited episodes of unilateral (left) leg spasm/stiffening — notably painless, with transient difficulty bearing weight afterward — following exertion (carrying a sick dog up and down stairs) and mild associated low back pain, with a completely normal neurologic exam except for asymmetric hyperreflexia (left greater than right, leg greater than arm) and an upgoing toe on the affected side, ultimately representing focal motor seizures with Todd's paralysis caused by a large but minimally symptomatic right cerebral hemisphere meningioma.

## Case Narrative

Aaron presented a case he'd seen a few weeks earlier in the emergency department: a very healthy woman in her mid-50s who woke at night with a spasm or cramp in her left leg, massaged it out, walked around, and went back to bed — only for it to happen again. On the second occasion she felt a little "funny" walking on the leg, though it got better; she decided to come to the ED, where the spasm recurred once more while she was waiting to be seen, again resolving completely. Aside from carrying her sick, roughly 30-pound dog up and down the stairs over the preceding days (with resulting mild, non-radiating midline low back pain), there was nothing else remarkable in her history.

Reza's initial framework organized involuntary muscle movement along the axis of neuron, neuromuscular junction, and muscle — distinguishing tremor, fasciculation, myoclonus, and muscle spasm/cramp — and noted that by base rate, ordinary benign muscle contraction (excited lower motor neurons releasing acetylcholine) is overwhelmingly the most likely explanation. He flagged that clinicians still need to keep a short list of more concerning possibilities in mind: ALS as the "can't miss" diagnosis, and reversible causes like electrolyte disturbances (potassium, magnesium, calcium) or stimulant substances (caffeine, illicit drugs) that can irritate muscle fibers directly.

Robbie's reaction, however, was immediate unease: he said he already suspected something was being missed from the story, reasoning that a patient waiting hours in the ED and ultimately being seen by neurology for three episodes of muscle spasm was several standard deviations outside of what he'd expect to trigger that level of concern. He framed this explicitly as a case where he might be "solving the wrong problem" rather than lacking knowledge of the right diagnosis — noting that being led astray, at his stage of training, is usually a framing failure rather than an information gap.

Aaron, speaking from the neurology consultant's perspective, named this dynamic directly as "signal-to-noise bias": most neurology consults turn out not to be neurologic (most altered mental status isn't stroke, most weakness or dizziness isn't neurologic), which trains consultants toward skepticism as a heuristic — a heuristic that simultaneously creates real risk of dismissing the rare true positive. He admitted he too initially assumed this would be an unremarkable case, especially seeing a completely healthy-looking patient who'd simply overexerted herself with an unusual chore.

On further questioning, this was the patient's first-ever episode of leg cramping; her back pain was midline, axial, non-radiating, and without red flags; there was no antecedent trauma or bite to the leg, and no medications (ruling out a dystonic drug reaction). The key discriminating question Aaron asked was whether the spasm was painful — and the patient said it was not, which was atypical, since a classic muscle cramp (like a nocturnal "Charlie horse") is painful. This detail redirected suspicion away from a purely muscular process. Screening otherwise was negative: no sensory symptoms, no bowel or bladder symptoms, no weakness on direct questioning — but, without prompting, the patient volunteered that she'd had difficulty bearing weight on the leg immediately after each episode, which then resolved completely.

Reza found the absence of pain intriguing, wondering whether this reframed the process toward peripheral neuropathy, and floated the idea that the patient might actually be observing a visible muscle phenomenon (like myokymia, a muscle "ripple") and interpreting what she sees as a cramp — suggesting that capturing a future episode on video could help characterize the movement more precisely. Robbie, for his part, admitted he'd simply assumed pain was present by default and was struck by how odd it was to have difficulty bearing weight without pain to explain it — wondering aloud whether the patient was experiencing a transient loss of muscle tone rather than a true cramp, and openly stating he found himself very stumped.

Aaron clarified the patient's own description further: she said the leg became stiff, rather than describing a typical cramping sensation. This shifted the working problem toward "stiffness." Reza referenced a stiffness-focused schema he and Aaron had previously built (from a Journal of Hospital Medicine case on anti-GAD-antibody stiff person syndrome), which included voluntary or psychiatric stiffening (a hypothesis they'd never lead with) and Isaac syndrome, a peripheral nerve hyperexcitability disorder he associated with a female-predominant population, though he admitted needing to review the details further.

Robbie, staying grounded in what he called "ER rule number one" — if a patient can eat, walk, and talk, use the test of time — said that in real practice, without more information, he would have simply reassured the patient, arranged a short-interval follow-up, and advised her to return if anything alarming recurred, openly acknowledging the limits of his own diagnostic reach at this point in the case.

Aaron then walked both hosts through a focused neuro exam. Reza asked for inspection of both legs for atrophy (which would raise concern for ALS and a lower motor neuron process with a subacute-to-chronic tempo), assessment for myalgia/tenderness, an attempt to trigger the spasm by touch (suggesting myoclonus), and reflexes — acknowledging that without a working hypothesis, this was essentially the generic neuro exam he'd perform on anyone. Aaron praised the instinct but emphasized that a full exam, including inspection under the patient's clothing, is essential, since atrophy and fasciculations are easy to miss with a partial exam. He shared a pattern-recognition pearl: in his experience, patients who present with fasciculations but no weakness rarely turn out to have ALS — ALS almost always presents with weakness as the driving complaint, with fasciculations discovered incidentally on exam rather than being the primary reason for the visit.

The actual exam: normal mental status, normal cranial nerves, full strength throughout, no atrophy — but reflexes were brisker on the left than the right, more pronounced in the leg than the arm, and stroking the sole produced an upgoing toe on the affected side. No spasticity was found on formal testing (the classic knee-drop maneuver, where a normal leg's heel drags along the bed while a spastic leg kicks up). This isolated finding — asymmetric hyperreflexia with an upgoing toe — was the only abnormality on an otherwise completely normal exam.

Robbie interpreted this immediately: hyperreflexia strongly implies an upper motor neuron lesion within the central nervous system, and when CNS disease is asymmetric, his rule of thumb is to prioritize the brain over the spinal cord as the likely site — reasoning that the brain's much larger size makes it statistically far easier to harbor a lesion large enough to be symptomatic while sparing the other side, whereas the narrow spinal cord usually requires any large enough lesion to affect both sides. He offered the analogy that it's much easier to get a unilateral pneumonia than a unilateral aortic dissection, while noting this rule can be broken (citing a case Aaron had previously taught them, of a focal demyelinating MS plaque presenting with asymmetric findings from a lesion confined to one side of the cervical cord). Aaron reinforced the logic with anatomic scale — the spinal cord is roughly the width of a thumb, so a lesion sparing one side while producing exam findings on the other requires an implausibly small, precisely localized process, especially with a completely normal sensory exam arguing against even a small tract-restricted cord lesion.

Reza raised multiple sclerosis as a brain-localizing possibility from a reference sheet he and Aaron had built together, and asked about other MS-associated symptoms (urinary, visual/ocular) while suggesting an MRI brain with gadolinium as the key next test to localize a probable right cerebral hemisphere lesion, given the left-sided findings. Turning to characterize the phenomenon itself now that a central lesion was suspected, the group referenced a "fasciculation and its mimics" teaching sheet they'd created (searchable on rlrcpsolvers.com), which distinguishes painful muscle spasm/cramp, myoclonus (movement across a joint), and myokymia (a muscle ripple, commonly experienced in the eyelid), with rarer central causes including Isaac syndrome or MS-related myokymia. Reza guessed myokymia based on demographic and common-things-common reasoning but admitted uncertainty about the underlying mechanism connecting it to MS.

Robbie then made the decisive leap: the only central, abrupt movement phenomenon he could think of was a seizure — which would explain both the stiffening sensation and, critically, the transient post-episode inability to bear weight as a Todd's paralysis (a transient, post-ictal focal weakness following a focal seizure). He was explicit that this was a hypothesis he was only generating in real time, not something he'd considered earlier in the case, drawing on prior experience with other central movement disorders (such as chorea from diabetic striatopathy) while acknowledging he had no direct prior experience recognizing this particular seizure-mimicking-as-cramp presentation.

Aaron confirmed this was correct, and explained that unusually, no imaging had been obtained before this bedside evaluation — atypical for a modern neuro consult, since most patients already have a head CT by the time neurology is called. He'd initially felt real skepticism (naming this his own "skepticism bias" as a consultant) given how well the patient looked and how mundane the trigger seemed. What changed his mind was a specific remembered case: years earlier, as an attending supervising a resident in Boston, he'd been presented with a patient whose unilateral nocturnal leg cramps had been attributed by the resident to lumbar spinal stenosis found on MRI. Aaron's gut reaction at the time — "cramps waking someone from sleep... hmm" — led him to recheck the patient's reflexes, which were markedly brisk on the affected side; he diagnosed intermittent focal seizures of that leg from a presumed cortical lesion, and the patient did indeed have one. That case directly informed his pattern recognition here, prompting him to ask specifically about pain and to perform a careful reflex and Babinski exam — something he stressed as a habit he insists on with trainees, describing "reflexes deferred" as a documentation pet peeve, since checking reflexes on every patient is what allows a clinician to notice when something is subtly different.

He explained the localization further: brisk reflexes (left greater than right, leg greater than arm) plus an upgoing toe suggested a lesion affecting the leg-predominant region of the right motor cortex (the parasagittal, medial strip corresponding to leg representation), and the mildness and focality of the findings made an infiltrative process seem less likely, favoring a discrete, well-demarcated lesion similar to his prior Boston case.

Imaging confirmed this: a head CT showed an abnormal right-hemisphere lesion, and an MRI with and without gadolinium (FLAIR and post-contrast sequences) revealed a round, homogeneously enhancing extra-axial mass with a "dural tail" — enhancement extending into the adjacent dura, the classic signature of a meningioma. CT also showed associated calcification contributing to some irregularity on the MR images. The lesion was notably large given how minimal the associated symptoms had been, which the group agreed was typical for meningiomas away from the skull base, where there's more room to grow before symptoms develop.

Reza worked through the differential for a brain mass: neoplasm versus infection, favoring neoplasm given the absence of fever, headache, or meningismus, and noting the mass had come to attention purely through localized mass effect (triggering seizures) rather than any systemic illness — a pattern favoring a slow-growing, benign process. Within neoplasm, he distinguished benign possibilities (meningioma, low-grade glioma) from malignant ones (glioblastoma, high-grade glioma, or less commonly metastasis), favoring meningioma given the patient's overall health and the largely radiographic nature of the diagnosis. He shared a personal connection: his own mother had a meningioma, presenting with loss of her corneal reflex (a sensory trigeminal/motor facial nerve reflex arc), a story he'd previously written about with Aaron's help in the book Finding Joy in Medicine. He discussed treatment options generally — ranging from single-session stereotactic (gamma knife) radiosurgery for smaller lesions to surgical excision for larger, more mass-effect-heavy ones like this one appeared to be.

Aaron confirmed the imaging read: a right-hemisphere extra-axial mass with homogeneous enhancement and a dural tail, classic for meningioma, in a surgically accessible location. He noted most meningiomas are low-grade (WHO grade 1) and benign, and that many people harbor small, entirely incidental meningiomas discovered on unrelated imaging that require nothing more than periodic surveillance. This lesion, by contrast, was large enough and symptomatic enough (causing seizures) to warrant active management.

Reflecting on the case, Aaron called it a rare instance of practicing pure bedside neurology — examining the patient before any imaging existed, atypical in an era where most neuro consults arrive with imaging already obtained. He credited the pivotal moment to a combination of specific case-based recall and simply performing a complete, unhurried exam, including checking reflexes and testing for a Babinski sign. He acknowledged candidly that without that particular remembered case, the presentation could very plausibly — and not unreasonably — have been dismissed as benign overexertion cramps and discharged.

Reza asked how a purely motor, non-convulsive stiffening episode gets labeled a seizure, given that seizures are often pictured as generalized convulsions. Aaron and Robbie explained that focal (partial) seizures can be restricted to the motor cortex, producing focal stiffening or posturing without classic jerking, followed by a Todd's paralysis explaining the post-episode weakness. Aaron elaborated on how varied seizure semiology can be — citing bizarre documented phenomena like ictal spitting, ictal crying, post-ictal nose-wiping, and complex automatisms such as somersaulting or pelvic thrusting in frontal lobe seizures — noting that even expert epileptologists sometimes disagree when reviewing ambiguous video footage. He shared a mentorship anecdote from his epilepsy fellowship program director, describing a career-long, roughly U-shaped calibration curve for diagnosing seizure: early on, fear of missing one leads to overcalling everything as possibly seizure; with more experience (including many negative EEGs revealing non-epileptic spells), confidence in ruling it out grows into undercalling; and with further seasoning, accumulated exposure to atypical presentations swings suspicion back upward again. He also offered a simplifying frame for undifferentiated episodic complaints: naming the "episodic neurologic problem" category (migraine, seizure, TIA) and running through it quickly — no headache argues against migraine, a positive/gain-of-function symptom argues against TIA (typically a loss-of-function phenomenon) — often leaves seizure as the best fit, and this simple framing alone can justify further workup even when the presenting complaint sounds mundane.

Aaron closed by quoting a mentor, Gurpreet Dhaliwal, who once described himself as "a master of diverticulosis" when discussing the choice between a community/VA-type practice setting and an academic quaternary-referral center — meaning that deep, sustained experience with common presentations builds the pattern recognition needed to catch subtle, atypical presentations of the same common things, in contrast to a "zebra-hunting" mindset drawn to rarer entities like CAR-T neurologic complications, CNS vasculitis, or autoimmune encephalitis. He framed this case — bread-and-butter seizure, from a bread-and-butter tumor type, caught via a bread-and-butter exam maneuver of simply checking reflexes — as exemplifying that philosophy, describing himself as "an aspiring master of the bread and butter of neurology" rather than a zebra hunter.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Effect on Probability |
|---|---|
| Three ED-level episodes of "simple" muscle spasm in a healthy patient | Raises suspicion (via gestalt/pattern mismatch) that the story is incompletely characterized, even before any exam finding |
| Spasm reported as painless | Lowers probability of a typical muscle-level cramp; raises probability of a peripheral nerve or central process |
| Transient difficulty bearing weight after the episode, without pain | Raises probability of a genuine transient sensory-motor phenomenon rather than pain-limited guarding |
| No medications, no trauma, first-ever episode | Lowers probability of a dystonic drug reaction or structural leg injury |
| Normal strength, no atrophy on exam | Lowers probability of ALS or another chronic motor neuron disease |
| Asymmetric hyperreflexia (left > right, leg > arm) | Raises probability of an upper motor neuron / central nervous system lesion |
| Upgoing toe (Babinski sign) on the affected side | Confirms an upper motor neuron process |
| Normal sensory exam | Lowers probability of a small, tract-restricted spinal cord lesion; favors a brain localization |
| Head CT showing a right-hemisphere lesion | Confirms brain localization |
| MRI: extra-axial, homogeneously enhancing mass with a dural tail | Strongly favors meningioma over glioma or metastasis |
| Absence of fever, headache, or meningismus | Lowers probability of infection (abscess) as the cause of the mass |
| Large lesion size with minimal associated symptoms | Consistent with, rather than atypical for, a slow-growing benign meningioma away from the skull base |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

This case is built almost entirely around the tension between "signal-to-noise bias" (a consultant's learned skepticism, since most consults for a specialty don't turn out to be that specialty's disease) and the rare true positive that skepticism risks dismissing. Aaron's own account is candid about how close this came to being closed as benign — the diagnosis hinged on a single remembered prior case with a similar, easily-overlooked pattern (unilateral nocturnal leg cramping plus asymmetric reflexes), illustrating how much of expert pattern recognition operates through case-based recall rather than textbook-style rule application. Robbie's early "this doesn't add up" instinct is presented as a valuable but separate skill from actually solving the case — useful for flagging that something is being missed, without necessarily pointing toward the answer. The episode is also explicit about calibration drift over a career (the U-shaped confidence curve for seizure), acknowledging that diagnostic suspicion for any given entity isn't static even for experienced clinicians.

## Teaching Discussion

The central teaching arc traces a single symptom word — "spasm" — through several reframings: muscle cramp, to stiffness, to myokymia, to finally a focal motor seizure with Todd's paralysis. Each reframing was triggered by a specific piece of information (painless, not painful; stiffening, not rippling; weight-bearing difficulty without pain) rather than by ordering a test, underscoring how much diagnostic progress in this case came from careful, curious history-taking and a complete exam rather than imaging. The episode also doubles as a teaching case on meningioma epidemiology and imaging (extra-axial location, dural tail, propensity to grow large before becoming symptomatic away from the skull base) and closes with an explicit meta-lesson on the diagnostic value of deep experience with common presentations over exposure to rare ones — the "master of diverticulosis" framing.
