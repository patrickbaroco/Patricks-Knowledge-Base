## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A neurology Virtual Morning Report in the CP Solvers "end **[[Neurophobia|neurophobia]]**" series with neurologist **Aaron Berkowitz**, framed — as ever — around a way of thinking rather than a single answer. A 71-year-old woman with **advanced dementia**, type 2 diabetes, and a decade of **prednisolone** for an ill-defined arthritis is brought in overnight with acute-on-chronic [[Altered Mental Status|altered mental status]]: one day of low-grade fever, nausea, vomiting, and headache, then a night of crying, screaming, and agitation. Berkowitz works [[Altered Mental Status|AMS]] through his master frame — differential = [[Anatomical Approach|localization]] × [[Illness Tempo|time course]] — with the first gate **medical vs neurologic vs psychiatric**, gently noting that a mnemonic like N-MIST omits the tempo that does the real work. He then applies a hard **[[Base Rate|base-rate]]** correction: in an elderly demented patient, fever plus AMS is *usually* a systemic insult (UTI, pneumonia, or pain), not [[Bacterial Meningitis|meningitis]] — but a decade of steroids makes her **[[Immunocompromised Host|immunocompromised]]**, reopening opportunistic infection and lowering the threshold to tap. The case turns on the exam: she is inconsolable in **pain**, with equivocal [[Kernig and Brudzinski Signs|Kernig/Brudzinski]] signs, right-lower-quadrant tenderness, and a guarded right leg — until **morphine** calms her enough to reveal a **[[Vesicular Rash|vesicular rash]] on the back**. Berkowitz reads a dermatomal eruption as [[Herpes Zoster|herpes zoster]], and — invoking **[[Zoster Sine Herpete|zoster sine herpete]]** — reframes the "abdominal" and leg pain as L2–L3 neuritic pain rather than a surgical abdomen. Empiric vancomycin/ceftriaxone/ampicillin/**acyclovir** are started before any tap; the rash over L2–L3 makes lumbar puncture unsafe, so the team treats blind. She disseminates (thigh and back vesicles, hypoxia, patchy infiltrates), develops [[Acyclovir Nephrotoxicity|acyclovir-associated AKI]], and is transferred, where **MRI shows meningeal enhancement** and — once the lesions crust — an LP confirms **VZV**. Final diagnosis: **[[Disseminated Zoster|disseminated varicella-zoster]] with [[Zoster Meningitis|zoster meningitis]]** in an elderly, steroid-immunosuppressed host. She survives a prolonged course but is discharged well below her baseline.

## Pearls

- **[[Altered Mental Status|Altered mental status]] = [[Anatomical Approach|localization]] × [[Illness Tempo|time course]], with a first gate of medical vs neurologic vs psychiatric.** AMS localizes to brain, so the work is tempo: *sudden* (vascular, [[Seizure|seizure]]/post-ictal, trauma, toxic-metabolic), *acute over hours-to-days* (bacterial/viral infection, immune-mediated), *subacute* (mass, [[Autoimmune Encephalitis|antibody-mediated]] disease), *chronic* (neurodegenerative). Berkowitz teaches this over the [[MIST Mnemonic|N-MIST]] mnemonic precisely because a cause-list omits the tempo that narrows the differential; "toxic-metabolic" must still be named as a specific toxin or metabolite, not waved at.
- **Common things are common — a [[Base Rate|base-rate]] discipline.** In an elderly patient with dementia, fever plus AMS is *far* more often a systemic insult — a UTI, a pneumonia, or simply uncontrolled **pain** producing [[Delirium|delirium]] — than [[Bacterial Meningitis|meningitis]] or encephalitis. Neurology morning report over-weights the CNS diagnoses; the bedside prior does not.
- **Chronic steroids flip that prior.** Ten years of prednisolone makes her an [[Immunocompromised Host|immunocompromised host]]: the infectious differential now spans not just pneumococcus and HSV but **opportunistic** organisms (fungal, tuberculous), the threshold to perform a [[Lumbar Puncture|lumbar puncture]] drops, and a "clear systemic source" is no longer enough to stand down. Also on the fever-headache-AMS list: [[Infective Endocarditis|endocarditis]] with septic emboli and its [[Austrian Syndrome|Austrian-syndrome]] triad.
- **Look at the skin — and the back.** A dermatomal [[Vesicular Rash|vesicular rash]] is [[Herpes Zoster|herpes zoster]] until proven otherwise, and sitting the patient up to inspect the back was the pivot of this case. Via **[[Zoster Sine Herpete|zoster sine herpete]]**, a dermatome can be exquisitely, neuritically painful before or beyond its visible vesicles — reframing her right-lower-quadrant "abdominal" tenderness and guarded right leg as L2–L3 zoster pain, not a surgical abdomen (the ultrasound for appendicitis was clean).
- **Empiric meningitis therapy first, diagnosis second.** When bacterial meningitis is plausible, start vancomycin + ceftriaxone + **ampicillin** (Listeria coverage for age > 50 or immunocompromise) + **acyclovir** *before* the tap: every hour of delay worsens bacterial-meningitis outcomes, and antibiotics do not sterilize the CSF chemistry/PCR for a day or two. Here the rash overlay the L2–L3 puncture site, so an LP was unsafe — the team had to treat blind and sit with the discomfort of stopping antibiotics without a confirmatory culture.
- **Two test-interpretation nuggets.** [[Kernig and Brudzinski Signs|Kernig and Brudzinski signs]] are fairly *specific* but only ~5–10% *sensitive* (JAMA Rational Clinical Examination), so their absence never excludes meningitis and their presence in an arthritic elder is hard to read. And for [[Varicella Zoster Virus|VZV]] in the CNS, match the test to the mechanism: **CSF PCR** for acute [[Zoster Meningitis|zoster meningitis]], **CSF VZV IgG** for the para-infectious [[VZV Vasculopathy|vasculopathy]]/encephalitis — when unsure, send both.

## Problem Representation

A 71-year-old woman with advanced dementia, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and roughly ten years of low-dose prednisolone for a poorly characterized arthritis is brought in overnight with an acute change from her demented baseline: one day of subjective low-grade fever, nausea, two episodes of vomiting, and headache, followed by a night of crying, screaming, agitation, and apparent pain, on a background of a week of irritability and intermittent back pain. She is febrile to 37.9 °C, borderline tachycardic, normotensive, and euglycemic, with equivocal meningismus, right-lower-quadrant tenderness, a guarded painful right leg, and — once analgesia allows a full exam — orientation to person only (her baseline) with no focal deficit and a dermatomal vesicular rash on the back. Labs show a mild neutrophil-lymphocyte-balanced leukocytosis, incidental new atrial fibrillation, negative HIV/hepatitis serologies, and a non-contributory CT head; an abdominal ultrasound excludes appendicitis. The evolving syndrome is a systemic, dermatomal-then-disseminated varicella-zoster infection with probable VZV meningitis in an elderly, steroid-immunosuppressed host, ultimately confirmed by meningeal enhancement on MRI and VZV-positive CSF after transfer.

## Case Narrative

The session is another entry in the CP Solvers campaign to **[[Neurophobia|end neurophobia]]**, with neurologist **Aaron Berkowitz** teaching and a senior medical student, Bayan, presenting; a second learner, "A," reasons alongside. The chief complaint is deliberately bare: a **71-year-old woman with altered mental status**, brought to the ER confused, screaming, and seemingly in pain she cannot localize.

**Framing AMS before the history.** Berkowitz builds the approach from first principles. His broad buckets are **medical, neurologic, and psychiatric** — the "medical" being systemic conditions that bathe an otherwise-intact brain in what he jokingly calls *bad humors* (liver failure, renal failure, toxic-metabolic states). Onto those buckets he superimposes **[[Illness Tempo|time course]]**: [[Altered Mental Status|AMS]] localizes to the brain, so this is not a hard [[Anatomical Approach|localization]] problem, and the tempo does the discriminating work — *sudden* (ischemia, hemorrhage, [[Seizure|seizure]]/post-ictal state), *acute but not instant* over hours-to-days (encephalitis, meningitis, immune-mediated disease), *subacute* (focal mass lesions, metastases), and *chronic* (neurodegenerative dementia). He contrasts this with the community-favorite **N-MIST** mnemonic — with "all due respect" to it — arguing that a cause-list, however complete, leaves out the time course that actually narrows the differential. His co-discussant A independently lands in the same place, generating a broad neuro differential (encephalitis/meningitis, [[Autoimmune Encephalitis|autoimmune encephalitis]] including anti-NMDA, cerebral malaria, CNS vasculitis) plus the systemic toxic-metabolic and drug causes.

**HPI.** The history comes from the son she lives with, because she cannot give it herself. One day before presentation she developed mild fever, nausea, headache, and two episodes of vomiting; the son planned urgent care in the morning until she woke in the night crying, screaming, and uncooperative. Review of systems adds a week of irritability and, at one point, **back pain** — hard to weigh against advanced dementia. Her history is the crux: **advanced dementia** (on no medications for it), type 2 diabetes (metformin/sitagliptin) and hypertension (amlodipine) with good compliance because her son administers them, a non-specific **arthritis treated with prednisolone 10 mg daily for nearly ten years**, a right ankle fracture within the month, and cataract surgery years prior. She is a retired primary-school teacher, lives with her son's family because of the dementia, and does not smoke, drink, or use drugs; childhood and recent immunizations are uncertain.

**Two priors in tension.** Berkowitz uses the history to teach a **[[Base Rate|base-rate]]** correction. Fever, headache, and AMS reflexively summon meningitis and encephalitis — "it's neurology morning report" — but he presses A on what actually happens at the bedside: in an elderly demented patient with fever and AMS, the overwhelming majority have a **systemic** driver (a UTI, a pneumonia), not a CNS infection, and both agree these consults are usually a *rule-out*. He tests and discards the fast-tempo alternatives: **[[Autoimmune Encephalitis|autoimmune encephalitis]]** is too subacute for this pace, and an acute worsening of **dementia** itself is possible only with a superimposed insult (Alzheimer's loses roughly 3 points a year — days is far too fast). Against that low prior, though, sits the decisive modifier: a **decade of prednisolone makes her [[Immunocompromised Host|immunocompromised]]**, so the infectious net widens to opportunistic organisms — fungal (a touch slow for this tempo) and tuberculous meningitis — and the **[[Lumbar Puncture|lumbar puncture]]** threshold falls ("if you've thought about an LP long enough, you should probably just do it"). Berkowitz recounts a cautionary case — an ear infection plus heavy NSAID use, a supple neck, and yet culture-positive bacterial meningitis — to argue that a soft systemic source does not license skipping the tap in a compromised host. He also widens the fever-headache-AMS differential to **[[Infective Endocarditis|endocarditis]]**: septic emboli causing strokes, a mycotic aneurysm rupturing into subarachnoid hemorrhage, meningeal seeding, and the **[[Austrian Syndrome|Austrian syndrome]]** triad (pneumococcal pneumonia, endocarditis, and meningitis).

**Examining the uncooperative patient.** Before the findings, Berkowitz and A rehearse how to examine a confused patient who cannot follow commands: grade arousal by what it takes to rouse her (voice, loud voice, light touch, noxious stimulus), watch spontaneous movements and grimace for **symmetry**, test cranial nerves through reflexes (pupillary, corneal) and visual fields through **blink-to-threat**, and probe motor/sensory through withdrawal to nail-bed pressure and reflexes/Babinski. He adds a subtler discipline: distinguish a **generalized encephalopathy** (inattention, disorientation) from a **focal cognitive deficit masquerading as confusion** — a Wernicke aphasia or an amnesia can make a patient "sound confused" without a diffuse process. In the spirit of "**[[Neurology of X|neurology of X]]**," he notes what her arthritis *could* have meant had it been rheumatoid: cervical spine and atlantoaxial (C1–C2) instability with cord, root, or even brainstem compression, plus rheumatoid pachymeningitis and vasculitis — probably too fast here, but a teaching aside.

**The exam, in two acts.** She is inconsolable and, the son insists, in **pain**; IV paracetamol fails, but **morphine** calms her — itself evidence the pain was real and possibly the engine of her [[Delirium|delirium]]. Vitals: temperature 37.9 °C, heart rate ~100, blood pressure 152/89, glucose 109. The ER had found neck stiffness with positive [[Kernig and Brudzinski Signs|Kernig and Brudzinski signs]], but on repeat these were softer and looked more like her pushing examiners away than true meningismus. Cardiopulmonary exam is normal; the abdomen shows **right-lower-quadrant tenderness** to light palpation severe enough to make her scream. Once comfortable she becomes **alert and oriented to person only** — her baseline — with sensation intact except over the abdomen and motor power intact everywhere except a **right lower limb she will not let anyone near**, swatting hands away as they approach the abdomen or leg. Crucially, sitting her up reveals a **[[Vesicular Rash|vesicular rash]] on the back**.

**Reading the rash.** A recognizes the herpesvirus family; Berkowitz narrows to a unilateral, painful, dermatomal eruption on the right mid-to-lower back — **[[Herpes Zoster|herpes zoster]]** (shingles, reactivated **[[Varicella Zoster Virus|varicella-zoster]]**). He then links the disparate pain: through **[[Zoster Sine Herpete|zoster sine herpete]]**, a dermatome can be exquisitely neuritically painful before or beyond its visible vesicles, so the right-lower-quadrant "abdominal" tenderness and the guarded leg are most parsimoniously **L2–L3 zoster pain**, not an acute abdomen. He layers on the **neurology of zoster** — [[Zoster Meningitis|zoster meningitis]], zoster encephalitis, and [[VZV Vasculopathy|VZV vasculopathy]] with strokes — and a nuanced testing point: zoster meningitis is an acute infectious phenomenon best caught by **CSF PCR**, whereas the more para-infectious vasculopathy/encephalitis is better served by **CSF VZV IgG**; in practice, send both.

**Initial workup and empiric treatment.** Reasoning she had meningismus and no clear alternative source, the ER started **empiric coverage before any tap** — vancomycin, ampicillin (for Listeria given her age and immunocompromise), ceftriaxone, and **acyclovir** — the correct instinct, since delay worsens bacterial-meningitis outcomes and antibiotics do not immediately erase CSF yield. An abdominal **ultrasound** for possible appendicitis (she had never had an appendectomy) was clean. Labs: WBC 12.8 (neutrophils 45%, lymphocytes 40%) with otherwise normal counts, chemistries, liver and renal function, and coagulation; an incidental **new atrial fibrillation** on ECG, felt unrelated; negative HIV, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C; pending blood cultures; glucosuria on urinalysis; normal thyroid studies. A contrast **CT head** showed only chronic dementia-related and non-specific lacunar change. She was admitted for a possible LP and MRI, and switched from oral prednisolone to **IV dexamethasone**.

**Deterioration.** By the third day of illness (first hospital day), before rounds, she desaturated to the high 80s–90s, became tachycardic to 120, and her mental status declined again; the exam now showed **new vesicles on the right upper thigh and spreading over the back**. She was placed on oxygen; a portable chest radiograph showed bilateral patchy infiltrates, and she was started on bronchodilators with fluids held for a presumed cardiogenic component. The **lumbar puncture was never done** — the vesicular rash overlay the **L2–L3** dermatome, making a tap through the eruption unsafe (with the added risk of seeding VZV into the canal) — so she remained on empiric acyclovir without a confirmatory CSF sample. Berkowitz uses this to name a recurring bind: when you cannot obtain the LP, you may be committing a patient to prolonged broad antibiotics without ever being able to stop them on a negative culture, and deciding when to *stop* the antibacterials on clinical grounds alone is genuinely hard.

**Resolution.** Dermatology confirmed **shingles** on vesicle biopsy and the patient was isolated. She developed bilateral lower-limb lymphedema and a rising creatinine/BUN — an **[[Acyclovir Nephrotoxicity|acute kidney injury]]** variously attributed to acyclovir (crystalluria) versus CT contrast; valacyclovir, less nephrotoxic, was considered but unavailable. Given rapid multi-organ deterioration at a small hospital, she was transferred to a tertiary center, where **MRI showed meningeal enhancement** confirming meningitis, and — once the back lesions crusted and an LP became safe — **CSF returned positive for varicella-zoster**. The final diagnosis was **[[Disseminated Zoster|disseminated varicella-zoster]] with VZV meningitis**, attributed to her advanced age and the immunosuppression of a decade of steroids. After a family discussion about goals of care in the setting of advanced dementia and multi-organ failure, treatment was continued; the antibacterials were stopped, she improved on acyclovir, and she was eventually discharged — but, as often happens when a demented brain sustains an acute insult, **markedly below her prior baseline**.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Localization × Time Course|Localization × time course]] as the organizing script.** Fixing the *where* (brain) and letting the *tempo* (acute, over hours-to-days) select the etiologic bucket collapses a vast AMS differential to a short list — the same Berkowitz frame that anchors [[CPS 153 - Intermittent Altered Mental Status and Non-Lesional Autoimmune Epilepsy|CPS 153]], here contrasted explicitly with the [[MIST Mnemonic|N-MIST]] cause-list for omitting tempo.
- **[[Base Rate|Base-rate]] correction against morning-report salience.** The [[Availability Heuristic|availability]] of meningitis/encephalitis in a neurology conference is countered by the bedside prior that elderly + demented + febrile AMS is usually systemic — a deliberate down-weighting of the dramatic diagnosis, then re-weighted by the steroid history.
- **The immunosuppression modifier.** A decade of prednisolone is the single fact that rescues the infectious CNS hypotheses from base-rate oblivion, reopening opportunistic organisms and lowering the [[Lumbar Puncture|LP]] threshold — a reminder that priors are conditional on host factors.
- **Unifying the pain — [[Occam's Razor|parsimony]] via zoster.** Rather than a demented woman with coincidental meningismus, an acute abdomen, and a leg problem, [[Zoster Sine Herpete|zoster sine herpete]] ties the right-lower-quadrant and leg pain to a single L2–L3 dermatome, and the vesicles make zoster the unifying thread — the most *specific* finding weighted above the noisier ones.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Test interpretation]] under constraint.** [[Kernig and Brudzinski Signs|Kernig/Brudzinski]] are read for what they are (specific, insensitive), and CSF testing is matched to mechanism (PCR for acute meningitis, IgG for para-infectious vasculopathy) — with the honest acknowledgment that the confirmatory test (LP) was simply unobtainable through an infected field.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Diagnostic humility]] and treating without proof.** The team commits to empiric therapy before — and, for a time, *without* — a diagnosis, and Berkowitz names the discomfort of stopping antibiotics on clinical judgment when no culture will ever return; closing the loop after transfer models the diligence that turns an open case into a learned one.

## Teaching Discussion

- Approach altered mental status as localization × time course over a first gate of medical vs neurologic vs psychiatric: AMS localizes to brain, so let tempo — sudden, acute, subacute, chronic — select the etiologic bucket, and if you invoke "toxic-metabolic," name the specific toxin or metabolite rather than deferring. A cause-mnemonic like N-MIST or MIST can enumerate categories but does not substitute for tempo.
- Calibrate to base rates: in an elderly patient with dementia, fever plus AMS is usually a systemic insult — a UTI, a pneumonia, or uncontrolled pain producing delirium — and CNS infection is more often a rule-out than a rule-in. Let the bedside prior, not conference salience, set the pretest probability.
- Re-weight that prior for host factors: chronic corticosteroids create an immunocompromised host in whom the infectious differential widens to opportunistic organisms (fungal, tuberculous) and the threshold to perform a lumbar puncture falls, so a soft systemic source no longer licenses standing down.
- Treat pain as a legitimate cause of delirium in the demented elderly, and remember that adequate analgesia can convert an unexaminable patient into an examinable one; distinguish a generalized encephalopathy from a focal cognitive deficit (aphasia, amnesia) that only mimics confusion.
- Inspect the whole skin, including the back: a unilateral dermatomal vesicular rash is herpes zoster, and via zoster sine herpete a dermatome may be neuritically painful before or beyond its visible vesicles — reframing seemingly unrelated abdominal or limb pain as a single-root process rather than separate emergencies.
- Start empiric meningitis therapy before the diagnostic workup when bacterial meningitis is plausible — vancomycin, ceftriaxone, ampicillin (Listeria coverage for age > 50 or immunocompromise), and acyclovir — because delay worsens outcomes and CSF yield persists for a day or two; when the LP cannot be obtained (a rash over the puncture site), treat empirically and accept the difficulty of later stopping antibiotics without a culture.
- Interpret the tests honestly: Kernig and Brudzinski signs are specific but only ~5–10% sensitive (JAMA Rational Clinical Examination), so their absence never excludes meningitis; and for VZV in the CNS, use CSF PCR for acute zoster meningitis and CSF VZV IgG for the para-infectious vasculopathy/encephalitis, sending both when uncertain.
- Consider endocarditis in any fever-headache-AMS patient (septic emboli, mycotic aneurysm with subarachnoid hemorrhage, meningeal seeding, and the pneumococcal Austrian-syndrome triad), and close the loop on transferred patients — the follow-up is where the learning lives.
