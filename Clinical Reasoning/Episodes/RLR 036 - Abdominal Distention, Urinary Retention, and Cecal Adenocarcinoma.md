## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A deliberately short, two-case episode built around a single teaching move rather than a single diagnosis. **Case A** (Rabih presents): a man in his 60s with eight days of [[Abdominal Distension|abdominal distension]] he blames on Chipotle, now anorexic and off food for three days, tachycardic and hypertensive. Reza works the [[Solid Liquid Gas Framework|solid/liquid/gas]] schema and lands, late, on urinary retention — while an experienced triage nurse, without waiting for the workup, grabs a bladder scanner, finds a hugely distended bladder (~1.8 L), places a Foley, and fixes the patient in five minutes before anyone has laid hands on the abdomen. The concealed history (incontinence, diapers, shame) and a triggering [[Urinary Tract Infection|UTI]] on a background of undiagnosed [[Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia|BPH]] complete the picture; because a bladder held for eight days develops [[Detrusor Atony|detrusor weakness]], he leaves with a Foley, tamsulosin, and finasteride for a later voiding trial. **Case B** (Reza presents): a man in his 60s with nausea, vomiting, distension, and a *tense* abdomen but disproportionately little pain — a mismatch that, married to acute [[Illness Tempo|tempo]], points through the same schema toward **gas → [[Bowel Obstruction|large bowel obstruction]]**. Worst-case layering ("it's not probability that matters, it's morbidity that matters") holds blood and obstruction until excluded. CT shows a **cecal mass**, bowel obstruction, and moderate-to-severe [[Ascites|ascites]]; a ferritin of 5 supplies the [[Lead Time|lead-time]] iron-deficiency signal, and the working diagnosis is a **[[Cecal Adenocarcinoma|cecal adenocarcinoma]]** with [[Malignant Bowel Obstruction|malignant obstruction]] and [[Peritoneal Carcinomatosis|malignant ascites]] — a lesion missed on a recent colonoscopy (cecal lesions, farthest from the scope's start, are the likeliest to be missed). The human spine of the case is [[Diagnostic Humility|trust]]: a family that records conversations and refuses procedures is won over across a one-hour, three-attending family meeting, and management pivots to palliative diversion before the obstruction makes surgery impossible.

## Pearls

- **[[Abdominal Distension|Abdominal distension]] resolves into a [[Solid Liquid Gas Framework|solid/liquid/gas]] problem — and *liquid* includes the bladder.** Solids (stool, organomegaly), liquid ([[Ascites|ascites]] *and* a distended bladder), and gas (bloating, ileus, [[Bowel Obstruction|obstruction]]) organize the space; [[Urinary Retention|urinary retention]] is the easily-missed liquid cause, which is why any patient with lower-abdominal complaints that aren't purely epigastric earns a bladder scan.
- **A bladder scan is a five-minute bedside cure hiding behind a two-and-a-half-hour CT.** The most experienced triage nurse diagnosed and treated retention *before* the physicians examined the abdomen; the lesson of [[Return to the Bedside|returning to the bedside]] is that the fastest, cheapest tool sometimes ends the case, sparing hours of discomfort and imaging.
- **Diagnosing retention is not the end — always name its trigger, and expect the detrusor to be stunned.** In an older man the background is [[Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia|BPH]] and the acute trigger is often a [[Urinary Tract Infection|UTI]] (or constipation, or an anticholinergic) — a *background-plus-trigger* pattern; after ~a week of distension the [[Detrusor Atony|detrusor muscle weakens]] ("if you don't use it, you lose it"), so plan a Foley, an alpha-blocker, and a later voiding trial rather than expecting immediate recovery.
- **Time-course marries the phase-of-matter schema: acute distension favors gas.** [[Illness Tempo|Space and time]] narrow the differential — solid and liquid rarely accumulate in days, but gas can, so acute distension with a tense abdomen and *disproportionately mild pain* points to [[Bowel Obstruction|large bowel obstruction]] (more distension, less pain = lower/larger bowel; more pain, less distension = higher/small bowel).
- **In the first minutes of a case, "it's not probability that matters, it's morbidity that matters."** [[Worst-Case Reasoning|Assume the worst-case identity]] of each finding until excluded — acute free liquid is blood, acute gas is obstruction — because with a patient you've known for minutes, [[Base Rate|base rate]] yields to what would kill them. For a large bowel obstruction, the base-rate cause is **colon cancer** (with weeks-to-months of [[Lead Time|lead time]]: anemia, weight loss), while the one that develops acutely is **[[Sigmoid Volvulus|sigmoid volvulus]]**.
- **The same diagnosis reads differently in different rooms — and trust is part of the treatment.** [[Contextual Reasoning|Contextual clinical reasoning]]: retention is reflexive at a VA where its base rate is high but easily missed elsewhere. And when a mistrustful family records conversations and refuses procedures, the work is bridging that gap — here, a one-hour meeting with the medicine, surgery, and oncology attendings — not taking the distrust personally ([[Diagnostic Humility|humility]] and disclosure over defensiveness).

## Problem Representation

Two older men, each in his 60s, presenting with abdominal distension. In **Case A**, eight days of progressive distension (initially blamed on new food) with anorexia, no oral intake for three days, tachycardia, and hypertension prove to be acute-on-chronic urinary retention (~1.8 L on bladder scan) from undiagnosed BPH triggered by a UTI, with concealed lower-urinary-tract symptoms and incontinence — relieved immediately by catheterization and managed with an alpha-blocker and 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor given probable detrusor atony. In **Case B**, nausea, vomiting, distension, and a tense abdomen with disproportionately mild pain, in a patient who had left another hospital against advice after NG decompression of a "small bowel obstruction," are found on CT to be a cecal mass causing bowel obstruction with moderate-to-severe ascites and iron-deficiency anemia (ferritin 5) — a cecal adenocarcinoma with malignant bowel obstruction and peritoneal carcinomatosis (stage IV), managed with NG decompression, staging (CT chest, MRI), and a plan for palliative surgical diversion amid intensive trust-building with a mistrustful family.

## Case Narrative

Rabih opens by naming the episode's unusual shape: this is not a 30-minute diagnostic odyssey but a short case with "a tension and a crux," where the goal is not to nail the diagnosis but to learn from *how it actually unfolded at the bedside*. **Case A** is presented in real-world order. On the triage board: abdominal pain and distension, seen quickly because the scan-or-not decision has to beat the departing radiology tech. In the triage chair sits a man tachycardic to 110, hypertensive to 175/110, afebrile, with a truly normal respiratory rate; beside him, a visibly frightened wife holds something back while giving him room to tell it. For eight days — "exactly one week and one day" — his belly has grown large. He blames Chipotle eaten for the first time; he tried Miralax without relief; he is alarmed because he has now eaten nothing for three days from complete [[Anorexia|anorexia]], though he still drinks and has no clear post-prandial pain.

Reza's first move is to reject the red herring: "it cannot be the Chipotle." He then reaches for a schema he uses whenever the abdomen is distended — the [[Solid Liquid Gas Framework|phases of matter]]. **Solid**: stool (constipation, whose high [[Base Rate|base rate]] keeps it on the list even as the preserved liquid intake argues against it) or organomegaly. **Gas**: this is where [[Bowel Obstruction|obstruction]] lives, large bowel distending more than small; the absence of prominent nausea, vomiting, or altered bowel movements makes it feel less likely. **Liquid**: [[Ascites|ascites]] from portal hypertension, which doesn't fit. He flags the combined tachycardia *and* hypertension as a sympathetic surge, most likely from pain, and lays out a bedside plan — palpate, ask about bowel movements, percuss for dullness versus tympany, listen for bowel sounds. Prompted by Rabih that the man has *no* intake at all (so "worsens with eating" is untestable), Reza circles: eight days without progression on eating makes obstruction and ascites both less compelling, and he is on the verge of a physical exam and a hard-to-avoid CT.

Then the crux. The seasoned triage nurse — the most experienced in the department — steps out, returns with a tool, and within five minutes the patient is *diagnosed, treated, and back to normal*, grateful and discharged twenty minutes later. Before she even returns, Reza (not yet knowing the answer) adds the missing liquid cause to his own differential: [[Urinary Retention|urinary retention]] presenting as distension. The bladder scan shows a hugely distended bladder — a transcribed "18 liters" that is physiologically closer to **~1.8 L** — and a Foley relieves everything at once. He had never carried a diagnosis of [[Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia|BPH]] despite long-standing lower-urinary-tract symptoms; he had pyuria and a leukocytosis and was treated for a [[Urinary Tract Infection|UTI]] as a probable trigger while cultures were pending, and was started on tamsulosin and finasteride. The wife's withheld history surfaces: he had been dribbling, had lost urinary control, and was using diapers — a source of shame that kept it out of his own account. Reza's takeaway is the [[Return to the Bedside|power of the bedside tool]]: any patient with abdominal complaints that aren't strictly epigastric deserves a bladder scan, because otherwise they sit for two-and-a-half uncomfortable hours awaiting a CT that reveals what five minutes could have.

Reza then adds the management nuance and a physiologic pearl. Retention is never an end-diagnosis — you must find its trigger (BPH here, but also constipation or a UTI, often multifactorial: a *vulnerable substrate plus a trigger*). And after eight days of a maximally stretched bladder, the [[Detrusor Atony|detrusor muscle weakens]] — "if you don't use it, you lose it" — so the bladder may not immediately wake; the man keeps his Foley for days, with primary care managing the first episode (urology for the refractory) and a voiding trial planned one-to-two weeks out to give the tamsulosin time to work. He closes on [[Contextual Reasoning|contextual clinical reasoning]]: in a VA ED this diagnosis is second nature because its base rate is high, but the same presentation in another practice setting might not trigger the thought at all.

**Case B**, presented by Reza in return, is a man in his 60s with nausea, vomiting, distension, and decreased oral intake. At the bedside his abdomen is *tense and distended* with absent bowel sounds, yet he lies comfortably and tolerates palpation without much pain — a mismatch between the alarming exam and the calm patient. Rabih layers Aaron's [[Illness Tempo|"space and time"]] framework onto the [[Solid Liquid Gas Framework|solid/liquid/gas]] schema: which phase accumulates *acutely*? Gas. Solid and liquid can occasionally rise fast (rapid ascites in acute heart failure, rapid hepatosplenomegaly with venous outflow obstruction, intraperitoneal hemorrhage), but the base rate plus the disproportionately mild pain and prominent vomiting point to a **[[Bowel Obstruction|large bowel obstruction]]**. He invokes the obstruction-level rule — more distension with less pain sits lower/larger, more pain with less distension sits higher/smaller — and reasons about cause by [[Lead Time|looking back at the past]]: the base-rate cause of large bowel obstruction is **colon cancer**, which carries a lead time of weeks to months (an obstructing tumor is large enough to have already caused anemia, weight loss, or intermittent symptoms), while the cause that can present *acutely* is a **[[Sigmoid Volvulus|sigmoid volvulus]]** — an actionable distinction, since volvulus summons GI as well as surgery.

Asked how he'd proceed, Rabih would put an ultrasound probe on the belly first as the most specific quick test of *what* is distending it: liquid free in the belly might redirect toward paracentesis, a full bladder toward a scan, and solid/gas toward CT. Reza offers a hypothetical POCUS showing *both* gas and liquid, and Rabih delivers the episode's memorable line: when two findings appear together you can't assume they arrived together, so [[Worst-Case Reasoning|assume the worst-case identity of each]] — acute gas is obstruction, acute free liquid is blood — because "in just minutes of knowing a patient, it's not probability that matters, it's morbidity that matters." The real CT (not an ultrasound) shows a **cecal mass**, bowel distension, and **moderate-to-severe [[Ascites|ascites]]**; a ferritin of **5** from months earlier supplies iron-deficiency anemia, and an NG tube to suction brings immediate relief. Learning the man had earlier gone to another hospital, been diagnosed with a small bowel obstruction, been NG-decompressed, improved, and then left against advice only to recur, Reza reasons from the base rate of ascites: a transudate from [[Cirrhosis|cirrhosis]] can't be linked to the obstruction, so the ascites is likely *exudative*, and the commonest exudative ascites in the US is cancer. That unifies everything — a **[[Cecal Adenocarcinoma|cecal adenocarcinoma]]** producing a [[Malignant Bowel Obstruction|malignant bowel obstruction]] with secondary [[Peritoneal Carcinomatosis|peritoneal seeding]] — a working hypothesis he'd hold at ~90%, with the remaining causes not worth chasing until tissue is in hand.

The back half of Case B is about people, not pathology. The family asks whether the cancer should have been caught on a recent colonoscopy read as normal; Reza's answer is a model of disclosure — a missed lesion is a predictable statistical certainty across thousands of exams, and a *cecal* lesion, farthest from the rectum where the scope begins, is among the likeliest to be missed, yet how much of this to say, and how slowly, is "the magic of medicine," read against who the family is and the weight of the moment. Trust frays further: the family begins recording conversations, and Reza asks them (for the sake of trainees who can't consent for themselves, and because covert recording is legally and relationally fraught) not to. On the fluid, the team declines a diagnostic [[Paracentesis|paracentesis]] — there's low concern for [[Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis|SBP]] (which needs a failing liver) or secondary peritonitis, and a mechanical obstruction needs a mechanical fix, with the OR affording a better sample than a needle. But the family, having lost relatives in the OR, wants answers before any procedure. Staging is worked pragmatically: an oncology friend advises that bowel cancers don't need a PET, so a CT chest completes it (no lung metastases), and an MRI for peritoneal nodularity confirms likely **peritoneal carcinomatosis** — stage IV. A one-hour family meeting with the medicine, surgery, and oncology attendings finally earns the family's trust. The plan is palliative: relieve the obstruction (possibly only a diverting ileostomy/colostomy if the mass can't be resected) before delay makes the patient a non-candidate and leaves only hospice — "the more time you waste, the less of a candidate you'll be." Reza closes on the arc from a family recording him to a family letting a surgeon operate, and on the emotional labor of sitting at the bedside asking the man to stay and be treated.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring]] on a benign trigger.** Both cases begin with a comforting attribution — the patient's "Chipotle," and (in Case B) the prior hospital's "small bowel obstruction" that transiently resolved. Reza's discipline is to *name and drop* the anchor ("it cannot be the Chipotle") before it steers the workup, the counter-move to a food-poisoning or benign-SBO frame that would delay the real diagnosis.
- **The [[Solid Liquid Gas Framework|solid/liquid/gas schema]] as the organizing engine.** Distension is made tractable by phase of matter, and the episode's payoff is remembering that *liquid* includes the bladder — the single addition that catches urinary retention, the diagnosis Reza reaches only after the nurse has already made it.
- **[[Illness Tempo|Tempo]] as a discriminator ("space and time").** Marrying time-course to the schema does real work in Case B: because solid and liquid rarely accumulate over days while gas can, acute distension with a tense abdomen and mild pain is steered toward large bowel obstruction — with the explicit humility that rapid ascites, rapid organomegaly, and hemorrhage are not fully exonerated.
- **[[Worst-Case Reasoning|Morbidity over probability]] in the opening minutes.** "It's not probability that matters, it's morbidity that matters" is a deliberate suspension of [[Base Rate|base-rate]] thinking for do-not-miss triage: when gas and liquid coexist, assume obstruction *and* blood until each is excluded, because early and lethal beats likely.
- **[[Base Rate|Base-rate reasoning]] with [[Lead Time|lead time]].** Once stabilized, base rate returns: the commonest large-bowel obstruction is colon cancer, and its *story* (weeks-to-months of anemia, weight loss, intermittent symptoms — here a ferritin of 5) both confirms the chronic lesion and separates it from an acute [[Sigmoid Volvulus|sigmoid volvulus]].
- **[[Contextual Reasoning|Contextual clinical reasoning]] and the nurse as diagnostician.** The same retention that is reflexive in a VA ED (high local base rate) may never be considered elsewhere; the most experienced nurse solves the case before the physicians examine the patient, a reminder that expertise is distributed across the team and calibrated to setting.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Humility, disclosure, and trust]] as clinical tasks.** The missed-colonoscopy conversation, the recorded encounters, and the hour-long tri-service family meeting frame trust-building and honest error disclosure as part of the treatment, not adjuncts to it — and Reza models not taking a family's earned distrust personally.

## Teaching Discussion

- Work abdominal distension through the solid/liquid/gas schema, and explicitly include the bladder under "liquid": any patient with lower-abdominal complaints that aren't strictly epigastric should get a bladder scan, because urinary retention is the cheap, fast, high-yield miss that a CT would only reveal hours later.
- Treat the bladder scan as both diagnosis and therapy — a distended bladder relieved by a Foley closes the loop in minutes — and let the case be a standing example of returning to the bedside before reaching for imaging.
- After diagnosing retention, always identify the precipitant (BPH background with a UTI, constipation, or anticholinergic trigger) and anticipate detrusor atony after prolonged distension: plan a Foley, an alpha-blocker (± 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor), and a delayed voiding trial rather than expecting the bladder to recover immediately.
- Marry illness tempo to the distension schema: acute distension favors gas, so a tense abdomen with disproportionately mild pain and prominent vomiting should raise large bowel obstruction; use the level rule (more distension/less pain = lower and larger; more pain/less distension = higher and smaller).
- In the first minutes of an undifferentiated abdomen, reason by morbidity rather than probability — assume coexisting gas and free fluid are obstruction and blood until excluded — then, once stabilized, return to base rate: colon cancer is the commonest large bowel obstruction (look for the lead-time signals of anemia and weight loss), with sigmoid volvulus as the acute alternative.
- Read ascites that can't be explained by cirrhosis as probably exudative, and remember malignancy is the commonest exudative ascites in the US; a cecal mass plus obstruction plus ascites plus iron-deficiency anemia unifies as a malignant bowel obstruction with peritoneal carcinomatosis.
- Choose the diagnostic test to the decision: a mechanical obstruction needs a mechanical fix, so a diagnostic paracentesis is low-yield when the OR will provide better tissue and SBP is unlikely (SBP essentially requires a failing liver); recognize that ascitic fluid can cushion and mask the signs of peritonitis.
- Handle missed diagnoses and mistrust as clinical work: disclose honestly (a cecal lesion is among the likeliest colonoscopy misses, being farthest from the scope's start), read how much to say to whom, don't take recorded encounters or refused procedures personally, and invest the time — even an hour with three attending services — to build the trust that makes treatment possible before delay forecloses surgical options.
