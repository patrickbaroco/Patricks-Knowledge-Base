> [!summary] Scan Summary
> This episode is the second installment of **The Consult Question**, a blinded-consult format distinct from the standard Neurology VMR: **Aaron Berkowitz** reasons through an unknown case in real time, aliquot by aliquot, with no advance knowledge. A 39-year-old man with a remote combined pancreas-kidney transplant on chronic immunosuppression presents with four weeks of worsening, now radicular back pain and two days of new binocular horizontal diplopia. Berkowitz builds parallel localization frameworks for back pain (extradural/intradural-extramedullary/intradural-intramedullary) and diplopia (binocular vs. monocular, then working proximally from muscle to nerve to nucleus to **[[Internuclear Ophthalmoplegia|MLF]]**), then works through an exhaustive infectious and neoplastic differential — post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder, HIV seroconversion, sarcoidosis, coccidioidomycosis — as exam findings accumulate into a pattern of multiple cranial neuropathies (a pupil-sparing right CN III palsy, a complete left CN VII palsy) plus a lumbosacral polyradiculopathy. After an extensive negative infectious and malignant workup, the diagnosis is **secondary syphilis** with a genuinely atypical presentation: osteitis, lymphadenopathy, and neurosyphilis manifesting as meningitis, cranial neuritis, and polyradiculitis — a disease Berkowitz never once names during his entire differential, and says so candidly afterward.

## Pearls

- **The extradural / intradural-extramedullary / intradural-intramedullary framework, usually taught only for spinal tumors, applies to all spinal pathology** — a generalizable first-pass localization schema for any case combining back pain with neurologic features: are we outside the dura (bone, disc — an "orthopedic" problem), inside the dura but outside the cord (CSF space, nerve roots, meninges), or inside the spinal cord itself?
- **The first branch point for any double vision is binocular vs. monocular**: cover one eye — if the diplopia resolves, it's binocular (a true ocular-motor/neurologic problem); if it persists, it's monocular, usually a lens or corneal problem, and not a neurologic emergency.
- **Pupil-sparing vs. pupil-involving CN III (oculomotor) palsy has a clean anatomic explanation.** The parasympathetic pupillomotor fibers run on the *outer* surface of the nerve, while the somatic motor fibers run more centrally — so a compressive lesion (aneurysm, uncal herniation) tends to hit the peripheral pupillary fibers first (pupil-involving), while an ischemic or infiltrative process tends to spare them (pupil-sparing) — the same mechanism behind the diabetic microvascular CN III/IV/VI palsy teaching, extended here to an infiltrative process.
- **A complete facial palsy (both upper and lower face) localizes to the facial nerve or its nucleus itself; a lower-face-only palsy localizes to a contralateral upper motor neuron lesion** — the same forehead-sparing-vs-not principle used throughout this vault's episodes, applied again here to distinguish central from peripheral CN VII involvement.
- **Guillain-Barré syndrome's full name — acute inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (AIDP) — is a reminder that it affects nerve roots, not just peripheral nerves**, and that roughly half of GBS patients have facial weakness, often bilateral, not just the "textbook" ascending leg-then-arm paralysis most people are taught.
- **Syphilis earns its nickname "the great imitator" by genuinely mimicking almost anything** — motor neuron disease, Parkinsonian syndromes, mass lesions (gummas) — and a rare disease's atypical presentation is far harder to recognize than a common disease's atypical presentation, because real-world pattern recognition depends on having seen enough variation to know what doesn't fit the textbook.

## Problem Representation

A 39-year-old man with a combined pancreas-kidney transplant six years earlier (for type 1 diabetes), on chronic immunosuppression with mycophenolate mofetil and tacrolimus plus TMP-SMX prophylaxis, presents with four weeks of progressively worsening, now diffuse and radicular back pain against a background of chronic post-traumatic mid-back pain, and two days of new binocular, non-fatigable horizontal diplopia worse on right gaze, without weakness or bowel/bladder symptoms. He lives in California's Central Valley and reports inconsistent condom use with male partners; a diarrheal illness five months earlier resolved fully. Exam shows a right pupil-sparing partial CN III palsy (ptosis with impaired adduction and elevation), bilateral abduction limitation worse on the right, a complete left CN VII palsy, an absent right patellar reflex, and patchy lumbosacral/thigh sensory loss without a discrete level — multiple cranial neuropathies plus a lumbosacral polyradiculopathy. MRI shows enhancement of multiple bilateral cranial nerves, leptomeningeal enhancement of the distal conus and cauda equina, and vertebral body edema/enhancement at T3, T6, and T8; CT shows mild diffuse lymphadenopathy; CSF shows a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis with normal glucose. After an extensive infectious and malignant workup returns largely unrevealing, RPR and TPPA confirm secondary syphilis, with osteitis, lymphadenopathy, and neurosyphilis (meningitis, cranial neuritis, and polyradiculitis) as a genuinely atypical presentation.

## Case Narrative

This episode is the second installment of **The Consult Question**, a Clinical Problem Solvers spinoff hosted by Dan Minter, Doug Pett, and Lindsay Shipley, in which a blinded case is presented aliquot by aliquot to a subspecialist discussant — here Aaron Berkowitz, introduced by Doug as the author of *Clinical Neurology and Neuroanatomy: A Localization-Based Approach* — who reasons through it extemporaneously with no advance knowledge.

**Chief concern (two words): back pain and double vision.** Before any history, Berkowitz builds two parallel localization frameworks. For back pain, he generalizes a schema usually reserved for spinal tumors — extradural (bone, disc: an orthopedic-type problem), intradural-extramedullary (meninges, CSF space, nerve roots), and intradural-intramedullary (the spinal cord itself) — to all spinal pathology, then layers on his **[[Localization × Time Course|E=mc² time-course framework]]**: sudden onset suggests a vascular event (spinal cord infarct, hemorrhage); acute (hours-to-days) suggests infection or inflammation (osteomyelitis, epidural abscess, infectious or inflammatory transverse myelitis); subacute suggests a mass lesion (metastases, far more common to the spine than a primary spinal tumor; prostate cancer in an older man; fungal or tubercular infection; paraneoplastic/antibody-mediated inflammation); chronic suggests degenerative disease. For double vision, he starts from first principles — the eyes must move in near-perfect coordination or diplopia results — and establishes the binocular-vs-monocular branch point (covering one eye resolves binocular diplopia but not monocular), then works proximally through the possible levels for binocular diplopia: neuromuscular junction (myasthenia, fatigable), muscle (thyroid eye disease, an orbital mass), cranial nerve (III, IV, or VI), brainstem nucleus (expected to come with much more brainstem real estate affected), or an **[[Internuclear Ophthalmoplegia|internuclear ophthalmoplegia]]** (a lesion of the MLF connecting the CN VI nucleus on one side to the CN III nucleus on the other, coordinating lateral conjugate gaze). Asked to imagine what could unify both complaints, he floats cancer with spinal and leptomeningeal or orbital/cavernous-sinus metastases; multiple sclerosis (INO plus transverse myelitis); or **neuromyelitis optica** — once considered an MS variant, now known to have its own anti-aquaporin-4 antibody, classically affecting the spinal cord ("M," myelitis) and optic nerves ("O," optica), sometimes the brain as well.

**History reframes everything around immunosuppression.** The patient is a 39-year-old man with a remote combined pancreas-kidney transplant (six years earlier, for type 1 diabetes) on chronic mycophenolate mofetil and tacrolimus plus TMP-SMX prophylaxis, four weeks of progressively worsening back pain (against three years of chronic post-traumatic mid-back pain, now diffuse, sharp, radiating down the legs, with no weakness or bowel/bladder symptoms), and two days of new horizontal binocular, non-fluctuating diplopia, worse on right gaze, plus mild recent headaches. He lives in California's Central Valley, has had three male sexual partners in the past year with inconsistent condom use, and had a diarrheal illness five months earlier that resolved completely. Berkowitz immediately flags the immunosuppressed context as opening two parallel differentials — a wider range of pathogens, and post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder or other malignancy from the impaired immune surveillance — while noting the geographic detail (Central Valley) without yet naming it explicitly. On the back pain, the long prodrome of prior injury followed by progressive, poorly-localized, radiating pain (without cord-level findings) suggests root compression from a worsening structural process; given the immunosuppression, he reasons that a bacterial epidural abscess would likely have made the patient much sicker by now, favoring a more indolent process — tubercular or fungal infection, or a malignant process eroding bone. On the diplopia, he works through horizontal gaze mechanics (right gaze requires the right lateral rectus to abduct and the left medial rectus to adduct) to ask whether this could be a right CN VI palsy, a left CN III/medial rectus problem, or an INO, and specifically asks whether the pancreas transplant has resolved the patient's diabetes — relevant because diabetic microvascular infarcts classically affect the ocular motor nerves. He also raises CN VI's status as a classic **false localizing sign**: because of its unusually long, tortuous subarachnoid course over the clivus, elevated intracranial pressure alone — with no local lesion — can produce a sixth nerve palsy. Considering how back pain and diplopia might unify, he proposes a diffuse meningeal process (the meninges extend around both brain and spinal cord, so a single meningitis — classically tubercular, but seen with bacterial disease too — could explain both), two separate lesions (a structural spinal process plus an unrelated cranial one), or the injury being a red herring entirely.

**Exam: multiple cranial neuropathies plus a polyradiculopathy.** Vitals and general exam are normal, without meningismus or rash; mental status is normal. There's ptosis with normal pupils; on horizontal gaze, both eyes have limited abduction (right worse than left, not crossing midline), and the right eye also fails to fully adduct; vertical gaze shows mild bilateral limited elevation. There's nasolabial flattening and impaired eye closure on the left, other cranial nerves are normal, strength is full (pain-limited in the legs), reflexes are 2+ throughout except an absent right patellar reflex, toes are downgoing, and there's patchy decreased pain/temperature/light-touch sensation over the mid-to-low back and thighs without a discrete sensory level; coordination is unremarkable and gait can't be assessed due to pain. Berkowitz first addresses the absent meningismus: citing the same JAMA *Rational Clinical Examination* literature invoked in [[CPS 174 - Right-Sided Hemiparesis and Multifocal Septic Embolic Stroke from Staphylococcus Aureus Endocarditis|CPS 174]] (Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs are only about 5% sensitive), and adding that immunosuppression can further blunt the meningeal inflammatory response, he keeps meningitis fully on the table. He then works the ptosis: with equal, reactive pupils, a compressive CN III lesion (which typically hits the peripheral pupillomotor fibers first) is less likely than an ischemic or infiltrative one (which tends to spare them); combined with the impaired adduction and elevation, this is a **pupil-sparing right CN III palsy**, missing only the pupillary component. The bilateral abduction limitation (right worse than left) could reflect bilateral CN VI involvement, possibly the false-localizing elevated-ICP mechanism again, layered on top of the more clearly localizing right CN III findings. The left facial weakness, involving both upper and lower face, localizes to the CN VII nerve or nucleus itself rather than a central (upper motor neuron) lesion. Taken together — a right CN III palsy and a left CN VII palsy — Berkowitz argues these can't come from one small brainstem lesion (CN III's nucleus is in the midbrain, CN VII's in the pons, on different sides, in a "banana-shaped" pattern that would require an anatomically implausible lesion while sparing everything else and leaving the patient awake and conversant); this pattern of **multiple cranial neuropathies** instead points to a skull-base, meningeal, or diffuse process — and specifically raises Guillain-Barré syndrome (correcting the common misconception that it's purely a polyneuropathy — its full name, acute inflammatory demyelinating **polyradiculo**neuropathy, includes the roots, and roughly half of GBS patients have facial weakness, often bilateral) and Miller Fisher syndrome (though the patient lacks the ataxia and global areflexia that syndrome would predict). Below the neck, the patchy sensory loss, absent right patellar reflex, and radicular pain complete a picture of **lumbosacral polyradiculopathy** — together with the cranial neuropathies, a process spanning both the cranial nerves and nerve roots, i.e., the peripheral nervous system broadly. Given the immunosuppression, Berkowitz raises CMV polyradiculitis (classically seen with CD4 <50, though this patient's clinical picture doesn't quite fit), a diffuse infectious or neoplastic meningeal process, and — reasoning from the pupil-sparing, infiltrative-appearing CN III pattern — **neurolymphomatosis**, a post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder infiltrating both cranial nerves and nerve roots.

**Predicting the imaging, then confirming a nonspecific inflammatory picture.** Before results return, Berkowitz predicts enhancement of cranial nerves and roots without parenchymal brain lesions — confirmed by contrast-enhanced MRI, showing enhancement of bilateral CN III, V, and VII and the right CN VI, plus leptomeningeal enhancement of the distal conus and cauda equina and edema/enhancement of the T3, T6, and T8 vertebral bodies (with normal marrow signal — an unusual, non-marrow-infiltrating pattern he flags without fully explaining). CT chest/abdomen/pelvis shows mild diffuse mediastinal, abdominal, and retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy, most prominent in the hila bilaterally. A recent hemoglobin A1c of 4.8% answers his earlier question: the pancreas transplant has normalized the patient's glucose metabolism, lowering diabetic microvascular disease as an explanation. CSF shows a mildly elevated opening pressure (22 cm H2O), a lymphocytic pleocytosis (28 WBC, 87% lymphocytes), a mildly elevated protein (68), and a normal glucose ratio — an abnormal but nonspecific profile, consistent with infection, inflammation, or neoplasia, and not sharply discriminating given this isn't a classic meningitic syndrome. Berkowitz performs an explicit debiasing exercise — asking what he'd think with the immunosuppression "removed" from the picture — which surfaces HIV at the time of seroconversion (which can itself cause a facial palsy and a meningeal process, and could occur *on top of* the existing transplant immunosuppression, with a standard antibody test potentially falsely negative during acute seroconversion) and sarcoidosis (fitting the lymphadenopathy and cranial neuropathies, though he notes it would be unusual for sarcoidosis — itself requiring an intact granulomatous immune response — to newly develop in a pharmacologically immunosuppressed patient). Pressed to commit, he explicitly declines a confident answer, offering instead a hedged pair: HIV seroconversion if trying to catch the "stump the attending" answer, or post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder with cranial nerve and root infiltration if trying to unify everything into one process — genuine uncertainty, stated plainly.

**An extensive negative workup.** Further CSF studies show no oligoclonal bands, a normal IgG index, negative Gram stain and bacterial culture, and unremarkable cytology and flow cytometry; CSF PCR/antibody testing for VZV, HSV, CMV, EBV, and Lyme are all negative. Serum anti-GQ1b antibody (for Miller Fisher) is negative. ACE is mildly elevated (87, nl 9–67) — nonspecific, though notable given the sarcoidosis discussion. SPEP/UPEP/free light chains are unremarkable; LDH is mildly elevated (248, nl 125–243). Serum HIV, CMV, EBV, and QuantiFERON-TB are negative; Lyme, coccidioidomycosis (specifically tested given the Central Valley residence), *Bartonella*, and *Brucella* serologies are all negative. A CT-guided para-aortic lymph node biopsy shows only a benign reactive node, with negative AFB, fungal cultures, and broad-range PCR. Berkowitz notes CSF flow cytometry and cytology are notoriously insensitive for lymphoma unless multiple large-volume lumbar punctures are performed, so neurolymphomatosis isn't fully excluded; he wonders whether the benign lymph node result reflects genuine absence of disease or a sampling error, given how cautious a biopsy near the aorta would need to be; and he raises West Nile virus as an untested possibility, but reasons against it since immunosuppressed patients with arboviral CNS infections are typically very sick, which doesn't fit this patient. He's now down to two leading hypotheses — HIV seroconversion or neurolymphomatosis/PTLD — while explicitly acknowledging there are surely possibilities he hasn't considered.

**Resolution: the great imitator.** An RPR returns positive at a titer of 1:64 — Berkowitz immediately exclaims "the great imitator" before formal confirmation. TPPA is reactive, and metagenomic sequencing of the CSF is positive for *Treponema pallidum*. The final diagnosis is **secondary syphilis** with an atypical presentation of osteitis (explaining the vertebral edema/enhancement without marrow replacement), lymphadenopathy, and **neurosyphilis** manifesting as meningitis, cranial neuritis, and polyradiculitis. Berkowitz reflects candidly that syphilis was never once uttered in his entire differential — reviewing the classic teaching (early meningovascular syphilis with meningitis and stroke, which this patient didn't fully fit; late tabes dorsalis with sensory ataxia and lancinating pains, or general paresis with dementia, neither present here) and syphilis's reputation as "the great imitator" for its genuinely protean presentations (motor neuron disease mimics, Parkinsonian syndromes, mass-lesion gummas). He notes the classic Argyll Robertson pupil (light-near dissociation) was absent here, consistent with the pupil-sparing exam, and that cranial polyneuropathy plus polyradiculitis isn't a classically taught syphilis presentation at all — making this a genuine outlier even to the treating infectious disease team, who found the osteitis particularly unprecedented. Doug notes syphilis testing was sent as part of a broad initial infectious panel (alongside HIV, given the exposure history) rather than being specifically hypothesized — a case where empiric broad-panel serology caught a diagnosis nobody was actively pursuing. Treatment was 14 days of intravenous penicillin G followed by two doses of intramuscular benzathine penicillin, requiring penicillin desensitization given a documented allergy; the patient developed fever, hypotension, and diffuse pruritus on day one of treatment, concerning for a **Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction** (more likely than a true penicillin allergy given the clinical context) — a systemic inflammatory reaction to the sudden lysis of large numbers of spirochetes. At two-month follow-up, his back pain had nearly resolved and only a residual, near-resolved right abducens palsy remained.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **"Predict before you look," a recurring habit.** Berkowitz correctly predicts the MRI's enhancement pattern (cranial nerves, leptomeninges, roots) without parenchymal lesions before it's revealed — the same **[[Test Interpretation|pretest-calibration]]** discipline seen across [[CPS 146 - Right-Sided Weakness and Numbness and Medial Medullary Syndrome from a Vertebral Artery Dissection|CPS 146]], [[CPS 160 - Hiccups and Vertigo from a PICA-Territory Stroke via Patent Foramen Ovale|CPS 160]], [[CPS 167 - Unilateral Sensory Changes and Multiple Sclerosis with a Brown-Séquard Pattern|CPS 167]], and [[CPS 174 - Right-Sided Hemiparesis and Multifocal Septic Embolic Stroke from Staphylococcus Aureus Endocarditis|CPS 174]].
- **"Remove the context and re-derive," reused.** As in CPS 174, Berkowitz explicitly asks what he'd think with the immunosuppression "taken out" of the picture — a deliberate, repeated debiasing technique for checking whether a differential is being appropriately or inappropriately anchored on one salient contextual fact.
- **A negative meningeal exam distrusted on principle, twice.** The same Kernig's/Brudzinski's sensitivity argument from CPS 174 recurs here nearly verbatim, reinforcing it as a standing habit rather than a one-off point.
- **Owning a genuine blind spot.** Berkowitz's post-reveal reflection — that recognizing an atypical presentation of a *rare* disease is far harder than recognizing an atypical presentation of a *common* one, because pattern recognition depends on accumulated real-world exposure — is candid **[[Diagnostic Humility|diagnostic humility]]** about the limits of expertise itself, not just about this one case.
- **A hedged, reasoned "I don't know."** Rather than force a single confident final answer when pressed, Berkowitz offers two named possibilities with his reasoning for each (HIV seroconversion vs. neurolymphomatosis/PTLD) — genuine uncertainty stated plainly, echoing the pending-diagnosis ending of [[CPS 153]].
- **Broad empiric testing catching what directed reasoning missed.** Syphilis serology was sent as part of a broad initial infectious panel (alongside HIV, prompted by the exposure history) rather than a specifically reasoned hypothesis — a reminder that well-chosen empiric panels can complement, not replace, directed clinical reasoning, especially for diseases rare enough to fall outside any clinician's personal pattern library.

## Teaching Discussion

- Generalize the extradural/intradural-extramedullary/intradural-intramedullary framework beyond spinal tumors to any back-pain-with-neurologic-features case.
- For any diplopia, start with binocular vs. monocular (cover one eye), then work proximally from neuromuscular junction to muscle to cranial nerve to brainstem nucleus to the MLF (internuclear ophthalmoplegia).
- Understand the anatomic basis for pupil-sparing vs. pupil-involving CN III palsy (peripheral pupillomotor fibers vs. central somatic fibers) to distinguish compressive from ischemic/infiltrative causes at the bedside.
- Distinguish complete (peripheral, both upper and lower face) from lower-face-only (central, upper motor neuron) facial weakness whenever CN VII is involved.
- Remember Guillain-Barré syndrome's full name — acute inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy — includes the nerve roots, and that facial weakness (often bilateral) occurs in roughly half of cases.
- In an immunosuppressed patient with multifocal neurologic findings, deliberately re-derive the differential with the immunosuppression "removed" to check for context-driven anchoring, and don't let a blunted meningeal exam lower suspicion for meningitis.
- Remember CSF cytology and flow cytometry are insensitive for lymphoma on a single sample — multiple large-volume lumbar punctures materially improve yield before ruling out neurolymphomatosis.
- Send broad empiric infectious serologies (including syphilis) in unexplained multifocal neurologic disease, especially with relevant exposure history — some diagnoses are more efficiently caught by a well-chosen panel than by hypothesis-driven testing alone.
- Recognize syphilis's "great imitator" reputation and its extremely broad range of atypical presentations (osteitis, polyradiculitis, and multiple cranial neuropathies among them), and hold genuine humility about diagnosing rare-disease presentations that fall outside personal or textbook pattern libraries.
