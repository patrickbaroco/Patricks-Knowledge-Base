## Pearls

- **Work the diplopia pathway from the eye backward:** orbit/extraocular muscles → neuromuscular junction → cranial nerves III/IV/VI → cavernous sinus → brainstem nuclei → the **[[Medial Longitudinal Fasciculus|MLF]]** (which links the CN III nucleus on one side to the CN VI nucleus on the other for conjugate horizontal gaze, and also carries vertical eye movement and vestibulo-ocular reflex signals) and the **PPRF** (the horizontal gaze center — its failure causes a conjugate gaze palsy, not diplopia from misalignment).
- **"Diplopia is always acute"** (a Marty Samuels aphorism): even when the underlying process has been slowly evolving, there is a discrete moment fusion finally breaks and double vision starts — so a patient reporting sudden-onset diplopia doesn't necessarily mean a sudden-onset lesion.
- **Multiple cranial neuropathies have a short list of places to look:** inside the brainstem (nuclei or fascicles), or outside it — subarachnoid/meningeal space, skull base (including the cavernous sinus), or the "target organ" (orbit, face, neck) — plus a set of neuromuscular **"fakeouts"** (Guillain-Barré/Miller Fisher/Bickerstaff, myasthenia gravis, orbital myopathies like OPMD) that mimic multiple cranial nerve deficits without one localizing lesion.
- **Pupil-sparing third nerve palsy is a useful but imperfect rule.** The classic teaching (pupil-sparing = ischemic/microvascular, not compressive) reflects the parasympathetic fibers' peripheral position within the nerve — but compressive lesions can, depending on exactly where along the nerve's course they act, still spare those fibers, so "pupil sparing" should lower but not eliminate concern for a structural cause.
- **Predict before you look.** Asked whether the MRI would be normal, a discussant explicitly reasons it out loud (the presence of fever argues against a "fakeout" and for a true lesion) before the scan is shown — a habit that sharpens interpretation and makes each image a genuine test of the leading hypothesis.
- **Fever and tachycardia are not proof of infection — beware the "problem of induction."** Treating empirically for a presumed infection and seeing improvement doesn't prove the infection was the cause; here the improvement likely reflected the natural remitting course of an inflammatory disease, and the causal story only unraveled when the patient relapsed.
- **The MRI "cascade" (or "waterfall") sign** — FLAIR/T2 hyperintensity tracking from the internal capsule down through the midbrain into the brainstem along the corticospinal tract at the mesodiencephalic junction — is classically described as fairly specific for neuro-Behçet's disease, though it can also be seen in PML, NMO, and neurosarcoidosis.
- **Behçet's disease is not confined to its classic "Silk Road" geography.** This patient had no Near/Middle Eastern or East Asian ancestry; in a globalized, mobile world, geographic "typicality" should inform but not exclude a diagnosis.

## Problem Representation

A 36-year-old woman with no prior medical history presents with a one-week history of sudden-onset diplopia (woke up seeing double), followed by fever, lethargy, unsteadiness, and evolving right-sided facial weakness and numbness — referred urgently from her general practitioner. She is HIV-negative with no toxin or substance exposure. Exam shows tachycardia, fever (38.7°C), diaphoresis, and disorientation to place and time without meningismus; cranial nerve exam reveals a pupil-sparing left third nerve palsy, a partial right sixth nerve palsy, right facial numbness to light touch, upper-motor-neuron-pattern right facial weakness, and direction-changing gaze-evoked nystagmus worse on right gaze; limb strength, tone, reflexes, and sensation are normal, but there is right-sided dysmetria and an ataxic, right-veering gait. Initial CT is normal; CSF shows a lymphocytic pleocytosis (253 cells) with elevated protein (1.27) and normal glucose, with negative cryptococcal antigen, TB GeneXpert/culture, and bacterial studies; blood cultures are negative. She is treated empirically for presumed Listeria rhombencephalitis with three weeks of IV antibiotics, improves, and is discharged, remaining stable at initial follow-up. Two months later she relapses with recurrent fever, tachycardia, similar neurologic findings, and new right hemiparesis. Further history from family at this second presentation reveals recurrent oral ulcers since adolescence and old genital ulcer scarring, alongside acneiform pustular skin lesions on exam. Repeat LP again shows a lymphocytic pleocytosis with elevated protein. MRI brain shows multifocal FLAIR-hyperintense lesions in the diencephalon, basal ganglia, and brainstem, with a "cascade"/"waterfall" sign of hyperintensity extending from the internal capsule into the midbrain; a pathergy skin test is positive, and an autoimmune panel is otherwise negative. She improves markedly with IV corticosteroids, and the combined clinical (recurrent oral/genital ulcers), radiologic (cascade sign), and pathergy findings establish a diagnosis of parenchymal neuro-Behçet's disease (brainstem/rhombencephalitis phenotype).

## Case Narrative

Yusuf (the presenting resident, Cape Town) gives the chief complaint: diplopia associated with fever. Berkowitz asks Valeria to start with an approach to the diplopia alone, folding the fever back in later. Valeria lays out a structural framework: first distinguish monocular from binocular diplopia (binocular implies misalignment from a neurologic or neuromuscular cause; monocular is usually ophthalmologic, though rare cortical/occipital causes exist), then work through the extraocular muscles (superior/inferior oblique, lateral/medial rectus), the nerves that innervate them (III, IV, VI) and the neuromuscular junction, the path those nerves take through the orbit, optic canal, and **cavernous sinus** (where a lesion is an emergency, especially with a "concomitant symptom" like fever), down through the skull base to the brainstem, where the relevant nuclei sit in the midbrain and pons. Berkowitz builds on this — noting it's often easier to work the diplopia pathway "from the eyes backward" — and adds one more structure: the **[[Medial Longitudinal Fasciculus|MLF]]**, a brainstem white-matter tract that links the CN III nucleus on one side to the CN VI nucleus on the other for conjugate horizontal gaze, and also carries vertical eye movement and vestibulo-ocular reflex signals ("a highway with exits for 8, 3, 4, and 6"). He recaps the full differential this pathway implies: orbital restriction (thyroid eye disease, orbital mass), the muscles themselves, the neuromuscular junction (myasthenia), the nerves (III/IV/VI), their brainstem nuclei, or their interconnections (MLF); a **PPRF** lesion causes a *conjugate* gaze palsy (supranuclear), not diplopia from misalignment. Sebastian adds that, given the fever, he's specifically worried about emergent structural causes like preseptal/orbital cellulitis and an infectious process affecting the cranial nerve nuclei themselves — a skull-base infection.

Berkowitz folds in the fever with the group: infection could occur anywhere along this pathway (orbital cellulitis, cavernous sinus syndrome), but there's also a less anatomically precise possibility worth remembering — a sixth nerve palsy as a **false localizing sign** of elevated intracranial pressure, since the abducens nerve's long, tortuous intracranial course over the clivus and through the cavernous sinus makes it especially vulnerable to pressure effects, opening the possibility of meningitis or abscess presenting this way. He raises **rhombencephalitis** (brainstem infection, from the embryologic *rhombencephalon*) as the classic fever-plus-brainstem-signs entity, and asks for the classic pathogen; Valeria answers **Listeria**, adding enteroviruses, herpesviruses, and (from a recent case) Mycoplasma as other organisms with brainstem tropism.

Asked what history he'd want as the receiving/triaging clinician, Sebastian prioritizes assessing level of consciousness and how emergent the presentation is, clarifying whether the "diplopia" is really binocular double vision versus oscillopsia or generalized blurring, establishing the tempo, and checking for other cranial nerve findings, ocular or facial abnormalities, mental status changes, and any long-tract (limb) findings — stressing that any neurologic complaint with fever is a red flag that shouldn't be sat on. Berkowitz agrees and adds useful bedside questions: is the diplopia worse looking in a particular direction, near or far; watching eye movements during history-taking; and, crucially, tempo — quoting his mentor Marty Samuels that **diplopia is always acute**, since ocular fusion holds an image together right up until, suddenly, it doesn't, even if the underlying lesion has been slowly evolving. He also asks about fatigability (suggesting myasthenia, which classically involves the eyes at presentation but shouldn't cause fever — unlike, he wonders aloud, botulism), other cranial nerve involvement (facial numbness suggesting the cavernous sinus; facial weakness suggesting a more skull-base/meningeal process), long-tract signs (weak or numb limbs, which would argue for or against a purely brainstem localization), and immune status.

Yusuf presents the HPI: a 36-year-old woman with no significant past history presents with one week of double vision, fever, lethargy, and unsteadiness, plus evolving right facial weakness and numbness. The diplopia began suddenly — she woke up one morning already seeing double — before the fever developed; her GP recognized this as a potential emergency and referred her in. She is HIV-negative (tested), with no significant smoking, alcohol, or toxin exposure (she works as an administrator), no bulbar, bowel, or bladder complaints, and no limb weakness at that point, though she reports unsteadiness, especially standing and walking. On arrival she is tachycardic (125), blood pressure 120/75, temperature 38.7°C, disoriented to place and time (though aware of herself) and profusely diaphoretic; the rest of the systemic exam is normal, and there is no meningismus. Cranial nerve exam: ptosis of the **left** eye with a down-and-out, but normally sized and reactive, pupil (a pupil-sparing left third nerve palsy); partial restriction of the **right** eye on lateral gaze (a partial right sixth nerve palsy); numbness to light touch over the whole right face; right facial weakness in an **upper-motor-neuron pattern** (forehead relatively spared); and direction-changing, gaze-evoked nystagmus, worse looking right. Limb strength, tone, reflexes, and sensation are all normal, but there is right-sided dysmetria on finger-nose and heel-shin testing, and an ataxic gait with a tendency to fall to the right.

Berkowitz asks Valeria to name the deficits in neurologic terms: a pupil-sparing left third nerve palsy, a partial right sixth nerve palsy, right-sided (light-touch only) fifth nerve involvement, an upper-motor-neuron-pattern right seventh nerve palsy, and cerebellar signs (dysmetria, ataxic gait) on the right — a confusing mix of left- and right-sided findings that Valeria tentatively localizes to the brainstem, favoring rhombencephalitis given the pattern of multiple cranial nerve nuclei being picked off. Sebastian, hearing the dysmetria and ataxic gait, moves away from a pure cavernous sinus process toward the vestibular/eighth-nerve territory and long tracts, similarly localizing to the right pons/medulla — though he notes the pupil-sparing left third nerve pattern is harder to fit, wondering if there's a process near the fourth ventricle spilling over to affect both sides.

Berkowitz pushes back gently on a pure brainstem localization. He first teaches that **pupil sparing in a third nerve palsy** classically reflects the parasympathetic pupillomotor fibers' peripheral position within the nerve (favoring an ischemic/microvascular cause acting on the nerve's core over a compressive cause acting from outside) — but flags this "rule" as imperfect, since compressive lesions (e.g., a basilar tip aneurysm pressing from below) can still spare those fibers depending on exactly where they act along the nerve's course. Taking the exam piece by piece: a pupil-sparing third nerve palsy argues somewhat against a brainstem process (where all the relevant fibers are tightly packed and would be harder to spare selectively); the reported upper-motor-neuron-pattern facial weakness is hard to reconcile with a lesion low enough to also hit the sixth nerve nucleus, since six and seven sit at the same brainstem level — if the seventh nerve involvement is truly upper-motor-neuron, the lesion should be above that level, which conflicts with a lesion also affecting the ipsilateral sixth nerve nucleus. He notes the gaze-evoked nystagmus may simply be gaze-paretic nystagmus (from an already-misaligned, overloaded gaze system) rather than a marker of true cerebellar pathology. Taken together — left three, right six, right five, an apparent right upper-motor-neuron seven, and right-sided cerebellar signs, in a patient who is confused but still awake and without long-tract limb findings — he finds it hard to draw one contiguous "zigzagging" brainstem lesion that hits all these structures while sparing arousal and the long tracts, though he doesn't rule it out.

He steps back to teach the general schema for **multiple cranial neuropathies**: either the lesion is inside the brainstem (hitting multiple nuclei or fascicles — the segment of nerve between its nucleus and its exit), or it's outside the brainstem — in the subarachnoid space (a meningeal process), the skull base (including, but not limited to, the cavernous sinus), or the "target organ" region (orbit, face, neck, for the lower cranial nerves). He adds a category of **"fakeouts"** that mimic multiple cranial neuropathies without truly localizing to one place: Guillain-Barré/Miller Fisher syndrome (since cranial nerves are peripheral nerves too), myasthenia gravis (ocular plus facial weakness), and orbital myopathies like oculopharyngeal muscular dystrophy. Asked whether the deficits arose all at once or evolved sequentially, Yusuf clarifies (from the spouse's history) that they marched over the week — diplopia first, then facial weakness, then fever — which, combined with the fever, keeps an infectious/inflammatory process in play and argues against a single instantaneous vascular event.

Working through the differential with the fever removed and then restored, the group raises (and largely sets aside) several entities: a rare paraneoplastic rhombencephalitis with hearing loss (anti-Kelch-like-protein-11 antibody, associated with testicular cancer — probably too fast a tempo here); leptomeningeal carcinomatosis (probably also too fast, and doesn't explain the ataxia as well); Miller Fisher syndrome and its variant Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis (ophthalmoplegia-ataxia, sometimes with altered mental status in Bickerstaff, but classically without fever unless a secondary process like aspiration is superimposed); botulism (inconsistently associated with pupillary involvement, and not typically febrile); Wernicke's encephalopathy (ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, and altered mentation, but no fever and no reported alcohol history here); and, zooming out further, meningitis/encephalitis broadly — bacterial felt too slow at one week (and the patient would likely be sicker), viral felt like a stretch to explain this many cranial nerves, and without HIV or known immunocompromise, fungal infection seems unlikely, though **tuberculosis** (endemic in South Africa) is kept firmly on the list for its capacity to indolently affect the meninges and cranial nerves via compression or vasculitis. Valeria adds a fungal possibility with brainstem tropism (aspergillosis) and raises **autoimmune/inflammatory disease**, including asking about dry eyes, dry mouth, or other systemic ROS features that are easy to miss. Berkowitz broadens the autoimmune branch: a flare of **neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder** (NMOSD) — no longer just "myelitis plus optica," but a spectrum including area postrema syndrome, an acute cerebral syndrome, an **acute brainstem syndrome** (which could fit this picture), and a diencephalic syndrome — and, prompted by Valeria's mention of "ulcers," **Behçet's disease**, which has a particular tropism for the brainstem when it affects the nervous system, alongside oral or genital ulcers (with fever plausible during an acute inflammatory flare).

Asked to name one test they'd order, Sebastian and Valeria both choose an MRI brain with and without contrast. Asked to predict the result, Sebastian reasons out loud that a normal MRI would be very informative (excluding much of the differential), but predicts it will **not** be normal, specifically because of the fever. Berkowitz agrees a normal scan is possible (it would fit a "fakeout" like Miller Fisher or botulism) but suspects either a brainstem lesion or leptomeningeal enhancement, or both, though he isn't certain a brainstem process, if present, will look the way he expects. Valeria adds that some autoimmune/paraneoplastic cases she's seen didn't show up on initial MRI at all — a reminder that a normal scan doesn't fully exclude an inflammatory process.

Yusuf describes the resource-limited setting: with only one MRI scanner shared across a large public hospital system, imaging is used judiciously and following protocols. Initial bloodwork shows a normal renal panel, elevated white cell count, CRP, and ESR. An initial CT brain with contrast is normal. LP shows a reactive result: 5 polymorphs, 253 lymphocytes, protein 1.27, glucose 4.2, negative cryptococcal antigen (CLAT), negative TB GeneXpert and (eventually) negative TB culture, and unremarkable microscopy/culture/sensitivity. Blood cultures are negative. Given the overall picture, the team treats empirically for possible **Listeria rhombencephalitis** despite the negative blood culture (acknowledging blood cultures aren't fully sensitive for Listeria), with three weeks of IV antibiotics; the patient improves and is discharged, remaining stable at an initial follow-up visit some weeks later.

Two months later, she returns markedly unwell again — tachycardic, febrile, with a similar constellation of findings, but now with a new **right hemiparesis**. She is readmitted; a repeat LP again shows a reactive, lymphocyte-predominant, high-protein picture similar to the first. Crucially, with more time and access to family members, the team obtains additional history and performs a more complete general examination: they find acneiform pustular skin lesions on the head and old genital scarring consistent with prior genital ulcers, and a sibling reports the patient has had recurrent oral ulcers since her teenage years — about four episodes in the preceding year alone. Berkowitz pauses the discussion here to reflect on resource-limited imaging access as a broader teaching point (a contrast CT, timed to catch the venous phase, can substitute reasonably for an MRI in many settings, including for venous sinus thrombosis or enhancement, though with less spatial resolution) and on the diagnostic value of an empiric treatment trial even without confirming a diagnosis outright.

An MRI brain (obtained after the second presentation, one week into IV corticosteroids) shows multifocal FLAIR-hyperintense lesions in the diencephalon and basal ganglia, additional lesions in the brainstem, and — on coronal imaging at the mesodiencephalic junction — the classic **"cascade"** or **"waterfall" sign**: hyperintensity tracking from the internal capsule down through the midbrain, along the corticospinal tract, into the brainstem. Yusuf notes this sign is described in the literature as fairly specific for neuro-Behçet's disease, but flags that his own group's case series (in a South African population with a high HIV burden) has found the same sign in other conditions — PML, NMO, and neurosarcoidosis — challenging that specificity. A **pathergy test** (a needle-prick skin test assessing for an exaggerated local inflammatory/autoimmune hypersensitivity response) returns positive after two days; an autoimmune panel is otherwise negative. With IV corticosteroids, the patient improves markedly.

Yusuf closes with several teaching points: Behçet's syndrome is uncommon and its systemic features aren't always prominent at first presentation, underscoring that history-taking is a dynamic, iterative process — sometimes requiring multiple family members and multiple encounters to assemble a complete picture — and that thorough general (not just neurologic) examination matters. He notes neuro-Behçet's disease occurs in about 10% of Behçet's patients, most often as the **parenchymal** (brainstem-predominant) phenotype, with a **non-parenchymal** phenotype presenting via venous sinus thrombosis and its downstream intracranial pressure effects. He also reflects explicitly on the "problem of induction" (attributed to David Hume): the team's initial reasoning — fever and tachycardia usually mean infection, the patient improved on antibiotics, therefore it was infection — turned out, in hindsight, to likely be a coincidence, since the patient may simply have been entering a spontaneous remission at the time antibiotics were started, and the relapse two months later unmasked the true, chronic-relapsing autoimmune process. Berkowitz adds that steroids were notably *not* given during the first, presumed-infectious admission — only later, once the Behçet's diagnosis was suspected. He also highlights the case's disruption of the classic "Silk Road" teaching about Behçet's geography: this patient had no Near/Middle Eastern or East Asian ancestry, and Yusuf notes his department diagnosed a second, concurrent patient with a similar presentation (oral and genital ulcers, long-tract signs, and the same cascade sign) around the same time — only the fourth and fifth cases the department has seen in its roughly 40-year history. Valeria mentions she'd recently discussed a separate neuro-Behçet's case the week before the conference, which primed her to raise ulcers in the review of systems for this case. The episode closes with a brief aside on pronunciation (the Turkish "Behçet" is pronounced roughly "beh-shets") and an invitation to submit the case and the waterfall-sign observations for publication.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Anchoring Bias|The "problem of induction."]]** The team's initial reasoning — fever and tachycardia usually indicate infection, the patient improved after antibiotics, therefore the infection was the cause — is explicitly named (via David Hume) as a causal-inference trap: correlation (improvement after treatment) was read as proof of causation, when the true explanation was a coincidental spontaneous remission of a relapsing autoimmune disease.
- **[[Availability Heuristic|Recent case exposure priming a diagnosis.]]** Valeria's discussion of "ulcers" in the review of systems — the thread that ultimately leads to Behçet's — is explicitly traced to a neuro-Behçet's case she had discussed the week prior, illustrating how recent exposure to a rare diagnosis raises its salience in a new, otherwise unrelated case.
- **[[Illness Script|Testing a single-lesion script against a discordant exam.]]** Berkowitz explicitly tries to fit the full exam (left third, right sixth, right fifth, right "UMN" seventh, right cerebellar signs) into one contiguous brainstem lesion, and uses the mismatches (pupil sparing, the VI/VII level discordance) as data against that script rather than forcing the pattern to fit.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Predicting before imaging.]]** Sebastian explicitly predicts the MRI will be abnormal, reasoning from the presence of fever, before the scan is shown — using the prediction itself as a test of the working hypothesis.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Revising the story on relapse rather than defending it.]]** Rather than treating the initial "successful" antibiotic course as closed, the team explicitly revisits and ultimately abandons their causal story once the patient relapses, modeling comfort with having been wrong about the mechanism of an apparent treatment response.

## Teaching Discussion

- Localize diplopia along the structural pathway (orbit/muscles → NMJ → CN III/IV/VI → cavernous sinus → brainstem nuclei/MLF/PPRF), and remember the MLF links the CN III and CN VI nuclei bilaterally for conjugate gaze while the PPRF governs horizontal conjugate gaze itself (its failure causes a gaze palsy, not diplopia from misalignment).
- Remember "diplopia is always acute" — there is always a discrete moment fusion breaks, even when the underlying lesion has evolved gradually — and know that an isolated or bilateral sixth nerve palsy can be a false localizing sign of elevated intracranial pressure given the nerve's long intracranial course.
- Use a short mental checklist for multiple cranial neuropathies: inside the brainstem (nuclei/fascicles) vs. outside it (subarachnoid/meningeal, skull base including the cavernous sinus, or target organ), plus neuromuscular "fakeouts" (Miller Fisher/Bickerstaff, myasthenia, orbital myopathies) that can mimic the pattern without one localizing lesion.
- Treat "pupil-sparing third nerve palsy implies ischemic, not compressive" as a useful heuristic, not an absolute rule — the parasympathetic fibers' peripheral position in the nerve makes sparing more likely with an ischemic cause, but some compressive lesions can still spare them depending on where along the nerve's course they act.
- Predict the imaging result before viewing it, and use the prediction as an explicit test of the working differential.
- Recognize the neuro-Behçet's "cascade"/"waterfall" sign (FLAIR/T2 hyperintensity from the internal capsule into the midbrain along the corticospinal tract) as a helpful but not fully specific finding — also reported in PML, NMO, and neurosarcoidosis.
- Be wary of the "problem of induction": an apparent response to empiric antibiotics does not prove an infectious cause, especially in a relapsing-remitting disease — revisit the causal story explicitly if the patient later relapses.
- Don't let "classic" geographic or demographic teaching (e.g., Behçet's and the Silk Road) exclude a diagnosis in an atypical population; ask about oral and genital ulcers as part of a broad multi-system review of systems when facing an undifferentiated inflammatory brainstem process.
- Know that neuro-Behçet's disease occurs in roughly 10% of Behçet's patients, most commonly as a parenchymal (brainstem-predominant) syndrome, with venous sinus thrombosis/intracranial hypertension as the non-parenchymal phenotype — and that a positive pathergy test can support the diagnosis at the bedside.
