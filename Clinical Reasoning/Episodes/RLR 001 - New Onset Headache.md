## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 32-year-old man with no prior headache history and a remote splenectomy presents with two days of severe headache, photophobia, and nausea. The clinical frame — asplenia plus headache/photophobia — pulls hard toward [[Meningitis]], but a fully reassuring exam and labs (afebrile, no leukocytosis, no meningismus) progressively deflate that hypothesis. The true diagnosis, uncovered only when a resident actually asked the patient about his history, was a [[Caffeine Withdrawal Headache]] after an abrupt cold-turkey stop from six cups of coffee a day. The case is really a case study in [[Anchoring Bias|anchoring]] and [[Premature Closure|premature closure]]: the discussant never abandoned migraine as the leading diagnosis, but the bedside clinician became fixated on ruling out meningitis and stopped listening to the patient.

## Pearls
- **Asplenia doesn't override a reassuring exam.** A history of splenectomy raises the *prior* for encapsulated-organism meningitis, but a normal exam, no fever, and no leukocytosis substantially lower the *posterior* — the framing shouldn't be applied so rigidly that normal data gets ignored.
- **Meningitis without fever is real and shouldn't be dismissed reflexively** — but its probability still has to be updated against the actual exam and labs in front of you, not just the frame that triggered your concern.
- **Nausea with headache can signal raised intracranial pressure**, not just be a bystander symptom — ask about positional worsening (supine, Valsalva) and get a fundoscopic exam when possible.
- **The POUND mnemonic** (Pulsatile, duration 4–72 hOurs, Unilateral, Nausea, Disabling) is a fast way to pattern-match to migraine, but a first-ever severe headache in someone with no migraine history is itself a red flag worth pausing on.
- **When data doesn't fit the working hypothesis, go back and take a better history** — the moment labs came back reassuring was the moment to return to the patient's story, not to keep pushing toward LP or imaging.
- **A learner "catching" something you missed doesn't mean their reasoning was more correct** — they may have found the right answer while under-weighting a genuinely dangerous possibility you were right to consider.

## Problem Representation
A 32-year-old man with a history of splenectomy (for hemangioma), depression, and anxiety presents with an acute, severe, first-ever headache of two days' duration accompanied by photophobia, nausea, and marked fatigue, in the setting of normal vital signs, a normal neurologic and ophthalmologic exam, and normal basic labs.

## Case Narrative
The case opens as a chief-concern exercise: a 32-year-old man with no prior headache history presents with an excruciating headache that began two days earlier, accompanied by photophobia so severe he can't tolerate light. Before any further data, the discussant reaches first for a [[30,000-Foot View of Headache|three-bucket framework for headache]] (credited to Aaron Berkowitz): external head/neck structures (eyes, sinuses, oral cavity), systemic disease processes for which headache is a bystander, and intrinsic intracranial structures (parenchyma, vessels, meninges). Given the photophobia and lack of any influenza-like symptoms, the systemic bucket looks unlikely up front, and attention shifts toward the eyes and, pending exam, the intracranial space.

Once vitals return normal and afebrile, and the chart reveals a splenectomy three years prior (for a splenic hemangioma) with unknown vaccination status, the framing sharpens: an asplenic patient is at increased risk for infection with encapsulated organisms (S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae), which raises concern for bacterial meningitis — notably, meningitis can occur without fever. The nausea adds another thread: it could be a simple bystander symptom, or a sign of elevated intracranial pressure, raising consideration of idiopathic intracranial hypertension. At this stage the plan is a low threshold for lumbar puncture, prioritized after a CT scan given the severity and the need to rule out a herniation risk before proceeding.

The exam and labs return essentially normal: no scleral injection, normal pupils, a completely normal neuro exam, no meningismus, and normal CBC, renal, and liver panels. With this data, the probability of meningitis drops substantially — no fever, no leukocytosis, no neck stiffness. Returning to the three-bucket framework, external and systemic causes are now unlikely, and within the intrinsic bucket the discussion moves to primary vs. secondary headache syndromes. The presentation — first severe headache, no history — matches the POUND criteria for migraine almost perfectly, but the total absence of any migraine history is itself flagged as a reason for caution, alongside the [[SNOOP Mnemonic|SNOOP red-flag mnemonic]] for secondary headache.

The bedside clinician, meanwhile, describes feeling caught between two extremes — reassuring data versus a framing built around asplenia and meningitis — and ultimately proceeds toward LP on a "just do it and get it over with" instinct. Before that happens, a resident picks up the case fresh, actually asks the patient about his recent history, and discovers he had abruptly stopped drinking six cups of coffee a day, cold turkey, two days prior — done specifically to reduce anxiety. The patient is given a migraine cocktail; two hours later he is symptom-free and walks out. A caffeine taper is arranged, and on follow-up the patient has had no further headaches.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates
| Data point | Effect on meningitis probability | Effect on migraine probability |
|---|---|---|
| Severe first-ever headache + photophobia | Raises concern broadly; triggers three-bucket framework | Considered but deprioritized due to lack of history |
| History of splenectomy, uncertain vaccination status | Raises prior meaningfully (asplenia → encapsulated organism risk) | No change |
| Afebrile, normal vitals at triage | Modest decrease | No change |
| Normal neuro exam, no meningismus, normal eye exam | Substantial decrease | Modest increase |
| Normal CBC, renal, liver panels (no leukocytosis) | Substantial decrease | Modest increase |
| Nausea present | Nonspecific; also raises concern for elevated ICP | Consistent with POUND criteria |
| History obtained: abrupt caffeine cessation | Effectively ruled out as driver of presentation | Superseded by [[Caffeine Withdrawal Headache]] as unifying diagnosis |
| Rapid, complete response to migraine cocktail | N/A | Supports a migraine-mechanism headache (withdrawal-triggered) |

### Illness Scripts and Bias
The asplenia history functioned as a strong anchor: once "splenectomy + headache + photophobia" was verbalized, the bedside clinician described becoming fixated on disproving meningitis, to the point of dominating the patient encounter and not leaving room for the patient to volunteer his own story. This is a clean example of [[Anchoring Bias]] compounding into [[Premature Closure]] — the reassuring exam and labs should have prompted a return to open-ended questioning, but instead they prompted movement toward more testing (CT, then LP) within the same anchored frame.

The resident who obtained the caffeine history is framed as having made the opposite error in the other direction: without ever registering the splenectomy or seriously entertaining meningitis, they moved straight to a benign explanation. The discussants note that getting the right diagnosis doesn't validate the reasoning process that produced it, just as getting the wrong diagnosis doesn't necessarily reflect bad reasoning — asplenia-driven concern for meningitis was a reasonable, arguably correct, thing to prioritize even though it wasn't the final answer. This tension — whether and how to share that observation with a resident who'd just made a good catch — is left deliberately unresolved as a real-world management-of-learners problem rather than a clean teaching point.

## Teaching Discussion
- The three-bucket (extrinsic/systemic/intrinsic) framework for headache is presented as a way to avoid tunnel vision toward intracranial causes, using the concrete example of missed acute angle-closure glaucoma in a patient who developed the "worst headache of her life."
- Meningitis can present without fever, and a history of splenectomy independently raises the pretest probability of infection with encapsulated organisms — this justifies a lower threshold for LP even in the absence of fever, though it doesn't override a fully reassuring exam and labs.
- Nausea accompanying headache should prompt consideration of elevated intracranial pressure (asking about positional worsening, Valsalva, and attempting fundoscopy for papilledema), not just being dismissed as a nonspecific bystander symptom.
- The POUND mnemonic is offered as a fast pattern-match for migraine, paired with the SNOOP mnemonic as the counterweight for red flags pushing toward secondary headache — the discussants explicitly hold both frameworks in tension rather than letting one resolve the case prematurely.
- The turning point identified in retrospect is the moment normal labs returned: that was the cue to step back and re-engage the patient with open-ended questions, rather than to keep escalating toward imaging and LP within the original anchored frame.
- Correct diagnoses and correct reasoning are not the same thing — a learner can reach the right answer while under-weighting a legitimately dangerous possibility, and an experienced clinician can reach the wrong initial frame for entirely defensible reasons.
