## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 56-year-old man with a liver transplant and a kidney transplant on chronic immunosuppression develops progressive, isolated thrombocytopenia found incidentally during treatment for a dental abscess. An unremarkable hemolysis and infection workup, combined with a markedly elevated D-dimer and normal fibrinogen, points toward a consumptive process superimposed on presumed immune thrombocytopenia — but the platelets prove refractory to steroids, IVIG, and transfusion. Refractoriness in an immunologically active transplant patient prompts a search for a secondary trigger; imaging reveals a multifocal mass-like process that ultimately proves to be metastatic small cell lung cancer, and the thrombocytopenia resolves with chemotherapy — a case of paraneoplastic (secondary) immune thrombocytopenia.

## Pearls

- A diagnosis of primary immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) should remain provisional until it responds to first-line treatment; true refractoriness to steroids, IVIG, and platelet transfusion is itself a diagnostic clue that should trigger a search for a secondary cause, not simply an indication to escalate ITP-directed therapy.
- In solid-organ transplant recipients on chronic immunosuppression, new cytopenias — especially ones that don't behave as expected — should prompt consideration of post-transplant lymphoproliferative disease (PTLD) even when a screening EBV PCR is negative, since EBV-negative PTLD exists and a single negative PCR does not exclude it.
- When building a differential from an unexpected finding, avoid anchoring on the first plausible unifying diagnosis; explicitly hold multiple live hypotheses (here, PTLD and a true solid malignancy) until the data forces a choice, rather than fitting every new finding into the first framework that came to mind.
- An elevated D-dimer with a normal fibrinogen suggests a more localized or lower-grade consumptive/fibrinolytic process than the combination of an elevated D-dimer with a low fibrinogen, which points toward more severe, systemic DIC.
- Cancer can cause thrombocytopenia through several distinct mechanisms — marrow infiltration, DIC, and immune-mediated (paraneoplastic) platelet destruction — and paraneoplastic ITP should be considered in a patient with a malignancy-compatible history whose "primary" ITP is unexpectedly refractory to standard treatment.
- Small cell lung cancer, though classically associated with paraneoplastic neurologic and endocrine syndromes, can also drive paraneoplastic immune thrombocytopenia — a reminder that a known paraneoplastic tumor can present through a mechanism outside its most "famous" associations.

## Problem Representation

A 56-year-old man with a remote liver transplant (for lisinopril-induced liver injury) and a more recent kidney transplant (for calcineurin-inhibitor nephrotoxicity), maintained on tacrolimus, mycophenolate, and prednisone, develops progressive isolated thrombocytopenia over roughly eight weeks, discovered incidentally during treatment of a dental abscess; an unremarkable hemolysis and infectious workup, an elevated D-dimer with normal fibrinogen, and refractoriness to empiric ITP treatment (steroids, IVIG, platelet transfusion) prompt a search for a secondary cause, ultimately revealing metastatic small cell lung cancer as the driver of a paraneoplastic immune thrombocytopenia.

## Case Narrative

A 56-year-old man is found to have a low platelet count on labs drawn incidentally during treatment for a dental abscess with clindamycin. Review of his recent trend shows a steady decline: 146,000, then 121,000, then 19,000, then 9,000 over about eight weeks. His history includes a liver transplant twelve years earlier for lisinopril-induced liver injury and a kidney transplant two years earlier for calcineurin-inhibitor nephrotoxicity; he is maintained on tacrolimus, mycophenolate, and prednisone, and carries a history of chronic, stable BK viruria.

On exam he has a large abdominal wall ecchymosis (roughly 24 by 10 centimeters), crackles at the left lung base, and a blood pressure of 154/99. The discussant frames the core problem as an isolated, progressive thrombocytopenia in an immunosuppressed transplant recipient, and begins by separating the major mechanistic categories of thrombocytopenia: decreased production (marrow suppression or infiltration), increased destruction (immune-mediated, as in ITP, or non-immune, as in disseminated intravascular coagulation or thrombotic microangiopathy), and sequestration (typically splenic). The absence of other cytopenias argues somewhat against a global marrow process, sharpening interest in a destructive or consumptive mechanism.

Initial workup: LDH is elevated at 689; haptoglobin is elevated at 239, arguing against an ongoing hemolytic process (which would be expected to lower haptoglobin); the peripheral smear shows no schistocytes and no blasts; ADAMTS13 activity is normal, arguing against thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura. D-dimer is markedly elevated at 7,195 with a normal fibrinogen — a pattern the discussant reads as more consistent with a lower-grade or localized consumptive/fibrinolytic process than with full-blown DIC, which would typically also depress the fibrinogen. An infectious workup — SARS-CoV-2, HIV, EBV, parvovirus B19, hepatitis B and C — returns entirely negative, and his chronic BK viruria is unchanged from baseline. Ultrasound of both transplanted organs shows no acute abnormality, and a chest X-ray shows an opacity at the left lung base, initially read as consistent with atelectasis or infection given his crackles on exam.

With infection, TTP, and an obvious hepatic or renal transplant complication all argued against, and no clear alternative destructive process identified, the working diagnosis becomes immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) — a diagnosis of exclusion that the discussant is explicit is being made provisionally, with the expectation that a genuine primary ITP should respond to standard treatment. He is treated with high-dose dexamethasone (40 mg daily for four days), two days of IVIG, and platelet transfusion for his severe thrombocytopenia and bleeding risk.

The platelets do not sustainably respond: transfusion produces only a transient bump before falling back down, and the counts remain refractory despite steroids and IVIG. The discussant treats this refractoriness itself as the pivotal new data point — a genuine primary ITP is expected to respond to this regimen, so failure to respond in an "immunologically busy" transplant patient reframes the differential toward a secondary cause of immune-mediated platelet destruction. Chief among these secondary considerations, given his transplant and immunosuppression history, is post-transplant lymphoproliferative disease (PTLD), which the discussant notes can itself trigger secondary ITP and can also present as an extranodal, mass-forming process that mimics a solid tumor with metastases. An EBV PCR is sent and returns negative — a finding the discussant is careful not to treat as ruling out PTLD, since EBV-negative PTLD is a recognized entity, particularly later after transplantation. At this point Rabih is explicit that he is not anchoring on PTLD alone and is deliberately keeping a true, unrelated solid malignancy on the differential as well, a stance Reza highlights approvingly as "betting across multiple possible diagnoses" rather than committing early to a single unifying story.

Cross-sectional imaging is obtained. CT, followed by PET, reveals mediastinal and left hilar lymphadenopathy, a mass-like consolidation in the left lower lobe (corresponding to the opacity seen on the earlier chest X-ray), an exophytic lesion on the native right kidney, and two soft tissue masses in the left iliac wing. The distribution — nodal plus multiple extranodal sites — is read as most consistent with PTLD, though the discussants continue to hold a primary solid tumor with metastatic spread as a live alternative given how atypical a distribution this would also be for many PTLD presentations.

A bone marrow biopsy is obtained from the left iliac crest, targeting one of the iliac masses; it shows a hypercellular marrow infiltrated by a malignant neoplasm expressing CD56, a marker that favors a neuroendocrine process over a typical PTLD (a B- or T-cell lymphoproliferation). This result reorients the differential away from PTLD and toward a primary neuroendocrine malignancy. Bronchoscopy with biopsy of a subcarinal lymph node is performed to sample the pulmonary and mediastinal disease directly, and returns a diagnosis neither discussant had specifically raised: metastatic small cell lung cancer, with the lung as the primary site and the renal, iliac, and marrow lesions all representing metastatic spread.

The patient is started on chemotherapy for his small cell lung cancer, and his platelet count normalizes over the following weeks — a response that had not occurred with steroids, IVIG, or platelet transfusion — confirming that his thrombocytopenia was a paraneoplastic phenomenon driven by the underlying malignancy rather than a primary autoimmune process. In discussion, the presenting discussant (drawing on a case from his time at Northwestern in Chicago, co-published with a resident and a hematologist, Anin Patel) reviews two proposed mechanisms for paraneoplastic ITP in this setting: tumor-associated antigens that cross-react with platelet surface antigens, triggering an antiplatelet autoantibody response (molecular mimicry), or a tumor-driven expansion of antibodies that target platelet receptors or components of the thrombopoietin axis, referencing elevated antibodies against platelet glycoprotein IIb/IIIa reported in the source publication. Closing the case, the discussants note the patient's modest smoking history (roughly two cigars daily for seven years) and raise, as an open and explicitly unresolved question for further reading, whether chronic immunosuppression lowers the threshold at which even limited smoking exposure can drive a smoking-associated malignancy such as small cell lung cancer.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

|---|---|
| Progressive isolated thrombocytopenia over 8 weeks in a chronically immunosuppressed transplant recipient | Favors an immune-mediated or consumptive destructive process over a global marrow process, given the absence of other cytopenias |
| Elevated haptoglobin, no schistocytes on smear, normal ADAMTS13 | Argues against hemolysis and against TTP/thrombotic microangiopathy as the destructive mechanism |
| D-dimer markedly elevated (7,195) with normal fibrinogen | Suggests a lower-grade or localized consumptive/fibrinolytic process rather than fulminant, fibrinogen-depleting DIC |
| Negative infectious workup (SARS-CoV-2, HIV, EBV, parvovirus B19, hepatitis B/C); stable chronic BK viruria; normal transplant organ ultrasounds | Down-weights infection and an acute transplant-organ complication as the driver; supports a provisional diagnosis of primary ITP |
| Refractory to high-dose steroids, IVIG, and platelet transfusion | Pivotal negative finding — argues against primary ITP and reframes the search toward a secondary cause of immune platelet destruction |
| EBV PCR negative | Lowers but does not eliminate concern for PTLD, since EBV-negative PTLD is a recognized entity |
| CT/PET: mediastinal/hilar lymphadenopathy, LLL mass, exophytic renal lesion, iliac masses | Favors a multifocal process — initially read as most consistent with PTLD, with a primary solid malignancy kept explicitly open |

| Platelet count normalizes with chemotherapy alone, after failing steroids/IVIG/transfusion | Confirms the thrombocytopenia was paraneoplastic, mechanistically dependent on the tumor rather than a primary autoimmune process |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The case is built around treatment-as-diagnostic-test reasoning: the discussants treat the platelets' failure to respond to standard ITP therapy not as a treatment failure to be pushed through with more aggressive ITP-directed therapy, but as evidence that the working diagnosis itself was incomplete — a "primary" label should be provisional until it is confirmed by an expected treatment response.

Rabih's explicit statement that he is deliberately keeping more than one diagnosis in play (PTLD and a primary solid malignancy) rather than anchoring on the first plausible explanation is highlighted by Reza as a model of diagnostic humility — resisting premature closure on a single hypothesis even when one candidate (PTLD) fits the transplant/immunosuppression context especially well.

Both discussants are candid that neither of them raised small cell lung cancer specifically before the bronchoscopy result returned, despite it technically falling within the "solid malignancy" bucket they had already kept open — an honest acknowledgment that holding a category open in principle does not guarantee that the correct specific diagnosis within that category will be generated in real time.

## Teaching Discussion

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is fundamentally a diagnosis of exclusion, and its label should remain provisional until treatment response confirms it: primary ITP is expected to respond to steroids, IVIG, or platelet transfusion, and genuine refractoriness to all three should prompt a search for a secondary driver rather than escalation of ITP-directed therapy alone.

In solid-organ transplant recipients, new or refractory cytopenias should raise concern for post-transplant lymphoproliferative disease (PTLD), which can present as an extranodal, mass-forming process mimicking a solid tumor with metastases, and can itself trigger secondary immune-mediated cytopenias; a negative EBV PCR lowers but does not eliminate this concern, since EBV-negative PTLD is a recognized entity.

An elevated D-dimer should be interpreted alongside fibrinogen: a markedly elevated D-dimer with a preserved fibrinogen suggests a more localized or lower-grade consumptive or fibrinolytic process, while a concurrently depressed fibrinogen points toward more severe, systemic disseminated intravascular coagulation.

Malignancy can cause thrombocytopenia through several distinct mechanisms that should be considered separately: direct marrow infiltration, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and paraneoplastic immune-mediated platelet destruction — the last of which can occur via tumor-antigen molecular mimicry or tumor-driven expansion of antibodies against platelet receptors or the thrombopoietin axis.

Small cell lung cancer is classically taught alongside its paraneoplastic neurologic (e.g., Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome) and endocrine (e.g., SIADH, ectopic ACTH) associations, but the differential of paraneoplastic phenomena it can drive is broader than these signature examples, and should include hematologic manifestations such as immune thrombocytopenia.

When an unexpected finding (like refractory thrombocytopenia in a transplant recipient) opens a broad differential, deliberately holding multiple plausible hypotheses in parallel — rather than anchoring on the one that best fits the most salient part of the history — helps avoid premature closure, even though it does not guarantee that the eventual correct diagnosis will be specifically anticipated in advance.
