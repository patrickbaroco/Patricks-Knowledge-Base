## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 23-year-old woman presents with two, then three, days of severe, prominently pleuritic right upper quadrant pain. A careful, stepwise workup — EKG, troponin, chest X-ray, D-dimer, LFTs, and ultimately two full-body CT scans with contrast — methodically and correctly excludes every dangerous diagnosis on the list: pericarditis, pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, and any visible intra-abdominal or pelvic pathology. She is discharged twice without a unifying diagnosis. The answer surfaces a week later, on a routine chart follow-up: a positive chlamydia test from her primary care doctor, confirming Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome (chlamydial perihepatitis) — a diagnosis Reza had named out loud, twice, during the original discussion, without recognizing he had already reasoned his way to it. The case is as much a teaching vehicle for diagnostic humility and the discipline of deliberate follow-up as it is for the disease itself.

## Pearls

- Right upper quadrant pain does not necessarily indicate hepatobiliary pathology — abdominal viscera are densely packed and prone to referred pain, and extra-abdominal structures (heart, lung) as well as pelvic organs (uterus, ovaries in a premenopausal woman) can all refer pain to the right upper quadrant.
- In any young patient presenting with abdominal pain, pregnancy testing and STI testing should be obtained as a matter of routine, regardless of how confidently the presentation seems to point elsewhere.
- Once pain is characterized as pleuritic (worse with breathing), it must be taken seriously and specifically prompts consideration of the "P's" of pleuritic pain — pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, pneumothorax, and pleuritis/pericarditis — since irritation of the pleura, pericardium, or diaphragm/phrenic nerve by an adjacent process can all produce this quality of pain.
- Tenderness to palpation carries localizing value: working through an anatomical approach from superficial to deep (skin/soft tissue, then muscle/bone, then the deeper viscera such as gallbladder, liver, and biliary tree) helps organize the differential for focal abdominal tenderness.
- Absence of a rash does not exclude herpes zoster — pain can precede the rash by days, and some cases never develop a visible rash at all.
- A chest X-ray is an asymmetric test: a positive finding (e.g., a consolidative pneumonia explaining pleuritic pain) is highly informative, but a negative chest X-ray does not meaningfully lower the probability of parenchymal, pleural, or vascular lung pathology — it simply is not a sensitive enough test to rely on for exclusion.
- Once pericarditis has been reasonably excluded (via clinical picture and EKG) in a patient with pleuritic chest/upper-abdominal pain and a negative chest X-ray, pulmonary embolism becomes the dominant remaining diagnosis that must be actively excluded, given the stakes of missing it.
- A white count in the low-to-mid teens (e.g., 13,000) in a patient without fever or other localizing signs of infection is often better explained as a nonspecific stress/pain-related leukocytosis than as clear evidence of an infectious or inflammatory process — it should raise suspicion without being treated as diagnostic on its own.
- Deciding whether to pursue advanced cross-sectional imaging (e.g., CT with contrast) in a young patient with a severe but undifferentiated presentation is a genuine shared decision involving trade-offs (radiation and cost versus the value of excluding sinister diagnoses like PE or subtle parenchymal/pleural pathology) rather than a decision with one universally correct answer.
- Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome (perihepatitis, most commonly from ascending chlamydial or gonococcal infection) classically presents with concomitant, radiologically and symptomatically evident pelvic inflammatory disease, which typically serves as the biggest diagnostic clue — but roughly 20% of patients present with hepatic-predominant disease and no pelvic symptoms, making the diagnosis substantially harder to reach in that subset.
- The pleuritic quality of Fitz-Hugh-Curtis pain arises from fibrotic adhesions forming between the inflamed liver capsule and the diaphragm — a microscopic, capsular-level process rather than parenchymal liver injury — which is why liver function tests and standard abdominal/pelvic imaging are frequently completely normal in this condition, even when the pain is severe.
- Deliberately maintaining a personal follow-up list for patients whose presentation was never fully explained at the point of discharge — and proactively checking their subsequent chart entries — is a concrete, learnable habit that can surface a delayed diagnosis and close the loop on genuine diagnostic uncertainty.
- It is easy, and generally unfair, to judge a missed diagnosis in retrospect from outside the clinical encounter — real-time decision-making is shaped by volume of competing patients, the discipline of prioritizing exclusion of emergent diagnoses first, and countless contextual pressures that a retrospective case discussion cannot fully replicate.
- A prior missed or delayed diagnosis, openly shared and reflected upon rather than concealed or allowed to become self-defining, is a mechanism for professional growth; treating a diagnostic miss as an isolated, correctable event — rather than evidence of a durable personal failing — supports sustainable clinical practice over a career.

## Problem Representation

A 23-year-old woman with two to three days of severe, prominently pleuritic right upper quadrant pain, refractory to NSAIDs and acetaminophen, with a mildly elevated white count and otherwise unremarkable labs and two negative contrast-enhanced CT scans (chest, then abdomen/pelvis), ultimately diagnosed a week later — via outpatient chlamydia testing and treatment by her primary care doctor — with Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome (chlamydial perihepatitis) presenting in its hepatic-predominant, pelvic-symptom-negative form.

## Case Narrative

Rabih presented a 23-year-old woman at the VA with two days of pain under her right rib margin, worse with deep breathing, without a clear relationship to food, constant and gradually worsening since onset, with no prior similar episodes. Before further data, Reza laid out an anatomical-plus-referred-pain framework for right upper quadrant pain in a young woman: the densely packed abdominal viscera make referred pain common, and extra-abdominal sources (heart, lung) as well as pelvic organs (uterus, ovaries) must be considered in a premenopausal woman. He recommended pregnancy and STI testing as routine first steps for any young patient with abdominal pain.

On exam, the patient appeared uncomfortable but had normal heart and lung exams, normal findings in the left upper, left lower, and right lower quadrants, focal tenderness to palpation in the right upper quadrant, and no rash. Reza emphasized that the pleuritic quality of the pain (worse specifically with breathing) demanded serious consideration of the "P's" of pleuritic pain — PE, pneumonia, pneumothorax, and pleuritis/pericarditis — since pleural, pericardial, or diaphragmatic/phrenic-nerve irritation from any of these processes could produce right upper quadrant pain of this character. He noted that focal tenderness to palpation carries localizing value, favoring a stepwise anatomical approach from skin/soft tissue through muscle/bone to the deeper hepatobiliary structures, and that the absence of a rash did not exclude zoster, since pain can precede or occur entirely without a visible rash.

The initial ED workup, obtained within the first ten minutes, returned: a normal EKG (screened specifically for both ACS and occult pericarditis), a normal troponin, a negative point-of-care pregnancy test, and a normal chest X-ray. Reza reasoned that the normal EKG and troponin, combined with low pretest probability for a coronary event, effectively removed the heart as the cause of the pain. He flagged the chest X-ray's asymmetric test characteristics — highly informative if positive, but not reassuring if negative — and outlined his next priority as liver chemistries: abnormal LFTs would support a hepatic-inflammatory explanation for both the right upper quadrant pain and, via diaphragmatic irritation, the pleuritic component; normal LFTs would push the decision toward CT imaging, a genuine shared decision given the trade-off between radiation/cost and the value of excluding PE or subtle parenchymal/pleural pathology. He reiterated that the pleuritic label itself demanded serious action regardless of how the rest of the picture evolved.

The subsequent labs showed normal LFTs, a normal renal panel, normal hemoglobin and platelets, a mildly elevated white count of 13,000, and a normal D-dimer. An empiric trial of Toradol for a possible musculoskeletal cause produced no response. Reza reasoned that with pericarditis essentially excluded and the chest X-ray negative, pulmonary embolism became the dominant remaining diagnosis that had to be actively excluded; he characterized the white count of 13,000 as intriguing but not clearly diagnostic of infection or inflammation, given the absence of fever or localizing infectious symptoms, and reasoned it more likely reflected a nonspecific stress/pain response. Given the severity of the pleuritic pain, he favored a CT pulmonary embolism protocol, which would also provide views of the pleura, lung parenchyma, bone, and upper abdomen (including the liver) in a single study — while candidly acknowledging he was uncertain what he would pursue next if that scan were negative, raising STI-related Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome as a consideration in young women but not prioritizing it, given the normal LFTs.

Rabih obtained a CT chest with contrast, which was completely normal, and reassured and discharged the patient with instructions for Tylenol and ibuprofen, deliberately avoiding a same-visit CT abdomen/pelvis to limit radiation exposure in a young patient. She returned the next day with the same exam and vital signs but worse, persistent pain; repeat labs (D-dimer, white count, LFTs) were unchanged, and this time a CT abdomen and pelvis with contrast was obtained — also completely normal.

Faced with a fully negative two-scan workup (CT chest, then CT abdomen/pelvis, both with contrast) in a patient with three days of severe, treatment-refractory pleuritic right upper quadrant pain and a persistently mild leukocytosis, Reza acknowledged he did not have a diagnosis to offer, framing his thinking as bounded by the pleuritic-chest-pain differential he had already worked through rather than manufacturing a new hypothesis to fit the negative imaging. Rabih discharged the patient a second time without a unifying diagnosis — but revealed to Reza that he had, in fact, already named the diagnosis twice during the discussion without recognizing it. Reza connected this to a case he had been "burned" on roughly four to five years earlier, presented on stage in Japan, in which a young woman's right upper quadrant pain turned out to be Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome; he had resolved at the time to build a formal illness script for the entity but never completed it.

Rabih credited his own contribution in this case not to a sharper diagnosis in the moment, but to deliberately placing the patient on a personal follow-up list — a discipline he described as knowing whose chart is worth revisiting when a case has been discharged without a satisfying answer. One week later, her chart showed a positive chlamydia test obtained by her outpatient primary care doctor, who diagnosed and treated Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome; on follow-up, her pain had resolved. Rabih explained the pathophysiology: the pleuritic pain in Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome arises from fibrotic adhesions between the inflamed liver capsule and the diaphragm, a microscopic, capsular-level process rather than one that produces parenchymal injury — explaining why LFTs and standard imaging are so often completely normal despite severe pain. He noted that the single biggest diagnostic clue in most cases is concomitant, radiologically or symptomatically evident pelvic inflammatory disease, which was notably absent here — a presentation pattern seen in up to roughly 20% of Fitz-Hugh-Curtis cases, in which hepatic-predominant symptoms occur without accompanying pelvic findings, making the diagnosis substantially harder to reach.

Reza closed with two reflections: first, that Rabih's transparency about following up on a case he sensed he was missing something on exemplifies the kind of growth-oriented humility RLR aims to model, distinct from and complementary to raw diagnostic accuracy; and second, that it would be unfair for any listener to judge the miss from outside the encounter, since real-time clinical reasoning is shaped by volume, competing priorities, and the discipline of first excluding emergent diagnoses — pressures a retrospective case discussion cannot fully replicate. He shared a parallel personal example (a missed stridor/upper airway obstruction case that "stung for two nights") as a model for treating diagnostic misses as growth opportunities rather than as self-defining events.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome illness script: severe, treatment-refractory, pleuritic right upper quadrant pain with normal LFTs and normal imaging in a sexually active young woman should raise suspicion for chlamydial (or gonococcal) perihepatitis, even in the absence of overt pelvic symptoms or radiologic PID findings — a presentation pattern seen in roughly 20% of cases that makes this diagnosis genuinely difficult to reach through imaging or standard labs alone.

The case is a direct, self-referential example of a clinician correctly generating the right diagnostic hypothesis (Reza explicitly named STI testing and, separately, Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome) without recognizing in the moment that the pieces had already converged on the answer — illustrating that having the right knowledge available is not the same as successfully deploying it under the cognitive load of an active, multi-threaded differential. The case also models active anti-anchoring behavior: rather than accepting "unexplained pain, everything's negative" as a stopping point, Rabih's follow-up-list habit kept the diagnostic question open past the point of discharge.

## Teaching Discussion

The central teaching point is Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome itself: its capsular (not parenchymal) pathophysiology explaining why labs and imaging are so often falsely reassuring, and the roughly 20% subset of hepatic-predominant, pelvic-symptom-negative presentations that make it disproportionately difficult to diagnose.

A second teaching point is the disciplined, stepwise exclusion of dangerous pleuritic-pain diagnoses (pericarditis, PE, pneumonia) using EKG, troponin, D-dimer, and imaging — a sequence presented as broadly generalizable regardless of the eventual diagnosis.

A third teaching point is procedural: deliberately building and checking a personal follow-up list for patients discharged without a satisfying diagnosis is a concrete habit that can close diagnostic loops days or weeks after the initial encounter, when the responsible clinician is no longer directly caring for the patient.

The final and most emphasized teaching point is about diagnostic humility and professional resilience: recognizing a missed diagnosis, sharing it candidly, and treating it as a bounded growth opportunity rather than a referendum on one's competence — paired with an explicit caution against judging any specific missed diagnosis without having been present for the full complexity of that real-time encounter.
