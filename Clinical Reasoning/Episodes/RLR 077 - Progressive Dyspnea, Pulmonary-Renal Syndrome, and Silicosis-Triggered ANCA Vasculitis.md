## Pearls

- For hypoxemia with crackles and a subacute tempo, ask whether the exam finding proportionally explains the degree of hypoxemia — disproportionate hypoxemia should prompt a search for an additional contributing process (such as an intracardiac shunt) rather than anchoring on the first explanation found.
- Pulmonary nodule distribution — centrilobular versus scattered/miliary versus perilymphatic — narrows the differential, but no schema is bulletproof: disseminated infection can occasionally present with a centrilobular pattern.
- New secondary hypertension in a young adult should trigger an age-based [[Base Rate]] approach (renal artery stenosis/fibromuscular dysplasia, sympathomimetic substances, endocrine causes) rather than a default diagnosis of essential hypertension.
- Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis reasoning starts with the renal biopsy's immunofluorescence pattern — immune-complex-mediated, pauci-immune (ANCA), or anti-GBM (linear staining, Goodpasture syndrome) — with each pointing toward a distinct serologic workup.
- When ANCA, ANA, and dsDNA are all strongly positive together, weigh the renal biopsy pattern over the serology alone; a pauci-immune, normal-complement crescentic glomerulonephritis argues against lupus nephritis despite positive lupus serologies.
- An "inelegant," overlapping serologic pattern — here, both cANCA/PR3 and pANCA/MPO positive — should prompt a search for a trigger (a drug such as levamisole-adulterated cocaine, an infection, or, as in this case, chronic silica exposure) rather than a forced overlap-syndrome label.

## Problem Representation

A young construction worker who immigrated from Mexico and lives in California's Central Valley presents with months of progressive dyspnea and hypoxemia, found on imaging to have diffuse centrilobular pulmonary nodules; after an extensive infectious workup is negative, he develops new severe hypertension followed by rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis with diffuse alveolar hemorrhage, ultimately localizing — through an unusually overlapping ANCA serology and eggshell mediastinal lymph node calcifications — to silicosis-triggered ANCA-associated pulmonary-renal vasculitis.

## Case Narrative

A 25-year-old man who immigrated from Mexico, lives and works in construction in California's Central Valley, and supports a family of four reluctantly takes a day off work for progressive dyspnea of several months' duration. In clinic he is found to have low-grade hypoxemia and crackles.

The discussant centers the framework on the hypoxemia rather than the crackles alone, reasoning that its subacute tempo (months, tolerated well enough to keep working) argues against non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema or ARDS and toward parenchymal disease from fluid, pus, blood, protein, cells, cancer cells, or interstitial lung disease. Given the epidemiology — Central Valley construction work disrupting soil and aerosolizing spores — [[Coccidioidomycosis]] and its cousin paracoccidioidomycosis (given his Mexican origin) are prioritized, alongside mycobacterial and other fungal infections; an HIV test is needed to interpret serologies reliably. Subacute heart failure is also kept on the list pending exam and imaging.

Labs and imaging: CBC, renal panel, LFTs, ESR, and CRP are all normal; the respiratory viral panel is negative; chest X-ray is normal; CT chest shows diffuse centrilobular nodules throughout all lung fields, and oxygen saturation is 93% on room air.

Applying a schema for pulmonary nodule distribution — centrilobular, scattered (hematogenous, as in TB or fungi, or miliary malignancy), or perilymphatic (lymphatic spread of cancer or sarcoidosis) — the centrilobular pattern favors aspiration or hypersensitivity pneumonitis (relevant given construction work), while acknowledging that no schema is bulletproof and disseminated infection can occasionally appear centrilobular. Given the very high regional base rate, coccidioidomycosis serology, beta-D-glucan, and galactomannan are all sent despite the atypical centrilobular (rather than eosinophilic, interstitial) pattern.

AFB and sputum cultures are negative, and endemic mycoses serologies and antigens are all negative; a BAL with biopsy is recommended but deferred because the patient cannot miss another day of work. He continues with slowly progressive dyspnea and, at a subsequent visit, is found to have new severe hypertension (systolic 170s) after a previously normal blood pressure.

The new severe hypertension prompts reconsideration of whether the nodules actually explain the hypoxemia — an echocardiogram with bubble study is proposed to evaluate for an undetected intracardiac shunt, with explicit acknowledgment of the limits of interpreting imaging without direct access to it. Age (25) makes essential hypertension unlikely and prioritizes a base-rate-driven secondary hypertension workup: renal artery stenosis/fibromuscular dysplasia and sympathomimetic substances (urine toxicology) are most common in young patients, alongside endocrine causes (hyperaldosteronism, hypercortisolism, rare ACTH-secreting paraneoplastic tumors). The practical plan is a urine toxicology screen, renal ultrasound with Doppler, repeat chest CT, renin and aldosterone levels, a sleep study if obese, and an echocardiogram with bubble study — while flagging that mycobacterial infection remains open despite the negative studies so far.

The pivot point: new anemia (hemoglobin 16 to 10), creatinine rising to 3, marked proteinuria, marked hematuria, and RBC casts. Repeat CT shows the centrilobular nodules worse, with new diffuse ground-glass opacities, no pleural effusions, a normal BNP (arguing against a cardiogenic explanation), and previously unnoticed calcifications of the mediastinal lymph nodes, suggestive of prior or current granulomatous disease.

This constellation is framed as rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis (RPGN), organized around the renal biopsy's immunofluorescence pattern into three buckets: immune-complex-mediated, pauci-immune (ANCA-associated), and anti-GBM (linear glomerular basement membrane staining, as first described by Ernest Goodpasture during an influenza epidemic). The diffuse ground-glass opacities raise concern for diffuse alveolar hemorrhage, supporting either anti-GBM (pulmonary-renal/Goodpasture syndrome) or ANCA-associated vasculitis; of MPA versus GPA, MPA is felt to better fit the nodules given the patient's age, though a biopsy is needed before empiric high-dose steroids given the calcifications' hint of granulomatous disease. The immune-complex bucket is assessed through complement levels: hypocomplementemic causes (lupus, endocarditis, cryoglobulinemic vasculitis) versus normocomplementemic IgA vasculitis (which would be atypical for nodules); rheumatoid arthritis is noted as the one autoimmune disease classically associated with pulmonary nodules but does not fit the renal findings. ANA, dsDNA, ANCA (cANCA/PR3, pANCA/MPO), complement levels, and hepatitis serologies (for cryoglobulinemic vasculitis) are all sent, alongside a BAL for diffuse alveolar hemorrhage and a simultaneous kidney biopsy to determine whether immunosuppression is needed.

Results: ANCA positive for both cANCA and pANCA, with both PR3 and MPO strongly positive at high titer; ANA positive at 1:512 with a strongly positive double-stranded DNA; complement levels normal; kidney biopsy shows pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis; BAL confirms diffuse alveolar hemorrhage; a transbronchial biopsy is performed.

Despite the strongly positive ANA and dsDNA, the pauci-immune (not immune-complex) pattern on renal biopsy and the normal complement levels argue against lupus nephritis, which would be expected to show an immune-complex, "full house" immunofluorescence pattern — the discussant explicitly names the discomfort of setting lupus aside against convention, but does so based on the biopsy pattern. Both cANCA/PR3 (associated with GPA) and pANCA/MPO (associated with MPA/EGPA) being strongly positive together is an atypical, "inelegant" pattern; EGPA is excluded for lack of a waxing-and-waning eosinophilic history, leaving an apparent overlap of GPA and MPA as the working diagnosis — with an explicit caveat that levamisole-adulterated cocaine is a classic drug trigger for this exact dual-ANCA pattern, though this patient's history does not suggest stimulant use.

The transbronchial biopsy reveals massive silicosis. The nodules, the eggshell mediastinal lymph node calcifications, and construction work together point to chronic occupational silica exposure as the trigger for the ANCA-associated pulmonary-renal vasculitis — silicosis impairs macrophage function (raising tuberculosis risk) and is a recognized driver of autoimmune disease, with documented spikes in regional ANCA vasculitis incidence following earthquakes that aerosolize ambient silica. He is treated with standard ANCA vasculitis immunosuppression (rituximab) plus source control to limit ongoing silica exposure, and improves — kidney function recovers, the nodules improve, and the diffuse alveolar hemorrhage resolves.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Despite a strong regional base rate for coccidioidomycosis, the discussants continue testing rather than anchoring once serologies and cultures return negative, and remain explicitly willing to entertain non-infectious triggers — a disciplined avoidance of [[Anchoring Bias]] on the epidemiologically most likely diagnosis.

Diagnostic humility appears repeatedly: an explicit acknowledgment of being "limited as discussants of unknowns" without access to primary imaging, and repeated statements of uncertainty about whether the nodules alone explained the hypoxemia — a model of not overcommitting a single finding to a single explanation when the two may be disproportionate.

The convention that "you're not supposed to ever put lupus aside" is deliberately overridden based on the renal biopsy's pauci-immune pattern rather than the serology alone — a disciplined example of weighting histology over serology when the two conflict, paired with an explicit acknowledgment of how uncomfortable that override feels.

Rather than forcing an "overlap syndrome" label onto the simultaneously positive cANCA/PR3 and pANCA/MPO results, the discussants treat the mismatch as a signal to search for an external trigger — using pattern inelegance itself as a bias check against premature closure on the first internally consistent (but forced) explanation.

Both hosts explicitly note that neither had a pre-existing illness script for silicosis-triggered vasculitis, and that this gap — not a reasoning error — is what made the trigger unguessable in real time, a candid acknowledgment that illness-script gaps limit diagnosis just as much as flawed reasoning does.

## Teaching Discussion

Pulmonary nodule distribution helps localize disease: centrilobular nodules point toward aspiration, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, or, less classically, disseminated infection; scattered/miliary nodules suggest hematogenous spread of infection (TB, fungi) or malignancy; and perilymphatic nodules suggest lymphatic spread of cancer or sarcoidosis.

Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis is organized by the renal biopsy immunofluorescence pattern into three buckets: immune-complex-mediated (lupus, endocarditis, cryoglobulinemic vasculitis, IgA vasculitis), pauci-immune (ANCA-associated), and anti-GBM (linear glomerular basement membrane staining, as in Goodpasture syndrome).

cANCA typically correlates with anti-PR3 antibodies and granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA); pANCA typically correlates with anti-MPO antibodies and microscopic polyangiitis (MPA) or eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA), though the correlation is not absolute.

Simultaneous strong positivity of both cANCA/PR3 and pANCA/MPO is an atypical pattern; levamisole-adulterated cocaine is a classic drug trigger for this combined pattern and should prompt a toxicology history even when the presentation otherwise looks like primary vasculitis.

Silicosis impairs macrophage function, raising tuberculosis risk, and is an established trigger for ANCA-associated vasculitis; documented spikes in regional ANCA vasculitis incidence following earthquakes that aerosolize ambient silica support a causal, dose-related relationship between silica exposure and autoimmune disease. Eggshell calcification of mediastinal or hilar lymph nodes is a recognized radiographic clue to silicosis and, in this case, was the finding that prompted transbronchial biopsy.

Management of silicosis-triggered ANCA vasculitis follows standard vasculitis immunosuppression (here, rituximab) together with source control — removing or reducing ongoing occupational silica exposure — both of which are necessary for durable improvement.
