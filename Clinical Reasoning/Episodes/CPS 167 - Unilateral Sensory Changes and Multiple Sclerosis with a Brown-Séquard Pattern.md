## Pearls

- **Pain/temperature and vibration/proprioception travel by different, oppositely-timed pathways.** The spinothalamic (anterolateral) tract carries pain and temperature and crosses immediately on entering the spinal cord, staying contralateral the rest of the way up; the dorsal columns carry vibration and proprioception and stay ipsilateral until they cross in the lower medulla. This split is what produces **Brown-Séquard syndrome**: a hemicord lesion causes contralateral pain/temperature loss with ipsilateral weakness and vibration/proprioception loss — the only place in the nervous system with this particular crossed/uncrossed sensory dissociation.
- **A "positive" sensory phenomenon (tingling, paresthesias, a "brickling" sensation) argues against a typical ischemic event, which classically produces "negative" phenomena** (numbness, loss of vision, loss of function) rather than added sensations — a subtle clue worth weighing early, before the rest of the history arrives.
- **Two temporally separate focal neurologic episodes in a young patient should raise multiple sclerosis** — "dissemination in time" — and, under the revised McDonald criteria, the diagnosis can now be made at the *first* clinical presentation using MRI (lesions in more than one location = dissemination in space) plus CSF oligoclonal bands or a mix of enhancing/non-enhancing lesions (substituting for dissemination in time), without waiting for a second clinical relapse.
- **The Romberg sign tests dorsal-column (proprioceptive) function, not cerebellar function** — a common point of confusion. A patient with cerebellar ataxia struggles to stand with feet together even with eyes open; a patient with a proprioceptive/sensory ataxia (dorsal column or peripheral nerve) can usually do this with eyes open but sways or falls once vision is removed.
- **Hyperreflexia without an extensor plantar (Babinski) response doesn't rule out an upper motor neuron lesion.** Patients don't always present the full textbook constellation, and some conditions (B12 or copper deficiency causing a myeloneuropathy, cervical spondylotic myelopathy with a coexisting peripheral neuropathy) produce a genuine mix of upper- and lower-motor-neuron signs.
- **Uhthoff's phenomenon** (heat-triggered, transient worsening of demyelinating symptoms — here, noticed in a hot shower) **and Lhermitte's phenomenon** (an electric-shock sensation down the spine with neck flexion, classically taught with MS but non-specific — seen with any cervical cord compression) are named bedside clues worth recognizing even before imaging returns.

## Problem Representation

A 19-year-old farmer in coastal Guatemala develops right arm-and-leg paresthesias and impaired hot/cold temperature discrimination beginning about two weeks after being bitten by roughly 300 ticks and self-decontaminating with diluted pesticide; the symptoms evolve over subsequent days and resolve spontaneously after about three weeks without treatment. Five months later he presents with four days of left arm and leg weakness, fatigue, and no other systemic symptoms. Exam shows mildly diminished crude sensation on the right hemibody, left-sided hyperreflexia with a negative Babinski sign, left-sided 3/5 strength (right hemibody normal), a negative Romberg, and an unsteady gait attributed to weakness rather than ataxia. HIV, urine heavy metals, and regional tick-borne serologies are negative. MRI of the spine shows small hyperintense C4–C5 lesions; MRI of the brain shows T1 "black holes" in the corpus callosum and T2/FLAIR subcortical and periventricular hyperintense lesions; CSF is oligoclonal-band positive and visual evoked potentials are abnormal — findings that together establish a diagnosis of multiple sclerosis with dissemination in both space and time at first clinical presentation.

## Case Narrative

The recording opens with the same standing Clinical Problem Solvers introduction and Aaron Berkowitz's **[[Neurophobia|end-neurophobia]]** framing captured in full in [[CPS 146 - Right-Sided Weakness and Numbness and Medial Medullary Syndrome from a Vertebral Artery Dissection|CPS 146]].

**Chief complaint: right arm and leg temperature/sensory changes in a 19-year-old man.** Discussants Kanu (India) and Mohammed (Egypt/Saudi Arabia) open by asking whether the sensory change is unilateral or bilateral and whether there's any associated weakness — the discipline of asking "is it *just* sensation?" before building a differential, since the presence or absence of weakness materially changes the localization. Mohammed works through an etiologic mnemonic (vascular, infectious, trauma, autoimmune, metabolic, iatrogenic/idiopathic, neoplastic, congenital, degenerative/drug, endocrine), while Berkowitz says he personally finds VITAMIN-style mnemonics hard to hold onto and prefers his usual **[[Localization × Time Course|localization × time course]]** approach instead. On localization, he walks through the sensory pathway from periphery (nerve endings, roots, plexuses) through the spinal cord, brainstem, thalamus, to the postcentral gyrus — emphasizing that pain/temperature and vibration/proprioception travel together in general trajectory but cross at different points, setting up the case's later Brown-Séquard reasoning. He also flags a subtle clue: the patient reports **paresthesias — a positive phenomenon** — whereas a typical ischemic stroke classically produces negative phenomena (loss), gently lowering stroke on the differential without excluding it.

**A vivid but likely irrelevant exposure history.** Maria, presenting the case, adds that the patient — a farmer — was bitten by roughly 300 ticks two weeks before symptom onset, tried to remove them by spraying diluted pesticide on himself, then showered; the sensory symptoms lasted about three weeks and resolved spontaneously. The group works through the "Venn diagram" of tick- and pesticide-related illness scripts against the actual presentation: tick paralysis is a flaccid, usually rapidly progressive motor syndrome (poor fit for an isolated sensory syndrome); Lyme disease's most common neurologic manifestation is a facial (CN VII) palsy, with meningitis or radiculopathy less common and radiculopathy expected to involve multiple contiguous roots rather than a diffuse arm-and-leg pattern (and Lyme, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, and babesiosis are all later confirmed non-endemic in Guatemala); organophosphate pesticide toxicity classically causes an immediate cholinergic toxidrome, not a two-week-delayed pure sensory syndrome, though Kanu raises "intermediate syndrome," a delayed organophosphate-related neuropathy, as a partial fit. Berkowitz ultimately calls the tick/pesticide story a likely **red herring** — a memorable, temporally-proximate exposure that the patient (and the discussants) are tempted to causally link to the neurologic symptoms, but one that doesn't hold up against the actual illness scripts for tick- or pesticide-related neurologic disease. Absent that story, he says, a 19-year-old with a new, somewhat rapidly evolving, focal sensory deficit would immediately raise **multiple sclerosis**.

**A second, contralateral episode reframes everything.** Five months after the first episode resolved, the patient returns with four days of *left* arm and leg weakness, plus fatigue but no fevers, night sweats, weight loss, or bowel/bladder/erectile dysfunction. The discussants immediately note the crossed pattern — right-sided sensory loss, now left-sided weakness — and debate whether this is one hemicord lesion (a **Brown-Séquard**-type picture: since pain/temperature has already crossed, a left-sided cord lesion produces contralateral, i.e. right, pain/temperature loss, while the corticospinal tract, already crossed higher up at the cervicomedullary junction, and the uncrossed dorsal columns produce ipsilateral, i.e. left, weakness and vibration/proprioception loss) growing radially from anterior (spinothalamic) to posterior (corticospinal) over months, or two temporally and possibly anatomically separate lesions — the latter reading as classic **dissemination in time**, and, if the lesions are in different locations, dissemination in space, the two pillars of an MS diagnosis. Berkowitz also raises HTLV-1 myelopathy (endemic in the Caribbean and Japan, uncertain in Central America, typically a chronic symmetric myelopathy), neurocysticercosis and schistosomiasis (both plausible given the farming/freshwater exposure history, though rarely cause isolated spinal disease), tuberculosis/Pott's disease (a real regional consideration for a months-long evolving cord lesion, though usually accompanied by systemic symptoms or spinal deformity not seen here), and an epidural abscess (expected to come with fever and back pain, both absent) or a primary spinal cord/meningeal tumor.

**Exam: refining the localization, and an exam gap the group openly regrets.** The patient is alert and oriented with normal cognition, no neck rigidity, and no cranial nerve findings — meaning the lesion is below the neck. Crude/light touch sensation is mildly diminished on the right hemibody; temperature and vibration, frustratingly, were never formally tested (Maria acknowledges this gap directly), which would have been the single most informative exam maneuver for confirming or refuting a Brown-Séquard pattern. Reflexes are normal on the right and hyperreflexic on the left, with a negative Babinski; strength is 5/5 on the right and 3/5 on the left, without atrophy; finger-nose-finger and rapid alternating movements are normal; the Romberg sign is negative; gait is unsteady with occasional falls. Berkowitz uses the Romberg result to correct a common misconception — the Romberg sign tests dorsal-column (proprioceptive) function, not cerebellar function — and, since there's no other evidence of ataxia or nystagmus pointing to the cerebellum, attributes the unsteady gait to the left-sided weakness itself. Kanu raises the puzzle of hyperreflexia without a Babinski sign; Berkowitz treats it as a genuine, if textbook-atypical, finding — technique matters (a properly performed Babinski starts at the heel and sweeps along the lateral sole), but mixed upper- and lower-motor-neuron pictures do occur, as in B12 or copper deficiency myeloneuropathy or cervical spondylotic myelopathy with a coexisting peripheral neuropathy. Taken together — left-sided hyperreflexia and weakness, right-sided sensory loss, no findings above the neck — the group converges on a **left-sided cervical spinal cord** localization.

**Predicting the imaging before it arrives.** Before Maria reveals the results, Mohammed and Kanu each commit to a guess (a growing compressive lesion, possibly a tuberculoma, given regional TB prevalence; or a structural cervical process like spondylosis, despite the patient's young age), while Berkowitz — invoking a favorite teaching anecdote about unexpectedly brisk reflexes in Malawian women he attributed to chronic neck-loading from carrying heavy items on their heads — considers whether the patient's farming work could contribute to a structural cervical myelopathy, but ultimately returns to his first impression: a young patient with two temporally distinct focal neurologic episodes, and a detail he specifically asks about and gets confirmed — the patient noticed his sensory symptoms **in the shower** — which he reads as **Uhthoff's phenomenon**, heat-triggered worsening of a demyelinating lesion's conduction, tipping him toward MS despite the absent Babinski sign.

**Resolution.** MRI of the spine (without contrast) shows small, round, hyperintense lesions at C4–C5; MRI of the brain shows T1 "black holes" (a marker of more destructive, chronic demyelinating lesions) in the corpus callosum, with T2/FLAIR subcortical and periventricular round hyperintense lesions; CSF shows positive oligoclonal bands; visual evoked potentials are abnormal, indicating subclinical optic pathway involvement. The final diagnosis is **multiple sclerosis**, with dissemination in space (spinal cord, corpus callosum, subcortical/periventricular white matter, optic pathways) and in time (two distinct clinical episodes, or, per the revised McDonald criteria, imaging/CSF findings alone would suffice even at a single presentation). Berkowitz contrasts the MS lesion morphology here — small, ovoid, radially arranged around the cord's cross-section, contrast-enhancing only when acute — against neuromyelitis optica, which causes a much more fulminant, longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis spanning three or more vertebral segments. He closes by naming a second bedside phenomenon, **Lhermitte's sign** (an electric-shock sensation down the spine on neck flexion — classically taught with MS but non-specific to any cervical cord compression), and notes the rapidly expanding disease-modifying therapy landscape for MS (from roughly two drugs when he was in medical school to over a dozen now), with treatment here consisting of steroids for the acute episode and consideration of disease-modifying therapy to reduce future relapses and disability accrual.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **A vivid, temporally-proximate exposure story as a red herring.** The 300-tick-bite-plus-pesticide narrative is dramatic and memorable — exactly the kind of detail an **[[Availability Heuristic|availability heuristic]]** could over-weight — but the group explicitly tests it against the actual illness scripts for tick paralysis, Lyme disease, and organophosphate toxicity, finds a poor fit on each, and sets it aside rather than anchoring on the patient's own causal narrative.
- **Positive vs. negative sensory phenomena as an early etiologic clue.** Berkowitz's distinction — added sensations (paresthesias) argue against typical ischemic stroke, which classically causes loss — is used as a lightweight but genuine Bayesian nudge early in the case, before any exam or imaging data exist.
- **Brown-Séquard reasoning as the master illness script for this case's localization.** The crossed (spinothalamic) vs. uncrossed (dorsal column) sensory pathways, combined with the already-crossed corticospinal tract, is what allows a single hemicord lesion to explain contralateral sensory loss with ipsilateral weakness — explicitly flagged as the only place in the nervous system with this particular dissociated pattern.
- **An acknowledged exam gap, held honestly rather than papered over.** Maria's candid admission that temperature and vibration were never tested — the single most diagnostic maneuver for this case's central question — is treated by the group as a real limitation on their confidence, not glossed past, an explicit instance of **[[Diagnostic Humility|diagnostic humility]]**.
- **An atypical exam finding (hyperreflexia without Babinski) treated as informative rather than dismissed.** Rather than discount the hyperreflexia because "the reflex exam doesn't fit the textbook," the group generates genuine alternative explanations (technique, mixed UMN/LMN syndromes) — "patients don't always read the textbook."
- **Committing to a prediction before the scan.** Mohammed and Kanu each stake out a specific guess (tuberculoma; structural cervical disease) before the MRI is revealed, and Berkowitz explicitly notes afterward that the group "knew exactly what the scan was going to look like" — the same **[[Test Interpretation|pretest-calibration]]** discipline seen in [[CPS 146 - Right-Sided Weakness and Numbness and Medial Medullary Syndrome from a Vertebral Artery Dissection|CPS 146]] and [[CPS 160 - Hiccups and Vertigo from a PICA-Territory Stroke via Patent Foramen Ovale|CPS 160]].

## Teaching Discussion

- Before building a differential for a sensory complaint, establish whether it is truly isolated (no weakness) or accompanied by motor findings, since the answer materially changes the localization strategy.
- Remember that pain/temperature (spinothalamic, crosses immediately, contralateral) and vibration/proprioception (dorsal columns, crosses in the lower medulla, ipsilateral until then) travel by different routes — the physiologic basis of Brown-Séquard syndrome and a powerful discriminator whenever a hemicord lesion is suspected.
- A dramatic, memorable exposure history (a mass tick bite, a pesticide exposure) deserves the same scrutiny as any other data point — test it against the actual illness scripts for the implicated conditions rather than accepting the patient's own causal narrative by default.
- Two temporally separate focal neurologic episodes in a young adult should raise multiple sclerosis, and under the revised McDonald criteria the diagnosis can be made at first presentation using MRI (dissemination in space) plus CSF oligoclonal bands or lesion-enhancement patterns (dissemination in time), without waiting for a second clinical relapse.
- The Romberg sign is a test of dorsal-column/proprioceptive function, not cerebellar function — a frequently mistaught point worth correcting explicitly.
- Hyperreflexia without an extensor plantar response, or other "incomplete" textbook constellations, should prompt consideration of mixed upper- and lower-motor-neuron syndromes (B12/copper deficiency myeloneuropathy, cervical spondylotic myelopathy with peripheral neuropathy) rather than being discounted.
- Recognize Uhthoff's phenomenon (heat-triggered, transient worsening of demyelinating symptoms) and Lhermitte's phenomenon (electric-shock sensation down the spine with neck flexion, non-specific to any cervical cord compression) as bedside clues that can tip a differential toward a demyelinating or structural cervical cord process, respectively.
- MS lesion morphology (small, ovoid, radially arranged, periventricular/subcortical/juxtacortical, enhancing only when acute) contrasts with neuromyelitis optica's fulminant, longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis (three or more vertebral segments) — a distinction worth keeping in mind whenever a spinal demyelinating process is suspected.
