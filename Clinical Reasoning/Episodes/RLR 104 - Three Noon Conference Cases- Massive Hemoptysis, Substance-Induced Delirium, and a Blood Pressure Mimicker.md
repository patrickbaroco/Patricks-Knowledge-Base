## Pearls

- Bleeding into a contained anatomic space (the lung, the pericardium) causes danger through organ dysfunction from the contained hemorrhage itself, not primarily through hemorrhagic shock, in contrast to bleeding into an open space (the GI tract), where the primary threat is volume loss.
- A practical, non-numeric bedside heuristic for gauging hemoptysis severity is what the patient/staff are using to manage the blood at the bedside — tissues suggest minimal hemoptysis, while towels suggest massive hemoptysis requiring immediate escalation (anticoagulation reversal, airway specialists, positioning).
- Roughly 70% of hemoptysis is due to bronchitis with an unremarkable workup; the "four D's" — dyspnea, drugs (anticoagulation), duration (hyperacute or subacute, incompatible with simple bronchitis), and distant symptoms (systemic findings like weight loss or purpura) — flag which patients need a more thorough workup (CT chest with contrast) rather than presumptive bronchitis management.
- In unilateral hemoptysis of known or presumed source, position the patient with the affected/bleeding lung down — this uses gravity to keep blood contained to the already-affected lung rather than letting it passively drain into and compromise the contralateral, healthy lung.
- Hyperacute altered mental status has three major buckets: seizure, stroke (in a strategic location), and substance (exogenous, including medications given during the current encounter) — explicitly considering whether recently administered medications could be the "substance" is essential, since it is easy to mentally categorize only pre-arrival exposures.
- A reflexive CO2 check in a hypoxemic, altered patient is a low-cost habit that can redirect an entire workup: hypoxemia by itself does not typically cause altered mental status (the brain extracts oxygen efficiently even at fairly low saturations), but hypercarbia does, and missing this distinction can lead a workup toward an aspiration/pneumonia framework when the actual driver is ventilatory failure.
- Hypertension in the setting of another symptom or syndrome should be evaluated as causal, contributory, or coincidental before treating it, the same "shrewdly agnostic" framework applied elsewhere to multi-finding cases — the presence of a symptom does not by itself mean the hypertension is responsible for it, and treating the wrong link (e.g., aggressively lowering blood pressure that is compensatory for pain or hypoxemia) can cause harm.
- For any patient being urgently treated for hypertensive emergency, a specific end-organ diagnosis (aortic dissection, intracranial hemorrhage, flash pulmonary edema, ACS) should be identifiable within about 30 minutes — outside of these true emergencies, blood pressure can typically be modestly, cautiously managed or even observed during that window without urgent IV antihypertensives.
- Isolated hypoxemia itself commonly triggers a catecholamine surge (from air hunger/distress) that can produce a reactive hypertension — this compensatory hypertension should not automatically be treated as the primary problem, since doing so risks masking or worsening the underlying hypoxemic process driving it.

## Problem Representation

Three parallel noon-conference cases: massive hemoptysis from cavitary TB requiring embolization; sudden altered mental status after sedating medications, ultimately hypercarbic respiratory failure from obesity hypoventilation; and hypertensive urgency in a post-procedure patient, ultimately explained by mucus-plug-related lung collapse rather than a primary hypertensive process.

## Case Narrative

### Mr. A: Massive Hemoptysis

Mr. A, a 33-year-old with a history of tuberculosis on treatment, was admitted with cavitary lung lesions and began coughing up blood at the bedside, with the nasal cannula increased to 4 liters. His vitals showed mild tachycardia, a normal blood pressure, tachypnea, and an oxygen saturation of 90% on 4 liters; he remained oriented and conversant despite active hemoptysis, with a chest x-ray showing right-sided opacification and crackles on the corresponding side.

The presence of both hypoxemia and hemoptysis together confirmed bleeding was occurring within the lung parenchyma itself. Because this represents bleeding into a contained anatomic space, the central danger is organ (lung) dysfunction from the contained hemorrhage — conceptually analogous to pericardial tamponade — rather than hemorrhagic shock, which is the dominant concern with bleeding into an open space like the GI tract. Immediate priorities were therefore securing IV access, addressing any reversible anticoagulation (not applicable here), and proactively engaging anesthesia/airway specialists given the trajectory toward probable intubation, rather than fixating on precise quantification of blood loss.

Distinguishing "massive" from "minimal" hemoptysis was addressed with both formal criteria (acknowledged to exist but less useful once a patient is already hypoxemic, since lung failure has effectively already begun) and a practical bedside heuristic: whether the patient/staff are managing the blood with tissues (suggesting minimal hemoptysis) or towels (suggesting massive hemoptysis warranting immediate escalation). In this patient, already hypoxemic with visible bleeding, the distinction was largely academic — the situation had already crossed into massive hemoptysis territory.

The broader approach to hemoptysis was outlined: roughly 70% of cases represent bronchitis with an unremarkable workup, so a set of "four D's" was proposed to flag which patients need more than presumptive bronchitis management — dyspnea (incompatible with simple bronchitis), drugs (anticoagulation on board), duration (hyperacute duration raising concern for a vascular catastrophe, or subacute duration raising concern for malignancy or a cavitary process), and distant symptoms (systemic findings like palpable purpura or weight loss that argue against a localized bronchitis). Meeting any of these criteria warrants CT chest with contrast (to opacify the abnormal vessel-airway connection responsible for the bleeding) in addition to basic labs and chest imaging.

A final management point addressed lateralization: once the side of pathology is known (here, the right lung, given the known cavitary TB lesions), positioning the patient with the affected lung down uses gravity to keep blood contained to that lung rather than letting it passively spill into and compromise the healthy contralateral lung — a source-control concept analogous to containing contamination rather than letting it spread. Mr. A was ultimately transferred to the ICU and taken for interventional radiology embolization, which successfully controlled the bleeding.

### Miss B: Sudden Altered Mental Status After Sedation

Miss B arrived by ambulance with an unknown medical history after calling emergency services herself for new cough and mild dyspnea; she described herself as a church leader "fighting Satan," and was given Benadryl, Haldol, and Ativan roughly three hours into her ED course for agitation. Shortly after receiving these medications, she developed acute altered mental status: tachycardic, normotensive, oxygen saturation 87% on 2 liters, intermittently mumbling, minimally responsive to painful stimuli, with labored breathing and upper airway sounds, though moving all extremities with equal, reactive pupils. A CT head was unremarkable, CBC showed only mild leukocytosis, point-of-care glucose was 77 (normal), and liver chemistries were normal.

The default assumption for a confused, hypoxemic patient — aspiration pneumonia or pneumonitis — was flagged as reasonable but incomplete without first checking a CO2, since hypoxemia alone does not typically impair mental status (the brain extracts oxygen efficiently even at fairly low saturations), whereas hypercarbia does; skipping this check risks anchoring on an aspiration-driven narrative when the actual driver is ventilatory failure. The three S's framework for hyperacute altered mental status — seizure, stroke, substance — was applied, with an explicit reminder that "substance" includes not only pre-arrival exogenous exposures (opiates, hypoglycemic agents) but also medications administered during the current encounter, raising the specific question of whether the recently given sedating medications were themselves causing or worsening the presentation, separate from whatever process brought her in initially. Given the circumstantial improbability of a new seizure or stroke, but the seriousness of missing either, the plan emphasized repeating the workup with attention to CO2 and considering whether intubation would be needed both to secure the airway and to safely obtain further diagnostic testing.

A subsequent venous blood gas confirmed a significant respiratory acidosis (hypercarbia), establishing that the altered mental status was a direct consequence of hypercarbic respiratory failure. The mechanism of hypercarbia was reviewed: an ineffective tidal volume, most commonly from obstruction to air movement out (COPD, asthma) or, less commonly, from insufficient air movement in, due to neuromuscular weakness or external chest restriction (obesity, kyphoscoliosis) — with treatment converging on positive airway pressure (BiPAP, if tolerated in a confused patient) or mechanical ventilation regardless of the specific mechanism. Absent wheezing, an obstructive process was considered less likely, leaving neuromuscular weakness and chest-wall restriction (including obesity) as the leading considerations, often layered on top of a low respiratory rate from the administered sedating medications.

Miss B was ultimately intubated and stabilized in the ICU; it was later revealed that she had a high BMI, providing the underlying obesity-hypoventilation substrate that made her especially vulnerable to respiratory depression from the sedating medications given for her acute psychosis.

### Mrs. C: A Blood Pressure Request That Wasn't What It Seemed

Mrs. C, a 75-year-old with a history of CVA with residual right-sided deficits and hypertension, was admitted for a dislodged gastrostomy tube (since repaired, with tube feeds resumed) and was awaiting discharge to a skilled nursing facility, with baseline mild hypoactive delirium and blood pressures running 130-150 throughout admission on losartan, insulin, and aspirin. A request came in for IV antihypertensive medication after her blood pressure rose to 195/110.

The approach to hypertension paired with a symptom or syndrome was framed using the same causal-contributory-coincidental structure applied elsewhere to multi-finding cases: hypertension may be the cause of a symptom, a contributor to it, or entirely unrelated, and the reflex to treat any elevated blood pressure accompanied by a symptom as automatically "symptomatic hypertension" requiring urgent lowering was explicitly challenged as a common but flawed heuristic (illustrated with the exaggerated example of unrelated knee pain accompanying an elevated blood pressure). The recommended discipline is to first translate the accompanying symptom or syndrome into an actual diagnosis, and only treat the hypertension emergently if that diagnosis is one actually caused or worsened by the elevated pressure (intracranial hemorrhage, aortic dissection, flash pulmonary edema, ACS) — while explicitly avoiding blood pressure reduction when the elevated pressure is compensatory for a different process (such as pain or a pulmonary embolism), where lowering it could cause harm. A practical time-bound rule was proposed: any of the true hypertensive emergencies should be identifiable within about 30 minutes via a quick imaging study, exam, or point-of-care test, meaning a brief period of watchful, non-emergent management is almost always tolerable while that determination is made.

Applied to Mrs. C, with oxygen requirements already escalating (2 to 4 liters) and no diagnosis yet established for why she was hypoxemic, the recommended next step was a quick chest x-ray, lung exam, or point-of-care ultrasound to determine whether her hypertension was a consequence of an underlying hypoxemic process (such as reactive, catecholamine-driven hypertension from air hunger) rather than the cause of one, before deciding whether IV antihypertensives were appropriate at all.

The chest x-ray provided the answer: hemiopacification of the left hemithorax with tracheal deviation toward the left, indicating lung collapse (atelectasis) rather than a pleural effusion (which would be expected to push the trachea away from the affected side) — most consistent with mucus plugging from aspirated material. The working conclusion was that her elevated blood pressure was a reactive, catecholamine-driven consequence of respiratory distress rather than a primary hypertensive emergency, meaning IV antihypertensive therapy (hydralazine specifically was flagged as having been under consideration) was not the appropriate first move. Suctioning and improved ventilation, rather than blood pressure medication, were expected to resolve both the hypoxemia and the hypertension together. This was confirmed: the mucus plug was suctioned and removed, and her blood pressure subsequently normalized to 120/80 without any antihypertensive having been given.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Contained-space hemorrhage illness script (hemoptysis, hemopericardium): danger arises from organ dysfunction due to the confined space, not primarily from volume loss — contrasts with open-space hemorrhage (GI bleeding), where hemorrhagic shock is the dominant threat.

Reactive/compensatory hypertension illness script: hypoxemia or pain triggers a catecholamine surge producing elevated blood pressure that is a downstream consequence, not the primary problem — treating it directly (rather than the underlying driver) risks harm and delays the correct intervention.

All three cases illustrate a shared cognitive bias correction: the temptation to treat the most visible or most urgently-flagged abnormality (the blood pressure number, the low oxygen saturation, the confusion) as the primary target, rather than pausing to establish the actual underlying diagnosis first. Mrs. C's case in particular is a direct rebuttal of the common but flawed "symptom + hypertension = symptomatic hypertension, treat it" heuristic.

## Teaching Discussion

Across all three cases, the unifying teaching point is diagnosis before treatment of a prominent but potentially secondary vital sign abnormality: massive hemoptysis management is organized around the physiology of a contained space rather than reflexive shock-resuscitation instincts; altered mental status after sedating medications is worked through with an explicit reflex (checking CO2) that prevents anchoring on the more common aspiration narrative; and a request for IV antihypertensives is deliberately delayed until the underlying diagnosis driving the elevated pressure is established, given the real possibility that the hypertension is compensatory rather than causal.

A secondary, recurring teaching point across the episode is the value of simple, low-cost habitual checks — a CO2 in a confused, hypoxemic patient; a chest x-ray or lung exam before treating hypertension accompanying a respiratory symptom — that are cheap to obtain but can completely redirect a workup away from an intuitive but incorrect framing.
