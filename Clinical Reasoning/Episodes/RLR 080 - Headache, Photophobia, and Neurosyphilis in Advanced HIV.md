## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 31-year-old woman with poorly controlled HIV (CD4 25, viral load over 3 million) restarted on antiretroviral therapy one month earlier presents with five days of headache and photophobia. The discussant's default operating hypothesis — migraine — is immediately overturned by her immunocompromised, recently-reconstituting status, which broadens rather than redirects the differential. A presumptive dose of ceftriaxone for incidental pyuria triggers an apparent [[Jarisch-Herxheimer Reaction]], and a subsequently noted palmar-plantar rash redirects the workup to secondary and neurosyphilis.

## Pearls

- For headache with photophobia, use the photophobia to localize: distinguish a surface-of-the-eye process (e.g., an abrasion) from a deeper process reached through the eye as the "passageway to the head" (e.g., migraine or meningitis); absent other information, migraine is the single most likely explanation in a young person.
- The single piece of history most likely to overturn an initial working diagnosis is an immunocompromising condition — it does not simply add rare diagnoses, it broadens the entire differential (increased risk of infection, malignancy, multiple simultaneous processes, and blunted/atypical symptom expression).
- Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) is the unmasking of a previously immune-suppressed, subclinical infection or malignancy once antiretroviral therapy restores CD4 function and enables an inflammatory response — the timing of symptom onset relative to ART initiation is a critical clue.
- Sudden fever, tachycardia, and transient hypotension shortly after an antibiotic dose, especially one effective against spirochetes, should raise concern for a [[Jarisch-Herxheimer Reaction]] (cell-wall lysis releasing inflammatory content) rather than a drug allergy, particularly when it resolves spontaneously within an hour without epinephrine.
- A palmar and plantar rash narrows a broad differential dramatically — prioritize secondary syphilis, alongside HSV-associated erythema multiforme and coxsackievirus (hand-foot-mouth disease), over more generic causes of rash.
- In a patient with an immunocompromising condition and a CNS process, always examine the eyes directly (fundoscopic and slit-lamp exam) before assuming a symptom like photophobia is simply a nonspecific feature of meningitis — ocular involvement (e.g., syphilitic uveitis or optic neuritis) can coexist with, or even explain, the presenting complaint.

## Case Narrative

A 31-year-old woman presents with five days of headache and photophobia. Before any other history is known, the discussant reasons from anatomy and base rate: headache alone is often a bystander finding — a feature of primary headache syndromes (migraine, tension, cluster), of external-to-the-face structures (the eyes, sinuses, neck), or of an underlying systemic process, only occasionally reflecting a true intracranial process (meningitis, giant cell arteritis, venous sinus thrombosis). Photophobia, however, provides localization: because light must pass through the eye, photophobia can arise superficially (e.g., a corneal abrasion) or from a deeper process reached through the eye as a passageway (migraine, meningitis). A five-day, non-hyperacute tempo argues against a vascular event. Absent any other information, and by base rate alone, the single most likely explanation for headache and photophobia in a young person is migraine — proposed explicitly as the reasonable "Vegas bet" and default operating hypothesis at this stage.

The past medical history dramatically reframes the case: HIV, poorly controlled due to adherence difficulties compounded by fentanyl use disorder, with a viral load over 3 million and a CD4 count of 25 — and she was re-enrolled in care and adherent to antiretroviral therapy for the past month. The discussant frames an immunocompromising condition as the single type of history most likely to transform a differential — not narrowing it to a specific rare disease, but broadening it: increased risk of infection, increased risk of malignancy (including virus-driven cancers and lymphoma at increased prevalence with HIV), increased risk of multiple simultaneous processes, and increased risk of blunted or absent classic symptoms, since the immune response that produces recognizable inflammation may itself be impaired. HIV's pathophysiology — a retrovirus targeting CD4 lymphocytes, which are essential to granuloma formation and viral/opportunistic-organism surveillance — is reviewed as the mechanistic basis for this broadened risk.

The sequence of events is emphasized as diagnostically important: headache and photophobia arising one month after restarting antiretroviral therapy raises concern for immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) — the unmasking of an infection or malignancy that was present but clinically silent while the immune system was too suppressed to mount an inflammatory response, now becoming symptomatic as CD4 function is restored. Given HIV with a very low CD4 count and new headache, the threshold for lumbar puncture is very low regardless of how benign the presentation might otherwise seem (in an immunocompetent patient, the same story might simply be treated as migraine); toxoplasmosis, CNS lymphoma, and cryptococcal meningitis are named as key concerns requiring imaging before LP, alongside a fundoscopic exam and attention to the ocular surface (dry eye is also flagged as a common, easily overlooked cause of photophobia).

Given the immunocompromised status, a CT head is obtained before LP per standard practice, with blood work sent beforehand and empiric meningitis coverage given given the low probability but high morbidity of untreated bacterial meningitis. Labs are notable only for hemoglobin 10; white count, platelet count, renal function, and liver function are normal. Urinalysis shows pyuria, and — before the CT — a dose of IV ceftriaxone is given for a presumed UTI. En route to the CT scanner (having also received vancomycin and ampicillin, though not yet acyclovir), she abruptly develops a fever of 39°C, hypotension, tachycardia, and agitation; the scan is completed despite these changes (thankfully normal), and she improves spontaneously roughly an hour after returning.

This reaction is analyzed as a differential in itself. A drug allergy (mast-cell/anaphylactic or bradykinin-mediated) is considered unlikely: an anaphylactic reaction should not resolve this quickly without epinephrine and would be expected to include rash or wheezing, and a bradykinin-mediated reaction would not typically produce this hemodynamic pattern. A Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction — an antibiotic-induced lysis of spirochete cell walls releasing inflammatory content, producing an anaphylactoid-like picture treated only with supportive care — is raised as a strong possibility if the underlying process involves a spirochete such as syphilis or leptospirosis, particularly since ceftriaxone (rather than vancomycin or ampicillin) is the agent most likely to have precipitated it. A third possibility, an underlying disease causing intermittent flares (a hormone-secreting neuroendocrine tumor such as carcinoid, or a mast cell disorder such as systemic mastocytosis), is also raised but felt less likely in this specific clinical context, where meningitis — and specifically neurosyphilis, given the reaction to ceftriaxone — is prioritized.

The admitting team, evaluating the patient after her lumbar puncture, notes on exam a chronically ill-appearing, cachectic patient without oral lesions (specifically screened for Kaposi sarcoma) or palpable lymphadenopathy, but with a pinpoint macular rash throughout the body, including the palms and soles. LP results: opening pressure normal, white cell count 7 (approximately 90% lymphocytes), protein 75 (mildly elevated), glucose normal.

With the CSF profile (mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, mildly elevated protein, normal glucose, normal opening pressure) and the new palmar-plantar rash, the discussant works through a viral CSF differential (HSV-1/2, CMV, EBV, VZV, enteroviruses including coxsackievirus, West Nile virus, given the immunocompromised host's risk of multiple concurrent processes) before focusing on the rash's distribution: ignoring the truncal component and concentrating on the palms and soles narrows the differential sharply toward secondary syphilis (unifying with the presumed Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction to ceftriaxone), with HSV-associated erythema multiforme and coxsackievirus (hand-foot-mouth disease) as the leading alternatives. Treponemal antibody testing (specific test first, given HIV, followed by RPR) and CSF VDRL are sent, alongside a request to examine the optic nerve for possible syphilitic optic neuritis, which could also explain the CSF pleocytosis and photophobia independent of meningitis alone.

Treponemal antibody returns positive, RPR returns at 1:64, and CSF VDRL is negative (as it commonly is despite true neurosyphilis). Fundoscopic exam shows no evidence of ocular involvement. She is treated with IV penicillin and improves substantially. The infectious disease team frames this as likely a paradoxical unmasking (IRIS) of latent syphilis by immune reconstitution rather than a purely coincidental new infection; antiretroviral therapy is continued throughout, as neurosyphilis — unlike CMV retinitis or cryptococcal meningitis — is not among the infections requiring ART interruption because of IRIS-related risk.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The discussant states explicitly that when asked what single piece of information would most change a case's differential, the correct category of answer is an immunocompromised state — not because it points to one specific diagnosis, but because it changes the pretest probability of an entire class of diagnoses (infection, malignancy, multiplicity, and atypical presentation) at once; this is offered as a generalizable meta-lesson about how history-taking should reshape a differential, not just add to it.

The [[Jarisch-Herxheimer Reaction]] is recognized quickly in real time specifically because of prior exposure to a similar case on a separate teaching episode — an explicit acknowledgment that pattern recognition for an uncommon phenomenon is built through repeated exposure (including case discussion, not only direct patient care), and that a presentation which would have been genuinely confusing without that prior exposure became tractable because of it.

The rash's distribution is treated as far more diagnostically valuable than its overall extent — deliberately down-weighting the truncal component of the rash and concentrating on the palms and soles is presented as a disciplined way to avoid diluting a specific, high-yield clue with lower-yield, nonspecific findings.

The reminder to examine the eyes directly, rather than assuming photophobia is simply a nonspecific accompaniment of meningitis, models resisting premature closure on a single unifying explanation for a symptom (photophobia) once a broader diagnosis (neurosyphilis/meningitis) has been secured — the symptom that started the case is revisited at the end to make sure it, too, has been adequately explained.

## Teaching Discussion

Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome describes the unmasking of a previously subclinical infection or malignancy once antiretroviral therapy restores CD4 function and enables an inflammatory response; the timing of new symptoms relative to ART initiation is a key diagnostic clue, and antiretroviral therapy is generally continued through IRIS except in specific intracranial infections (such as CMV retinitis or cryptococcal meningitis) where reconstitution-driven inflammation within the closed cranial vault carries particular risk — neurosyphilis is not conventionally among these exceptions.

A Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction occurs when an antibiotic effective against spirochetes (or other organisms such as Leptospira) lyses the organism's cell wall, releasing inflammatory content and producing a transient, anaphylactoid-like clinical picture (fever, hypotension, tachycardia) that resolves with supportive care alone, typically within hours, and without epinephrine.

CSF profiles with a lymphocyte predominance and mildly elevated protein are classically associated with viral meningitis but can also represent an early or atypical presentation of bacterial or spirochetal meningitis, particularly in an immunocompromised host who may not mount the expected neutrophilic response.

CSF VDRL is highly specific but poorly sensitive for neurosyphilis and is frequently negative even in confirmed cases; the diagnosis of neurosyphilis in a patient with reactive serum treponemal testing therefore often rests on the overall clinical and CSF picture (pleocytosis, elevated protein) rather than a positive CSF VDRL.

A palmar-plantar rash is a high-yield distributional clue that should prioritize secondary syphilis in the differential, alongside HSV-related erythema multiforme and coxsackievirus (hand-foot-mouth disease), and should not be diluted by attention to lower-specificity truncal rash findings.
