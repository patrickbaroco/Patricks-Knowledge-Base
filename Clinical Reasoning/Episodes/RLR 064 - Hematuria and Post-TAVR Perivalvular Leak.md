## Pearls

- **Approach [[Hematuria|hematuria]] like a GI bleed: find the lesion and get mechanical source control.** Most hematuria is an anatomical lesion — worst is cancer, commonest are infection/bladder-wall irritation or trauma on a vulnerable prostate (Foley + BPH) — and urology usually fixes it endoscopically. When they *can't*, you are by definition in unusual territory, so step back and re-derive.
- **Know what [[Continuous Bladder Irrigation|CBI]] does and doesn't do.** Continuous bladder irrigation does **nothing** to the underlying cause; it buys time for native bleeding to stop while preventing stasis clots from obstructing the bladder. Needing CBI means the bleeding is severe (or has already clotted off) — not that it's being treated.
- **Bleeding comes from the [[Bleeding Framework|blood, the wall, or the bridge]].** Three buckets: a problem in the **blood** (platelets or coagulation), the **vessel wall** (a structural/angiogenic lesion), or the **bridge** ([[Acquired von Willebrand Syndrome|von Willebrand factor]], which tethers the forming clot to the wall). A known tumor explains a wall lesion — but bleeding **[[Law of Proportionality|out of proportion]]** to it forces you to interrogate the blood and vWF.
- **Quadrant the "blood" problem: quantitative vs qualitative × platelet vs coagulation.** Platelets **>50k** are hemostatically sufficient in most patients (60k is a minor contributor at most), and normal PT/PTT excludes a quantitative coagulopathy — which pushes you to the **qualitative** axes: platelets (meds, [[Uremic Platelet Dysfunction|uremia]], genetic) and coagulation (hypothermia, hyperviscosity, and the screen-invisible **factor XII deficiency**).
- **[[Acquired von Willebrand Syndrome|Acquired von Willebrand syndrome]] hides in patients with shear.** Congenital dominates, but acquired vWD comes from **vascular shear** (valves, AV fistulas destroy high-molecular-weight multimers — the Heyde physiology), **anti-vWF antibodies**, or **chelation** by cancers (myeloproliferative neoplasms). A patient with a TAVR and dialysis access has two shear sources.
- **"Chronic" thrombocytopenia that began abruptly is really [[Acute but Persistent|"acute but persistent."]]** A count that dropped in a single step in February and then stayed flat is not a slowly-sneaking chronic process — reframing the tempo points straight at the inciting event (the TAVR), not at HIV/HCV/ITP.
- **Solve [[Thrombocytopenia|thrombocytopenia]] through its accompanying cell line.** Platelets lack a "differential" and good destruction markers, so read the anemia instead: acute blood loss, iron/EPO deficiency (dialysis), and — timing-matched to the valve — **[[Valve Hemolysis|valve hemolysis]]** consuming both hemoglobin and platelets. (And recall the two causes of *truly* chronic thrombocytopenia: **splenic sequestration and myelodysplasia** — check an old scan for spleen size for free.)

## Problem Representation

An early-80s man with urothelial bladder cancer (recent repeat transurethral resection two days prior, with a prior prolonged post-resection hematuria course), end-stage renal disease on hemodialysis (frequently self-truncated) via a tunneled line, and severe aortic stenosis status-post TAVR in February (apixaban for 3 months, then aspirin, held pre-procedure), is referred by urology for refractory hematuria requiring continuous bladder irrigation, seeking a non-urologic cause. He is on no anticoagulant with a normal PT/PTT, a platelet count of 60k, and an anemia in the setting of active hematuria; the thrombocytopenia is new since February (previously ~200k), abrupt in onset and persistent. The reasoning reframes a structural (tumor) bleeding source amplified by a systemic bleeding diathesis — uremic platelet dysfunction and acquired von Willebrand syndrome from vascular shear — and identifies the post-TAVR "acute but persistent" thrombocytopenia and anemia as pointing to valve hemolysis from a perivalvular leak, prompting echocardiography, hemolysis labs, and hematology consultation.

## Case Narrative

Reza, on med consult, presents a case referred by **urology** for **refractory hematuria** — a role reversal he savors — in an early-80s man with bladder cancer, asking medicine for a *non-urologic* cause while the patient runs **continuous bladder irrigation**. Rabih frames [[Hematuria|hematuria]] like a GI bleed: most is an anatomical lesion (cancer the worst; infection/bladder irritation or Foley-plus-BPH trauma the commonest), and you find it and get **mechanical source control** with cystoscopy or CT. Because urology *usually* fixes this immediately, their inability to means something unusual, so he steps back — confirm it's truly blood (not another pigment, which rarely needs CBI), and understand that **[[Continuous Bladder Irrigation|CBI]] does nothing to the cause**: it prevents stasis clots from obstructing the bladder while native bleeding stops on its own. Needing CBI signals bleeding severe enough to clot off the bladder. The fork ahead: is this **image-positive** (a visible lesion that won't stop) or **image-negative** hematuria?

The history deepens the picture: bladder cancer with a repeat **TURBT two days ago** (and a prior prolonged post-resection hematuria course), **ESRD on hemodialysis** (which he keeps cutting short) via a tunneled line, and a **February TAVR** for severe AS (apixaban ×3 months → aspirin → held pre-procedure). Preliminary bleeding labs: **no anticoagulant**, normal **PT/PTT**, **platelets 60k**. Rabih anchors in the **big-picture [[Bleeding Framework|bleeding framework]]**: patients bleed from a problem in the **blood** (platelet or coagulation), the **vessel wall** (a structural/angiogenic lesion — the tumor here), or the **bridge** ([[Acquired von Willebrand Syndrome|von Willebrand factor]], tethering clot to wall). Because he's bleeding **[[Law of Proportionality|out of proportion]]** to the known lesion, medicine must interrogate the blood and vWF.

He quadrants the "blood" problem — **quantitative vs qualitative × platelet vs coagulation**. Quantitatively, platelets **>50k** are hemostatically sufficient in most patients, so 60k is a minor contributor at most, and normal PT/PTT excludes a quantitative coagulopathy. That leaves the **qualitative** axes. Qualitative **platelet** dysfunction has three causes — medications (aspirin/clopidogrel), **[[Uremic Platelet Dysfunction|uremia]]**, and genetic — and he eliminates meds (none) and genetic (new at 80), leaving **uremic platelet dysfunction** (relevant given curtailed dialysis). Qualitative **coagulopathy** likewise has three — **hypothermia** (factors work poorly cold), **hyperviscosity**, and **factor XII deficiency** (so high in the cascade it's invisible on PT/PTT, but exceptionally rare) — none of which fit. He then pivots to the **bridge**: **[[Acquired von Willebrand Syndrome|acquired vWD]]**, whose flavors are **vascular shear** (valves and AV fistulas shred high-molecular-weight multimers — Heyde physiology), **anti-vWF antibodies**, and **chelation by cancers** (myeloproliferative neoplasms). With a **TAVR and dialysis access**, this patient has two shear sources. On thresholds for DDAVP, platelet transfusion, or vWF testing, Rabih is candid: none land in the general internist's wheelhouse — he'd **consult hematology**.

Reza redirects to the **[[Thrombocytopenia|thrombocytopenia]]**: even if it isn't the main driver, *why* is he low? Rabih's schema — classify **acute vs chronic** and **isolated vs accompanied** — meets a key detail: it started **exactly in February** (was ~200k), coinciding with the TAVR. He reframes the tempo brilliantly: a count that dropped in one step and stayed flat isn't "chronic" (which implies a slow sneak) but **[[Acute but Persistent|"acute but persistent."]]** He then teaches his core move — **solve thrombocytopenia through its accompanying cell line**, because platelets lack a "diff" and good destruction markers, whereas the **anemia** is interpretable: acute blood loss, iron/EPO deficiency (dialysis), and, timing-matched to the valve, **[[Valve Hemolysis|valve hemolysis]]** consuming both hemoglobin and platelets. He adds Rebecca Berger's pearl that *truly* chronic thrombocytopenia is essentially **splenic sequestration or myelodysplasia**, and would check an old scan for spleen size "for free."

The case cracks on the timeline. Reza, writing a thoughtful attestation, noticed the platelet drop began in February and traced it to the TAVR the team hadn't emphasized; Googling "TAVR + thrombocytopenia" showed a transient dip is expected but a **persistent** one is unusual, so he **texted a structural colleague (Ahmed Gohil)**, who predicted a **[[TAVR Perivalvular Leak|perivalvular leak]]** — often **inaudible** on exam — recommended echocardiography, and flagged that persistent post-TAVR thrombocytopenia is a **marker of poor outcome** (a structural valve deficiency). So the unifying reframe is that a **valve problem is presenting as hematuria**: not HIV/HCV/ITP, but a perivalvular leak driving hemolysis and platelet consumption atop a real bladder-cancer bleeding nidus. The plan becomes **TTE, hemolysis labs, and a hematology consult** (to own the DDAVP/transfusion/vWF decisions). Rabih closes with a further idea — measure **[[Free Hemoglobin vs RBC Hematuria|urine (or plasma) free hemoglobin]]** and the actual RBC count, because with both true bleeding *and* intravascular valve hemolysis you'd otherwise attribute all the red urine to bleeding — and likens it to his prior "leg pain that turned out to be heart failure" case: systemic valve failure surfacing in an unexpected organ. Both reflect on the supervising attending modeling: take what a learner gives, add to it, and let a careful attestation force you to question the timeline.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|Layered schemas for bleeding]].** The blood/wall/bridge framework, the quantitative-vs-qualitative × platelet-vs-coagulation quadrant, and the acquired-vWD flavors convert "why won't he stop bleeding?" into a finite, checkable tree.
- **[[Law of Proportionality|Proportionality as the trigger to look wider]].** A known tumor could "explain" hematuria, but bleeding *out of proportion* to it is exactly what mandates interrogating the blood and vWF rather than accepting the anatomic lesion as sufficient.
- **[[Acute but Persistent|Tempo re-labeled]].** Recasting a "chronic" low platelet count that began in one February step as **acute but persistent** relinks it to the inciting TAVR — a small semantic move with a large diagnostic payoff (and a new tempo category alongside hyperacute/acute/subacute/chronic/recurrent).
- **[[Thrombocytopenia|Reasoning through the accompanying cell line]].** Because platelets lack a differential and clean destruction markers, Rabih interprets the *anemia* to infer the platelet mechanism (valve hemolysis) — a transferable heuristic for isolated-seeming cytopenias.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Thresholds and screen-invisible causes]].** Explicit interpretation — 60k platelets as hemostatically minor, factor XII invisible on PT/PTT, and free-hemoglobin measurement to distinguish hemolysis from RBC bleeding — keeps numbers in context.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Knowing the lane, and reasoning collaboratively]].** Rabih defers DDAVP/transfusion/vWF decisions to hematology; Reza's diagnosis emerges from a careful attestation and a text to a structural cardiologist — modeling that the answer often comes from the timeline and the right colleague.

## Teaching Discussion

- Approach hematuria like a GI bleed — localize the lesion and pursue mechanical source control — and treat urology's inability to stop it as a signal that an unusual, often systemic, contributor is present.
- Understand that continuous bladder irrigation only prevents clot-obstruction while native bleeding stops; it does not treat the cause, so its necessity marks severity, not therapy.
- Localize any bleeding to the blood, the vessel wall, or the bridge (von Willebrand factor); when a structural lesion exists but bleeding is out of proportion, interrogate the blood and vWF.
- Quadrant the blood problem (quantitative vs qualitative × platelet vs coagulation): platelets above ~50k and a normal PT/PTT push you to qualitative causes — uremia and medications for platelets, and hypothermia, hyperviscosity, or screen-invisible factor XII deficiency for coagulation.
- Consider acquired von Willebrand syndrome in patients with shear (prosthetic valves, AV fistulas destroy high-molecular-weight multimers), anti-vWF antibodies, or cancer-related chelation.
- Reframe a "chronic" cytopenia that began abruptly and then plateaued as acute but persistent, which relinks it to its inciting event rather than to indolent chronic causes.
- Solve thrombocytopenia through its accompanying cell line: interpret the anemia (blood loss, iron/EPO deficiency, valve hemolysis) to infer the platelet mechanism, and remember that truly chronic thrombocytopenia is largely splenic sequestration or myelodysplasia.
- Recognize that a failing prosthetic valve can present in a distant organ: persistent post-TAVR thrombocytopenia and hemolysis suggest a perivalvular leak (often inaudible) and portend worse outcomes — pursue echocardiography, hemolysis labs, and specialist input, and consider free hemoglobin to separate hemolysis from true RBC bleeding.
