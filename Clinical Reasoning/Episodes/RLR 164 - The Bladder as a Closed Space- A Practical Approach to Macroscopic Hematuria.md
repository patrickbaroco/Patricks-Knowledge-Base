Robbie reframes the entire case around a single organizing analogy: bleeding into a closed space (like the bladder, or the brain) is dangerous not because of hemorrhagic shock but because of loss of organ function — so the single most important first test is a bladder scan to confirm the bladder is still "online," not a CBC or an IV. ## Pearls

- Macroscopic blood loss noticed after a patient uses the bathroom is not automatically a GI bleed — a focused exam to localize the actual source (GI, genitourinary, or even a simple skin/perineal laceration) should come first, since the correct next steps diverge sharply depending on the source.
- Organize bleeding by whether it's into an open space (external trauma, GI bleeding — where the central threat is hemorrhagic shock and cardiovascular support) or a closed space (intracranial hemorrhage, genitourinary bleeding into the bladder — where the central threat is loss of organ function from obstruction, not blood volume loss).
- In macroscopic hematuria, the single most important first step — before an IV, a CBC, or a creatinine — is a bladder scan (post-void residual). A low PVR means the bladder is still "online" and there is time to think; an elevated PVR means bladder function has already been lost and a Foley catheter is needed emergently.
- A quick, gentle urethral irrigation with a syringe (no more forceful than flushing skin) can identify a superficial urethral meatal injury as the bleeding source — a genitourinary analogue of finding a GI bleed is really just a bitten lip.
- About 90% of macroscopic hematuria originates from the bladder, with smaller fractions from the kidneys or urethra.
- If a Foley catheter is needed for gross hematuria with suspected clots, start with a two-way catheter (around 20 French) and irrigate forcefully and repeatedly to clear all clots — a two-way catheter irrigates through a much larger single lumen than a three-way. Only escalate to a three-way catheter for continuous bladder irrigation (CBI) once clots are confirmed cleared (by irrigation return and point-of-care ultrasound); irrigating a clot-obstructed bladder with a three-way catheter's continuous inflow risks bladder rupture if the outflow is occluded.
- Roughly 90% of ED patients with macroscopic hematuria can be managed with irrigation alone and discharged; only about 10% require admission for continuous bladder irrigation.
- Traumatic hematuria (e.g., related to an existing Foley catheter) is usually transient and low-risk; spontaneous hematuria without trauma, infection, or stone symptoms carries a high probability of bladder cancer, which accounts for over 90% of genitourinary cancers presenting with gross hematuria — bleeding severity that waxes and wanes over time is consistent with, not against, a malignant cause.
- The genitourinary bleeding workup parallels the GI bleeding workup: most patients don't need an acute intervention (Foley/irrigation, or endoscopic hemostasis), they need a diagnostic evaluation (cystoscopy plus renal imaging, or endoscopy) aimed chiefly at excluding cancer — hospitalized patients are a skewed, intervention-heavy population that can distort trainees' sense of how often intervention is actually needed.
- For patients discharged with resolved hematuria and no catheter, two simple measures reduce the risk of clot formation: aggressive hydration to dilute the urine, and scheduled voiding (e.g., every 3–4 hours) rather than waiting for urge, to avoid bladder stasis.

## Case Narrative

Reza sets the scene: he's covering the hospital's dementia care unit, a locked skilled-nursing-style floor that isn't formally inpatient, when a page comes through that a patient went to the bathroom and emerged to find the toilet bowl full of blood, with blood on the floor as well. Robbie's first response resists the reflexive assumption that this must be a GI bleed: external blood found after a bathroom trip can come from many sources — he lists prior cases of scrotal bleeding, Foley-catheter-related bleeding, vaginal bleeding, and even a simple perineal skin laceration mistaken for something more serious — and argues a careful exam to localize the actual source has to come first, since the correct next steps (taping a cut vs. worrying about urinary obstruction vs. preparing for a GI bleed) diverge completely depending on the answer.

Reza describes his exam: vitals are reassuring (heart rate 85, normal blood pressure and oxygenation), there's blood visible on the patient's hand and on the floor, no perineal abrasion or laceration, and — after being told a caretaker saw blood and clots coming from the penis — Reza confirms visible drips of blood from the urethral meatus itself, with no bowel movement having occurred.

Robbie reframes the entire case around a single organizing principle: whether bleeding occurs into an open space or a closed space. Open-space bleeding (external trauma, GI bleeding) threatens the patient primarily through hemorrhagic shock, so cardiovascular support is central. Closed-space bleeding — his leading example is intracranial hemorrhage — threatens the patient not through volume loss but through accumulation disrupting the function of the organ itself. He argues genitourinary bleeding behaves much more like the brain than like the GI tract: cardiovascular support is rarely the concern, but loss of bladder function (obstruction from clot) is. This reframing dictates the actual first move: before an IV, before a CBC, even before a creatinine, the priority is a post-void residual to confirm whether the bladder is still "online." An elevated PVR means bladder function is already lost and a Foley is needed emergently; a low PVR buys time to think. Reza reports the bladder scan: 22cc — reassuringly low.

With time now available, Robbie turns to localizing the bleeding source. Statistically, about 90% of macroscopic hematuria comes from the bladder, with smaller fractions from the kidneys or the urethra itself. A simple, low-risk first test is a gentle urethral irrigation with a syringe (no more forceful than flushing skin) to see if the bleeding is coming from a superficial injury right at the meatus — he draws a direct parallel to a prior case where an apparent GI bleed turned out to be nothing more than a bitten lip, found by simply flushing and directly inspecting the mouth. Short of that simple test resolving things, he frames this as fundamentally a waiting game: obtain a baseline CBC and creatinine, then wait for the next void and assess how dark the urine is. Persistent frank blood likely means intervention (a Foley, placed specifically to irrigate and clear clots, not merely to drain the bladder) is needed; lighter urine allows continued observation.

Reza contrasts this with his own reflexive hospitalist instinct — as a hospitalist, essentially every case of hematuria he's encountered has gotten an immediate three-way Foley with continuous bladder irrigation, and it had never occurred to him that watchful waiting was even an option. Robbie validates that reflex isn't wrong so much as incomplete, and lays out who actually warrants watchful waiting: patients with recent instrumentation (where the trauma itself is expected to resolve), patients with prominent dysuria (treatable with antibiotics if infection-driven), or patients on anticoagulation (where holding the agent may be sufficient) — situations where there's a specific, actionable reason to expect the bleeding to stop on its own.

If a Foley does become necessary, Robbie details a specific, non-obvious catheter-selection teaching. Because clinically significant macroscopic hematuria requiring catheterization is usually accompanied by clots, the instinctive move — placing a three-way Foley and starting continuous bladder irrigation (CBI) immediately — is actually the wrong first step. A three-way catheter's irrigation port is a much smaller lumen (roughly one-third the diameter) than the full lumen available on a two-way catheter, making it far less effective at physically clearing existing clots; more dangerously, continuously irrigating fluid into a bladder that still contains an obstructing clot risks the clot blocking outflow while inflow continues unimpeded — a bladder rupture risk. The correct sequence is to place a two-way Foley (around 20 French), irrigate forcefully and repeatedly until no more clot fragments return and a point-of-care bladder ultrasound confirms no residual clot, and only then — if bleeding persists — exchange for a three-way catheter to begin CBI. In Robbie's ED experience, this stepwise irrigation resolves the bleeding in roughly 90% of patients who present with macroscopic hematuria, with only about 10% ultimately requiring admission for CBI.

Reza's actual clinical course: the patient was transferred to the inpatient setting (his own reflexive plan being a Foley with irrigation), with a CBC showing a stable hemoglobin and normal coagulation studies (INR, PTT, platelets), and no medications implicated in a coagulopathy. Before placing a catheter, he consulted urology, who — echoing Robbie's framework exactly — recommended holding off, monitoring bladder volume, and waiting to see if the patient could continue voiding on his own, specifically to avoid the infectious and traumatic risks of catheterization if it could be avoided. The patient's next void was only lightly pink with no urinary retention, the void after that was clear, and a urinalysis at the third void showed zero red blood cells — the patient was ready to return to the dementia care unit without ever needing a Foley.

Turning to the underlying cause, Robbie reframes hematuria as parallel to GI bleeding almost point for point: in both, transient causes (infection, stones, or — most commonly of all — Foley-related trauma) are far less important than the one diagnosis that must never be overlooked, cancer. Over 90% of genitourinary cancers presenting with gross hematuria are bladder cancer, so essentially every patient with unexplained macroscopic hematuria needs a cystoscopy (direct visualization by urology) plus renal imaging — a renal ultrasound for lower-risk patients, or a three-phase CT urogram (non-contrast, contrast, and delayed post-contrast phases) for higher-risk patients (older, male, smokers), which offers higher sensitivity and also reasonably images the bladder itself, though it can still miss small bladder tumors that only cystoscopy would catch. Notably, waxing-and-waning bleeding severity, as seen in this patient, is consistent with a malignant cause rather than reassuring against one.

Robbie extends the GI-bleeding parallel explicitly: just as most stable GI bleeds don't need therapeutic endoscopy so much as a diagnostic one (aimed mostly at excluding cancer, and less commonly inflammatory conditions like IBD or celiac disease), most stable GU bleeds don't need a Foley so much as a diagnostic workup aimed at excluding bladder cancer. He also names a structural bias: because hospital-based clinicians disproportionately see the subset of hematuria patients sick enough to need acute intervention, it's easy for trainees to overestimate how often intervention is actually necessary. Reza obtains a CT urogram, which comes back normal, with no white cells, no UTI symptoms, and no stone symptoms; the patient is referred to urology for cystoscopy, with Robbie estimating the odds of an eventual bladder cancer diagnosis as still roughly even, given the patient's age, sex, and likely smoking history.

The episode closes with a short coda after the formal sign-off: for patients discharged without a catheter, Robbie adds two simple preventive measures to reduce the risk of clot re-formation — aggressive hydration to dilute the urine, and scheduled voiding (roughly every 3–4 hours) rather than waiting for urge, since bladder stasis promotes clot formation.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The episode is built around correcting a specific, easy-to-hold misconception: that genitourinary bleeding should be managed with the same cardiovascular-support-first instinct as GI bleeding. Reza's own admission — that as a hospitalist he reflexively catheterizes and irrigates essentially every case of hematuria he sees — is presented not as an error so much as a product of a skewed clinical denominator: inpatient physicians disproportionately encounter the subset of hematuria patients whose bleeding was severe enough to require hospitalization and intervention in the first place, which can distort a trainee's or even an experienced clinician's sense of how often intervention is truly necessary. The GI-bleeding parallel is used deliberately as a cognitive anchor: attaching an unfamiliar problem (GU bleeding) to a familiar, well-internalized framework (GI bleeding — mostly diagnostic, occasionally therapeutic, ultimately about excluding cancer) is offered explicitly as a general technique for building durable clinical reasoning in an unfamiliar domain.

## Teaching Discussion

This case is a compact masterclass in a single reframing move: recognizing that not all bleeding threatens the patient the same way, and that "closed space" bleeding (brain, bladder) is dangerous because of organ dysfunction from accumulation, not because of volume loss. That reframing correctly reorders the entire initial workup — a bladder scan before an IV, before labs — and gives a portable mental model (open space vs. closed space) that could be extended to other anatomic compartments in future cases. The catheter-selection teaching (two-way first to clear clots, three-way only after clots are confirmed cleared) is a specific, memorable, and clinically actionable pearl that runs counter to a common reflexive shortcut (jumping straight to a three-way catheter and CBI), with a clear mechanistic rationale (lumen size for effective irrigation, and the rupture risk of irrigating against an obstructed outflow).

The broader diagnostic-versus-therapeutic framing — most bleeding into a hollow viscus needs a workup, not an intervention, and the workup exists mainly to exclude cancer — is a durable, transferable heuristic that the hosts explicitly generalize beyond this single case, extending a theme already well-established on the show (diagnostic tests as tools for risk-stratification and cancer exclusion, not just for guiding acute management) into a new organ system. The structural-bias point about hospital-based clinicians seeing a skewed population is a useful, generalizable caution for any clinician reasoning about "how often does X actually need intervention" using only their own practice setting as the reference class.
