> An atypical, structurally different episode: novelist and primary care physician Shantanu Rai joins as an on-mic guest to discuss his book "A Dangerous Diagnosis," then presents — from his own memory as a med student — a real diagnostic case for Reza and Robbie to reason through blind (standard mapping holds: S2 = Reza, S3 = Robbie). A previously healthy 26-year-old female Marine develops daily spiking fevers for four months, bounced between military medical facilities across three continents, with an extensive negative workup (imaging, cultures, endemic-disease testing, empiric antibiotics) and dramatically abnormal inflammatory markers (ESR 94, CRP 89, ferritin 13,000, LDH in the high 800s) but a persistently unrevealing exam. ## Pearls

- A fever that persists for months without ever producing an identifiable end-organ consequence sets up a diagnostic paradox: the purpose of fever is to warn of impending harm, so the longer it persists without any actual harm materializing, the more the immune response itself — rather than a specific underlying disease — becomes the thing to interrogate.
- Cytokine-mediated inflammation explains the whole classic fever triad mechanistically: cytokines raise the hypothalamic temperature set point (driving chills, as the body shivers to reach the new higher target), and when cytokine levels transiently wane, the set point drops back down while the body is still relatively overheated — producing night sweats as the excess heat is dissipated.
- For fever without a localizing source, the "IMADE" schema (infection, malignancy, autoimmune disease, drug fever, DVT/thromboembolic disease, and endocrinopathy) offers a structured differential; a Southeast Asia travel/deployment history is a tempting anchor toward exotic endemic infections (e.g., melioidosis) that should generally be resisted in favor of more common explanations first.
- In a young patient with an unexplained inflammatory syndrome, liquid (hematologic) malignancies — especially lymphoma — deserve more weight than solid tumors; some forms, like intravascular lymphoma, can remain occult on standard cross-sectional imaging and may only be found through a high index of suspicion (in one prior case, via essentially random skin biopsies).
- A prolonged fever cannot formally be called a fever of unknown origin until cross-sectional imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis has been obtained; inflammatory markers like ESR, CRP, and ferritin add relatively little independent diagnostic value on their own but are still worth trending.
- Unexplained weight loss can be simplified into a practical binary: it is either explained by a structural, imaging-positive disease, or by a "substance" (something ingested or endogenously produced) — roughly 95% of cases are solved by imaging, another 5% by an HIV test and TSH, and the residual sliver requires a detailed dietary history.
- No disease confined to the extremities causes weight loss on its own — a peripheral finding (like mild joint tenderness) should not be treated as diagnostically load-bearing unless there is also evidence of visceral organ involvement.
- When an LDH climbs into the high hundreds, it generally needs to be explained by the eventual diagnosis; a useful hierarchy ("LDH pyramid") starts by excluding hemolysis (haptoglobin, peripheral smear — especially relevant when AST is disproportionately elevated over ALT, since neither enzyme is liver-specific), then considers tissue infarction, before broadening toward malignancy.
- An extreme ferritin elevation (>10,000) classically narrows the differential to a short list — adult-onset Still's disease, HLH, hemochromatosis, and histoplasmosis — but this should be treated as a useful heuristic rather than an absolute rule, since sufficiently severe inflammation from many other causes can also drive ferritin into this range.
- A disease defined by an intermittent or transient physical sign (like the salmon-colored rash of adult-onset Still's disease) should never be excluded simply because that sign isn't present on the current exam — the more reliable move is to specifically ask whether it was ever present earlier in the illness course, since most patients are actually diagnosed while the sign happens to be visible, making a months-delayed diagnosis like this one unusual.

## Problem Representation

A previously healthy 26-year-old female Marine develops daily spiking fevers that persist for four months despite extensive evaluation across multiple military medical facilities — including negative imaging, cultures, and endemic-disease testing, and multiple ineffective courses of empiric oral antibiotics — with markedly abnormal inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP, ferritin, LDH) but no localizing symptoms or consequential organ injury on repeat exam, ultimately diagnosed as adult-onset Still's disease after a targeted history uncovers a transient, since-resolved salmon-colored rash from early in the illness, and successfully treated with high-dose ibuprofen.

## Case Narrative

Before the case begins, guest Shantanu Rai — a primary care physician in the safety-net setting and author of the novel "A Dangerous Diagnosis" — sets up the exercise: this is a real patient he met and examined as a medical student, one he still thinks about roughly monthly, years later, and one he ultimately wove into his book's plot. Reza and Robbie agree to reason through the case blind, in real time, as data is progressively revealed.

The patient is a 26-year-old active-duty female Marine, previously healthy, stationed in Southeast Asia, who develops daily spiking fevers. By the time she is evaluated by the protagonist (a stand-in for Shantanu's own med-student self), she is four months into the illness and has already been evaluated by roughly a hundred different providers, having been progressively transferred across military medical facilities — from her base in Southeast Asia, to Okinawa, to Germany, and finally to Walter Reed. She has had a chest x-ray, blood cultures, and testing for infections endemic to her deployment region, all negative, an isolated elevated white count at one point that wasn't present on later testing, and multiple courses of empiric oral antibiotics that provided no durable benefit — by the time she's seen, she has been off antibiotics for a full month and is still spiking fevers daily, alongside night sweats and shaking chills, with associated weight loss and a low mood connected to the toll of the prolonged, undiagnosed illness.

Reza opens the reasoning by translating "fever" into "inflammation" and framing a paradox specific to this case: fever exists to warn the body of impending harm, so the longer a fever persists without any actual harm — any real consequence — materializing, the more it raises the question of whether the alarm system itself, rather than a genuine external threat, is the problem. His diagnostic instinct is to inventory the actual consequences of the inflammation seen so far: if specific organ injury turns up (liver, kidney, lung), the problem representation shifts toward an organ-specific inflammatory disease and the workup follows that thread; if four months in there are still no clear consequences, that absence becomes its own diagnostic riddle. He also takes a moment to explain the underlying physiology of the fever pattern itself: cytokines raise the hypothalamic temperature set point, producing chills as the body shivers to reach that new, higher target; when cytokine levels periodically wane, the set point falls back down while the body remains relatively overheated, and night sweats are how that excess heat gets dissipated.

To organize the differential for a fever without a localizing source, Reza invokes the "IMADE" schema used at Clinical Problem Solvers — infection, malignancy, autoimmune disease, drug fever, DVT/thromboembolic disease, and endocrinopathy. He flags a specific trap worth avoiding: the deployment history to Southeast Asia is tempting bait toward an exotic, endemic infection like melioidosis, but reaching for the unusual explanation this early would be a mistake. Malignancy, especially in a young patient, deserves real consideration — citing a teaching from Robbie that liquid (hematologic) cancers are disproportionately likely to cause fever, with lymphoma as a leading candidate, including atypical, imaging-occult forms like intravascular lymphoma (which Reza recalls from a formative Baltimore case that took a hematology-oncology chief resident nearly a year to diagnose, ultimately via essentially random skin biopsies). Citing a lesson from mentor Gapreet (Dhaliwal), Reza notes that a fever can't formally be labeled "of unknown origin" until cross-sectional imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis has been obtained — inflammatory markers like ESR, CRP, and ferritin add relatively little independent value on their own, but remain worth checking. If forced to pick a single next test, he'd choose the pan-CT scan.

Robbie approaches the same problem through a different, self-described "overly simplistic" lens built around the patient's weight loss: nearly all unexplained weight loss traces back to either a structural, imaging-positive disease, or a "substance" — something ingested from the outside world or produced by the body itself. By his own rough estimate, imaging resolves about 95% of these cases, an HIV test and TSH catch most of the remainder, and only a small residual sliver requires a deep dietary history. He emphasizes wanting to personally examine the patient rather than reason purely from data — looking for cachexia, tremor, diaphoresis, or a hidden structural lesion (a sarcoma or melanotic lesion) that imaging alone might have missed.

The imaging comes back first: the full pan-CT scan (chest, abdomen, pelvis) is entirely negative — no lymphadenopathy, no serositis, no tumor. Reza's response is to interrogate the negative result itself rather than accept it at face value, given how unusual a truly imaging-negative process is at this degree of inflammation: specifically, he raises the patient's thin body habitus as a limiting factor, since CT detection of intra-abdominal pathology often relies on fat stranding, and a cachectic patient has less visceral fat available to generate that signal, lowering the test's real-world sensitivity in her case.

The labs follow: hemoglobin 11.3 with a normal MCV of 85 (a mild normocytic anemia), a white count that is essentially normal at roughly 10,500 (despite an isolated elevation earlier in her course), ESR 94, CRP 89, ferritin 13,000, AST 100 with ALT only 39, albumin 3.9, LDH in the high 800s, ANA positive at a low titer of 1:320 with negative double-stranded DNA, rheumatoid factor, and anti-CCP, and uniformly negative cultures. Reza works through the labs methodically: the low-titer ANA, especially with the rest of the autoimmune panel negative, argues against lupus or classic rheumatoid arthritis; the disproportionate AST-over-ALT elevation (neither enzyme being liver-specific) raises hemolysis as a possible contributor to the LDH elevation, which he'd want to exclude first (haptoglobin, peripheral smear) before attributing the LDH purely to hepatocellular injury. He frames a broader rule of thumb: once LDH climbs into the high hundreds, the eventual diagnosis needs to explain it — starting with hemolysis at the top of the differential, then tissue infarction, before circling back to malignancy. The markedly elevated ferritin, above 10,000, classically narrows the field to a short list — adult-onset Still's disease, HLH, hemochromatosis, and histoplasmosis — though Reza is careful to caveat this against a paper he recalls (attributed to Andrew Olson) showing that sufficiently severe inflammation from many other causes can also push ferritin this high. He initially rates adult-onset Still's as less likely specifically because the patient lacks leukocytosis and lacks a visible rash, while acknowledging it remains a diagnosis of exclusion.

Before the physical exam is revealed, Robbie is explicit about discarding the accumulated "chart lore" entirely — he hasn't personally verified that she's actually lost weight, or that the fevers are actually real, so he doesn't yet know what the actual problem is. On exam, as later recounted: the patient looks thin but not cachectic, tearful at points while telling her story, but overall deceptively well — well enough that it raises a passing doubt about whether the fevers are even real. She appears fit, consistent with her being an active-duty Marine, and the only notable exam finding is mild, symmetric tenderness across both larger and smaller joints. Robbie explicitly discounts this joint finding as too nonspecific to be diagnostically meaningful, invoking a rule that no disease confined to the extremities causes weight loss on its own — a peripheral clue only matters once a visceral organ is also shown to be involved. A normal TSH rules out hyperthyroidism. An overnight hospital admission finally documents a genuine fever, above 39°C, confirming that the fevers are real rather than an artifact of unreliable outpatient reporting.

At this point Reza, working from the ferritin-driven differential, begins actively considering adult-onset Still's disease and references the Yamaguchi diagnostic criteria by name. Robbie, reasoning independently and still centered on his "fire alarm without a fire" framing plus the hepatocellular injury, elevated LDH, and transiently elevated white count, asks the question he'd want asked of the patient directly: did she have a sore throat around the time this started, a classic feature of Still's disease onset? He is told no — but that she did have a rash. Reza's mind goes immediately to a salmon-colored rash, and the patient confirms it: a transient, mildly itchy, salmon-colored rash on her trunk, present near the start of her illness but long since resolved and absent at this visit.

The actual diagnostic moment, as Shantanu recounts it, didn't come from either host's line of reasoning directly, but from a retired infectious disease physician who happened to walk into the exam room while a group of medical students were awkwardly working through a clinical-skills exercise on the patient. Without any of the accumulated chart context and without much patience for the students already in the room, he went straight to examining her joints and asking pointed questions — specifically when her illness started, and whether she'd had a rash and where. Learning about the transient salmon-colored truncal rash, he declared the diagnosis on the spot, told the patient she'd be better within weeks, and prescribed high-dose ibuprofen rather than the more commonly reached-for naproxen or prednisone. Within weeks, her daily fevers resolved completely, and she returned to full duty.

The hosts close by reflecting on how diagnostic clarity erodes over a long, undiagnosed course: an early, genuinely classic clue (the transient rash) gets buried under months of subsequently accumulated normal labs and negative imaging, until the patient is reduced, in practice, to "a bunch of lab values" rather than a coherent clinical story that anyone is still actively re-interrogating. They also note the probabilistic irony of the case: once enough common explanations have been serially excluded by extensive negative testing, an initially "unlikely"-seeming diagnosis like adult-onset Still's disease becomes, by elimination, the most probable explanation for the full constellation of findings.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The episode's central cognitive lesson concerns the erosion of diagnostic signal over a long, multi-provider course: a genuinely classic early clue (the transient salmon-colored rash) gets buried under months of subsequently accumulated normal labs and negative imaging, until the patient is effectively reduced to "a bunch of lab values" that no one is still actively re-interrogating as a coherent story. Robbie's explicit refusal to accept "chart lore" — inherited prior documentation of weight loss and fevers that he hasn't personally verified — models a deliberate discipline of re-establishing ground truth directly, rather than passively accumulating and re-transmitting prior conclusions. The case is also a clean illustration of a specific bias to avoid: treating the absence of a defining sign on the current exam as evidence against a diagnosis that depends on an inherently transient finding. Both hosts explicitly reason that a rash-defined disease shouldn't be excluded simply because no rash is visible today — the correct move is to ask whether it was ever present earlier in the illness, since most patients with this kind of disease are actually diagnosed while the rash happens to be present, which is exactly what makes this months-delayed diagnosis unusual. Finally, Reza's closing observation — that with enough common explanations serially excluded, an initially "unlikely" diagnosis becomes the most probable one by elimination — is a recurring probabilistic theme on the show.

## Teaching Discussion

The episode's structure — a real case narrated secondhand by a guest with no ongoing outcome bias, reasoned through blind by both hosts in real time — produces an unusually clean natural experiment in diagnostic reasoning. Both hosts independently converge on major elements of the correct differential before the reveal: Reza reaches adult-onset Still's disease by name through the ferritin-driven short differential, while Robbie converges on the same territory by specifically asking about a preceding sore throat, a classic Still's prodrome. Several transferable frameworks are introduced or reinforced in quick succession — the fever-to-inflammation-to-consequence-inventory reasoning chain, the "IMADE" fever/weight-loss schema, Robbie's structural-versus-substance simplification for weight loss, and the LDH-pyramid approach to an elevated LDH — while the extreme ferritin elevation doubles as a teaching moment about heuristics functioning as guides rather than absolute rules.

The case's actual resolution is a memorable argument for periodically returning to a fresh, unbiased history in a prolonged undiagnosed illness: a retired ID physician, walking in without any of the accumulated chart context, resolves a four-month, hundred-doctor diagnostic odyssey purely by re-asking a basic history question — rash at onset? — that had been asked and answered once, early on, and then lost in the noise of subsequent negative testing. It's a durable counterpoint to the instinct to keep layering new testing onto an increasingly stale problem representation, and a good discussion anchor for how diagnostic teams might build in deliberate checkpoints to re-take a case's history from scratch.
