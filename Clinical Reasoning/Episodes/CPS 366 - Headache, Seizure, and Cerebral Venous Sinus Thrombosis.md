> A neurology Virtual Morning Report with neurologist **Aaron Berkowitz** on **headache and seizure** — and a lesson in resisting the story the history hands you. A **33-year-old man** has **four days of progressively worsening headache** culminating in a **generalized tonic-clonic seizure**, with fever, photophobia, blurred vision, nausea/vomiting — and a **street fight four days earlier** in which he was punched and hit his head. Berkowitz frames [[Headache|headache]] as [[Secondary Headache|primary vs secondary]] with **red flags** (sensitive, not specific), and — because a worsening headache over days ending in seizure is a **[[Secondary Headache|secondary]]**, "in the head," acute process — builds the differential across **brain, meninges, vessels, and ventricles**: [[Bacterial Meningitis|meningitis]]/[[Encephalitis|encephalitis]]/[[Brain Abscess|abscess]], **[[Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis|venous sinus thrombosis]]**, and acute [[Hydrocephalus|hydrocephalus]]. He names the trap directly — the trauma is a **[[Framing Effect|framing bias]]**: humans draw a line from "it started after the fight" to the diagnosis, and could miss meningitis or CVST by staying yoked to a [[Subdural Hematoma|subdural]]. The exam shows raised [[Intracranial Pressure|intracranial pressure]] — [[Papilledema|papilledema]], **bilateral sixth-nerve palsies** ([[False Localizing Sign|false localizing]]) — plus left-sided weakness (a right-sided lesion) in an **awake, oriented** patient, a dissociation Berkowitz reads as *focal lesion + ICP from something other than a big mass*. He **anchors (openly) on CVST**, teaches the venous-hemorrhage clues (the [[Cord Sign|cord sign]]; a small hemorrhage with disproportionate edema), and the CT confirms **[[Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis|extensive cerebral venous sinus thrombosis]]** (transverse/sigmoid/straight/superior-sagittal sinuses, a right thalamic infarct, and **[[Mastoiditis|right mastoiditis]]**). Treatment is **anticoagulation despite the infarct**, plus targeted antibiotics for an **[[Otogenic CVST|otogenic (septic) CVST]]** — and he recovers on apixaban.

## Pearls

- **[[Headache|Headache]] = [[Secondary Headache|primary vs secondary]]; work red flags but know they're sensitive, not specific.** Secondary headache is caused by something identifiable, grouped as *in the head* (brain, meninges, vessels, ventricles), *of the head/neck* (eyes, sinuses, ears, jaw, carotid dissection), or *systemic*. Red flags — sudden/"thunderclap" onset, change in pattern, waking from sleep, and context (HIV, cancer, hypercoagulability, pregnancy) — should be **imaged**, but most red-flagged headaches with a normal exam still turn out primary; a headache **with a seizure** is not one of those — it is secondary until proven otherwise.
- **Worsening headache over days ending in seizure → think brain, meninges, vessels, *and* ventricles.** Beyond [[Bacterial Meningitis|meningitis]]/[[Encephalitis|encephalitis]]/[[Brain Abscess|abscess]], remember the two that get forgotten: **[[Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis|cerebral venous sinus thrombosis]]** (rising [[Intracranial Pressure|ICP]] → seizure, ± venous hemorrhage) and acute **[[Hydrocephalus|hydrocephalus]]** (ICP crises can seize). A tumor headache does not worsen meaningfully over four days.
- **Don't let trauma become a [[Framing Effect|framing bias]].** "It started after the fight" invites a cause-and-effect line to a [[Subdural Hematoma|subdural]] — but the trauma may be a **red herring** (independent meningitis), or related only *by several steps* (skull fracture → bacteria seeding the meninges or a CSF leak; post-traumatic **[[Otogenic CVST|mastoiditis → septic CVST]]**). Strip the trauma out and "young person with headache, seizure, fever, photophobia, vomiting" reads as meningitis — keep the differential broad.
- **Papilledema + bilateral sixth-nerve palsies = raised [[Intracranial Pressure|ICP]], not a brainstem lesion.** A [[False Localizing Sign|sixth-nerve palsy]] is *focal but non-localizing* — its long intracranial course over the clivus and through the cavernous sinus makes it vulnerable to raised ICP. And a key **dissociation**: a focal deficit + raised ICP in an **awake, alert, oriented** patient argues *against* a large herniating mass or diffuse cerebral edema and *for* ICP from another source (e.g., venous outflow obstruction).
- **"[[Could This Be Venous|Could this be venous?]]" — ask it with every hemorrhage or stroke.** [[Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis|CVST]] is uncommon, so it's missed if not sought. On non-contrast CT look for the **[[Cord Sign|cord/dense-sinus sign]]** (hyperdense clot); suspect a venous cause when a hemorrhage sits **near the cortical surface/vertex or is bilateral**, or when a **small hemorrhage carries disproportionate edema** (unlike hypertensive/[[Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy|amyloid]] bleeds). Confirm with **CT/MR venography** — or just read the **post-contrast sequences** already obtained for sinus filling. Beware the harder **[[Cortical Vein Thrombosis|cortical vein thrombosis]]** (convexal SAH, one swollen gyrus, a worm-like SWI lesion) that may need catheter angiography.
- **Anticoagulate CVST even when there is intracerebral hemorrhage.** The bleed is *caused by* venous outflow obstruction, so the treatment is to relieve it — trials support anticoagulation despite blood on the scan, with hemicraniectomy or endovascular thrombectomy for an ICP crisis. And treat the **source**: an [[Otogenic CVST|otogenic/septic CVST]] from mastoiditis needs targeted antibiotics alongside anticoagulation.

## Problem Representation

A 33-year-old man with only occasional migraine presents with four days of new, progressively worsening headache (distinct from his usual migraine) culminating in a witnessed generalized tonic-clonic seizure, with subjective fever, photophobia, blurred vision, diplopia, and vomiting, four days after a street fight in which he was punched and struck his head. He is febrile and tachycardic with neck stiffness and lateral tongue lacerations, and shows signs of raised intracranial pressure (bilateral papilledema, bilateral sixth-nerve palsies with diplopia) plus subtle left facial and left-sided limb weakness and a small sluggish left pupil, while remaining awake, alert, and oriented. Labs show a neutrophilic leukocytosis; non-contrast CT reveals edema with a hyperdense right transverse sinus, and MR venography confirms extensive cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (transverse, sigmoid, straight, and superior sagittal sinuses) with a right thalamic infarct and ipsilateral mastoiditis. The diagnosis is extensive cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, most likely otogenic/septic from post-traumatic mastoiditis, treated with anticoagulation and targeted antibiotics with recovery.

## Case Narrative

A CP Solvers Neurology VMR with **Aaron Berkowitz** teaching, **Bayan** presenting, and **A** (Athant) discussing. The chief concern: **four days of progressively worsening headache culminating in a seizure**.

**Framing the headache.** A lays out the [[Headache|headache]] schema — [[Secondary Headache|primary]] (tension, migraine, and the rare trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias) vs **secondary** (an identifiable cause) — and Berkowitz refines it: secondary headaches are *in the head* (brain, meninges, vessels, ventricles), *of the head/neck* (eyes, sinuses, ears, jaw, carotid dissection), or *systemic*. The tool for sorting them is **red flags** (tempo, character, positional features, and context — HIV, cancer, hypercoagulability, pregnancy), which he calls **sensitive but not specific**: most red-flagged headaches with a normal exam still prove primary, but you image anyway. Here there is no localization mystery — a worsening headache **with a seizure** is a **secondary** process *in the head*, headed for a CT. Over a **four-day** (acute) tempo culminating in seizure, A leads with infection (meningitis, encephalitis, abscess); Berkowitz adds the two that get forgotten — **[[Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis|venous sinus thrombosis]]** (progressive ICP → seizure ± venous hemorrhage) and acute **[[Hydrocephalus|hydrocephalus]]**.

**HPI and the framing trap.** The patient is a **33-year-old man** with only occasional migraine (this headache is different), whose four days of worsening headache and seizure followed a **street fight** — punched in the face, fell, hit his head — after which he felt feverish. He has back pain, progressive dizziness, blurred vision, photophobia, and vomiting; he is an ex-convict (imprisoned in 2017). Berkowitz seizes on the trauma as a teaching moment about **[[Framing Effect|framing bias]]**: humans crave cause and effect ("it started after my accident"), and the one-liner "developed symptoms after trauma" tempts you to draw a line straight to a **[[Subdural Hematoma|subdural hematoma]]**. But the fight may be a **red herring** (independent meningitis) or related only *by several steps* — a skull fracture seeding bacteria into the meninges, a post-traumatic **[[CSF Leak|CSF leak]]** causing recurrent meningitis (his own case), or **post-traumatic [[Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis|venous sinus thrombosis]]**. Strip the trauma out and "young person with headache, seizure, fever, photophobia, vomiting" is a **meningitis** vignette. He adds the [[Subdural Hematoma|epidural/subdural]] "lucid interval" caveat (a step-one rule that real life breaks).

**The exam.** T 38.2 °C, HR 103; in pain with eyes closed (photophobia), **neck stiffness**, and **lateral tongue lacerations** (post-ictal). GCS 14, **awake, alert, oriented**. Pupils: right 2 mm reactive, **left 1 mm sluggish** (anisocoria). **Binocular diplopia with bilateral abduction weakness** (bilateral **[[False Localizing Sign|sixth-nerve palsies]]**), subtle left facial weakness, **left-sided limb weakness (3/5)**, a wide-based gait, and **bilateral optic disc swelling** ([[Papilledema|papilledema]]). Berkowitz reads the package: **raised [[Intracranial Pressure|ICP]]** (papilledema + false-localizing bilateral CN VI — the nerve's long course over the clivus and through the cavernous sinus tethered by pressure), left weakness implying a **right-sided lesion**, and a small sluggish left pupil he can't fully explain (a traumatic **[[Carotid Dissection|carotid dissection]]** with [[Horner Syndrome|Horner]]?). He teaches the pupil rules — uncal herniation compresses CN III to give a *dilated* pupil (with Ropper's autopsy caveat of contralateral/bilateral dilation), while a small pupil suggests the oculosympathetic pathway — and names the crucial **dissociation**: a focal deficit and raised ICP in an **awake, oriented** patient argues *against* a large herniating mass or diffuse edema (they'd depress consciousness) and *for* ICP from **another source**.

**Anchoring on venous, and the data.** Berkowitz openly **anchors on [[Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis|venous sinus thrombosis]]** — a focal lesion causing the weakness, with the ICP driven by venous outflow obstruction. Labs show a **neutrophilic leukocytosis (WBC 21K, 88% neutrophils)** with otherwise normal chemistries, coags, tox, and serologies; a CXR (for possible TB, given incarceration) and spine film are normal. The patient **deteriorates** (agitation, a second seizure), is too unstable for MRI, and is started on empiric **vancomycin/ceftriaxone/Keppra**. The **non-contrast CT** shows edema and a **hyperdensity over the right transverse sinus** — the dense-sinus (**[[Cord Sign|cord]]**) sign Berkowitz had predicted — and **heparin** is started. He teaches the venous read: ask "**[[Could This Be Venous|could this be venous?]]**" with every hemorrhage/stroke (Steve Feske's pearl); suspect it when a bleed sits **near the cortical surface/vertex or is bilateral**, or when a **small hemorrhage carries disproportionate edema**; and use **CT/MR venography** or the **post-contrast sequences** already in hand. The **MRV** confirms **extensive CVST** — right transverse, sigmoid, and straight sinuses plus the superior sagittal sinus and torcula — with a **right thalamic infarct**, vasogenic edema, and **right mastoiditis**.

**Treatment and resolution.** He is **anticoagulated despite the infarct** — because the bleed/infarct is *caused by* venous obstruction, so relieving it is the treatment (trials support anticoagulation even with hemorrhage; hemicraniectomy or endovascular thrombectomy for ICP crises). Improvement is slower than expected, and with the **mastoiditis**, persistent fever, and leukocytosis, ID switches to **linezolid + moxifloxacin** for an **[[Otogenic CVST|otogenic/septic CVST]]**, after which he improves. An exhaustive young-CVST work-up (hypercoagulable, autoimmune — ANA, dsDNA, anticardiolipin, β2-glycoprotein, complement — echocardiogram, CT abdomen/pelvis) is unrevealing; he is discharged on **apixaban**, doing well. Meningitis was covered empirically but judged unlikely (no meningeal enhancement on a late MRI; LP unsafe with fever/mastoiditis). Final diagnosis: **extensive cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, likely otogenic from post-traumatic mastoiditis**. Berkowitz closes on the hardest-to-see cousin — **[[Cortical Vein Thrombosis|cortical vein thrombosis]]** (convexal SAH, one swollen gyrus, a worm-like SWI lesion, sometimes needing catheter angiography).

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **The four-compartment [[Secondary Headache|secondary-headache]] schema.** Working headache + seizure through brain / meninges / **vessels** / ventricles is what surfaced [[Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis|CVST]] and [[Hydrocephalus|hydrocephalus]] alongside the reflexive meningitis/encephalitis — the same tempo-plus-compartment engine as [[CPS 269 - Headache and Leptomeningeal Lymphomatosis|CPS 269]].
- **[[Framing Effect|Framing bias]], named and resisted.** The trauma one-liner pulls toward a subdural; Berkowitz explicitly strips it out to reveal the meningitis/CVST vignette underneath, then reconnects it *by steps* (post-traumatic mastoiditis → septic CVST) — a model of not being yoked to the presenting story.
- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring]], named by the teacher.** He announces his own anchor on venous thrombosis and keeps testing it against the data (the awake-despite-ICP dissociation, the cord sign) rather than clinging silently.
- **The awake-despite-raised-ICP dissociation.** Reasoning that a herniating mass or diffuse edema would depress consciousness — but this patient is oriented — is what redirected the ICP source from "big mass" to "venous outflow obstruction."
- **[[Test Interpretation|Test interpretation]] as the crux.** The [[Cord Sign|cord/dense-sinus sign]], the venous-hemorrhage clues (cortical-surface location, small bleed with disproportionate edema), the trick of reading post-contrast sequences for sinus filling, and the counterintuitive rule to **anticoagulate through hemorrhage** — each finding weighted for what it proves and what it demands.
- **"[[Could This Be Venous|Could this be venous?]]" as a standing question.** Feske's habit of asking it with every stroke/hemorrhage is the completeness heuristic that keeps a rare, missable, treatable diagnosis on the board.

## Teaching Discussion

- Separate primary from secondary headache and use red flags (tempo, character, positional features, context) as sensitive-but-not-specific triggers to image; a headache accompanied by a seizure is secondary until proven otherwise and warrants prompt imaging.
- Work a worsening headache-with-seizure across all four intracranial compartments — brain, meninges, vessels, ventricles — so that cerebral venous sinus thrombosis and acute hydrocephalus are not forgotten behind meningitis, encephalitis, and abscess; and recall that a tumor headache does not meaningfully worsen over four days.
- Guard against framing bias when a salient event (trauma) precedes an illness: the event may be a red herring or related only by several steps (skull fracture seeding meningitis, a CSF leak, or post-traumatic mastoiditis causing septic CVST), so keep the differential broad rather than yoking the diagnosis to the story.
- Read papilledema plus bilateral sixth-nerve palsies as raised intracranial pressure with a false-localizing sign, and treat an awake, oriented patient with focal signs and raised ICP as a dissociation that argues against a large herniating mass and toward an alternative ICP source such as venous outflow obstruction.
- Ask "could this be venous?" with every intracerebral hemorrhage or stroke; look for the cord/dense-sinus sign, a cortical-surface or bilateral hemorrhage, or a small hemorrhage with disproportionate edema, and confirm with CT/MR venography or the post-contrast sequences already obtained.
- Anticoagulate cerebral venous sinus thrombosis even in the presence of intracerebral hemorrhage, because the bleed results from venous outflow obstruction; escalate to hemicraniectomy or endovascular thrombectomy for an intracranial-pressure crisis, and treat the underlying source (targeted antibiotics for an otogenic/septic CVST from mastoiditis).
- Keep cortical vein thrombosis in mind as the harder-to-see relative, presenting with convexal subarachnoid hemorrhage, a single swollen gyrus, or a worm-like susceptibility lesion, sometimes requiring catheter angiography to diagnose.
