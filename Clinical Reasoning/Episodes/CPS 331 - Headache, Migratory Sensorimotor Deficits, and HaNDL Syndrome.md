## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A Neurology VMR built around a **21-year-old woman** with three weeks of severe throbbing headaches and **migratory, transient sensorimotor deficits** — left leg, then right leg, then right arm and face, with an episode of confusion — that repeatedly resolve and recur, confounding a straightforward primary-vs-secondary **[[Headache|headache]]** framework built on Berkowitz's **[[Anatomical Approach|anatomical]]** three-bucket **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|schema]]** (of the head and neck / in the head and neck / systemic). Discussants weigh a prior mild **[[Illness Script|migraine illness script]]** against an expanding secondary differential driven by red-flag features — a change in headache pattern, migrating focal deficits, and eventual confusion. Two negative medications matter: no oral contraceptives (a hypercoagulability/venous-thrombosis risk factor) and no retinoid/vitamin-A exposure (a **[[Pseudotumor Cerebri|pseudotumor cerebri]]** trigger). Two normal head CTs do not settle things, since CT can miss the meninges, small vessels, and CSF-based disease; **[[Reversible Cerebral Vasoconstriction Syndrome|RCVS]]** and an **[[ADEM|ADEM/MOGAD]]**-type aggressive demyelinating process are both raised and eventually set aside. The exam clinches localization to **[[Elevated Intracranial Pressure|elevated intracranial pressure]]**: bilateral **[[Papilledema|papilledema]]** plus bilateral **[[Cranial Nerve VI Palsy|CN VI palsies]]** (a classic **[[False Localizing Sign|false localizing sign]]**, since the abducens nerve's long intracranial course makes it vulnerable to stretch from raised pressure), alongside a genuinely focal right arm/face deficit. Brain MRI with contrast plus MRA/MRV is entirely normal, ruling out mass, venous sinus thrombosis, and vasculitis/RCVS; EEG shows nonspecific slowing without epileptiform activity. The answer emerges from the CSF: an opening pressure of 40 cm H₂O with a lymphocytic pleocytosis (310 cells, 95% lymphocytes), normal protein and glucose, negative oligoclonal bands, and a fully negative infectious PCR panel — the syndrome of **[[HaNDL Syndrome|HaNDL]]** ("headache and neurological deficits with cerebrospinal fluid lymphocytosis"), part of a small family of rare immune-mediated brain/eye/ear conditions alongside **[[Susac Syndrome|Susac syndrome]]** (eye) and **[[Cogan Syndrome|Cogan syndrome]]** (ear/vestibular), with HaNDL the most benign. The diagnosis is confirmed retrospectively: the patient is treated empirically while cultures/PCR return, and a repeat lumbar puncture three months later shows the opening pressure and cell count have normalized on their own.

## Pearls

- **A three-bucket headache schema organizes the secondary-headache differential**: of the head and neck (eye, ear, sinus, dental, jaw, cervical spine disease), in the head and neck (brain, meninges, vessels, ventricular system), and systemic (infection, fever, dehydration, vaccine reaction). Apply a red-flag review of systems — pattern change, positional or Valsalva-triggered headache, worst-of-life onset, patient-specific risk factors (cancer, HIV, age) — to decide how hard to chase a secondary cause.
- **Elevated intracranial pressure has its own exam signature.** **[[Papilledema]]** plus a false-localizing bilateral (or unilateral) **[[Cranial Nerve VI Palsy|CN VI palsy]]** — the abducens' long subarachnoid course makes it especially vulnerable to stretch from raised pressure — together with a headache that is worse lying flat, worse at night, and worse with coughing or Valsalva.
- **"Positive symptoms suggest migraine or seizure; negative symptoms suggest stroke or TIA" is a useful but imperfect rule.** Spreading visual aura or paresthesias favor migraine; loss of vision, sensation, or strength favors ischemia. Exceptions exist — hemiplegic migraine, a stroke that triggers a secondary migraine — so a change in an established migraine's pattern is itself a red flag, not reassurance.
- **[[Reversible Cerebral Vasoconstriction Syndrome|RCVS]] causes convexal or sulcal subarachnoid hemorrhage, not the classic cisternal "star" pattern of an aneurysmal bleed.** It is triggered by SSRIs, cannabis, other vasoactive substances, or even excess licorice; it is far more common than primary CNS vasculitis, and steroids — sometimes given when vessel beading is mistaken for vasculitis — can make it worse.
- **When brain MRI is normal despite a real clinical picture, look to the CSF.** **[[HaNDL Syndrome|HaNDL syndrome]]** — headache with transient focal deficits, sometimes an elevated opening pressure, and a lymphocytic pleocytosis with a negative infectious/autoimmune workup — is a self-limited diagnosis of exclusion that resolves without specific treatment.
- **Medication reconciliation is diagnostic data, not paperwork.** No oral contraceptives lowers (without eliminating) suspicion for a hypercoagulable venous sinus thrombosis; no retinoid/vitamin-A exposure removes one drug-induced cause of pseudotumor cerebri — both relevant negatives in a young woman with headache.

## Problem Representation

A 21-year-old woman with a several-year history of infrequent, milder migraines presents with three weeks of severe throbbing headaches accompanied by episodic confusion and migratory, transient sensorimotor deficits — successively affecting the left leg, the right leg, and then the right arm and face — each episode resolving before recurring days later. Exam ultimately reveals bilateral papilledema, bilateral abducens palsies, and a persistent right arm/face sensorimotor deficit, with two prior head CTs unremarkable. Brain MRI with contrast and MRA/MRV are normal, EEG shows nonspecific slowing without epileptiform activity, and infectious and autoimmune serologies are negative, but CSF reveals a markedly elevated opening pressure (40 cm H₂O) with a lymphocytic pleocytosis (310 cells, 95% lymphocytes) and a negative infectious PCR panel — a presentation and CSF profile that, together with spontaneous resolution over three months, is diagnostic of HaNDL syndrome (headache and neurological deficits with cerebrospinal fluid lymphocytosis).

## Case Narrative

Hans opens with the chief complaint: a 21-year-old woman with throbbing headaches and loss of sensation and weakness in the left leg. Andrew frames the case with a primary-versus-secondary split and an **[[Anatomical Approach|anatomical]]** approach to secondary headache — working from outside the skull inward (ocular disease like glaucoma, bony disease, meningoencephalitis, and then intracranial CSF/vascular processes). Maria adds that primary headache should be a diagnosis of exclusion given the presence of red flags, and that the focal sensory/motor loss raises concern for something ischemic or demyelinating in a young woman, though she isn't yet sure whether the deficit is truly episodic or leaves a residual.

Berkowitz builds out the **[[Headache|headache]]** **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|schema]]** in full: primary headache disorders (tension, migraine, cluster, and the rarer trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias — SUNCT/SUNA, paroxysmal hemicrania) are common but under-recognized in morning report because of case-selection; secondary headaches are grouped into three buckets — of the head and neck (eye, ear, sinus, nose, dental, jaw, cervical spine), in the head and neck (brain, meninges, blood vessels, ventricles, cervical arteries), and systemic (infection, fever, dehydration, vaccine reaction). He walks through the red-flag review of systems for elevated intracranial pressure — headache worse lying flat, worse on waking, worse with cough/sneeze/Valsalva ("brain orthopnea," his term), plus blurred or double vision from papilledema or a stretched CN VI — and other features that raise concern: thunderclap onset, patient-specific risk factors (cancer, HIV, age with jaw claudication favoring giant cell arteritis, or a younger patient with recent weight gain favoring pseudotumor cerebri), and — critically — any change in the character, frequency, or severity of an established primary headache disorder. He also distinguishes migraine and seizure (typically "positive" phenomena — auras, spreading paresthesias) from stroke/TIA (typically "negative" phenomena — loss of function), while flagging exceptions such as hemiplegic migraine and stroke-triggered secondary migraine.

Hans then gives the full history: three weeks of throbbing headaches, sometimes with nausea and photophobia, and loss of sensation/weakness that moved from the left leg, to the right leg, to the right arm and face over three emergency-department visits. The first CT was unremarkable and symptoms resolved spontaneously; she was discharged on analgesics, returned two days later with right-leg weakness and was started on amitriptyline, then returned a third time ten days later with confusion, right-arm weakness, and sensory loss in the right arm and face. She has had infrequent, milder migraines since her teens, no family history of headache, and is on no medications — notably, not on oral contraceptives (relevant because of hypercoagulability and venous sinus thrombosis risk) and not on retinoids or other acne medications (relevant because vitamin-A excess can trigger pseudotumor cerebri, a fact Berkowitz calls out as more "neurotrivia" than everyday practice).

Maria synthesizes: this doesn't fit a clean primary headache picture given the systemic features, the scattered (not truly focal) deficits, and especially the confusion, which she flags with a "headache, fever, or confusion — two of three worries me" illness script pointing toward meningoencephalitis; she wants to know the patient's HIV status and any immunosuppression, wants cardiac imaging to look for an embolic source, and wants MRI with vascular imaging rather than another CT. Asked directly whether this could still be migraine, she says no — confusion isn't on her migraine illness script, though Berkowitz notes that confusional migraine is a real, described entity. He observes that three flares in three weeks is atypical even for an aggressive presentation of relapsing demyelinating disease, and works through **[[Reversible Cerebral Vasoconstriction Syndrome|RCVS]]** — introduced by Andrew from a case he had previously presented — as vasospasm of the cerebral arteries, triggered by SSRIs, cannabis, cough suppressants, or even excess licorice tea, visible on angiography as vessel "beading," and notable for causing convexal or sulcal (not the classic cisternal "star") subarachnoid hemorrhage; steroids, sometimes given when the beading is mistaken for vasculitis, can worsen it, and RCVS is far more common than primary CNS vasculitis. The headache's throbbing (not classic thunderclap) character argues somewhat against RCVS, leading Berkowitz to introduce **secondary migraine** — a migraine-like headache caused by an underlying lesion rather than a primary disorder.

Considering **[[Illness Script|multiple sclerosis]]**, Berkowitz and the discussants set it aside: MS lesions are typically small and don't cause mass effect or headache, and three relapses in three weeks is far faster than even an aggressive first MS presentation. This opens the door to demyelinating disease's more acute "cousin," **[[ADEM|acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM)]]** — the CNS analogue of Guillain-Barré syndrome, often post-infectious or post-vaccine, with large, fluffy, enhancing lesions treated with steroids, and increasingly linked to the **[[ADEM|MOG antibody (MOGAD)]]**.

Hans then reveals the exam: normal vitals; a patient initially confused but, once settled, fully oriented to person, time, place, and situation; all cranial nerves intact except for bilateral CN VI (abducens) palsies with normal visual acuity; right arm strength 2/5 with sensory loss, left arm 5/5 with normal sensation, both legs 5/5 with no sensory deficit reported; reduced facial sensation; no neck stiffness or cerebellar signs; a normal gait; and bilateral papilledema on fundoscopy, with an otherwise unremarkable systemic exam. Andrew and Maria both key in on the papilledema and bilateral CN VI findings. Berkowitz assembles the synthesis: bilateral abducens palsy is a classic **[[False Localizing Sign|false localizing sign]]** of raised intracranial pressure, because the nerve's long, tortuous intracranial course makes it vulnerable to stretch regardless of where the pressure is coming from; together with papilledema, this establishes **[[Elevated Intracranial Pressure|elevated intracranial pressure]]**. He frames the skull as a rigid, unforgiving container of roughly 80% brain, 10% CSF, and 10% blood (mostly venous) — small increases in any one component can move pressure sharply up the steep part of the pressure-volume curve. The puzzle he leaves open: a lesion big enough to raise ICP should do more than cause isolated arm/face findings, while a lesion small enough to cause only arm/face findings shouldn't raise ICP — pointing toward either a diffuse process (elevated pressure from a non-focal cause, like impaired CSF drainage) with a coincidental or superimposed focal event, or a multifocal process such as ADEM.

Hans supplies the labs and imaging: complete blood count and chemistries unremarkable, beta-hCG negative, serologies for Borrelia, toxoplasmosis, syphilis, HIV, Coxiella burnetii, and Bartonella all negative, and ANA, ANCA, and rheumatoid factor negative. Brain MRI with contrast plus MRA and MRV (venogram) are entirely normal — no lesions, normal vessels — and EEG shows no epileptiform activity, only asynchronous frontotemporal delta slowing (more prominent on the left) with a slow background. Berkowitz uses the normal MRI as a teaching moment about predicting a test's result before ordering it, to calibrate one's own reasoning against the outcome, and introduces the diagnosis he'd been holding in reserve: **[[HaNDL Syndrome|HaNDL]]** ("headache and neurological deficits with cerebrospinal fluid lymphocytosis"), a rare, imaging-negative syndrome he recalls from a single striking case of his own — and situates it within a family of rare immune-mediated brain/eye/ear conditions that includes the more severe **[[Susac Syndrome|Susac syndrome]]** (retinal/cochlear) and **[[Cogan Syndrome|Cogan syndrome]]** (vestibular/ocular), with HaNDL the mildest and self-limited.

Hans reports the CSF: opening pressure 40 cm H₂O (normal 7–18), white blood cells 310/µL with 95% lymphocytes, normal protein (0.52) and glucose (3.5), negative oligoclonal bands, and a fully negative CSF PCR panel (enterovirus, mumps, HSV-1/2, VZV, echovirus, EBV, CMV, JC, BK, measles, HHV-6/7) with a morphologically normal cytospin. Maria says she would not feel comfortable relying on HaNDL given this opening pressure and cell count and would keep pushing an infectious workup; both discussants agree they would have started empiric antimicrobial coverage (vancomycin, ceftriaxone, consideration of ampicillin or acyclovir) while awaiting cultures and PCR — Berkowitz frames this as "nighttime thinking" (treat what could kill the patient overnight) followed by "daytime thinking" (reassess once more data and more eyes are available) — and notes the discomfort of continuing broad coverage once the patient is already improving. Hans confirms the diagnosis: this is HaNDL, with an elevated opening pressure as an occasional feature of the syndrome. The patient's headache and pressure improved over roughly ten days as an inpatient, and on repeat lumbar puncture three months later the opening pressure had fallen to about 20 cm H₂O, the white cell count to 7, and the protein to 0.16 — a spontaneous, untreated resolution that confirms the benign, self-limited diagnosis.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Availability Heuristic|Availability/base-rate distortion — "morning report bias."]]** Maria explicitly names her own bias toward assuming a secondary, serious cause because VMR cases are curated for teaching value — a reminder that the true population base rate of headache (overwhelmingly primary) is not the base rate of a morning-report case.
- **[[Framing Effect|Framing from the venue itself.]]** Discussants describe their "worry meter" as high largely because the case is presented on VMR at all, priming a secondary-headache interpretation before any red flag had been elicited from the history.
- **[[Illness Script|Illness script flexibility around migraine.]]** Rather than fixing on "definitely migraine" or "definitely not migraine," Berkowitz repeatedly re-tests his own migraine script against new data (confusion, papilledema, CSF findings) — modeling revision over premature closure in either direction.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Predict the result before you order the test.]]** Berkowitz's explicit habit of asking discussants what they expect a test to show before it returns is used here around the "surprisingly" normal brain MRI, calibrating reasoning and catching anchoring early.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Diagnostic humility with a diagnosis of exclusion.]]** Sitting with an abnormal, worrisome CSF profile and choosing empiric "nighttime thinking" treatment rather than premature closure onto either "definitely infection" or "definitely HaNDL" — and ultimately accepting a diagnosis with no confirmatory biomarker — echoes the humility theme built out in [[CPS 372 - Generalized Weakness and Acute Intermittent Porphyria|CPS 372]] and [[CPS 438 - Bilateral Leg Weakness and an Unresolved Immune-Mediated Neuropathy|CPS 438]].

## Teaching Discussion

- Organize secondary headache into three anatomical buckets — of the head and neck, in the head and neck, and systemic — before applying a red-flag review of systems (pattern change, positional or Valsalva-triggered pain, worst-of-life onset, patient-specific risk factors) to decide how hard to chase a secondary cause.
- Recognize elevated intracranial pressure at the bedside from papilledema and a false-localizing bilateral CN VI palsy — the nerve's long, stretch-vulnerable intracranial course explains why it is affected regardless of where the pressure originates — together with a headache pattern that is worse lying flat, at night, and with Valsalva.
- Use medication reconciliation as data: the absence of oral contraceptives lowers suspicion for a hypercoagulable venous sinus thrombosis, and the absence of retinoid/vitamin-A exposure removes a drug-induced trigger for pseudotumor cerebri.
- Treat "positive versus negative symptoms" (migraine/seizure versus stroke/TIA) as a useful but imperfect heuristic, and treat any change in an established primary headache disorder's pattern as a red flag rather than reassurance.
- Recognize reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome by its imaging signature — beaded, alternately narrowed and dilated vessels causing convexal or sulcal (not cisternal) subarachnoid hemorrhage — its common triggers (SSRIs, cannabis, other vasoactive substances), and the pitfall of steroids worsening it when beading is mistaken for vasculitis, which is far rarer.
- When a clinical picture strongly suggests intracranial pathology but brain MRI with vessel imaging is normal, shift attention to the CSF: HaNDL syndrome — headache with transient focal deficits, sometimes an elevated opening pressure, and a lymphocytic pleocytosis with a negative infectious workup — is confirmed by exclusion and by spontaneous resolution over weeks to months.
- Situate HaNDL within a small family of rare immune-mediated brain/eye/ear conditions — Susac syndrome (retinal and cochlear involvement, more severe) and Cogan syndrome (vestibular and ocular inflammation) — with HaNDL as the mildest, self-limited member.
