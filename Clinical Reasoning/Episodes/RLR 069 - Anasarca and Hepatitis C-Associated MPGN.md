## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A middle-aged man out of care presents with months of progressive **[[Anasarca|anasarca]]**, exertional dyspnea, spider angiomata, and pitting edema to the thighs and hands. Rabih builds a **three-prong anasarca schema**: (1) the common bilateral-edema causes (heart/liver/kidney) manifesting unusually — but *especially* **[[Nephrotic Syndrome|nephrotic syndrome]] and hypothyroidism** among them; (2) those two prioritized; (3) rare **[[Vascular Leak Syndrome|vascular]] and lymphatic leak** syndromes (watch the Hgb/albumin disconnect). The exam (no JVP, no jaundice/caput, normal BP) pushes past the base rate, and spider angiomata tempt a liver story. Labs: albumin **1.8**, UA **100+ protein with RBCs**, normal INR/bilirubin, platelets 165k, and CT read as **cirrhosis with portal hypertension**. Rabih refuses to close on cirrhosis — the **[[Law of Proportionality|hypoalbuminemia and anasarca are out of proportion to *mild* cirrhosis]]**, the albumin (the least specific synthetic marker) is perturbed while INR/bilirubin are normal, and platelets are >150 — so he keeps the **kidney** in play. The clincher is a [[Spot vs 24-Hour Urine Protein|24-hour urine of **12 g**]] (nephrotic range) with albumin-facilitated diuresis. Serologies reveal **new hepatitis C** (VL 4.4 million, plus isolated HBcAb and secondary iron overload); the [[Nephritic vs Nephrotic|mixed nephritic-nephrotic]] picture and low C4 point to **[[MPGN|Hep C–associated MPGN]]**, confirmed on biopsy (cryocrit 1%, no vasculitis). The lesson: the **kidney (the "third thing") was camouflaged by the liver (the "second thing")** — obey the law of proportionality and always get a **urine protein-to-creatinine ratio** for hypoalbuminemia.

## Pearls

- **Work [[Anasarca|anasarca]] with a three-prong schema.** (1) The common bilateral-lower-extremity-edema causes — **heart, liver, kidney** — manifesting *unusually* (gravity's effect altered by positioning); (2) among them, **prioritize [[Nephrotic Syndrome|nephrotic syndrome]] and hypothyroidism**, which cause whole-body edema more than isolated leg edema; (3) rare **[[Vascular Leak Syndrome|vascular leak]] and lymphatic leak** syndromes. ([[RLR 069 - Anasarca and Hepatitis C-Associated MPGN|RLR 069]])
- **A [[Vascular Leak Syndrome|vascular leak]] shows a Hgb/albumin *disconnect*.** Albumin leaks into the interstitium but red cells are too big, so the **Hgb runs high (hemoconcentration) while albumin runs low** — seen in sepsis (edematous yet euvolemic/hypovolemic), pancreatitis (high Hgb as a poor-prognostic sign), angioedema, and dengue (the commonest worldwide). Prime yourself to read both labs. ([[RLR 069 - Anasarca and Hepatitis C-Associated MPGN|RLR 069]])
- **Obey the [[Law of Proportionality|law of proportionality]]: don't let "cirrhosis" explain an out-of-proportion albumin.** When anasarca and profound hypoalbuminemia exceed what *mild* cirrhosis should cause — and the **albumin (least specific synthetic marker) is perturbed while INR and bilirubin are normal**, with platelets >150 — the liver is not the whole story; keep the **kidney** on the hook. ([[RLR 069 - Anasarca and Hepatitis C-Associated MPGN|RLR 069]])
- **For any hypoalbuminemia/anasarca, get a [[Spot vs 24-Hour Urine Protein|urine protein-to-creatinine ratio]] — and don't over-trust the spot.** The spot UPCR assumes ~1 g/day creatinine excretion; when that assumption fails, it misestimates. In high-pretest-probability nephrotic syndrome, the **gold standard** (here a 24-hour of **12 g**) is worth it — the easy clincher that keeps the kidney from sneaking by. ([[RLR 069 - Anasarca and Hepatitis C-Associated MPGN|RLR 069]])
- **Diuretic resistance in deep hypoalbuminemia can respond to [[Albumin-Facilitated Diuresis|albumin-facilitated diuresis]].** If loop diuretic isn't reaching the kidney, **IV albumin (≈25 g) chased with IV furosemide (20–40 mg), twice daily** can produce brisk diuresis (and improve a "prerenal" creatinine). ([[RLR 069 - Anasarca and Hepatitis C-Associated MPGN|RLR 069]])
- **A mixed [[Nephritic vs Nephrotic|nephritic-and-nephrotic]] pattern narrows sharply — and [[MPGN|MPGN]] is ~100% secondary.** Proteinuria *plus* hematuria points to lupus, IgA, or **membranoproliferative GN** — the latter classically from **[[Cryoglobulinemia|hepatitis C ± cryoglobulinemia]]** (low **C4**, positive **[[Rheumatoid Factor|rheumatoid factor]]** — and beware a missed **IgA-RF**). Unlike membranous/FSGS/minimal-change (70–80% primary), **MPGN is essentially always secondary**. ([[RLR 069 - Anasarca and Hepatitis C-Associated MPGN|RLR 069]])
- **Interpret the hepatitis serologies and iron carefully.** Isolated **HBcAb positive** (surface Ag and Ab negative) means prior exposure — get **HBV DNA** to tell waned-immunity from occult infection. A high **percent-transferrin-saturation with ferritin elevation** in chronic liver disease is usually **secondary iron overload**, not proven hemochromatosis; and **ANA/anti-smooth-muscle** are often falsely positive in hepatitis C (autoimmune hepatitis requires *excluding* viral disease). ([[RLR 069 - Anasarca and Hepatitis C-Associated MPGN|RLR 069]])

## Problem Representation

A middle-aged man, long out of medical care, presents with several months of progressive whole-body swelling (≈20-lb gain), exertional dyspnea without orthopnea or PND, spider angiomata on the chest, an anicteric exam without caput medusae, normal blood pressure, and pitting edema extending to the lateral thighs and hands. Labs show a normal hemoglobin and near-normal platelets (165k), creatinine 0.8, mildly elevated transaminases with normal alkaline phosphatase/bilirubin/INR, a pro-BNP of 56, albumin 1.8, and a urinalysis with 100+ protein and some red cells; CT shows a small cirrhotic liver with moderate ascites and mild portal hypertension. The profound hypoalbuminemia and anasarca are out of proportion to the mild cirrhosis, and a 24-hour urine reveals 12 g of protein (nephrotic range); serologies establish new hepatitis C (viral load 4.4 million, with isolated hepatitis B core antibody and secondary iron overload), and a renal biopsy shows membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (cryocrit 1%, without cryoglobulinemic vasculitis) — hepatitis C–associated MPGN driving a nephrotic syndrome camouflaged by concurrent mild cirrhosis.

## Case Narrative

Reza presents a service case, asking Rabih to define **[[Anasarca|anasarca]]** before approaching it. Rabih: whole-body, **symmetric** edema of arms and legs (± face, chest wall, abdomen, and viscera). His approach is practical — the same systemic causes as bilateral leg edema (**heart, liver, kidney**) are at play, but because those are gravity-dependent, anasarca requires either **altered positioning** (arms swell when not elevated) or one of the causes that *inherently* produces whole-body edema. So he builds a **three-prong schema**: (1) the common causes manifesting unusually; (2) among them, **prioritize [[Nephrotic Syndrome|nephrotic syndrome]] and hypothyroidism**; (3) the rare **[[Vascular Leak Syndrome|vascular leak]] and lymphatic leak** syndromes. He teaches the vascular-leak physiology — albumin leaks out while red cells stay in, giving a **high Hgb / low albumin disconnect** — with examples in sepsis, pancreatitis (high Hgb as a poor sign), angioedema, and dengue (commonest worldwide).

The history: months of swelling (~20 lb), exertional dyspnea, **no orthopnea/PND**. Exam: normal vitals, **anicteric**, no jaundice, **no caput medusae**, distended abdomen, a few **spider angiomata** on the chest, **pitting edema** to the lateral thighs, and swollen hands. Rabih reads the exam as deliberately excluding the liver's usual stigmata and notes that **normal blood pressure** argues somewhat against nephrotic syndrome (often hypertensive) and the **absent JVP** against heart failure — enough momentum to move past the base rate while still testing it. The spider angiomata, however, pull toward chronic liver disease/portal hypertension. He would send a **UA and urine protein-to-creatinine ratio off the bat** — not from interest in nephrotic syndrome per se, but to characterize the anticipated **hypoalbuminemia** without delay, and he primes himself on the **Hgb and albumin** for a vascular-leak disconnect.

The data arrive as on admitting day. **Hgb normal** (no polycythemia — against vascular leak), **platelets 165k**, **creatinine 0.8**, mild AST/ALT elevation with **normal alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin, and INR**, **pro-BNP 56** (against heart failure), UA with **100+ protein and ~24 RBC / 10 WBC**, and **albumin 1.8**. CT reads **cirrhosis with moderate ascites and mild portal hypertension**. Reza notes the radiologist was confident, and that the patient has tattoos and methamphetamine on tox screen.

Rabih refuses to close on cirrhosis, invoking the **[[Law of Proportionality|law of proportionality]]**: the **anasarca and albumin of 1.8 are out of proportion to *mild* cirrhosis**; the **albumin — the least specific synthetic marker — is the perturbed one while INR and bilirubin are normal**; and **platelets >150** don't fit portal hypertension severe enough to swell all four limbs. He recites the cirrhosis construct (hepatic dysfunction + chronic hyperestrogenism/spider angioma + portal hypertension, *plus* an underlying cause) and flags that the missing risk factor and the disproportion make him keep the **kidney** on the hook — adopting **portal hypertension** loosely as a frame while staying open to *non-cirrhotic* portal hypertension or even a pure vascular-leak process. Reza's resident Jen had independently named the same disconnect.

The workup unfolds. Hepatitis serologies: **Hep A total positive** (immune), **isolated Hep B core antibody** (surface Ag and Ab negative → prior exposure, needs **HBV DNA**), and **Hep C antibody positive with a viral load of 4.4 million** — **new hepatitis C**. An iron panel shows **95% transferrin saturation, ferritin 800** — iron overload, but likely **secondary** to liver disease rather than proven hemochromatosis. ANA and AMA negative; **anti-smooth-muscle 1:40** — an autoimmune-hepatitis marker that is **often falsely positive in hepatitis C** (and AIH requires *excluding* viral disease). Then the renal data: **spot UPCR 3.27**, **24-hour urine 12 g** — nephrotic range. Rabih explains the **[[Spot vs 24-Hour Urine Protein|spot-vs-24-hour]] discrepancy** (the spot assumes ~1 g/day creatinine excretion, and misestimates when that fails) and endorses the gold standard here. IV furosemide fails and he's **oliguric**; a **cystatin-C** shows a GFR lower than creatinine predicted, and **[[Albumin-Facilitated Diuresis|IV albumin (25 g) chased with furosemide]]** produces brisk diuresis with an improving creatinine.

Now with the triad — **nephrotic-range proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, edema** — Rabih diagnoses **[[Nephrotic Syndrome|nephrotic syndrome]]** and pivots the workup to the kidney. Heavy proteinuria with a **relatively preserved GFR** favors **membranous or amyloid**; **hepatitis B/C** are potent triggers of **membranous** (plus consider **HIV**-associated disease). He lists the four common histologies (membranous, FSGS, minimal change, amyloid), the secondary workup (viral serologies — add **HIV, syphilis**; autoimmune/lupus; cancer; drugs; and, with meth/injection risk, **amyloid**), the **PLA2R** biomarker for *primary* membranous, and that **biopsy is the gold standard**. Crucially, the **RBCs** make it a **[[Nephritic vs Nephrotic|mixed nephritic-nephrotic]]** pattern — narrowing to **lupus, IgA, or MPGN from hepatitis C ± [[Cryoglobulinemia|cryoglobulinemia]]** — so a **[[Rheumatoid Factor|rheumatoid factor]]** (usually positive in HCV cryo, though an **IgA-RF** can be missed) and **complement** (a disproportionately low **C4** suggesting cryo, tempered because the cirrhotic liver lowers complement) become key. HIV and RPR are negative; C4 is low-ish.

The **biopsy shows MPGN**; cryos are positive with a **cryocrit of 1%** but **no cryoglobulinemic vasculitis** (and a polyclonal gammopathy). A **T-spot is positive** (latent TB, relevant before immunosuppression). The team lands on **hepatitis C driving both the kidney (MPGN) and the liver (mild cirrhosis)**, with plans for antiviral therapy, a liver biopsy, and latent-TB treatment; Rabih notes **MPGN is ~100% secondary** (the inverse of the 70–80%-primary membranous/FSGS/minimal-change), so hepatitis C ties it together. In the [[Cognitive Autopsy|autopsy]], Reza names three misses: they **didn't frame it as anasarca** (called it leg edema); they nearly **reflexively closed on cirrhosis** for the low albumin (**[[Diagnostic Momentum|momentum]]**); and they had to remember the **[[Law of Proportionality|law of proportionality]]**. Both marvel that **a common disease (nephrotic syndrome) was hidden by another common disease (cirrhosis)** — "the third thing camouflaged by the second" — and that nephrotic syndrome, usually diagnosed by *exclusion* of heart/liver, is hard to see when the liver *also* explains the albumin. Rabih even wonders whether the **normotensive** nephrotic state was masked by cirrhotic/portal-hypertensive vasodilation (analogous to a mixed hypo-/hyper-osmolar state producing "iso-osmolar" hyponatremia). The practical takeaway: **hypoalbuminemia mandates a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio** — the easy test that keeps the kidney from sneaking by.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Law of Proportionality|Proportionality as the key]].** The whole case hinges on refusing to let *mild* cirrhosis explain *profound* hypoalbuminemia and anasarca — and on noticing that the albumin, not INR/bilirubin, is perturbed and that platelets are preserved. The disproportion kept the kidney alive.
- **[[Diagnostic Momentum|Momentum/anchoring on cirrhosis]].** "Low albumin + edema + a cirrhosis read = done" is the reflex the team caught themselves in; the antidote was the proportionality check and a mandatory urine protein quantification.
- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|The three-prong anasarca schema]].** Common-causes-unusually → nephrotic/hypothyroid → vascular/lymphatic leak organizes a daunting sign, and the vascular-leak Hgb/albumin disconnect is a crisp discriminator.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Interpreting each test]].** Albumin as the least specific synthetic marker; spot vs 24-hour protein; isolated HBcAb needing HBV DNA; secondary vs primary iron overload; false-positive autoimmune serologies in HCV; low C4 and RF (and IgA-RF) for cryo; cystatin-C — every result is read, not tallied.
- **[[Base Rate|Common hiding common]].** The memorable lesson: a common disease (nephrotic syndrome) camouflaged by another common disease (cirrhosis), both from one cause (hepatitis C) — and MPGN being essentially always secondary made the unifier findable.
- **A unifying diagnosis.** Hepatitis C explains the kidney (MPGN), the liver (cirrhosis), and the iron (secondary overload) — parsimony rewarded once the kidney was uncovered.

## Teaching Discussion

- Approach anasarca with a three-prong schema: common bilateral-edema causes manifesting unusually, then prioritized nephrotic syndrome and hypothyroidism, then rare vascular and lymphatic leak syndromes.
- Recognize vascular leak by a high-hemoglobin/low-albumin disconnect (sepsis, pancreatitis, dengue, angioedema).
- Apply the law of proportionality: profound hypoalbuminemia and anasarca out of proportion to mild cirrhosis — with a perturbed albumin but normal INR/bilirubin and preserved platelets — should keep the kidney in play.
- Quantify proteinuria for any hypoalbuminemia/anasarca with a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio, and don't over-trust the spot when creatinine excretion may deviate from ~1 g/day.
- Treat diuretic resistance in deep hypoalbuminemia with albumin-facilitated diuresis (IV albumin chased with a loop diuretic).
- Use a mixed nephritic-nephrotic pattern to narrow to lupus, IgA, or MPGN, and remember MPGN is essentially always secondary — classically hepatitis C ± cryoglobulinemia (low C4, positive RF, and a possible IgA-RF).
- Interpret hepatitis serologies and iron studies carefully: isolated HBcAb needs HBV DNA, transferrin-saturation/ferritin elevation in liver disease is usually secondary, and ANA/anti-smooth-muscle are often falsely positive in hepatitis C.
- Watch for one common disease camouflaging another; a unifying cause (hepatitis C here) can drive both liver and kidney disease.
