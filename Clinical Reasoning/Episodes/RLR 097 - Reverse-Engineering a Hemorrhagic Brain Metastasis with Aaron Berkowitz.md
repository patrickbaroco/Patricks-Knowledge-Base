## Pearls

- Reasoning through neuroimaging sequence-by-sequence (FLAIR → T1 post-contrast → GRE/susceptibility → DWI/ADC), rather than jumping straight to the "answer" sequence, builds a systematic approach that generalizes across lesion types, not just the one in front of you.
- A ring-enhancing lesion at the gray-white junction with associated blood products on GRE is a classic combination pointing toward hemorrhagic metastasis rather than a primary CNS process or abscess.
- The MRCT mnemonic (Melanoma, Renal cell carcinoma, Choriocarcinoma, Thyroid) organizes the short differential for tumors that classically cause hemorrhagic brain metastases.
- Vasogenic edema disproportionate to the size of a small lesion is itself a clue toward a metastatic or aggressive process rather than a more indolent one.
- When multiple abnormal findings turn up on staging imaging (a renal mass, a pleural effusion, cavitary lung lesions), the reflex should not be to force them into a single unifying diagnosis — "cause versus consequence" reasoning asks whether each finding is a direct manifestation of the same disease, a downstream consequence of it, or an entirely separate, coincidental process.
- Multiple true diagnoses can and do coexist in the same patient (Hickam's dictum in action): this patient had known metastatic RCC, a separate chronic pulmonary infection already responding to treatment, and a prior PE, each requiring its own management decision rather than a single explanation.
- Anticoagulation decisions in a patient with a new intracranial lesion require weighing the competing risks (hemorrhage into a met versus recurrent thromboembolism) and can be resolved with a temporary bridge (e.g., to a shorter-acting agent) while further data (like interval imaging showing infection improvement) clarifies the safety of resuming standard anticoagulation.
- Holding a targeted therapy (TKI) for a drug toxicity (nephrotic-range proteinuria) is not a free action — it can allow the underlying malignancy to progress, illustrated here by new metastatic disease appearing after the TKI was held.

## Problem Representation

A patient in their 70s with a hemorrhagic left frontal lobe mass on neuroimaging, subsequently found on PET-CT to have known metastatic renal cell carcinoma, a separate improving pulmonary infection, and a prior pulmonary embolism.

## Case Narrative

This episode used a reverse-engineering format: rather than presenting a history first, Reza organized an exercise in which imaging was shown to guest host Aaron Berkowitz without clinical context, and Aaron reasoned aloud through the images to build up a differential before any history was revealed.

The starting point was a CT head showing a left frontal lobe hypodensity in a patient in their 70s. Aaron walked through the systematic approach to characterizing an intracranial lesion using MRI, taking each sequence in turn. On FLAIR, the lesion was associated with a disproportionately large amount of surrounding vasogenic edema relative to its size — itself a clue that the lesion behaves aggressively rather than indolently. On T1 post-contrast, the lesion enhanced in a ring pattern, and was notably small — a tiny lesion producing an outsized amount of surrounding edema. On GRE (susceptibility-weighted) sequences, there was evidence of blood products within the lesion, indicating a hemorrhagic component. DWI/ADC sequences were used to help distinguish this from an abscess (which classically shows restricted diffusion) versus a tumor.

Putting these features together — a small, ring-enhancing lesion at the gray-white junction, with hemorrhage and disproportionate vasogenic edema — Aaron favored a hemorrhagic metastasis over a primary brain tumor or infectious process, and organized the differential for tumors classically associated with hemorrhagic brain metastases using the mnemonic MRCT: Melanoma, Renal cell carcinoma, Choriocarcinoma, and Thyroid carcinoma.

With the neuroimaging differential established, Reza then revealed a PET-CT that had been obtained as part of staging. The PET-CT showed several additional abnormal findings simultaneously: a large, FDG-avid mass in the left kidney; an FDG-avid right-sided pleural effusion; and cavitary lesions in the lungs.

At this point Rabih joined the discussion and introduced a "cause versus consequence" framework for approaching the multiplicity of findings: rather than assuming every abnormal finding must be explained by one single unifying disease process, each finding should be evaluated on its own for whether it is a direct manifestation of the same underlying process, a downstream consequence of another finding, or an entirely separate, coincidental process. Applying this framework, the renal mass and the hemorrhagic brain lesion were reasoned to be directly linked (renal cell carcinoma with brain metastasis), while the pleural effusion and cavitary lung lesions were reasoned to be more consistent with a separate, non-malignant process given their imaging characteristics and the overall clinical trajectory.

The full clinical picture was then revealed: the patient had known metastatic renal cell carcinoma. A tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) targeting the RCC had been held because the patient developed nephrotic-range proteinuria, a recognized toxicity of this drug class — and in the interval off therapy, the cancer had progressed, producing the new hemorrhagic brain metastasis. Separately, the patient had a roughly six-month-old pulmonary and pleural infection with Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) and Aspergillus, which was already being treated and was improving — explaining the cavitary lung lesions and pleural effusion as a distinct, coincidental process rather than a manifestation of the cancer. The patient also had a prior pulmonary embolism and was maintained on apixaban.

Management decisions were then worked through. The new hemorrhagic brain metastasis raised a competing-risk question about anticoagulation: continuing apixaban risked worsening intracranial hemorrhage, while stopping it risked recurrent thromboembolism given the prior PE. The resolution was a temporary bridge to enoxaparin (a shorter-acting, more easily reversible agent), followed by a return to apixaban once interval imaging confirmed the pulmonary infection was improving — supporting the assessment that the metastatic disease process, not an unrelated acute event, explained the current findings, without the need for further biopsy. The patient was started on dexamethasone (with symptomatic improvement), given a brief 48-hour course of antiseizure medication, and received three fractions of radiation therapy. They were discharged on dexamethasone, apixaban, and the TKI, with a poor overall prognosis and palliative care involvement flagged.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Hemorrhagic brain metastasis illness script (MRCT): small, often multiple, ring-enhancing lesions at the gray-white junction, disproportionate vasogenic edema, blood products on susceptibility imaging, in a patient with a known or occult primary malignancy from melanoma, renal cell carcinoma, choriocarcinoma, or thyroid carcinoma.

The case is a deliberate teaching exercise against premature unification — the instinct to explain a renal mass, a brain lesion, a pleural effusion, and cavitary lung lesions with one diagnosis is strong (a single grand unifying theory feels more satisfying), but "cause versus consequence" reasoning and ultimately the full history revealed genuine Hickam's dictum: multiple true, separately-caused findings coexisting in one patient.

## Teaching Discussion

The core methodological teaching point is the discipline of reasoning through neuroimaging sequence by sequence rather than pattern-matching to a final answer. Each sequence answers a different physiological question — FLAIR for edema and chronicity, T1 post-contrast for blood-brain-barrier breakdown and enhancement pattern, GRE/susceptibility for blood products or calcification, DWI/ADC for restricted diffusion (favoring abscess or acute infarct over tumor) — and building the differential from these individual answers, rather than skipping to gestalt pattern recognition, is what let Aaron systematically arrive at "hemorrhagic metastasis" before any clinical history was available.

The second major teaching point is the "cause versus consequence" framework for handling multiple simultaneous abnormal findings on staging or survey imaging: for each finding, ask whether it is caused by the same primary process, whether it is a downstream consequence of that process or of treatment, or whether it is an independent, coincidental process that happens to be occurring in the same patient at the same time. This prevents both premature unification (forcing unrelated findings into one diagnosis) and missed diagnoses (dismissing a real second process because it doesn't fit the leading narrative).

A secondary clinical teaching point involves the tension in holding a TKI for a drug toxicity like nephrotic-range proteinuria — the decision is not free, and in this patient, holding therapy allowed disease progression to a new brain metastasis, illustrating the ongoing risk-benefit calculation inherent in oncologic drug toxicity management.
