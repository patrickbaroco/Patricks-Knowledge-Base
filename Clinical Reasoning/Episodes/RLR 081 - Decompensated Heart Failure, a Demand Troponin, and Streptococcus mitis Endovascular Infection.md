## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 72-year-old man with ischemic HFrEF, ESRD on hemodialysis, an ICD, and atrial fibrillation presents with two weeks of respiratory symptoms and intermittent dark stools, found to have decompensated heart failure attributed to missed dialysis sessions. A rising-then-falling "demand" troponin and an AST-predominant transaminitis with an unexplained macrocytosis are worked through in detail, but the pivotal decision — ordering blood cultures despite a normal white count — reveals [[Streptococcus mitis]] bacteremia, probably from GI mucosal translocation given a prior gastrectomy and CT-demonstrated duodenitis, raising urgent concern for [[Endovascular Infection|endovascular infection]] of his cardiac hardware.

## Pearls

- When a patient presents with two competing chief concerns (here, chest symptoms and dark stools), let the physical exam adjudicate where to invest first: marked resting tachypnea points toward a thoracic/cardiac process, while dyspnea without tachypnea points toward anemia as the driving lead.
- In a hemodialysis patient with decompensated heart failure, missed or shortened dialysis sessions are the most common explanation, but this is a diagnosis of exclusion — first rule out the three more morbid, emergent causes (an endocardial process such as endocarditis or acute valve rupture, an acute myocardial process, and a pericardial effusion) with a murmur check, EKG, and point-of-care ultrasound.
- Once emergent causes are excluded, organize the remaining explanation around the three A's — arrhythmia, acute kidney injury, and afterload — all of which can independently or jointly precipitate decompensated heart failure in a dialysis-dependent patient.
- A troponin that is actively rising and falling (rather than static) signals an evolving vascular process — either plaque rupture or increased demand on vulnerable vessels — and should be trended serially alongside EKGs rather than interpreted as a single value; but systemic illness with a catecholamine surge (e.g., sepsis from a respiratory virus) can itself precipitate plaque rupture, so "demand" should not be assumed just because a clear precipitant for demand (hypertension, volume overload) is present.
- On a blood culture gram stain, gram-positive cocci in chains or pairs (streptococcus or enterococcus) should essentially never be dismissed as a contaminant, unlike gram-positive cocci in clusters (coagulase-negative staphylococcus, the most common true contaminant) — treat it as real, especially in a patient with indwelling cardiac hardware.
- Any patient with endovascular hardware (an ICD, a tunneled dialysis catheter) and a nonspecific systemic complaint deserves a very low threshold for blood cultures; do not settle for a benign explanation (a URI, missed dialysis) without first excluding endovascular infection, since organisms like coagulase-negative staphylococcus and enterococcus can cause minimally symptomatic bacteremia in this population.

## Problem Representation

A 72-year-old man with ischemic heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, end-stage renal disease on hemodialysis via a tunneled catheter, an ICD, and atrial fibrillation on apixaban presents with two weeks of cough, congestion, and dyspnea plus intermittent dark stools, found to have decompensated heart failure attributed in part to reduced dialysis adherence, a rising-then-falling demand-pattern troponin, and an AST-predominant transaminitis with unexplained macrocytosis; blood cultures — sent despite a normal white count, given his burden of indwelling hardware — reveal Streptococcus mitis bacteremia, probably from GI mucosal translocation given his prior gastrectomy and CT-demonstrated duodenitis, raising urgent concern for endovascular infection.

## Case Narrative

A 72-year-old man presents with two weeks of congestion, cough, and shortness of breath, and also reports an episode of black stool. With only this much information, the discussant reasons that the URI-like symptoms are initially low-yield on their own, but the shortness of breath implies deeper chest involvement, while the dark stools raise concern for a GI bleed — and that the patient's overall appearance (specifically, the presence or absence of marked resting tachypnea versus dyspnea without tachypnea) should determine whether to invest first in the chest or in anemia as the leading thread, while remaining open to both processes coexisting.

Past medical history: ischemic HFrEF (EF 30–35%) following four-vessel CABG a decade earlier; end-stage renal disease of unclear etiology on hemodialysis three times weekly via a tunneled catheter (with a left upper extremity AV fistula also present); an ICD placed for the low EF; and atrial fibrillation on apixaban. On exam he is hypoxemic (low 90s, improved with supplemental oxygen), tachypneic-appearing, tachycardic (115, regular), and hypertensive (systolic 160), with a distended JVP; he reports that although he attends every dialysis session, recent sessions have been shortened to about one hour instead of three because of feeling unwell. An S3 is difficult to appreciate; there are crackles without lower-extremity edema; digital rectal exam shows brown, not grossly melanic, stool.

The discussant frames decompensated heart failure in a dialysis-dependent patient as analogous to the common inpatient teaching that most decompensated heart failure reflects medication or dietary nonadherence — here, "dialysis is the medication," and its reported disruption is a reasonable leading hypothesis, best confirmed by first excluding an acute cardiac issue via EKG, chest X-ray, and point-of-care ultrasound. The past medical history is a "massive red flag" for GI bleeding independent of the cardiac picture, given anticoagulation, uremic platelet dysfunction, and the known predisposition of both ESRD and heart failure patients to GI arteriovenous malformations; because melena is reported by history but not confirmed on exam, either the bleeding is not currently active (or is from an over-the-counter iron or bismuth product) or it is intermittent, consistent with AVM-type bleeding.

Chest X-ray shows cardiomegaly without overt pulmonary edema; EKG shows an incomplete left bundle branch block in sinus rhythm, unchanged from five years earlier (i.e., old, not new); point-of-care ultrasound shows reduced systolic function consistent with prior echocardiograms, without pericardial effusion. Labs: VBG pH 7.4, pCO2 45; COVID negative; INR 1.68 (on apixaban); potassium 4.1; BUN 52; phosphorus 5; AST 312, ALT 223, total bilirubin 1.2; CBC with a normal white count, hemoglobin 8 (MCV 100, versus a prior baseline hemoglobin 9 with a similarly elevated MCV); BNP near 1,000 (baseline under 100); high-sensitivity troponin 916, rising to a peak of roughly 3,000 over the next 24 hours.

The troponin elevation is analyzed first: given the long-standing (not new) left bundle branch block and the absence of a classic ACS presentation, and given that the troponin is actively trending (rising, then presumably falling) rather than static, this is interpreted as an evolving vascular process — either acute plaque rupture or increased demand on already-vulnerable coronary vessels — rather than a chronic, static elevation from reduced renal clearance or myocarditis. Because there is no compelling evidence for an emergent cardiac cause (no murmur, no dynamic ischemic EKG changes, no pericardial effusion on POCUS), the working framework becomes the "three A's" of decompensated heart failure in a dialysis patient: arrhythmia, acute kidney injury, and afterload (here, significant hypertension) — both of which, along with under-dialysis, are plausible contributors, and none of which requires emergent intervention beyond reinitiating adequate dialysis and monitoring serial troponins and EKGs. The clinician who ultimately assumes the case notes that, despite favoring demand physiology, cardiology is still consulted, informed by a colleague's teaching that systemic illness (e.g., sepsis from a respiratory virus) can itself trigger plaque rupture via a catecholamine surge — meaning a clear source of demand does not fully exclude concurrent plaque rupture, and because PCI is not time-critical in the absence of an ST-elevation MI, there is time to trend the picture with cardiology involved rather than assume demand outright.

The liver chemistries (AST 312, ALT 223, both higher than baseline, AST greater than ALT) together with the macrocytosis (MCV 100) are analyzed as a secondary thread. Congestive hepatopathy from right-sided venous congestion is the leading explanation, but an acute congestive process would not be expected to elevate the MCV, which typically reflects a more chronic process; working through a systematic approach to macrocytosis (marrow issues such as a healthy marrow releasing reticulocytes, or an unhealthy marrow as in MDS, versus peripheral causes such as thyroid disease, medication effect, nutritional deficiency, or liver disease), the combination of an AST:ALT ratio greater than one, an elevated MCV, and heart failure without a fully satisfying explanation raises the possibility of unrecognized chronic alcohol use as a contributor to both the cardiomyopathy and the liver chemistry pattern — flagged as a "loose end" rather than a settled explanation. Separately, the expected direction of MCV change with GI blood loss is discussed: chronic blood loss should trend the MCV down (iron deficiency), while acute blood loss with intact iron stores should, in principle, trigger a reticulocytosis and a higher MCV — though this is explicitly noted as a pattern rarely actually confirmed in practice, since acute GI bleeds are usually diagnosed and treated well before reticulocyte kinetics are checked, and because patients who bleed are often too frail to have had a healthy baseline marrow reserve in the first place.

Serial AST/ALT values over the next 24 hours (AST 312→596→250s; ALT 223→416→320s, with AST rising and falling faster than ALT) prompt the question of whether the abnormal AST:ALT pattern could be partly cardiac rather than purely hepatic in origin, illustrated by a prior case where an elevated AST attributed to a biliary source was ultimately traced to an unrecognized STEMI. The most practical resolution offered is pragmatic rather than diagnostic: dialyze and ultrafiltrate the patient, trend serial EKGs and troponins, and let the response to volume removal indicate whether under-dialysis alone explains the full clinical and laboratory picture, or whether a new superimposed process is present — while noting that the chronic left bundle branch block could still mask a true ACS.

Blood cultures, obtained times two in the emergency department despite a normal white count, become the pivotal data point. The discussant reasons that decompensated heart failure can, in fact, be a presentation of bacteremia specifically in a patient with prosthetic cardiac material, where organisms such as coagulase-negative staphylococcus and enterococcus can cause minimally symptomatic endovascular infection; a low threshold for blood cultures is appropriate given the burden of indwelling hardware (ICD, tunneled dialysis catheter) even without classic signs such as fever or Osler's nodes. Additional history is then given: a prior gastric adenocarcinoma treated with gastrectomy roughly seven years earlier, and a CT abdomen/pelvis (obtained because of the liver chemistry abnormality) showing findings consistent with duodenitis.

Both blood culture bottles show gram-positive cocci in chains and pairs on gram stain. This morphology is explicitly distinguished from the most common blood culture contaminants — coagulase-negative staphylococcus (clusters) and Bacillus/Corynebacterium species (gram-positive rods) — meaning chains or pairs essentially cannot represent a contaminant and must reflect either a streptococcal or enterococcal species; with two positive bottles and an ICD in place, concern rises further. Given his gastrectomy (a recognized risk factor for enterococcal translocation) and enterococcus's tendency to cause minimally symptomatic endovascular infection, enterococcus is initially favored, which complicates empiric antibiotic selection (vancomycin reliably covers streptococcus but not necessarily enterococcus, raising the question of daptomycin or ampicillin coverage for possible VRE). A rapid PCR-based blood culture identification panel ("BioFire") instead detects Streptococcus, not enterococcus or staphylococcus; the organism is later speciated as Streptococcus mitis (also known as Streptococcus oralis), an oral commensal that can also be found throughout the GI tract.

Piecing this together with the gastrectomy history and the CT finding of duodenitis, the working hypothesis becomes GI mucosal translocation — possibly related to mucosal inflammation or even recurrent malignancy breaching the mucosal barrier — as the portal of entry for Streptococcus mitis, an organism recognized for adhesion properties that allow it to bind readily to both tissue and foreign material (including cardiac hardware). Infectious disease is consulted regarding both antibiotic adequacy (whether ceftriaxone alone is sufficient) and hardware management, given the presence of a fistula, a tunneled dialysis catheter ("permcath"), and an ICD; their guidance is structured as a conditional plan — remove the dialysis catheter if the AV fistula is functional, otherwise exchange it, and separately consider that the ICD itself may need removal given how adherent this organism is to hardware, acknowledging the complexity of that decision. Repeat blood cultures are pending, and a TEE is ordered with a PET CT planned if the TEE is negative, reflecting the seriousness with which the team is pursuing a possible endovascular source; the discussant notes learning that this species carries a comparatively high reported mortality.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The presenting discussant performs an explicit [[Cognitive Autopsy]] on his own initial reasoning: he identifies that his framework for ruling out an emergent endovascular cause (endocardial, myocardial, pericardial) was structurally sound, but that he over-weighted the reassuring, low-acuity *clinical syndrome* (URI-like symptoms, decompensated heart failure attributable to missed dialysis) and under-weighted the patient's *structural risk profile* — the sheer number of indwelling devices (ICD, tunneled catheter, AV fistula) — which alone should have lowered the threshold for blood cultures regardless of how mundane the presenting complaint seemed.

This is framed as a generalizable lesson distinct from a knowledge gap: the error was not failing to know that bacteremia can present as decompensated heart failure, but failing to apply that knowledge because the initial framing (a URI explained by dialysis nonadherence) felt sufficiently complete — a caution against accepting a benign unifying explanation too readily once one is found, particularly when a patient's hardware burden independently elevates risk.

The gram-stain morphology rule (chains/pairs versus clusters) is used as a disciplined alternative to probabilistic contaminant-scoring systems: rather than weighing "how likely is this to be a contaminant," the organism's morphology itself is treated as near-diagnostic, illustrating how a well-chosen anatomic/microbiologic fact can substitute for a slower, more uncertain heuristic.

The troponin-kinetics reasoning models comfort holding a leading hypothesis (demand ischemia) while still pursuing confirmatory specialist input (cardiology), rather than treating "most likely" as "confirmed" — an example of proportionate humility that does not slow down time-sensitive care but also does not foreclose a less likely, more dangerous possibility (plaque rupture).

## Teaching Discussion

For a dialysis-dependent patient with decompensated heart failure, the practical initial workup is an EKG, chest X-ray, and point-of-care ultrasound to exclude the three most morbid, time-sensitive causes — an endocardial process (endocarditis or acute valve rupture), an acute myocardial process, and a pericardial effusion — before attributing the presentation to the more common, non-emergent explanation of reduced dialysis adherence, dietary indiscretion, or substance use.

Once emergent causes are excluded, the "three A's" — arrhythmia, acute kidney injury, and afterload — provide a practical framework for the remaining drivers of decompensated heart failure in this population, alongside the default explanation of under-dialysis itself.

A troponin value can be roughly converted between high-sensitivity and older-generation scales by dividing by 1,000 (e.g., a high-sensitivity troponin of 2,000 corresponds to roughly 2.0 on the older scale) — a practical bedside conversion for comparing values across different assay eras or institutions.

Systemic illness with a catecholamine surge (described in the context of sepsis from respiratory viruses such as RSV or influenza) is a recognized trigger for atherosclerotic plaque rupture, meaning a clinically obvious source of myocardial oxygen demand (hypertension, volume overload, tachycardia) does not by itself exclude a concurrent type 1 (plaque rupture) myocardial infarction.

On blood culture interpretation, gram-positive cocci in clusters (coagulase-negative staphylococcus) are the most common true contaminant, followed by gram-positive rods (Bacillus and Corynebacterium species); gram-positive cocci in chains or pairs, by contrast, essentially always represent a real streptococcal or enterococcal bacteremia and should prompt urgent evaluation, particularly in a patient with prosthetic cardiac material.

Streptococcus mitis (Streptococcus oralis) is part of normal oral flora but can also be found throughout the GI tract; it has adhesion properties that allow it to bind readily to both tissue and foreign material, making it a notably "sticky" organism with a strong predilection for infecting indwelling cardiac hardware once it reaches the bloodstream — via either an oral or a GI mucosal route of entry, the latter especially relevant in a patient with prior gastrectomy or mucosal disruption such as duodenitis.

Management of hardware-associated endovascular infection is highly individualized and often structured as conditional decision-making: for example, whether to remove or exchange a tunneled dialysis catheter may depend on whether the patient's AV fistula is mature and functional, while decisions about explanting an ICD weigh the organism's adherence properties and infection risk against the procedural complexity and risk of device replacement.
