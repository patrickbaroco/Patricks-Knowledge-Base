## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Robbie presents his own ED case; standard mapping holds. A 65-year-old woman with severe alcohol use disorder presents with three days of fatigue and malaise, tachycardia, hypertension, and tremulousness — a picture that reflexively (and incorrectly) evokes thyrotoxicosis before her labs reveal hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, and a normal-gap metabolic acidosis. Reza and Robbie work through the standard framework (GI loss, renal loss, transcellular shift; the magnesium-potassium relationship via ROMK channels; the urine anion gap), but the acidosis persists even after her electrolytes are repleted, and a targeted beta-hydroxybutyrate comes back markedly elevated despite a normal gap — proving true ketoacidosis. Robbie unveils the physiologic explanation he spent hours researching afterward: intact kidneys convert what should be an anion gap ketoacidosis into a normal-gap acidosis by exchanging excreted ketone anions for retained chloride, a compensatory mechanism almost never seen clinically because the very processes that cause ketosis (DKA, starvation, alcoholic ketoacidosis) also typically impair renal perfusion — except in this patient, whose compulsive overhydration preserved her renal function well enough to let the mechanism play out in full.

## Pearls

- Tachycardia with hypertension and tremulousness should trigger a sympathetic-overactivity framework before landing on thyrotoxicosis: pain/discomfort is the most common cause, followed by early sepsis (before cardiovascular collapse), and substance withdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other sedatives) — thyrotoxicosis is a real but comparatively less common cause that's easy to reflexively reach for once "tremulous" is mentioned.
- Most electrolyte derangements — especially potassium and sodium disorders — can be substantially diagnosed from the history alone (a directed question about vomiting, diarrhea, or diuretic use goes a long way before ordering more tests).
- Approach to hypokalemia: order the differential as GI loss, renal loss, and transcellular shift, with decreased dietary intake alone being a rare primary driver that shouldn't lead the framework.
- Vomiting and diuretic use both tend to produce a low potassium WITH a metabolic alkalosis (via volume contraction activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and driving hydrogen secretion), whereas diarrhea produces a low potassium WITH a normal-gap metabolic acidosis (via direct bicarbonate loss) — a useful discriminator when the acid-base picture is known.
- Hypomagnesemia permits increased ROMK channel activity in the distal nephron, driving renal potassium wasting; when hypokalemia doesn't correct with potassium repletion alone, check and correct the magnesium, since the potassium derangement may really be a magnesium problem.
- On a basic metabolic panel, an anion gap metabolic acidosis is the only acid-base disturbance that can be diagnosed with confidence in isolation, because the body never compensates for a primary alkalosis by generating an anion gap acidosis; a low bicarbonate alone could reflect either primary metabolic acidosis or renal compensation for a primary respiratory alkalosis, so a blood gas is required to be certain.
- Normal-gap (hyperchloremic) metabolic acidosis divides into GI causes (diarrhea, GI fistulas) and renal causes (renal tubular acidosis, advanced CKD with impaired ammoniagenesis), distinguishable using the urine anion gap: a negative value ("GUT, negative") favors a GI cause, while a positive value favors a renal cause.
- Ketoacidosis classically produces an anion gap acidosis, but when renal function is well preserved, the kidney actively excretes ketone anions in exchange for retained chloride — converting what would otherwise be an anion gap acidosis into a normal-gap acidosis. This mechanism is rarely observed clinically because the processes that generate ketosis (DKA, starvation, alcoholic ketoacidosis) are almost always accompanied by volume depletion or acute kidney impairment that prevents the kidney from completing this exchange.
- The same "excrete the toxic anion, retain chloride" mechanism (producing an initial normal-gap acidosis that only becomes an anion gap acidosis once the kidneys are impaired) has also been described in toluene toxicity and in D-lactic acidosis (D-lactate, unlike L-lactate, is cleared renally rather than hepatically).
- Repeated exposure to "boring," high base-rate presentations (e.g., alcohol withdrawal) is what builds the internal denominator needed to recognize when a superficially familiar case is actually behaving atypically — treating common presentations as beneath continued attention is what allows rare deviations to be missed.

## Problem Representation

A 65-year-old woman with severe, longstanding alcohol use disorder and chronically low potassium and magnesium (but a previously normal bicarbonate) presents with three days of fatigue, tachycardia, hypertension, and tremulousness, found to have hypokalemia (2.9), hypomagnesemia (0.9), and a persistent, VBG-confirmed true normal-gap metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate 14, pH 7.29) that does not resolve with electrolyte repletion — ultimately explained by ketoacidosis (beta-hydroxybutyrate 4) presenting atypically without an anion gap because her preserved renal function was actively exchanging ketone anions for chloride.

## Case Narrative

Robbie opens with a deliberately sparse presentation: a 65-year-old woman with three days of fatigue and malaise. Reza's first move is to note that the chief complaint itself is unhelpfully non-specific — it doesn't narrow the problem frame at all — but that the three-day duration does useful work, favoring an acute process over a subacute or chronic one just now surfacing. He builds a minimal initial problem representation (acute onset, middle-aged) and waits for more.

Robbie adds vital signs and exam findings: heart rate 110, blood pressure 165/75, and the patient appears restless, fidgety, and tremulous. Before reasoning clinically, both hosts riff on how many times this exact constellation (tremor plus tachycardia) has caused them to miss thyrotoxicosis in the past — a running, self-deprecating joke about a diagnosis that has evaded them repeatedly over the years. Reza's substantive answer, though, deliberately resists the reflexive jump to thyroid disease: tachycardia plus hypertension most often reflects sympathetic nervous system overactivity, and by far the most common cause of that in a hospitalized or acutely ill patient is pain or discomfort, followed by early sepsis (before the cardiovascular system decompensates into hypotension), and then withdrawal from a substance the patient regularly uses (alcohol, benzodiazepines, or another sedative). He notes that tremor and restlessness specifically raise excess thyroid hormone or an exogenous stimulant, so he'd want a urine toxicology screen and a TSH, alongside a focused cardiopulmonary exam and attention to diaphoresis, plus basic labs looking for other catecholamine-driven signs like hypokalemia or an elevated lactate. He also explicitly invokes a "common things are common" principle attributed to Gurpreet Dhaliwal: it's more likely this patient simply has baseline hypertension and the actual problem to solve is the tachycardia, rather than assuming both vital sign abnormalities share one unifying cause.

Robbie reveals the TSH is normal, but the patient has a long history of severe alcohol use disorder with repeated withdrawal episodes; she reports no abdominal pain and only mild nausea without vomiting, and has continued to drink heavily up until presentation. He then delivers her chemistry panel in full: sodium 139, potassium 2.9, chloride 114, bicarbonate 14, anion gap 11 (with a presumed normal albumin), glucose 86, BUN 18, creatinine 0.9.

Reza works through the findings in order. The potassium of 2.9 is low enough to warrant an EKG (given the risk of cardiac electrical disturbance) and a search for the driving mechanism, using the standard framework of GI loss, renal loss, and (less commonly) transcellular shift — deliberately setting aside decreased dietary intake as a rare primary cause. He credits nephrologist John Sperati with the teaching that most potassium and sodium derangements can be substantially diagnosed by history alone, and asks directly whether the patient has had vomiting or diarrhea; Robbie confirms neither. Reza connects this to the acid-base picture: with a low bicarbonate and no anion gap (assuming normal albumin), the pattern doesn't fit vomiting or diuretic use (both of which tend to produce hypokalemia with a metabolic alkalosis via volume-contraction-driven renin-angiotensin-aldosterone activation) but would fit diarrhea (which causes hypokalemia with a normal-gap metabolic acidosis via direct bicarbonate loss). Robbie confirms she's not on diuretics either, and Reza asks about her prior labs — her potassium and magnesium have been chronically low for a long time, but her bicarbonate has never previously been low, meaning the acidosis itself is new.

Reza contextualizes the chronic electrolyte derangements as a well-described consequence of alcohol use disorder (via decreased intake, alcohol's inhibition of vasopressin acting like a diuretic, and transcellular shifts from intermittent refeeding-type insulin spikes), and pivots to the magnesium-potassium relationship: hypomagnesemia permits increased activity of ROMK channels in the distal nephron, driving ongoing renal potassium wasting, so severe or refractory hypokalemia often needs magnesium repletion before potassium will normalize. Robbie confirms he'd already checked the magnesium — 0.9, low — and that repleting it resolved the hypokalemia as expected.

What doesn't resolve is the acidosis. Robbie explains that he was surprised not to see a large anion gap in a patient like this, so he obtained a VBG (pH 7.29, with appropriately compensatory respiratory alkalosis) and re-sent a full BMP after electrolyte repletion — confirming a true, persistent, VBG-verified normal-gap metabolic acidosis with a normal albumin. Reza credits mentor "Uncle Bob" with the underlying acid-base principle here: an anion gap acidosis is the only acid-base disturbance diagnosable with confidence from a BMP alone, since the body never compensates for a primary alkalosis by manufacturing an anion gap acidosis — so a low bicarbonate could theoretically reflect renal compensation for a primary respiratory alkalosis rather than a primary metabolic acidosis, making the VBG a necessary confirmatory step (though Reza notes respiratory alkalosis severe enough to explain a bicarbonate this low would be unusual, even accounting for the patient's tremulousness and possible anxiety-driven tachypnea).

With a confirmed, isolated normal-gap acidosis, Reza pivots to the renal-versus-GI framework, invoking nephrologist Joel Topf ("the kidney whisperer") and the urine anion gap: a negative value favors a GI cause (mnemonic "GUT, negative"), a positive value favors a renal cause. He notes GI causes are usually clinically obvious (diarrhea, or a history of GI surgery creating fistulous connections), and raises a cautionary parallel — the tendency to reflexively blame a patient's presentation on their known substance use rather than considering an independent diagnosis, illustrated by a prior patient whose pulmonary hypertension was blamed on methamphetamine use for years before systemic sclerosis was recognized on exam. He considers renal tubular acidosis, but notes the normal creatinine argues against the most common renal cause (advanced CKD with impaired distal acid secretion), while acknowledging a distal RTA remains possible even with preserved GFR.

At this point Robbie proposes a reflex exercise: rapid-fire, what causes an acute normal-gap acidosis in general (diarrhea) versus what causes an acidosis specifically in a patient with alcohol use disorder (an anion gap acidosis, from ketones or lactate)? He then reveals that despite the patient denying diarrhea, his own reflexes — independent of the (structurally, non-gap) presentation in front of him — led him to order a lactate and a beta-hydroxybutyrate anyway. The lactate is normal; the beta-hydroxybutyrate is markedly elevated (4 mmol/L), confirming true ketosis in a patient whose gap looked entirely normal.

Reza reasons through the apparent contradiction live, drawing an analogy to isotonic ("isosmolar") hyponatremia, where a normal-appearing lab value can conceal two opposing processes (hyperosmolar and hypoosmolar contributions) canceling out numerically. He notes that ketones should never be present at all, so regardless of the calculated gap, the beta-hydroxybutyrate alone proves ketosis is occurring — and that a bicarbonate of 14 is actually on the lower end of what's typically seen in pure alcoholic ketosis (usually not below about 18), suggesting either two simultaneous processes or something actively masking an expected anion gap.

Robbie then shares the physiology he researched extensively after the case: normal-gap acidosis can be conceptually framed as either a loss of bicarbonate (by far the more common mechanism — diarrhea, chronic kidney disease, renal tubular acidosis) or a gain in chloride (classically from normal saline administration). But there's a third, much rarer pathway specific to ketosis and certain other negatively charged toxic molecules: when the kidneys are healthy enough to actively excrete ketone anions, they do so in exchange for retained chloride, converting what would otherwise be an anion gap ketoacidosis into a normal-gap acidosis. The reason this is rarely seen clinically is that essentially every process that generates significant ketosis — diabetic ketoacidosis, starvation, alcoholic ketoacidosis — is also typically accompanied by volume depletion or acute kidney impairment that prevents the kidney from completing this exchange, which is why an anion gap is the expected, "textbook" finding in ketoacidosis. The same mechanism has been described in toluene toxicity (an initial normal-gap acidosis that only progresses to an anion gap once the kidneys become impaired) and in D-lactic acidosis (since D-lactate, unlike the more familiar L-lactate, is cleared by the kidney rather than the liver). What made this particular patient different from essentially every other alcoholic-ketosis patient Robbie has cared for is that, rather than becoming nauseated and vomiting (typical, and volume-depleting), she was unusually focused on staying hydrated and drank heavily throughout her illness — preserving her renal perfusion well enough to let this normally hidden compensatory mechanism play out in full. Treating her ketosis resolved both the elevated beta-hydroxybutyrate and normalized her bicarbonate, confirming the ketoacidosis, atypically presenting as a normal gap, as the unifying diagnosis.

The episode closes with Reza crediting a separate mentor, Tiago, with the observation that everyone develops mild ketosis overnight during a normal fast, but healthy kidneys excrete it before an anion gap ever develops — directly reinforcing Robbie's mechanism. Robbie offers a broader reflection on the value of not treating any case as "boring": years of seeing hundreds of alcohol-use-disorder patients built the pattern-recognition denominator that made this single atypical presentation immediately stand out as different, a payoff that wouldn't have been possible without the accumulated experience of the ordinary cases that came before it.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The episode's central bias lesson is explicit and double-edged, as Reza names directly: blaming a presentation entirely on a patient's known alcohol use risks missing an independent diagnosis (illustrated by the methamphetamine/systemic sclerosis anecdote), while refusing to attribute anything to alcohol use risks overcomplicating an otherwise straightforward presentation — there's no algorithmic answer, only calibrated suspicion. The recurring thyrotoxicosis near-miss (explicitly flagged by both hosts as a diagnosis that has eluded them multiple times across the show's history) illustrates how a single classic textbook trigger (tremor) can pull attention toward a comparatively rare diagnosis before more common causes of the same finding (sympathetic overactivity from pain, sepsis, or withdrawal) are worked through. The ketoacidosis-without-a-gap finding is itself a lesson in trusting a specific abnormal test (an elevated beta-hydroxybutyrate) over a reassuring composite calculation (a normal anion gap) — the gap is a useful screening heuristic, not a definitive rule-out, and Robbie's willingness to order ketones "on reflex" despite a lab picture that argued against needing them is what caught the diagnosis.

## Teaching Discussion

This case rewards close attention to acid-base fundamentals that are easy to under-use in practice: the distinction between what a BMP alone can and cannot diagnose (only an anion gap acidosis, never an alkalosis or a compensatory pattern with confidence), the mechanistic link between vomiting/diuretics (alkalosis) versus diarrhea (normal-gap acidosis) via volume contraction and RAAS activation, and the magnesium-potassium relationship via ROMK channel physiology. The urine anion gap ("GUT, negative") is offered as a practical bedside tool for separating GI from renal causes of a normal-gap acidosis once the more common explanations have been excluded.

The centerpiece teaching, though, is a genuinely novel physiologic mechanism for most listeners: intact renal function can convert a ketoacidosis from its "textbook" anion gap presentation into a normal-gap presentation, by actively exchanging excreted ketone anions for retained chloride. This is presented not as a curiosity but as a structural blind spot in typical teaching, since the clinical scenarios that produce significant ketosis (DKA, starvation, alcoholic ketoacidosis) nearly always come bundled with the volume depletion or renal impairment that prevents this compensation from completing — meaning most clinicians will go an entire career without seeing it, exactly as described here. The parallel examples (toluene toxicity, D-lactic acidosis) reinforce that "protective" renal anion-exchange mechanisms are a generalizable physiologic theme, not a ketone-specific quirk, worth building into a broader schema on why some toxic or metabolically generated anions produce a gap and others transiently don't. The episode closes on a meta-level point about expertise: the ability to recognize this rare presentation was only possible because of extensive prior exposure to "ordinary" alcohol-related presentations, reinforcing that pattern recognition for the unusual case depends on genuinely engaging with the routine ones rather than treating them as rote.
