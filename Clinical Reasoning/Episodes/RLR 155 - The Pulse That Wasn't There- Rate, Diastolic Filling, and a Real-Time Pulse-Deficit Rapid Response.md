## Pearls

- A palpated or pulse-oximetry-detected peripheral pulse rate is not a direct proxy for the heart's electrical rate — it reflects only the subset of electrical contractions that generate enough forward mechanical output to be detected downstream.
- Pulseless electrical activity (PEA) is the clearest illustration of this principle taken to its extreme: the heart's electrical activity can be entirely normal while essentially no mechanical output reaches the periphery, most classically from an obstructive process like massive pulmonary embolism blocking forward flow despite normal electrical conduction.
- There are three general reasons an electrically-generated contraction fails to produce an effective, palpable pulse: the rate is too fast to allow adequate diastolic filling time, the beat is premature (as with PVCs, which fire before adequate filling), or there is loss of atrioventricular synchrony (as in ventricular tachycardia, where uncoordinated atrial and ventricular contraction reduces effective output despite a fast overall rate).
- A pulse-versus-telemetry rate discordance in a patient with a severely reduced ejection fraction should raise a rate-related diastolic filling problem: at very fast rates, there is too little time between beats for the ventricle to fill, so a meaningful fraction of electrically triggered contractions fail to eject a detectable pulse wave.
- When a peripheral pulse is faint, unreliable, or discordant with telemetry, a central measurement such as brachial blood pressure is a better proxy for whether vital organs are being adequately perfused than the peripheral pulse itself.
- New tachycardia should prompt one central question before any rate- or rhythm-control intervention: is this a warning sign of current or impending cardiovascular collapse (with hypotension either already present or about to manifest), or is it more likely driven by a reversible, non-hemodynamic trigger of excess sympathetic tone?
- Isolated new-onset AFib with RVR, by itself, is rarely the sole harbinger of impending shock — when a patient is hemodynamically stable (normal blood pressure), there is usually time to search for a reversible physiologic driver before reflexively treating the rhythm.
- Common reversible, non-perfusion-related drivers of sympathetic tone/tachycardia that deserve a focused search before beta-blockade include pain (including easily overlooked sources like sacral or decubitus ulcers, or pain with repositioning) and urinary retention or constipation — a bladder scan and a review for constipation are simple, high-yield maneuvers, especially in a nonverbal patient who cannot report symptoms.
- Sustained, uncontrolled AFib with RVR over two to three weeks carries a real risk of tachycardia-induced (dilated) cardiomyopathy — a genuine reason for eventual rate control — but this longer-term risk is a separate consideration from the acute question of whether the patient is in immediate danger right now, and should not drive urgent intervention on its own.
- In a patient whose goals of care are already oriented toward comfort (e.g., following severe anoxic brain injury with an established goals-of-care conversation underway), the threshold for further invasive workup (serial labs, urine output monitoring) should be explicitly reconsidered against the patient's overall trajectory and comfort, rather than defaulting to a full standard workup.
- Digoxin remains a reasonable rate-control option specifically in a patient with a low ejection fraction and AFib, since it improves both rate control and, unlike many rate-control agents, does not impair contractility.
- Discrepant reports of ejection fraction between studies read by different clinicians are a reminder to weigh which read is more reliable/recent rather than automatically deferring to whichever number is most recent chronologically.
- Real-time teaching content created for a completely different purpose can become directly useful during an actual bedside emergency — a team facing a genuinely puzzling pulse-telemetry discordance was able to pull up and apply a previously recorded teaching video in the middle of a live rapid response.

## Problem Representation

A nonverbal patient roughly a month out from an anoxic brain injury following cardiac arrest, with an established goals-of-care conversation underway and a severely reduced ejection fraction, triggered a rapid response for an apparent new tachyarrhythmia — telemetry showing a narrow-complex, irregular rhythm at 133 beats per minute (favoring AFib or atrial flutter) while the manually palpated pulse and pulse oximeter both registered roughly 60-63 beats per minute — a discordance ultimately explained by rate-related loss of effective diastolic filling and therefore of mechanically effective beats in the setting of a severely reduced-EF heart, managed conservatively by searching for reversible triggers of sympathetic tone rather than immediate rate- or rhythm-control intervention, and which resolved spontaneously as the rate settled during the evaluation itself.

## Case Narrative

Reza opens by texting in live from the middle of a 16-hour overnight shift at the VA, describing a rapid response that had unfolded just a short time earlier and that he immediately recognized as ideal RLR material. An intern, new to working with him but already picking up the habit (started by prior residents) of calling him "Prof," paged him about a patient in new AFib with rapid ventricular response, asking about giving IV metoprolol on a general floor bed rather than a monitored step-down unit, since the bedside nurse wasn't comfortable administering it there. Reza cleared the logistics with the nursing supervisor, who agreed a physician could administer a couple of doses on the floor — and then, half-joking about the cynicism that comes with experience, predicted a rapid response would end up being called anyway. It was.

At the bedside, a nurse had palpated the patient's pulse manually and found it running around 60 beats per minute, while the cardiac monitor read 133 beats per minute — a striking discordance that prompted the rapid response team to escalate rather than simply proceed with rate control. Reza confirms the discordance himself: counting the pulse for 20 seconds and extrapolating gives roughly 60-63 beats per minute, matching a direct stethoscope auscultation of the heart, while the telemetry strip clearly shows 133 beats per minute, narrow-complex and irregular, without an obvious sawtooth flutter pattern — most consistent with AFib with RVR. The patient himself cannot report symptoms: he is nonverbal since a cardiac arrest a month prior left him with anoxic brain injury, and the primary team has already begun discussing goals of care. On exam, his forehead is diaphoretic, but his blood pressure is normal (systolic around 110), and his temperature and glucose are both normal.

Robbie takes the case as an opportunity to disentangle a set of hardwired reflexes (AFib with RVR calls for a beta blocker; bradycardia at the wrist calls for the opposite) that don't obviously apply here, since both are ostensibly present at once. He starts from a basic question: what does a palpated pulse actually tell you? It reflects only the subset of the heart's electrical contractions that generate enough forward mechanical output to be felt downstream — not the electrical rate itself. He illustrates this with pulseless electrical activity (PEA), the clearest example of the two being completely dissociated: in PEA, the heart's electrical conduction is entirely normal, yet no pulse is generated at all, classically because a mechanical obstruction — a massive pulmonary embolism, for instance — blocks forward blood flow despite intact electrical activity.

Applying that framework here, Robbie reasons that of the 133 electrical beats per minute recorded on telemetry, only about 60-63 are making it to the periphery as a detectable pulse — meaning roughly half of the electrically triggered contractions are mechanically ineffective. He outlines three general reasons a contraction can fail to produce an effective, palpable beat: the rate is simply too fast, leaving inadequate diastolic filling time between beats; the beat is premature, as with PVCs, which fire before the ventricle has had time to fill; or there is loss of atrioventricular synchrony, as in ventricular tachycardia, where the atria and ventricles no longer contract in a coordinated sequence, reducing the effectiveness of each beat even at a fast overall rate. Given this patient's already severely reduced ejection fraction, Robbie's leading explanation is the first: the heart is simply beating too fast for adequate diastolic filling in a ventricle that already has limited reserve, so a meaningful fraction of beats fail to generate a detectable peripheral pulse.

He then pivots to the practical question of what to do about it. Since the peripheral pulse is unreliable here, he argues the brachial blood pressure — which is currently normal — is a better proxy for whether the patient's vital organs (brain, kidneys) are being adequately perfused than the weak peripheral pulse itself. This reframes the central clinical question: is this new tachycardia a warning sign of current or impending cardiovascular collapse (hypotension either already present or about to manifest), or is it more likely driven by something else entirely — inappropriately excessive sympathetic tone from a nonperfusion-related trigger, most commonly pain or anxiety? He notes that isolated new AFib with RVR, on its own, is very rarely the sole clue to impending shock; a truly abrupt shock physiology usually announces itself in other ways. He also flags a real, but separate, longer-term concern: uncontrolled AFib with RVR persisting for two to three weeks can produce a tachycardia-induced dilated cardiomyopathy — a genuine reason to eventually control the rate, but not an argument for urgent intervention in the next few minutes given the patient's current stability.

With a normal blood pressure buying time, and the patient unable to report symptoms directly, Robbie recommends a close physical exam focused on occult, reversible drivers of sympathetic tone before reaching for a beta blocker: checking carefully for pain, including easily overlooked sources like sacral or decubitus ulcers or pain with repositioning, and performing a bladder scan to rule out urinary retention, along with a chart review for constipation — both notably common, uncomfortable, and easily missed triggers in a nonverbal patient recovering from severe brain injury.

Reza then reveals what actually happened at the bedside. Struck by the same pulse-telemetry discordance in real time, he remembered that Robbie had recorded a teaching video years earlier on RLRCPSolvers.com covering exactly this phenomenon. With the patient's blood pressure stable and time on their side, he stepped away mid-rapid-response, pulled up the video on his phone at 1.5x speed, and returned to the room to walk the team through the same diastolic-filling explanation Robbie had just independently reconstructed live. One of the residents, Armand, extended the reasoning further, suggesting there simply wasn't enough blood in the ventricle at that rate to open the aortic valve on every beat — mirroring the same physiology causing the discordance at the periphery. As if to confirm the hypothesis, the patient's heart rate drifted down to about 103 beats per minute over the course of the conversation, and the peripheral pulse oximeter reading rose in lockstep to 103 as well — resolving the discordance essentially in real time and validating the diastolic-filling explanation.

Given the patient's goals-of-care context and now-stable, resolving rhythm, the team elected not to pursue further invasive workup — serial lactate, creatinine, or urine output monitoring — reasoning that with a normal blood pressure and an already comfort-oriented care trajectory, the marginal value of further testing was low. They did discuss restarting digoxin, previously discontinued when the patient had converted back to sinus rhythm, given digoxin's utility for rate control specifically in a low-EF patient with AFib, though they left this as a consideration rather than an immediate action. Reza also notes an unreconciled discrepancy between two ejection fraction readings — a recent one from a different cardiologist reported the EF at roughly 40%, unchanged from three weeks prior, while Reza remains skeptical of that number given the patient's overall clinical trajectory and sticks with the earlier, lower estimate of roughly 20%.

The episode closes on a personal note about the meta-loop the case represents: Robbie's own recorded teaching content, made without this specific patient in mind, became the direct tool that helped his real-time clinical team make sense of a genuinely puzzling bedside discordance — a rare, explicit closing of the circle between the show's educational mission and real clinical practice. Both hosts reflect briefly on the years-long partnership behind the show and on returning to make more standalone teaching videos going forward.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Data Point | Effect on Differential |
|---|---|
| Telemetry: narrow-complex, irregular rhythm at 133 bpm, no clear sawtooth | Supports AFib with RVR (or atrial flutter with variable conduction) as the underlying rhythm |
| Manually palpated pulse and pulse oximeter both ~60-63 bpm, discordant with telemetry | Raises concern for a pulse-generation problem rather than accepting the palpated rate as the true heart rate; prompts consideration of PEA-like mechanisms and rate-related diastolic filling failure |
| Known severely reduced ejection fraction | Raises probability that rate-related inadequate diastolic filling (rather than PVCs or AV dyssynchrony) is the specific mechanism for the pulse deficit |
| Normal blood pressure, normal temperature, normal glucose | Lowers probability of current or imminent hemodynamic collapse; supports "there is time" to search for reversible triggers before intervening |
| Diaphoresis without other signs of shock | Keeps a reversible sympathetic-tone trigger (pain, anxiety, urinary retention, constipation) on the differential rather than pointing definitively toward hemodynamic compromise |
| Heart rate and peripheral pulse both spontaneously converge toward ~103 bpm during evaluation | Confirms the rate-related diastolic filling explanation for the initial discordance; supports a self-limited, rate-dependent process rather than a fixed structural or conduction abnormality |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Pulse-versus-electrical-rate dissociation framework, anchored on PEA**: using the most extreme example of electrical-mechanical dissociation to teach a generalizable principle applicable to milder, partial dissociation (a true pulse deficit).
- **Three-mechanism framework for ineffective contractions**: rate-too-fast/inadequate filling, premature beats (PVCs), and AV asynchrony (VT) — a reusable structure for reasoning about any pulse-telemetry discordance.
- **"Is there time?" as the organizing question for new tachyarrhythmia**: reframing acute management around current/impending hemodynamic collapse versus a reversible non-perfusion trigger, rather than reflexively treating the rhythm itself.
- **Reversible-sympathetic-tone search, tailored to a nonverbal patient**: substituting a targeted exam (pain sources, bladder scan, constipation review) for history the patient cannot provide.
- **Goals-of-care-calibrated diagnostic threshold**: explicitly weighing the marginal value of further invasive workup against a patient's established comfort-oriented trajectory, rather than defaulting to a standard full workup.
- **Teaching-content-as-clinical-tool, closing the loop**: a rare explicit instance of the show's own educational material being retrieved and applied in real time during an actual bedside emergency, reinforcing the broader "case-based/content-based recall as a diagnostic trigger" theme in a new form (recorded teaching content rather than a remembered colleague case).

## Teaching Discussion

The episode is a compact lesson in not trusting a single vital sign in isolation: a peripheral pulse rate and a telemetry rate can diverge substantially, and the divergence itself is diagnostic information rather than a measurement error to be dismissed. Robbie's PEA analogy usefully anchors a spectrum — from complete electrical-mechanical dissociation (PEA) to partial dissociation (a rate-related pulse deficit) — that clinicians can use to reason about any situation where a peripheral pulse doesn't match the monitor.

The second teaching thread is about calibrating urgency to actual physiologic risk rather than to the alarming appearance of a monitor number. A rate of 133 with a normal blood pressure is treated as an invitation to investigate reversible drivers of sympathetic tone rather than an automatic trigger for beta-blockade — particularly valuable in a nonverbal patient, where pain, urinary retention, and constipation are easy to overlook without a directed exam.

Finally, the case models goals-of-care-aware clinical reasoning in real time: rather than pursuing a full escalation pathway by default, the team explicitly weighed further invasive testing against the patient's overall trajectory and comfort — and the meta-narrative of the team retrieving and applying Robbie's own teaching video mid-code is a vivid, unusually direct illustration of educational content translating into real bedside decision-making.
