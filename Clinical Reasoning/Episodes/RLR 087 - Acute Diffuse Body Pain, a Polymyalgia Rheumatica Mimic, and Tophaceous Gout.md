## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 73-year-old man wakes up with acute, diffuse shoulder, hip, and low back pain and no weakness, initially building a textbook illness script for polymyalgia rheumatica with possible giant cell arteritis given markedly elevated inflammatory markers. But the inflammatory markers prove too high for typical PMR, new point tenderness over the lumbar spine and facet enhancement on MRI raise concern for an inflammatory or infectious spinal process, and a subsequent swollen ankle — together with an unrecognized history of self-resolving first-toe pain — redirects the diagnosis to gout, ultimately confirmed by synovial fluid crystals, a markedly elevated uric acid, and dual-energy CT showing tophaceous gout extending into the spine.

## Pearls

- Acute, diffuse body pain with no antecedent buildup can be approached anatomically by asking what tissue exists throughout the whole body — bone, muscle, blood/vascular structures, and nerve — with the sudden, non-progressive tempo favoring a muscle or joint-based process over an insidious skeletal one.
- The hip is a deep joint that can "repress" pathology: a septic or otherwise inflamed hip joint may show no visible swelling or redness at all, so pain with passive range of motion (with normal strength) is a critical maneuver to detect intra-articular hip pathology that inspection alone will miss.
- Shoulder pain localized to the trapezius with a normal-appearing joint and no weakness with active range of motion is myalgia, not arthritis or tendinopathy; when this pattern extends to the hip girdle and low back with morning stiffness in a patient over 50, it should build the illness script for polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR).
- Because PMR and giant cell arteritis (GCA) exist on a spectrum, any patient being worked up for PMR should be explicitly screened for GCA symptoms (headache, jaw claudication, visual changes) even if the patient hasn't volunteered them, since pain elsewhere may be dominating what the patient chooses to report.
- Inflammatory markers markedly higher than expected for a working diagnosis (here, ESR 110 and CRP 140 in presumed PMR) should prompt reconsideration of a broader or more severe process — such as large-vessel vasculitis — rather than being accepted at face value as simply confirmatory.
- A history of self-resolving, severe first-toe pain (podagra) is a high-yield historical clue for gout that is easy to miss unless specifically asked about, and gout can mimic or coexist with a polyarticular, periarticular, and even axial (facet joint) inflammatory presentation that closely resembles PMR.

## Problem Representation

A 73-year-old man with an unremarkable past medical history develops acute-onset, diffuse pain in the trapezius/shoulder region, bilateral hips, and lower back with mild bilateral knee swelling and markedly elevated inflammatory markers (ESR 110, CRP 140) but normal initial imaging, building an initial illness script for polymyalgia rheumatica with possible giant cell arteritis; new lumbar point tenderness with facet enhancement on MRI, followed by an evolving ankle effusion and a previously unrecognized history of podagra, ultimately redirect the diagnosis to tophaceous gout involving the peripheral joints and the spine.

## Case Narrative

A 73-year-old man presents to the emergency department telling the physician, "Doc, I was fine three days ago. I woke up yesterday and my whole body was hurting like heck." Given this chief concern of acute, diffuse full-body pain with no buildup, the discussant reasons through it anatomically: what structures exist throughout the entire body that could plausibly cause pain everywhere at once? The candidates are the skeletal system, the muscles, the blood/vascular system, and the nervous system. A sudden, non-progressive onset argues against an insidious skeletal process and makes a muscular or joint-based etiology (as could occur with a viral illness causing diffuse myalgias) more likely than a purely hematologic or neurologic one, though those remain in the broader differential pending more data.

Further history reveals the pain is concentrated in the trapezius and shoulder region and, bilaterally, in the hips and lower back. His medical, social, and behavioral history is unremarkable. On exam, vital signs are normal. There is mild bilateral tenderness and swelling at the patellofemoral region of both knees. The hips show no visible or palpable abnormality but are markedly painful with passive range of motion, with no associated weakness. The shoulders similarly show no pain with palpation and no visible deformity. The lower back pain is midline in the lumbar region. Pertinent negatives include no rash, no other joint findings beyond the mild knee swelling, no oral or ocular lesions, and a normal cardiopulmonary and abdominal exam.

The discussant highlights the patient's age, 73, as central to the case and begins by noting that the hip is a "deep" joint that can repress pathology — a septic or otherwise inflamed hip can look and feel entirely normal on inspection and palpation, making passive range-of-motion testing (as performed here) essential to detect intra-articular disease that would otherwise be missed. Turning to the shoulders, pain in the trapezius region with a structurally normal-appearing joint is myalgia rather than true shoulder pathology; combined with hip girdle and low back pain, this builds the illness script for polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR) — a condition defined by pain and stiffness without true weakness, classically affecting patients over 50 and associated with giant cell arteritis (GCA). The discussant recalls a patient with PMR so weak with pain that she couldn't brush her hair, who improved dramatically with prednisone 20 mg by the next day. He contrasts this with rotator cuff pathology, where an inability to actively raise the arm (true weakness) points toward a tendon tear rather than referred, periarticular myalgia.

Because the exam does not suggest true joint inflammation (no tachycardia, no visibly "angry" joints), the discussant leans toward an arthralgia/periarticular-myalgia picture over a true polyarthritis, and toward a rheumatologic rather than infectious process overall. He proposes basic labs to look for markers of inflammation (thrombocytosis, leukocytosis, hypoalbuminemia), an ESR and CRP to establish whether this is an inflammatory or non-inflammatory process, and knee x-rays to look for fracture or chondrocalcinosis — recalling a prior RLR case of CPPD in which crystal deposition in a tendon spilled over into a joint and mimicked true arthritis. His leading hypothesis remains PMR, tempered somewhat by the knee swelling, which is not a classic PMR feature.

Results: CBC shows a mild leukocytosis of 13,000 with no anemia and no thrombocytosis; renal function and liver enzymes are normal; ESR is 110 and CRP is 140; x-rays of the shoulders, hips, and knees are all normal.

The discussant reads the markedly elevated ESR and CRP as confirming systemic inflammation, and notes that in a patient this age with an ESR of 100, giant cell arteritis must be considered — a concern that becomes more pressing, not less, if PMR is already suspected, since PMR and GCA occupy the same clinical spectrum and GCA cannot be missed. He would specifically ask about jaw claudication, headache, and visual symptoms, cautioning that a patient whose predominant complaint is diffuse muscle pain may simply not think to volunteer these symptoms unprompted. He also flags that the degree of inflammatory marker elevation seems too high for uncomplicated PMR alone, raising concern for an underlying large-vessel vasculitis and prompting consideration of vascular imaging (MRA or PET-CT of the head and neck) to look for vessel wall inflammation.

On repeat questioning, the patient denies headache, jaw claudication, weight loss, fevers, or any symptoms preceding the acute three-day course. However, he now reports that his lower back has become his most bothersome symptom, and exam reveals marked point tenderness over the thoracolumbar and lumbar spine. The discussant treats this combination — inflammatory markers, new back pain, and focal point tenderness — as a red-flag triad that should deviate management away from the base rate of benign, self-limited mechanical back pain toward a more sinister process driving both the inflammation and the back pain, all while keeping the shoulder and hip findings in view. He notes that percussion tenderness over the vertebrae is a useful maneuver for detecting vertebral infection (discitis, osteomyelitis). The recommended next step is MRI of the lumbar spine with gadolinium (assuming no contraindicating renal disease), given concern for an inflammatory spinal process — infectious (osteomyelitis, discitis, epidural abscess) or non-infectious (crystalline disease, which can affect the spine and even cause crowned dens syndrome when crystal deposits accumulate around the odontoid process).

Blood cultures are drawn first, followed by the MRI. It shows mild contrast enhancement at the L2-L3 facet joints without evidence of vertebral osteomyelitis or epidural abscess. Shortly after returning from the MRI, the patient develops new right ankle pain.

The discussant explains that gadolinium enhancement reflects a breach in normal tissue barriers (absent trauma, this indicates inflammation) and reframes the case as a 73-year-old man with a multi-articular, articular-versus-periarticular pain syndrome. He approaches the new ankle pain the same way as the knees, distinguishing intra-articular from periarticular pathology by assessing for swelling and passive range of motion. His leading concern shifts to a non-infectious inflammatory articular/periarticular process, reasoning that neither an indolent bacterial infection nor cancer would typically produce this acute, fever-free, rapidly evolving tempo. Crystalline disease becomes the leading hypothesis, and if the ankle exam suggests periarticular tendinopathy rather than true joint involvement, he would broaden the differential to include spondyloarthropathies (such as ankylosing spondylitis, though typically a younger-onset disease) and inflammatory bowel disease-associated arthropathy, which has a second incidence peak at this age and can cause both facet-joint pain and reactive tendinopathy.

On exam, the ankle mirrors the knee findings but is now more obviously swollen. New history emerges: the patient has never had joint pain or swelling like this before, but he has had prior episodes of severe, self-resolving pain at the base of his first toe, treated at home with ibuprofen.

The discussant recognizes this as a classic history of podagra and recalls a similar teaching case at Johns Hopkins — a patient with fever and back pain, presumed infectious, whose subsequently swollen knee was tapped and revealed CPPD crystals. He emphasizes the diagnostic power of tempo: an acute, inflammatory presentation without fever or other infectious findings argues against infection, though infection can coexist and must still be excluded (blood cultures, joint fluid gram stain and culture). Crystalline disease becomes the leading diagnosis. He recommends arthrocentesis with gram stain, culture, and polarized microscopy for crystals (including assessment for intracellular crystals within macrophages, per a prior RLR case), and wonders aloud whether crystalline deposition in the cervical spine could also explain the shoulder pain.

Arthrocentesis of the ankle yields approximately 30,000 white cells with monosodium urate (gout) crystals, negative gram stain, and negative culture. Serum uric acid, added to the initial labs, returns markedly elevated at 13 with a normal creatinine. Dual-energy CT confirms gouty tophi not only peripherally but also in the lower back, consistent with tophaceous involvement of the facet joints. The patient is treated with prednisone, experiences dramatic symptomatic relief, and is ultimately admitted for further management.

In discussion, the presenters note that despite the extensive workup, the patient ultimately received the same initial treatment (prednisone) he might have received empirically for either PMR or gout — but they emphasize that the distinction carries substantial downstream consequences for long-term management (chronic steroid taper and GCA surveillance for PMR versus urate-lowering therapy and lifestyle modification for gout). They also discuss whether crystalline disease may have been responsible for the patient's shoulder pain via a crowned dens-type mechanism in the cervical spine, which they consider plausible though unconfirmed.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The case is structured as a "sixth sense"-style narrative in which the initial illness script (PMR, with GCA as a must-not-miss companion diagnosis) is built carefully and appropriately from classic features, but the discussants remain alert to disconfirming signals — disproportionately high inflammatory markers, an atypical knee finding, and later an atypical ankle finding — rather than forcing every new data point into the initial framework.

The discussants explicitly discuss base-rate reasoning in the context of back pain: the presence of inflammatory markers and point tenderness is used to justify deviating from the base rate assumption that most back pain is benign and self-limited, prompting more aggressive imaging than would otherwise be pursued.

A key historical clue — prior episodes of self-resolving first-toe pain — was not elicited until late in the case, illustrating how a decisive piece of history can remain undiscovered until a directed, hypothesis-driven question is finally asked; the discussants reflect that letting the patient's own priorities guide the interview (the patient volunteering that his back, not his shoulder, was now bothering him most) helped redirect the workup productively.

The discussants close with an honest reflection on outcome bias: because prednisone was the treatment ultimately given (as it might have been for either leading hypothesis), one might mistakenly conclude the elaborate diagnostic workup made no practical difference — but they push back on this, noting that the specific diagnosis matters greatly for long-term management even when the acute treatment happens to converge.

## Teaching Discussion

Acute, diffuse body pain with no antecedent buildup can be approached anatomically: consider what tissue is present throughout the entire body (bone, muscle, blood vessels, nerves), and use tempo (sudden versus insidious) to help prioritize among these categories.

The hip is a deep joint that can conceal significant intra-articular pathology, including septic arthritis, without any visible swelling or redness; pain with passive range of motion in the setting of preserved strength is an essential maneuver for detecting hip joint pathology that inspection alone will miss.

Polymyalgia rheumatica is built from a specific pattern: myalgia (not weakness) affecting the shoulder girdle, hip girdle, and low back, typically in patients over 50, with normal strength testing; because PMR exists on a spectrum with giant cell arteritis, every patient with a PMR-type presentation should be explicitly screened for GCA symptoms (headache, jaw claudication, visual changes), even when those symptoms are not volunteered.

Inflammatory markers that are markedly higher than expected for the working diagnosis should not simply be accepted as "confirmatory" — here, ESR and CRP disproportionate to typical PMR appropriately raised concern for a more severe or different underlying process (large-vessel vasculitis), prompting consideration of vascular imaging.

New or point-tender back pain in the setting of systemic inflammatory markers is a red flag that should deviate management away from the base rate of self-limited mechanical back pain and toward evaluation for an inflammatory or infectious spinal process (osteomyelitis, discitis, epidural abscess, or crystalline disease of the facet joints); percussion tenderness over the vertebrae is a useful bedside clue to vertebral infection.

Crystalline arthropathies, including gout and CPPD, can present atypically — with polyarticular, periarticular, and even axial/facet-joint involvement (including crowned dens syndrome in the cervical spine) — closely mimicking an inflammatory rheumatologic syndrome like PMR; a history of self-resolving, severe first-toe pain (podagra) is a high-yield clue that should be specifically elicited.

When a presumed infectious process fails to show the expected buildup or fever, and the tempo instead suggests an acute inflammatory process, crystalline disease should rise on the differential — but infection should still be excluded in parallel (blood cultures, synovial fluid gram stain and culture) since crystalline disease and infection can coexist.
