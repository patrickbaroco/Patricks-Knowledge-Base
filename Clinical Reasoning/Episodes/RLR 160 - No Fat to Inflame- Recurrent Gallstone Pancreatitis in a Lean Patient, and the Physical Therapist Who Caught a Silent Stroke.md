## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Reza presents two of his own recent cases. In the first, a lean young man has his second episode of lipase-elevated epigastric pain with gallstones on ultrasound but a structurally normal pancreatic CT — twice — plus an incidental finding of celiac artery compression that tempts the team toward median arcuate ligament syndrome. Robbie reasons the CT is falsely reassuring because the patient has too little peripancreatic fat to generate a stranding signal, and that a trending (rather than static) lipase argues against macrolipase and for true, recurrent gallstone pancreatitis. In the second case, a diabetic man recovering from a below-knee amputation is flagged not once but twice by his physical therapist — first for possible cognitive impairment, then for a possible neurologic deficit — despite reporting no symptoms himself and an unremarkable bedside exam by the team. Reza and the team ultimately defer to her concern, and neurology's exam plus MRI confirm a subacute right posterior cerebral artery stroke causing a left homonymous hemianopsia, breaking through therapeutic apixaban. The episode closes with an extended discussion of how to give the team feedback about the delayed catch without either minimizing or over-punishing it, and a personal, celebratory aside marking Robbie's 9,000th CPSolvers teaching schema.

## Pearls

- CT sensitivity for pancreatitis (and intra-abdominal inflammation generally) depends on having peripancreatic/adjacent fat available to show stranding; a profoundly lean or cachectic patient can have a falsely negative scan simply because there's no fat to inflame.
- Trending the lipase over time can distinguish macrolipase (a large, persistently elevated enzyme complex that stays high regardless of clinical status) from true recurrent pancreatic inflammation (a dynamic value that rises and falls with disease activity) — this is a targeted troubleshooting tool, not a routine strategy.
- Radiographic celiac artery compression (the substrate for median arcuate ligament syndrome) is a common incidental finding; most people who have it are asymptomatic, and it should only be treated as causative when there's a specific clinical correlate (chronic postprandial pain, food aversion, involuntary weight loss) rather than an acute, episodic pain pattern.
- Weight loss of any cause is an underappreciated risk factor for gallstone formation — the "classic" higher-BMI gallstone phenotype is not the only one, and rapid weight loss (intentional or not) predisposes to cholelithiasis independent of baseline body size.
- Anticoagulation reduces but does not eliminate the risk of embolic stroke; a clot occurring on therapeutic anticoagulation should not be reflexively attributed to "breakthrough AFib" — it still warrants a structured secondary workup for other causes.
- A "pumps, pipes, plasma" framework organizes the secondary stroke workup when the presumed cause (e.g., AFib) doesn't fully explain a clot despite adequate anticoagulation: pumps (cardioembolism — endocarditis, tumor, other cardiac sources), pipes (vasculopathy/vasculitis, e.g., giant cell arteritis), and plasma (hypercoagulable blood-borne states, especially malignancy and antiphospholipid antibody syndrome).
- Homonymous hemianopsia with macular sparing localizes to the occipital cortex (which has dual blood supply protecting the macular representation); hemianopsia without macular sparing more often localizes to the retrochiasmal white matter — a distinction that helps narrow the anatomic level of a visual field cut.
- Partial vision loss is easy for both patients and clinicians to miss: the brain's compensatory mechanisms for visual field loss are extensive, and patients frequently don't spontaneously report a deficit they may not even be aware of.
- Non-physician team members' persistent, specific clinical concern is legitimate diagnostic signal, not an obstacle to disposition planning — going to examine the patient together, rather than dismissing or working around the request, is a low-cost way to resolve disagreement about what's actually driving a colleague's concern.
- An outcome (a missed or delayed diagnosis) does not retroactively prove the preceding reasoning was faulty — a useful framing for debriefing a team after a diagnosis is caught later than it could have been.

## Problem Representation

**Case 1:** A thin (~120 lb) man in his early 30s with recurrent episodes of acute epigastric pain and lipase elevations above 500, without significant alcohol use, with known gallstones on ultrasound but two structurally normal pancreatic CT scans obtained months apart, plus an incidental radiographic finding of celiac artery compression.

**Case 2:** A man with poorly controlled diabetes, recently status-post below-knee amputation for severe limb infection, admitted to complete antibiotics and await disposition, who reports no neurologic symptoms and has an unremarkable bedside exam by the primary team, but is flagged twice by his physical therapist — first for possible cognitive impairment, then for a possible neurologic deficit — ultimately found to have a subacute right posterior cerebral artery stroke with a left homonymous hemianopsia despite therapeutic apixaban for atrial fibrillation.

## Case Narrative

**Case 1.** Reza describes a man in his early 30s presenting for a second time with acute upper abdominal pain. Six months earlier he'd had an identical episode: epigastric pain without radiation to the back, a lipase around 500, a CT scan that showed no pancreatic inflammation, no history of significant alcohol use, and gallstones on imaging. He was treated presumptively for gallstone pancreatitis, improved, and left the hospital before a diet challenge could be completed. Now he's back with the same picture — epigastric pain, lipase in the 500s, gallstones on ultrasound, normal liver chemistries, and again a CT with no evidence of pancreatic inflammation. This time, though, the radiologist comments on apparent compression of the celiac artery, raising the possibility of median arcuate ligament syndrome as an alternative or contributing explanation.

Robbie's first move is to note how poorly the tests line up with each other — even the "positive" first episode never had imaging-confirmed pancreatitis — and to ask how the patient does between episodes. If he returns to a true baseline between flares, that argues for an episodic disease process rather than a chronic, progressive one, and it argues against clinically significant median arcuate ligament syndrome, which typically produces chronic postprandial pain rather than discrete acute attacks. Reza confirms the patient is entirely normal between episodes, with a history of H. pylori gastritis that was never fully treated. Robbie reasons that the clinical syndrome — recurrent, self-limited, acute epigastric pain with an elevated lipase — is much more compatible with recurrent gallstone pancreatitis than with a vascular compression syndrome, and frames the surgeon's dilemma as one of diagnostic humility: the natural history of biliary stone disease predicts that small, self-passing stones will eventually cause worse or more frequent episodes, and advanced biliary imaging plus cholecystectomy are the tools in reserve, but the celiac artery finding is very likely a red herring.

Reza then raises a subtly abnormal liver panel (mildly above the patient's own baseline) and asks Robbie to reason through why the CT keeps coming back negative despite a plausible pancreatitis picture. Robbie lists the classic causes of a falsely elevated lipase (macrolipase, DKA, renal failure) and the classic causes of a falsely negative pancreatic CT (very early or very mild disease), but the key reveal comes when Reza shares the patient's weight: about 120 pounds. Robbie immediately connects this to a teaching he'd given Reza previously — CT's sensitivity for any intra-abdominal inflammatory process depends on the presence of adjacent fat to show stranding, and a profoundly lean patient may simply lack the substrate to generate a positive imaging signal, even with real pancreatic inflammation underneath. This same leanness reframes the celiac artery finding, too — Robbie briefly wonders whether the compression itself might be causing food aversion and weight loss, before the pair converge on a simpler explanation: the patient is just naturally lean, has no postprandial pain, and gallstones (potentially related to a history of weight loss, a risk factor separate from and often forgotten alongside the classic higher-BMI phenotype) are the more parsimonious cause of his recurrent pancreatitis. A trend of lipase values — 31 the month before, 596 and 505 during the two acute episodes — confirms a dynamic rather than static pattern, arguing against macrolipase and reinforcing the pancreatitis diagnosis. The patient again leaves before an MRCP could be completed, and is discharged with a referral to general surgery for cholecystectomy.

**Case 2.** Reza's second case is a man with poorly controlled diabetes (A1c improved from 12 to 8) recently transferred from an outside hospital after a below-knee amputation for severe limb infection, admitted to complete antibiotics and await disposition planning with the amputee clinic. He reports no complaints on daily rounds. A physical therapist evaluating him for rehab candidacy raises a concern that he isn't cognitively "there" enough to participate meaningfully in rehab and recommends a neuropsychological evaluation — an unwelcome complication for a patient without insurance who needs a clean rehab disposition. Reza asks her to re-screen him to clarify the picture; she does, and returns with an escalated concern: not a neuropsych referral this time, but a request for a neurology consult or MRI, because she suspects a neglect syndrome.

Reza frames this explicitly as a test of how to handle a consultant (or, here, an allied health colleague) whose recommendation runs counter to the team's operational goal of getting the patient to rehab. Robbie's answer is to reject the older hierarchical model in which physicians are presumed to know more than everyone else on the team, and instead to recognize domains — like functional/cognitive assessment during physical therapy — where the physical therapist's expertise plausibly exceeds his own. Rather than dismiss or reflexively order a broad workup, his instinct is to go examine the patient together with her and let her point out specifically what she's noticing.

The team ultimately obtains an MRI/MRA and also calls neurology (both agree an MRI is reasonable off the story alone). Neurology's own exam — performed before the imaging returns — documents a patient with no subjective neurologic complaints but findings on exam consistent with a homonymous hemianopsia. Robbie, working the case blind, reasons that homonymous hemianopsia localizes to a lesion contralateral to the visual field defect, either in the occipital cortex or the retrochiasmal white matter feeding it, and that macular sparing (preserved central vision) argues for a cortical lesion, since the occipital pole representing the macula has redundant blood supply. Since the patient hasn't noticed any visual change, Robbie infers his central vision is likely spared, favoring an occipital cortical process — most likely, given the overall picture, a silent stroke. The MRI confirms restricted diffusion in the right PCA territory, three to six days old.

The case then pivots to why a stroke occurred despite the patient being on apixaban for a known history of atrial fibrillation. Robbie frames this with a "pumps, pipes, plasma" hypercoagulable-workup structure: pumps (cardioembolic sources beyond AFib, such as endocarditis or a cardiac tumor), pipes (vasculopathy or vasculitis, such as giant cell arteritis in an older adult with vision loss), and plasma (a more aggressively thrombogenic hypercoagulable state, especially malignancy or antiphospholipid antibody syndrome). He notes that anticoagulation reduces but never eliminates stroke risk, and that a single vascular territory affected — rather than multiple simultaneous emboli — makes an unusually aggressive hypercoagulable state less likely, even though the workup should still be pursued. Reza adds a live clinical question about whether apixaban's efficacy against intracranial atherosclerotic disease is as well established as it is for AFib and coronary disease (it may not be), which becomes relevant once outpatient neurology raises the possibility that the presumed AFib-mediated stroke may actually be atherosclerotic, prompting discussion about whether to add antiplatelet therapy on top of the apixaban.

The episode closes on the team debrief. When Reza's team learns the MRI is positive, individual members spontaneously express guilt: an intern recalls the patient reaching for his phone and not finding it days earlier, wondering if that was a missed clue; a senior resident wishes they'd done a more thorough neuro exam. Reza describes coaching the team through this without either dismissing their feelings or letting them collapse into self-blame — validating their emotional response as a sign they care, deliberately separating the acute emotional reaction from a later, calmer conversation about what (if anything) should change going forward, and explicitly naming that an outcome doesn't necessarily mean the prior reasoning was flawed. He also points out that the patient never reported any symptom to trigger an earlier workup, that thrombolysis would have been contraindicated regardless of timing given the patient's therapeutic anticoagulation, and that the case doesn't change his baseline practice of not performing a full neurologic exam on every patient — while still praising the team, and especially the physical therapist, for taking a non-physician colleague's escalating concern seriously rather than dismissing it in service of a faster disposition.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Case 1 turns on two adjacent illness-script corrections. First, the standard "positive CT confirms pancreatitis" script is challenged by recognizing that CT sensitivity for any intra-abdominal inflammatory process is fat-dependent — a lean patient can have real pancreatitis and a structurally normal scan, an imaging-sensitivity caveat that generalizes well beyond pancreatitis to abdominal imaging broadly. Second, the appearance of a plausible-sounding radiographic diagnosis (celiac artery compression) illustrates a base-rate trap: a common, usually incidental finding can hijack the differential unless it's held to its actual clinical illness script (chronic postprandial pain, food aversion, weight loss from eating avoidance) rather than accepted on imaging appearance alone. Trending the lipase — rather than treating it as a single static data point — is offered as a targeted way to distinguish signal (dynamic, disease-tracking) from noise (a persistently elevated macro-enzyme).

Case 2 is built around a different kind of bias: the instinct to treat a non-physician colleague's escalating concern as an obstacle to a desired disposition rather than as diagnostic signal. Robbie explicitly frames the older, hierarchical "physician knows all" model as outdated, and argues that the correct response to disagreement isn't unilateral dismissal or reflexive over-ordering, but going to see the patient together. The subsequent debrief models a similar corrective for outcome bias: an outcome (a stroke discovered days after it likely occurred) doesn't retroactively indict the reasoning that preceded it, especially when the patient never reported a symptom to act on and a treatment change (thrombolysis) would have been contraindicated regardless of timing given his anticoagulation status.

## Teaching Discussion

The pancreatitis case is a compact lesson in respecting the limits of a diagnostic test rather than trusting it uncritically. A negative CT is often treated as effectively ruling out pancreatitis, but its sensitivity is contingent on the presence of peripancreatic fat capable of showing inflammatory stranding — a caveat that applies to cachectic or simply lean patients across abdominal imaging generally, not just pancreatitis. Layered onto this is the seductive pull of an incidental "found" diagnosis (celiac artery compression): the reflex is to explain the whole picture with the new finding, but the correct move is to hold it to its own illness script and ask whether the clinical pattern (acute, episodic, resolving completely between attacks) actually fits, rather than accepting the radiographic label. The episode also reinforces an underappreciated risk factor for gallstones — weight loss, independent of the classic higher-BMI phenotype — and offers trending lipase as a narrow, situational tool (not a routine practice) for separating a real, evolving process from a persistently elevated but clinically inert macro-enzyme.

The second case centers on model of care: as allied health professionals' expertise deepens, treating their persistent concern as noise to be managed around — rather than signal to be examined — risks missing real pathology, as it nearly did here. The concrete remedy offered (go examine the patient together) is a low-cost way to resolve disagreement about what's actually being observed, without either physician unilateralism or over-ordering. Clinically, the case supplies two durable frameworks: macular sparing as a bedside-reasoning tool for localizing a homonymous visual field cut to cortex versus white matter, and "pumps, pipes, plasma" as a structured secondary-stroke workup for a clot that breaks through adequate anticoagulation. The closing debrief is itself a teaching moment on how to lead a team through a delayed diagnosis: validate the emotional response, deliberately delay the more analytical conversation until the acute reaction has settled, and separate the (bad) outcome from the (potentially reasonable) process that preceded it — while being honest that not every case should change future practice.
