## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> An 80-something man is sent to the ED for falls and placement while his longtime caretaker travels abroad — no loss of consciousness, no cognitive red flags on history, a reassuring gait exam with physical therapy, a normal head CT and neuro exam. The inpatient team reasonably concludes he is deconditioned and mildly cognitively impaired, and plans skilled nursing facility placement. Only in retrospect does Reza notice a heart rate that never rose above 50-52 — not even when his systolic blood pressure was 220 on arrival — prompting Rabih to build the case for chronotropic incompetence, and an EKG reveals complete heart block. The episode closes with an unusually direct joint reflection on whether an EKG should have been ordered much earlier, and what "no-miss" thinking should look like in a vulnerable, imprecise historian.

## Pearls

- Falls should be understood through the physiology of walking: sensory input (vestibular, visual, proprioceptive) must be gathered, integrated (cortex, basal ganglia, cerebellum), and converted into motor output — a fall reflects a lesion somewhere in that chain, and identifying which link is broken organizes the workup.
- Cortical status is the single biggest variable in fall risk and diagnostic yield: patients with impaired cognition (chronic or acute/intoxicated) commonly fall from a diffuse combination of mild contributing factors that are individually low-yield to chase acutely, whereas a faller with genuinely pristine cognition should raise real concern for a single, severe, treatable lesion — classically undiagnosed Parkinsonism or cryptic cervical myelopathy.
- Watching a patient walk, with and without their assistive device, before committing to a detailed neurologic exam is a "free" and highly informative data point — gait quality, base width, stooping, tremor, and foot drop are all visible without formal testing.
- Any older adult (roughly 60-65+) with a fall warrants a low threshold for head CT to exclude a subdural hematoma, regardless of how reassuring the rest of the presentation seems, given how easily this diagnosis can be missed on history and exam alone.
- When a reassuring gait and neuro exam leave a clinician without a clear next hypothesis, it's useful to explicitly ask "what am I actually trying to accomplish right now?" and build a "no-miss" checklist (here: head CT, basic neuro exam for proprioception/weakness/reflexes) rather than drifting toward an anchoring default explanation like deconditioning.
- The normal physiologic response to standing is an increase in heart rate (and stroke volume) to compensate for reduced venous return; a heart rate that fails to rise appropriately on standing (or in response to a physiologic stressor generally) is chronotropic incompetence, and should prompt consideration of a cause rather than being dismissed as an incidental, age-related number.
- The base rate of "abnormal-looking" numbers in older adults is so high that many are genuinely incidental and asymptomatic — but the absence of a robust, provable symptom-vital-sign correlation is not the same as proof that a number is meaningless, and a low-cost test (an EKG) can resolve the uncertainty cheaply.
- The differential for an inappropriately low or non-rising heart rate divides into extrinsic causes (medications, most classically beta blockers or other rate-slowing agents) and intrinsic causes (sinus node dysfunction, AV conduction system disease, or autonomic failure/dysautonomia) — hypovolemia and obstructive intracardiac lesions like aortic stenosis or HOCM do not, by themselves, blunt the heart rate response and should be excluded from this specific differential.
- Bradycardia paired with hypertension (rather than the more intuitively expected hypotension) can reflect a structurally healthy heart's response to a slow rate: more time for ventricular filling increases preload and, via the Frank-Starling mechanism, increases stroke volume — in a heart without concomitant myocardial disease, this can produce a compensatory hypertensive response rather than hypoperfusion, the same principle that lets elite endurance athletes tolerate resting bradycardia.
- Before committing an older patient with new complete heart block to a permanent pacemaker, a brief but deliberate search for reversible or secondary causes is worthwhile, organized as "two I's and two G's": Infection (Lyme disease, geography-dependent; Chagas disease, notably more prevalent in some U.S. regions like Los Angeles than commonly assumed, but typically accompanied by cardiomyopathy/heart failure), Infiltrative disease (cardiac amyloidosis, particularly if echo shows suggestive features like pan-chamber hypertrophy with apical sparing), and Granulomatous disease (cardiac sarcoidosis, essentially never presenting for the first time after age 70) and Genetic disease (rare without a strong family history) — with age, geography, and the absence of heart failure or echo red flags together supporting a default diagnosis of age-related fibrotic conduction system disease when the picture is otherwise clean.

## Problem Representation

An elderly man sent to the ED for recurrent falls and caregiver-driven placement, whose reassuring gait, neuro exam, and head CT initially supported a deconditioning/possible-dementia framing, until a persistently low heart rate — including during a hypertensive episode — led to the diagnosis of complete heart block.

## Case Narrative

Reza described picking up this patient on a hospitalist service after the ED admitted him with a chief concern of "falls." Before any case-specific data was given, Rabih built out a general approach: falls represent the failure of a chain running from sensory input (vestibular, visual, proprioceptive) through central integration (cortex, basal ganglia, cerebellum) to motor output, with a lesion possible at any point in that chain. He emphasized that cortical status is the single most important variable determining both fall risk and diagnostic yield — patients with cognitive impairment (chronic dementia or acute intoxication) commonly fall from a combination of mild, individually low-yield contributing factors, whereas a faller with genuinely intact cognition raises real concern for a single, severe lesion, most classically undiagnosed Parkinsonism or a cryptic cervical myelopathy, both of which are recognized as frequently diagnosed late.

The patient was in his early 80s and had lived with a friend for 15 years; that friend, departing for a trip to Europe, had asked him to go to the ED specifically to arrange placement until they returned. He reported two falls over the preceding month, no loss of consciousness, an episode of dizziness that resolved after receiving his home blood pressure medications in the ED, and attributed his walker use to deconditioning rather than a specific new deficit. Rabih noted that this history, on its face, did not suggest prominent substance use or cortical dysfunction, but flagged that the patient's age alone gave a non-trivial pretest probability of at least mild cognitive impairment, and recommended watching him walk — with and without the walker — as a "free," highly informative data point before committing to a detailed formal neuro exam.

This unfolded essentially as predicted: a physical therapist, present at bedside, observed the patient ambulating with his walker at a reasonable pace, without tremor or an apparent focal neurologic deficit, though flagging a possible underlying dementia and recommending skilled nursing facility placement given his reliance on his caretaker's familiar environment. With this reassuring screening exam in hand, Rabih reasoned that the probability of a single severe lesion (like Parkinsonism) had dropped substantially, and pivoted to an explicit "no-miss" framework: given the patient's age and fall history, a head CT to exclude a subdural hematoma was non-negotiable, alongside a basic neuro exam focused specifically on proprioception, focal weakness, and reflexes — while flagging that a mild metabolic disturbance (an elevated CK, unrecognized anemia) could be quietly unmasking or worsening an older patient's baseline vulnerability without being obvious on a surface presentation.

The neuro exam was normal (reflexes symmetric, 5/5 strength throughout, intact proprioception), basic labs were unremarkable (no anemia, no renal insufficiency), and the head CT showed no acute intracranial abnormality. The inpatient team, discussing the case, converged on a reasonable-sounding narrative: an older, likely mildly demented, deconditioned patient reliant on his caretaker, for whom PT-guided placement was the appropriate plan. Rabih, working through the case in real time without yet knowing what came next, agreed this seemed like a case where the "problem list" had effectively gone empty, and where he, as an attending, would have been comfortable letting the plan proceed as an essentially outpatient issue being managed within a hospital admission.

Reza then revealed the piece of data that reframed the entire case: the patient's presenting vital signs in the ED, during his dizziness, showed a systolic blood pressure of 220 alongside a heart rate of 50 — and it turned out he had not been taking his home antihypertensive or oral diabetes medications, so he was given amlodipine and hydralazine in the ED, which brought his systolic down to the 130s. Orthostatic vitals were then checked: supine heart rate 50, standing heart rate 52 (essentially unchanged), with systolic blood pressure 120-130 in both positions and no reported symptoms during the maneuver — though Reza candidly wondered aloud whether the patient's ability to reliably report a subtle symptom like orthostatic dizziness might itself have been limited by unrecognized cognitive impairment.

Rabih built the physiologic case from this: normally, standing reduces venous return, and the heart compensates by increasing both stroke volume and heart rate to preserve cardiac output and protect against presyncope; a heart rate essentially unchanged on standing (50 to 52) represents a blunted, insufficient compensatory response — chronotropic incompetence — if the patient truly has symptoms attributable to standing. He explicitly acknowledged the challenge in interpreting this: the base rate of "abnormal" numbers in older adults is high enough that many are asymptomatic and incidental, and there was no cleanly provable symptom-vital-sign correlation in this specific patient. Still, given that no other explanation for the falls had been found, he recommended at minimum an EKG to exclude significant intrinsic conduction disease, framing the differential for an inappropriately low or non-rising heart rate as either extrinsic (a rate-slowing medication — not applicable here, since the patient was only on amlodipine and hydralazine, neither of which slows heart rate; he specifically noted that hydralazine is often paired with a rate-controlling agent precisely because it tends to provoke reflex tachycardia, making its use here without any resulting tachycardia itself a subtle retrospective clue) or intrinsic (significant conduction system disease visible on EKG, or dysautonomia) — while noting that hypovolemia and obstructive intracardiac lesions like aortic stenosis or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy do not, on their own, blunt the heart rate response and could be set aside from this specific line of reasoning.

The EKG, reviewed live, showed a slow but regular rhythm with visible P wave activity (excluding sinus arrest), but a P-to-QRS relationship that shifted chaotically from beat to beat — the P wave appeared at a different position relative to each QRS complex across the strip. Using his fingers to "march out" the P waves and QRS complexes independently, Rabih confirmed that the P waves marched at their own steady rate and the QRS complexes marched at a different, independent rate — the P waves and QRS complexes were not communicating with each other at all, confirming complete (third-degree) AV block.

Reza then asked Rabih to explain a puzzling piece of the puzzle: why this patient had presented with hypertension rather than the more intuitively expected hypotension, given his bradycardia. Rabih explained the physiology (credited to a mentor, Harry Hollander): in most bradycardic patients, hypotension is expected because the conduction system lesion typically coexists with underlying myocardial disease that limits the heart's ability to compensate — but a slow heart rate, on its own, in a structurally healthy heart, allows for maximal ventricular filling during the prolonged diastolic period, which via the Frank-Starling mechanism increases stroke volume; if the myocardium is otherwise healthy and can eject that larger volume effectively, the result is a higher, not lower, blood pressure — the same physiologic principle that allows elite endurance athletes to tolerate resting bradycardia without symptoms. This likely also explained why the patient's presentation had gone unrecognized for as long as it had: an otherwise healthy heart compensating well enough to remain largely asymptomatic.

The patient was transferred to cardiology, with a plan for permanent pacemaker placement, initially attributed simply to age-related degeneration of the conduction system. Before proceeding, Rabih outlined a brief but deliberate framework for excluding reversible or secondary causes of complete heart block, organized as "two I's and two G's": Infection (Lyme disease, essentially excluded by geography in this case; Chagas disease, notably more common in some U.S. regions, including Los Angeles, than commonly appreciated, but typically accompanied by a cardiomyopathy/heart failure picture this patient lacked), Infiltrative disease (cardiac amyloidosis, which would be far more concerning with suggestive echo findings like pan-chamber hypertrophy with apical sparing), Granulomatous disease (cardiac sarcoidosis, essentially never presenting for the first time after age 70, in sharp contrast to a hypothetical 45-year-old with prominent lymphadenopathy), and Genetic disease (relevant primarily with a strong family history). With this patient's age, U.S. residence without relevant travel, absence of heart failure symptoms, and unremarkable echo findings, age-related fibrotic conduction system disease was judged a reasonable default diagnosis — while Rabih explicitly walked through how each of several hypothetical variations on the same case (residence in a Lyme-endemic area, an amyloid-suggestive echo, younger age with lymphadenopathy, a strong family history) would each independently mandate ruling out a specific alternative etiology before proceeding to a permanent device. His pacemaker was ultimately delayed briefly by an incidental, asymptomatic COVID diagnosis rather than any diagnostic uncertainty about the heart block itself.

The episode closed with an unusually direct, jointly conducted reflection on whether an EKG should have been ordered earlier — by the ED, or by Reza's own team on admission. Rabih concluded that, in retrospect, yes: specifically because the team had not had a clear sense of exactly what problem they were solving for, the "no-miss" checklist built at that point of uncertainty should have explicitly included an EKG alongside the head CT, particularly given the patient's imprecise and possibly cognitively-limited ability to report his own symptoms. He generalized this into a heuristic for vulnerable, poor-historian patients: when the story is unclear and the stakes of missing something are high, "more is more, not less is more." Reza, reflecting separately, described the case as a "perfect storm" of situational distractors (a caregiver-driven, placement-motivated visit; no loss of consciousness reported; an easy deconditioning narrative) that led the team to underweight a persistently low heart rate as an incidental, age-related finding rather than actively investigating it, and committed to treating bradycardia with the same reflexive EKG threshold he already applies to tachycardia going forward.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| No loss of consciousness reported, caregiver-driven visit for placement | Frames the presentation toward a benign, situational/deconditioning narrative |
| Reassuring PT-observed gait with the walker | Lowers probability of a single severe neurologic lesion (e.g., Parkinsonism) |
| Normal neuro exam, normal basic labs, normal head CT | Lowers probability of an acute intracranial or peripheral neurologic cause of falls |
| Heart rate 50 at initial presentation despite SBP 220 | Raises suspicion for an inappropriately blunted heart rate response (chronotropic incompetence) |
| Heart rate essentially unchanged (50 → 52) on standing | Confirms a blunted compensatory heart rate response to orthostatic stress |
| Hydralazine given without any resulting reflex tachycardia | Retrospective clue supporting an intrinsic chronotropic problem rather than normal physiology |
| EKG showing independently "marching" P waves and QRS complexes | Confirms complete (third-degree) AV block |
| No heart failure symptoms, unremarkable echo, no travel/exposure history, no lymphadenopathy, no family history | Supports age-related fibrotic conduction system disease over infectious, infiltrative, granulomatous, or genetic causes |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Complete heart block in an older adult illness script: an elderly patient with vague, nonspecific symptoms (falls, dizziness, fatigue) and a heart rate that fails to rise appropriately with physiologic stress (including, paradoxically, presenting with hypertension rather than hypotension if the myocardium is otherwise healthy), diagnosed on EKG by finding P waves and QRS complexes marching independently at different rates.

The case is an explicit, jointly-narrated example of anchoring on a plausible, low-effort narrative (deconditioning, mild dementia, a benign situational reason for the ED visit) once several individually reassuring data points accumulate — and of the discipline required to keep asking "what am I actually trying to rule out here" even after a workup feels functionally complete. Both hosts explicitly credit the eventual catch to a single clinician (the inpatient resident) simply noticing and acting on a heart rate of 48, rather than to any earlier, more systematic step in the workup.

## Teaching Discussion

The episode's central teaching point is the discipline of maintaining an explicit "no-miss" list even after an initial workup feels reassuring and complete — in this case, recognizing in retrospect that an EKG belonged on that list from the outset, specifically because the clinical picture never resolved into full diagnostic confidence and the patient's ability to reliably report subtle symptoms may have been limited.

A second teaching point is the physiologic reasoning connecting chronotropic incompetence to hypertension rather than hypotension via preserved ventricular filling and the Frank-Starling mechanism — a counterintuitive pairing of findings that becomes logical once traced through the underlying physiology, and one that likely explains why this patient's condition escaped detection for as long as it did.

A third, more systems-oriented teaching point is the "two I's and two G's" framework for screening out reversible or secondary causes of heart block before committing an older patient to a permanent pacemaker, alongside the explicit demonstration of how the same clinical picture would warrant a completely different workup path under different contextual features (geography, echo findings, age, family history).
