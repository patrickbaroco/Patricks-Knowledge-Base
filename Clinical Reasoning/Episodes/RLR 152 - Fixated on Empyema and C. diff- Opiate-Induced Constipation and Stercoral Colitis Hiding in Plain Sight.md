## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Robbie presents an unusual case for him — receiving an outside referral call rather than working ED triage — about an 82-year-old man two months removed from a severe empyema, now sent in by his primary care doctor for an isolated new neutrophilic leukocytosis. Both hosts narrate their real-time reasoning in parallel, both anchoring first on empyema recurrence and then C. diff colitis, before a subtle history detail (stool leaking with urination) and a CT scan reveal the actual diagnosis: opiate-induced constipation with stercoral colitis, a dangerous but overlooked complication of the very opiates prescribed for the patient's prior chest tube pain.

## Pearls

- The validity of a clinical decision is independent of its outcome — ordering or not ordering a test can be the right call given the information available at the time, regardless of what that test (or a later one) ultimately shows.
- A useful framework for managing complex hospitalized patients: on day one, ask "what is the day-one hospital problem" and focus cognitive energy there first, without ignoring other issues but without letting them distract from the primary reason for admission — credited to a colleague's ("Vivek's") teaching.
- Getting comfortable with diagnostic uncertainty and leaving some threads intentionally loose (without neglecting them) is protective against burnout and allows more focused investment of cognitive energy where it matters most.
- Speaking directly with the referring/outside provider before reacting to an alarming chart history helps prevent premature anchoring on a dramatic prior diagnosis before knowing why the patient is actually presenting now.
- Leukocytosis should be interpreted in context: an isolated leukocytosis in a patient with a recent serious infection deserves real respect (illustrated by a personal case of missed epidural abscess/discitis initially dismissed), while an isolated leukocytosis in a patient with a plausible benign explanation (e.g., a healing fracture) can be reasonably attributed to that cause without an exhaustive workup.
- Empyema follows a defined illness-script progression: simple pneumonia, to complicated parapneumonic effusion (an effusion meeting specific criteria), to empyema (confirmed by pleural fluid findings — positive gram stain, low glucose, frank pus).
- Empyema should be conceptually treated like a septic joint — a walled-off collection of pus requiring source control (chest tube drainage), since antibiotics alone are insufficient; oral antibiotic courses following adequate source control can run up to six weeks given the severity of the infection.
- Post-empyema discharge antibiotic choice (e.g., Augmentin over amoxicillin alone) often specifically accounts for anaerobic organisms that are difficult to culture but commonly implicated in empyema.
- A patient reporting stool leakage specifically with attempts to urinate, or a "brownish liquid" without true diarrhea, can represent overflow fecal incontinence around a fecal impaction rather than genuine diarrhea — a distinction with major management implications.
- Bowel and bladder dysfunction together classically raises concern for a spinal cord process (e.g., cauda equina syndrome), but this should be weighed against the presence or absence of other supporting findings (a sensory level, focal weakness) before pursuing that workup aggressively.
- Stercoral colitis results from fecal impaction distending the rectum enough to compress the rectal wall's blood supply, causing ischemic inflammation of the rectal lining; this makes the rectum fragile and predisposes to perforation — a life-threatening complication of severe, unaddressed constipation.
- Management of stercoral colitis/fecal impaction requires action from both directions: below (enema or manual disimpaction to evacuate the rectal vault) and above (a promotility/osmotic laxative regimen, escalating to a more potent osmotic agent like magnesium citrate if renal function allows), with the patient kept in a monitored setting until evacuation is achieved.
- Methylnaltrexone, a peripherally-acting mu-opioid receptor antagonist, is a targeted option for confirmed opiate-induced constipation when standard laxative regimens are insufficient.
- Opiates prescribed for pain should routinely be paired with a bowel regimen; a missed or inadequate laxative co-prescription is a preventable driver of severe complications like stercoral colitis.
- The reasoning path that leads to ordering a test does not need to be correct for the test itself to be the right one to order — a "right test, wrong reason" scenario can still produce the correct diagnosis, but shouldn't be mistaken for validating the original flawed reasoning in retrospect.
- Starting clinical reasoning from the patient's own words, rather than secondhand summaries filtered through prior chart history or other providers' framing, is often the fastest route to the correct diagnosis — a theme the presenting host explicitly credits for why he prefers direct triage encounters over receiving secondhand handoffs.

## Problem Representation

An 82-year-old man with moderate, asymptomatic aortic stenosis and a severe empyema treated two months prior with chest tube drainage and antibiotics (including a prolonged opiate course for chest tube pain), presenting with an isolated new neutrophilic leukocytosis and vague abdominal distension with stool leakage during urination, was sequentially worked up under the anchoring hypotheses of empyema recurrence and then C. diff colitis before a CT scan revealed severe fecal impaction with stercoral colitis — ultimately attributed to unaddressed opiate-induced constipation from his recent hospitalization.

## Case Narrative

Before the case, Reza shares a personal story from his own inpatient service: his intern, Paul, wanted to order a CT scan that Reza and his senior resident, Emma, initially declined; palliative care later independently recommended the same CT, which revealed a significant, care-changing abnormality. Reza publicly credited Paul for advocating for the scan, only for Emma to point out that the CT's validity as a decision is independent of its outcome — ordering or not ordering a test can be reasonable given the information available at the time, regardless of what it later shows. Reza reflects on how much courage it took Emma, early in her training, to openly disagree with her attending's plan, and credits her for the caliber of feedback and self-reflection involved.

Robbie then presents an unusual case for him — he typically works ED triage and rarely receives outside referral calls, but did on this occasion. A referring hospital left a callback number without patient details; before Robbie even reached the caller, pulling the chart revealed the patient had, two months prior, suffered a severe empyema requiring chest tube drainage (growing streptococcal organisms), a three-week hospitalization including thrombolytics (DNase/tPA) and multiple chest tubes, followed by three weeks at a skilled nursing facility, and discharge home just two weeks before this call. Robbie asks Reza what frame of mind this history alone puts him in, before any details of the current presentation are known.

Reza credits a colleague, Vivek — a former Hopkins colleague, now a rheumatologist/hospitalist at the San Francisco VA — for a framework that has made hospital medicine more manageable: on day one of a hospitalization, ask "what is the day-one hospital problem," and focus cognitive energy there first, without ignoring other issues but without letting a complicated chart distract from the primary reason for the current presentation. Reza states that regardless of how alarming the chart looks, his first move would be to hear directly from the outside provider about why the patient is presenting now, before investing energy speculating about complications or recurrence.

The caller turns out to be the patient's primary care physician from Clear Lake, not an ED physician. He explains he hadn't yet seen the patient since his SNF discharge, but had ordered labs ahead of a scheduled visit; those labs showed a white count of 16,000 (up from a discharge white count of 7), with otherwise normal hemoglobin, platelets, renal function, and liver enzymes — and a repeat SNF-drawn set of labs confirmed the elevation. Unable to reach the patient directly, he reached the patient's daughter, who couldn't relay much about how the patient was doing, prompting the primary care doctor to send the patient to the VA ED with his daughter, arriving in about two hours.

Reza's real-time reasoning: leukocytosis functions as a marker of inflammation, and a neutrophil-predominant pattern implies mature neutrophils demarginating under physiologic stress. He recalls a formative case of his own — a patient with back pain and an unexplained leukocytosis he nearly discharged despite a negative gadolinium MRI, until ID insisted on a repeat MRI that revealed an epidural abscess and discitis — crediting that case with teaching him to respect leukocytosis. He contrasts this with a current inpatient of his own whose isolated leukocytosis is reasonably attributed to a healing fracture given the absence of any infectious findings. Given this patient's recent empyema, Reza acknowledges he is appropriately biased toward considering recurrence or incomplete source control, especially since the patient may not present with a dramatic fever given his recent partial treatment. He plans to look for other markers of systemic inflammation (thrombocytosis or thrombocytopenia, night sweats, hypoalbuminemia, ESR/CRP) and is already anticipating a CT scan, while acknowledging he hasn't yet seen the patient.

Robbie fills in more chart detail: the patient is an otherwise healthy 82-year-old with moderate, closely-followed but asymptomatic aortic stenosis, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. His empyema was treated with IV antibiotics, grew strep, and he was discharged on Augmentin rather than amoxicillin alone, specifically to cover difficult-to-culture anaerobic organisms often implicated in empyema. He experienced significant pain from his chest tube and was discharged still on opiates; he finished his course of Augmentin about a week prior to this presentation but was still taking opiates.

Reza reviews the empyema illness script (crediting a colleague, Jack Penner, for the framework): a simple pneumonia progresses to a complicated parapneumonic effusion once the effusion meets specific criteria, and progresses further to a true empyema once pleural fluid sampling shows a positive gram stain, low glucose, and frank pus. He likens an empyema to a septic joint — a walled-off collection of pus requiring source control (the chest tube) since antibiotics alone are insufficient — and notes oral antibiotic courses following adequate drainage can run up to six weeks given the severity of the infection. Because empyema is a comparatively unambiguous diagnosis, Reza doubts the original diagnosis was wrong, and instead broadens his differential: the new leukocytosis could be entirely unrelated to the empyema and instead reflect a complication of treatment — specifically C. diff from the Augmentin course, prompting questions about diarrhea or abdominal pain — or, less likely given no signs pointing that way, endocarditis seeding the stenotic aortic valve, or a medication reaction. He otherwise applies a standard infectious framework across respiratory, GI, skin/soft tissue, hepatobiliary, and bloodstream sources, with a low threshold for blood cultures given the possibility of transient bacteremia from the empyema not being fully addressed by oral antibiotics alone.

The actual bedside history: the patient looks well, denies chest pain or shortness of breath, and reports his main concern is mild abdominal distension and occasional "runny stool." On clarification, this turns out to mean that every time he tries to urinate, stool also comes out, and he's noticed a brownish liquid when wiping. He denies fever, chills, cough, dysuria, or rash. Vitals are normal and the exam is unremarkable aside from mild abdominal distension.

Reza's reaction: the story "sets up" for C. diff, and he'd prioritize a stool C. diff test before reaching for a CT scan — describing a range of reasonable approaches from outpatient watchful waiting pending the study to empiric treatment for higher-risk patients, while noting that C. diff severity (mild versus severe) is risk-stratified using white count and creatinine and can influence antibiotic choice (oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin per IDSA guidance). He acknowledges an ED-based clinician might reasonably have a lower threshold for CT given the setting, but states his own initial approach would be a repeat CBC, BMP, and stool C. diff test.

This is exactly what was done. The repeat white count comes back down to 11 with otherwise normal labs; a chest x-ray shows no pleural effusion and is otherwise normal; the C. diff result won't be available until the next day. Reza discusses this update through an explicit shared decision-making lens with the patient: presenting the reassuring imaging and labs, offering empiric treatment for presumed antibiotic-associated or C. diff diarrhea with close follow-up if the patient prefers to go home, while also validating the alternative of staying for a firmer diagnosis given the effort involved in coming in — deliberately avoiding false certainty about what the diagnosis definitively is.

A key new detail emerges: after four to five hours in the ED, the patient has had no actual episodes of loose stool. On rectal/pelvic exam prompted by his report of stooling with urination, a small amount of liquid brown material is found in and around the anus — but no active diarrhea. Reza, initially unsure what to make of this, recalls a personal aside about the physiologic link between urination and bowel movements, and raises (then largely sets aside) concern for a spinal cord process given the bowel-and-bladder association, since there's no sensory level or focal weakness to support it. Given the combination of distension, minimal true stool output, and the apparent overflow pattern, he decides a CT abdomen and pelvis with contrast is warranted to evaluate for a more severe colonic process, including the possibility that a simple C. diff picture has evolved into complicated C. diff colitis.

The CT reveals a large stool burden, mild proximal bowel dilation, and stercoral colitis of the rectum.

Reza immediately recognizes he had overlooked a detail Robbie had repeatedly emphasized earlier — the patient's ongoing opiate use — and reframes the entire case around opiate-induced constipation as the better-fitting diagnosis: it explains the large stool burden and rectal inflammation on imaging, the absence of leukocytosis to support an acute infectious process, the abdominal distension, and the overflow incontinence pattern, better than either empyema recurrence or C. diff. He explains the pathophysiology of stercoral colitis: fecal impaction distends the rectum enough to compress the blood supply to the rectal wall, causing ischemic inflammation; this renders the rectal lining fragile and predisposed to perforation, a life-threatening complication of severe, unaddressed constipation. Management requires action from both directions — an enema (he favors a mineral oil-based enema, assuming no electrolyte derangements) or manual disimpaction to evacuate the rectal vault, combined with an oral laxative regimen from above (typically a promotility agent like senna plus an osmotic agent like Miralax, escalating to magnesium citrate as a more potent osmotic option if renal function allows) — with the patient kept in a monitored hospital setting until evacuation is achieved. Given his ongoing opiate requirement, Reza also considers methylnaltrexone, a peripherally-acting opioid antagonist specific to opiate-induced constipation, and flags the broader question of whether the patient was ever prescribed an adequate bowel regimen alongside his opiates in the first place.

Robbie draws out the episode's central teaching point: the validity of a test's outcome doesn't retroactively validate the reasoning that led to ordering it. The CT scan that revealed the answer was ordered while both hosts were still fixated on empyema recurrence and then C. diff colitis — not on opiate-induced constipation — illustrating that a case can look, in retrospect, like a triumph of diagnostic reasoning when the actual reasoning process was substantially off-target until very late. He notes the diagnosis was present in the patient's own words from the start: the patient wasn't describing diarrhea, he was describing straining with overflow leakage — a "gut outflow obstruction," directly analogous to urinary outflow obstruction.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Data Point | Effect on Differential |
|---|---|
| Recent severe empyema (2 months prior) plus new isolated neutrophilic leukocytosis | Strongly raises concern for empyema recurrence or incomplete source control as the leading hypothesis |
| White count trending down (16,000 → 11,000) without intervention, normal CXR, no pleural effusion | Substantially lowers probability of empyema recurrence or an acute pulmonary process |
| Recent Augmentin course, now completed; report of "runny stool" | Raises suspicion for C. diff colitis or antibiotic-associated diarrhea |
| No actual loose stool over several ED hours; small amount of liquid brown material on rectal exam without active diarrhea | Reframes the presenting complaint from true diarrhea to overflow leakage around a fecal impaction |
| Ongoing opiate use since hospitalization, previously underweighted in the differential | Raises opiate-induced constipation to the leading explanation once explicitly reconsidered |
| CT: large stool burden, mild proximal bowel dilation, stercoral colitis of the rectum | Confirms severe fecal impaction with early ischemic rectal complication, consistent with opiate-induced constipation rather than infectious colitis |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Anchoring on a dramatic recent diagnosis (empyema)**: both hosts' initial reasoning was substantially shaped by the patient's severe, recent hospitalization, illustrating how a prominent prior diagnosis can dominate framing of an unrelated new presentation.
- **Sequential anchor-shifting (empyema → C. diff) without reaching the true diagnosis**: even after moving off the empyema anchor, the second anchor (C. diff) was also incorrect, showing that correcting one bias doesn't guarantee landing on the right hypothesis — it can simply substitute a new one.
- **Underweighting a repeatedly-stated detail (opiates)**: Robbie explicitly emphasized the patient's opiate use multiple times, but Reza did not incorporate it into his working differential until the CT forced a reassessment — a concrete illustration of how salient historical framing (empyema, leukocytosis) can crowd out equally important but less dramatic details.
- **"Right test, wrong reason" as a distinct category of diagnostic success**: explicitly named by Robbie — ordering the correct study while reasoning toward the wrong diagnosis, which can look identical to skilled diagnostic reasoning in retrospect without actually being it.
- **Starting from the patient's own words as a bias-correction strategy**: Robbie's explicit preference for direct triage encounters over receiving filtered secondhand histories, reflecting a broader theme that unfiltered patient language ("when I pee, I poop") often contains the diagnostic answer more directly than downstream clinical framing.
- **Decision validity independent of outcome**: reinforced twice in the episode — once in the Emma/Paul framing story, and again in Robbie's closing reflection on the CT scan — as a recurring meta-lesson about not over- or under-crediting a decision based on how it turned out.

## Teaching Discussion

The episode is structured as a live demonstration of anchoring bias and its correction: both hosts narrate their reasoning in near-real time, both initially and reasonably prioritizing empyema recurrence given the recent severe infection, both pivoting to C. diff once antibiotic exposure and loose-stool language appeared, and both only reaching the true diagnosis — opiate-induced constipation with stercoral colitis — once a granular bedside detail (no actual diarrhea, overflow leakage on exam) forced a reassessment. Reza's explicit, on-air acknowledgment that he had "forgotten" the opiate detail Robbie kept emphasizing is presented not as an embarrassment but as an honest illustration of how easily a salient historical anchor can suppress attention to an equally important but less dramatic data point.

The stercoral colitis pathophysiology teaching — impaction leading to pressure-induced ischemia and perforation risk — is presented as a genuinely dangerous, underappreciated complication of unaddressed opiate-induced constipation, reframing "just constipation" as a potentially life-threatening diagnosis in the right clinical context (severe distension, minimal true stool output, overflow incontinence).

The meta-lesson, made explicit twice, is that the correctness of a downstream test result doesn't validate the reasoning that led to ordering it — a companion to the earlier Emma/Paul story about decision validity being independent of outcome. Robbie's closing reflection extends this into a broader philosophy of practice: medicine's difficulty lies precisely in its non-linearity relative to more procedurally masterable fields, and the antidote to that difficulty is returning, as consistently as possible, to the patient's own words rather than layered secondhand framing.
