## Pearls

- Odynophagia (pain WITH swallowing) and dysphagia (difficulty WITH swallowing) point to different disease categories and should not be treated as synonyms — odynophagia behaves like a mucosal/inflammatory process ("colitis of the esophagus"), while dysphagia behaves like a mechanical/motility process ("obstruction of the esophagus").
- A prior diagnosis should be revisited, not assumed correct, when the same symptom recurs — this patient's earlier "candidal esophagitis" label did not survive scrutiny once the actual biopsy result (negative for candida) was checked.
- A low hemoglobin should never be interpreted in isolation from the rest of the CBC — checking the white count and platelet count converted an "acute anemia" framing into an acute pancytopenia, which changes the differential entirely.
- Checking ferritin early in anemia/pancytopenia workups is useful both diagnostically (elevated ferritin as an acute-phase reactant can suggest inflammation, or in the context of low B12/folate can reflect ineffective erythropoiesis) and as a way of building a case against occult GI bleeding as the cause.
- The "4 S" framework for pancytopenia — Systemic disease, Substance (drugs/toxins/alcohol), Space-occupying process in the marrow, and Stem cell disorder — helps generalists prioritize the workup, since the first two categories are readily evaluable without hematology or a bone marrow biopsy.
- EKG changes that do not resolve with transfusion should not be dismissed as an artifact of anemia — this is a signal to pursue the changes as a primary cardiac finding, which in this case revealed genuine occult triple-vessel coronary artery disease.
- A normal or elevated ferritin, combined with no other evidence of bleeding, can and should be used to actively exclude gastrointestinal blood loss as a cause of anemia — even when the patient's GI symptoms make a bleeding source clinically tempting.
- Finding an essentially identical prior episode in the chart (same labs, same trajectory, same spontaneous resolution with abstinence roughly one year earlier) is powerful corroborating evidence for a diagnosis and should prompt an active search of old records rather than treating each presentation as isolated.

## Problem Representation

A well-controlled HIV-positive man with recurrent odynophagia and acute pancytopenia with incidental non-resolving EKG changes, ultimately unified by an alcohol relapse causing combined B12/folate deficiency, with occult triple-vessel coronary artery disease identified as a separate concurrent finding.

## Case Narrative

The patient, an HIV-positive man with well-controlled disease, presented with recurrent odynophagia — pain with swallowing. The discussion opened with a framework for distinguishing odynophagia from dysphagia: dysphagia, difficulty getting food to move, behaves analogously to a bowel obstruction, pointing toward mechanical or motility causes; odynophagia, pain during swallowing, behaves analogously to colitis, pointing toward an inflammatory or infectious mucosal process such as esophagitis. Given the patient's odynophagia, esophagitis was the leading consideration.

A key retrospective point was then raised: the patient had a similar episode roughly a year earlier that had been diagnosed as candidal esophagitis. On reviewing that prior workup more carefully, the biopsy from that admission was actually negative for candida — meaning the prior diagnosis had never been histologically confirmed and should be treated with more skepticism rather than simply carried forward as an established fact.

On this admission, the patient's hemoglobin returned at 7.2 with an MCV of 100 (macrocytic). The consulting GI and cardiology teams initially framed this purely as "acute anemia," prompting urgent scheduling of an upper endoscopy to look for a bleeding or inflammatory source. Rabih pushed back on this framing by first advocating for checking a ferritin level early, and by insisting on looking at the entire CBC rather than the hemoglobin in isolation. Doing so revealed a white count of 3 and a platelet count of 66 — meaning the patient did not have isolated anemia at all, but acute pancytopenia, a substantially different and broader diagnostic problem.

To organize the pancytopenia workup, a "4 S" framework was applied: Systemic disease (e.g., infection, autoimmune disease, HIV itself, liver disease with hypersplenism), Substance (medications, alcohol, other toxins suppressing the marrow), Space-occupying process (marrow infiltration by malignancy, granulomatous disease, fibrosis), and Stem cell disorder (primary bone marrow failure syndromes, myelodysplasia). The discussion emphasized that the first two categories — systemic disease and substance exposure — are readily evaluable by a generalist without invasive testing or hematology involvement, and should be worked through first before escalating to marrow-directed categories.

A separate thread emerged from the admission EKG, which showed subtle ST depressions in leads V2 through V4. The initial instinct was to attribute this to demand-related changes from the anemia, expecting it to resolve with transfusion. However, the ST depressions persisted even after the hemoglobin was corrected with transfusion, which was treated as a signal that this represented a true, independent cardiac finding rather than a secondary phenomenon of anemia. This was pursued further and ultimately traced to genuine triple-vessel coronary artery disease on cardiac catheterization, managed with coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG); a follow-up EKG after revascularization normalized.

Returning to the pancytopenia and esophagitis, the unifying explanation was an alcohol relapse: the patient had returned to drinking 7 to 8 alcoholic beverages per day with poor oral intake. Laboratory workup showed an elevated ferritin (437), a low B12 (approximately 200), and elevated methylmalonic acid and homocysteine, along with a low folate — consistent with combined B12 and folate deficiency from alcohol use and poor nutrition, sufficient to explain both the pancytopenia (megaloblastic marrow suppression) and, via the same mechanism of impaired mucosal turnover and possible direct alcohol-related mucosal injury, the recurrent odynophagia/esophagitis. Strong corroborating evidence was found in the chart: an essentially identical episode exactly one year prior, with a similar lab pattern, that had also spontaneously resolved within about a month of abstinence from alcohol.

The discussion closed on whether to pursue intrinsic factor antibody testing to evaluate for pernicious anemia as a contributing or alternative cause of the B12 deficiency. Rabih's position was that this was probably not the right test to prioritize at this point, given how thoroughly the patient's overall clinical picture was currently dominated by active alcohol use disorder, and that pursuing a secondary, less clinically urgent diagnosis risked distracting from the primary problem requiring intervention.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Alcohol-related combined B12/folate deficiency illness script: heavy alcohol intake with poor oral intake, macrocytic anemia progressing to pancytopenia via marrow suppression, elevated ferritin (as an acute-phase reactant / marker of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than iron overload), low B12 and folate with elevated MMA and homocysteine, esophagitis/odynophagia from direct mucosal effects and nutritional deficiency, and a pattern of spontaneous resolution with abstinence.

The case demonstrates anchoring bias at two points: first, in carrying forward an unconfirmed "candidal esophagitis" diagnosis without checking the actual biopsy result; second, in the consulting teams' framing of the low hemoglobin as isolated "acute anemia" requiring urgent endoscopy, which anchored the workup toward a GI-bleeding pathway before the rest of the CBC was even considered. Both anchors were corrected by returning to primary data — the actual pathology report, and the actual complete blood count — rather than accepting the summary label handed off by a prior team.

## Teaching Discussion

The central teaching point is a caution against interpreting a single abnormal lab value in isolation: framing this patient's presentation as "acute anemia" (driving toward endoscopy) versus "acute pancytopenia" (driving toward a fundamentally different, marrow-focused differential) depended entirely on whether the white count and platelet count were reviewed alongside the hemoglobin. The habit of routinely reviewing the full CBC, not just the flagged abnormal value, is presented as a generalizable safeguard against premature procedural momentum.

A second teaching point concerns the discriminating use of ferritin: rather than treating it only as an iron-deficiency marker, ferritin was used here as evidence against active gastrointestinal bleeding, since ongoing blood loss would be expected to eventually deplete iron stores and lower ferritin, not elevate it. This use of a normal/elevated ferritin to actively rule out bleeding — even in a patient with symptoms that could plausibly be blamed on a GI source — is highlighted as a broadly applicable diagnostic move.

A third teaching point is the discipline of not dismissing an EKG finding as secondary to a more obvious abnormality (anemia) without confirming that correcting the presumed cause (transfusion) actually resolves the finding. Persistence of the ST changes after transfusion is what justified pursuing cardiac catheterization and ultimately uncovered genuine, previously occult triple-vessel disease — an important reminder that a plausible secondary explanation for an abnormal finding should be tested, not simply assumed.
