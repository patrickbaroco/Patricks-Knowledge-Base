## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 66-year-old man with 45 years of schizophrenia, dependent on family and on olanzapine, with diabetes (A1c 7.2), is brought in for a few days of being "not himself" — going to bed early, more fatigued, less engaged — and an off gait. Exam is at baseline (he doesn't communicate with strangers), vitals normal, but the CBC shows a **neutrophilic [[Leukocytosis|leukocytosis]] of 17,000** and the [[Urinalysis|UA]] shows [[Pyuria|pyuria]] (32 WBC), positive leukocyte esterase, **negative nitrite**, and mild glucosuria despite a serum glucose of only 126. With two days of dysuria, the operating diagnosis is a [[Urinary Tract Infection|urinary tract infection]] and he is started on ceftriaxone. But the white count *climbs* — 14k → 18k → ~18k — with no fever and a patient who looks clinically **back to baseline**, and the urine culture is initially no-growth. The hosts elevate the discrepant lab over the reassuring exam — **"[[Respect the White Count|respect the white blood cell count]]"** — and read the [[Sterile Pyuria|sterile pyuria]] and **nitrite-negativity** as clues pointing away from a routine gram-negative UTI. A fever spike triggers CT chest/abdomen/pelvis: [[Stercoral Colitis|stercoral colitis]], cystitis, and an enlarged prostate with **cystic lesions that could be abscesses**. Then the urine grows **[[Staphylococcus aureus Bacteremia|Staphylococcus aureus]]** — which, absent a Foley, means **hematogenous seeding, not colonization**: staph in the urine is treated like staph in the blood. Framed as disseminated staph (portal: chronic skin-picking; host risk: [[Diabetes and Infection|diabetes]], independent of A1c), he is treated for MSSA, de-escalated from vancomycin to **cefazolin** (superior for MSSA), and the count falls 18k → 10k → 8k with a normal TTE. The teaching spine: a **rising neutrophilic leukocytosis** overrules a comfortable diagnosis, and its two explanations are a **source-control problem** or a **misidentified organism** — both in play here.

## Pearls

- **Respect the white blood cell count.** A single high white count is noise; a *persistently rising* neutrophilic [[Leukocytosis|leukocytosis]] is a signal that something is wrong. When the count keeps climbing under treatment for your working diagnosis, only two explanations survive — you have a **[[Source Control|source-control problem]]**, or you have the **wrong organism** — and this case turned out to be both. Do not let a small down-tick (18,000 → 17,880) masquerade as improvement; that is lab-assay noise, not a response.
- **When exam and labs disagree, decide which one governs — and here the lab wins.** The patient looked back to baseline per a brother who knows him intimately, yet the count rose. [[Test Interpretation|Weighing discordant data]], an *unexplained, presumably neutrophilic* leukocytosis carries enough weight to keep pursuing occult disease even when the person in front of you looks well.
- **Nitrite-negative pyuria should make you name the organisms that can't make nitrite.** Leukocyte esterase merely reports white cells; the **nitrite** reflects nitrate-reducing *Enterobacteriaceae*. A [[Urinalysis|UA]] with pyuria but **negative nitrite** should prompt the question — *could this be [[Staphylococcus aureus Bacteremia|Staph aureus]], [[Enterococcus|Enterococcus]], group B strep, or Pseudomonas?* — but that is a **conversation, not a mandate to broaden**: nitrite can be falsely negative from low urine pH, high specific gravity, or too-short bladder dwell time, so its absence raises the question without proving the answer.
- **Staph aureus in the urine, without a Foley, is hematogenous until proven otherwise.** With a chronic indwelling catheter, urinary [[Staphylococcus aureus Bacteremia|S. aureus]] is usually skin colonization and means little. **Without** one, it implies **[[Bacteremia|hematogenous dissemination]]** — occult or cleared bacteremia seeding the urinary tract — and should be treated as *staph in the blood*. Staph "comes from" the skin and "goes to" three favored sites: the **vessels/valves, the bones, and the urine**.
- **Inflammation anywhere lights up its neighbors — the key to sterile pyuria.** [[Sterile Pyuria|Sterile pyuria]] has three buckets: prior **antibiotics** sterilizing the urine, a **culture-negative organism** (gonorrhea, chlamydia, tuberculosis), or **inflammation on the *outer* wall of the bladder** from an adjacent process. Appendicitis famously causes sterile pyuria; an ectopic pregnancy can irritate the bladder into dysuria and leukocytosis — disease *outside* the bladder read through the urine.
- **For MSSA, the beta-lactam beats vancomycin.** Once susceptibilities return oxacillin-sensitive, **cefazolin (or nafcillin)** is superior to vancomycin for methicillin-sensitive *S. aureus*; vancomycin is inferior against the actual organism. And diabetes raises the risk of disseminated gram-positive infection (*S. aureus*, group B strep) through immune effects that are **independent of the A1c** — a "controlled" A1c of 7.2 does not lower this guard.

## Problem Representation

A 66-year-old man with long-standing schizophrenia (baseline minimally communicative, dependent on family), diabetes with an A1c of 7.2, and recent skin-excoriation behavior presents with several days of subtle encephalopathy (hypersomnolence, reduced engagement) and a subjectively abnormal gait, plus two days of dysuria. Vitals and a limited exam are at baseline, but labs show a neutrophilic leukocytosis (WBC 17,000), a mild creatinine bump (1.3 from 1.0), and a UA with pyuria, positive leukocyte esterase, negative nitrite, and transient glucosuria at a serum glucose of 126. Treated empirically as a urinary tract infection with ceftriaxone, he defies the arc of a benign UTI: the white count rises to 18,000 with an initially sterile urine culture and clinical return to baseline, then he spikes a fever. CT chest/abdomen/pelvis reveals stercoral colitis, cystitis, and an enlarged prostate with cystic lesions suspicious for abscess, and the urine ultimately grows *Staphylococcus aureus* (MSSA). Reframed as hematogenous *S. aureus* dissemination (cutaneous portal from skin-picking; diabetes-related susceptibility) with possible prostatic seeding, he improves promptly on cefazolin — white count falling to 8,000 with a normal echocardiogram.

## Case Narrative

**Aliquot 1.** Reza presents a "bread-and-butter" case he insists is rich in decision points. A 66-year-old man, diagnosed with schizophrenia ~45 years ago, lives with and is fully dependent on his brother; at baseline he speaks only a word or two to strangers and communicates meaningfully only with family. Over several days the brother notes he is "not acting the same" — going to bed at 8 p.m. rather than 10, waking later, more fatigued, less engaging — and, decisively, *walking oddly* on a trip to the bathroom. Past history is diabetes (A1c 7.2, on no antidiabetic medication) and schizophrenia treated with olanzapine; a **chart biopsy** shows a hospitalization a month earlier for worsening **skin-picking**. In the ED he does not interact — but that is his baseline — with normal vitals and a grossly normal exam. Labs: **WBC 17,000, neutrophil-predominant**; creatinine 1.3 (baseline 1.0); normal CXR; **UA with 32 WBC, 7 RBC, positive leukocyte esterase, negative nitrite, mild glucosuria, specific gravity 1.020**. The admitting resident elicits two days of **dysuria**. Serum glucose is only **126**.

**Aliquot 2.** Rabih builds the [[Problem Representation|problem representation]]: a man with a severe chronic psychiatric illness presenting with two threads — an **alteration in mental status** and an **alteration in gait**. He resists collapsing them prematurely: gait can fail from a **motor**, **sensory**, or **coordination/central** problem, and although it is fair to suspect the brain is "on the hook" for both the encephalopathy and the gait, that assumption does not *exonerate* a motor or sensory cause. The encephalopathy is not a depressed-arousal picture but **subtle behavioral change**, and a chronic MRI-negative brain (schizophrenia) is a substrate that lets a **generalized toxic-metabolic insult present focally**. He stays in the nonspecific world of **[[MIST Mnemonic|MIST]]** — metabolic, infectious, structural, toxin — hunting for clues, and finds them: **pyuria and leukocytosis**, most simply a [[Urinary Tract Infection|urinary tract infection]] that can confuse a vulnerable brain. Why a UTI in this man? Age-related BPH, the **anticholinergic/autonomic effects of olanzapine**, or diabetic autonomic neuropathy — any could produce a **neurogenic bladder** with retention. His plan: empiric antibiotics, a **bladder scan** to exclude retention, and then to "hem and haw" over the case's one loose thread — **glucosuria at a serum glucose of 126**, well below the ~180 mg/dL renal reabsorption threshold, raising proximal (type 2) [[Renal Tubular Acidosis|renal tubular acidosis]] versus the likelier explanation of earlier hyperglycemia now resolved. A **repeat UA shows no glucose**, confirming transient hyperglycemia.

**Aliquot 3.** For empiric therapy Rabih chooses **ceftriaxone** (pending prior urine-culture data), and the patient is admitted. The next morning the story bends: the **white count rises from 14,000 to 18,000**. The patient looks fine. Reza, recalling a recent RLR episode, reasons through how a UTI could ever explain a gait problem — via **[[Batson Venous Plexus|Batson's valveless vertebral venous plexus]]**, which links pelvic and thoracic drainage to the epidural space and can seed a **[[Spinal Epidural Abscess|spinal epidural abscess]]** from a urinary source. He percusses and palpates the entire spine: **no tenderness**, and knowing ~75% of epidural abscesses present with back pain, his excitement is appropriately subdued. Over subsequent days the count reads **18,000 then ~17,880** with no fever on ceftriaxone, and the brother reports the patient is **back to normal** behaviorally and in gait. Reza flags a subtle cognitive trap the team fell into — reading 18,000 → 17,880 as "improvement" when it is within **laboratory margin of error**; the count is *not* improving.

**Aliquot 4.** Rabih delivers the case's spine: a **persistent leukocytosis is a decent argument against an uncomplicated UTI**. Either this is a **complicated** UTI (retention, ascending infection, renal abscess) or **the diagnosis is simply wrong** — because the tools for diagnosing a UTI (symptoms, pyuria) are **non-specific**. He opens the [[Leukocytosis|leukocytosis differential]]: first *confirm the differential is neutrophilic* (assuming wrongly wastes time), scrutinize the **medication list** (lithium can drive a neutrophilic leukocytosis), and characterize the **gait** more precisely (wide-based? weak? reflex changes?). He would get a repeat CXR, a viral swab, and **discuss CT abdomen/pelvis for occult visceral disease**, "most worried about something brewing — because a high white count is noise, a persistently increasing white count is much more likely cause for alarm." Reza crystallizes it as the lesson he wants trainees to carry: **"respect the white blood cell count."**

**Aliquot 5.** The urine culture returns **no growth**, and the brother reaffirms the patient is at baseline. Rabih names the core tension — **when data conflict, which datum governs?** The exam says better; the labs say worse; and here the **weight of the laboratory data is strong** enough to keep pursuing an "unexplained, presumably neutrophilic leukocytosis." He would obtain cross-sectional imaging. Reza adds a reasoning principle: **when he cannot acquire history** — a patient who *cannot* give an HPI — his **threshold for cross-sectional imaging drops**, because the scan substitutes for the story he can't take. The brother, worried the patient won't tolerate a CT and noting he seems well, asks whether it's truly necessary; Reza, honest that there is **no single right answer**, agrees to a short period of inpatient monitoring — *if the count falls and he stays well, nothing more is needed.* **An hour later the patient spikes to 39.3°C.** The CT proceeds (tolerated without sedation).

**Aliquot 6.** **CT chest/abdomen/pelvis:** chest clear; abdomen/pelvis shows a moderately distended rectosigmoid with impacted stool, mild wall thickening and fat stranding — **[[Stercoral Colitis|stercoral colitis]]** — despite a *benign* abdominal exam; **mild circumferential bladder-wall thickening with perivesical fat stranding** ("cystitis — correlate with UA"); and an **enlarged, heterogeneous prostate with prominent cystic lesions**, "most likely cystic degeneration, but **abscesses cannot be excluded**." Reza asks Rabih to reconcile the bladder findings first. Rabih reframes the whole picture as **[[Sterile Pyuria|sterile pyuria]]** (pyuria, dysuria, on antibiotics, culture negative) and teaches its logic through two stories — a young woman with "dysuria and a positive UA" who in fact had a **ruptured ectopic pregnancy** irritating the bladder, and the **meningeal reaction of HSV encephalitis**: *inflammation anywhere makes adjacent structures light up.* Sterile pyuria resolves into three questions: **antibiotic-sterilized urine, a culture-negative organism (GC/chlamydia/TB), or inflammation on the *outer* bladder wall** from an adjacent process — here the **colon and prostate** supply that adjacency.

**Aliquot 7.** Reza recalls his nephrology mentor "Manny": leukocyte esterase is only a WBC enzyme, but **nitrite is more specific**, reporting nitrate-reducing *Enterobacteriaceae* — so a **nitrite-negative** UTI concern should raise **Staph aureus and Enterococcus**. He frames the case for the team as a **nitrite-negative UTI** and, asked by the resident whether to empirically cover staph and enterococcus, **declines to broaden** absent hemodynamic instability: stercoral colitis rarely drives a leukocytosis unless it **perforates** (and then the patient is very sick with a rigid abdomen — not this benign exam). Planning an MRI to characterize the prostate and an aggressive bowel regimen, he goes home — and that evening the resident texts: **the urine culture is growing *Staphylococcus aureus.*** (Notably, **blood cultures were not drawn until the fever spike**, and are still pending.)

**Aliquot 8.** Rabih interprets: with a Foley, urinary *S. aureus* is skin colonization and "means almost nothing." **Without** one, it signals **hematogenous dissemination** — overt or subclinical/cleared **[[Bacteremia|bacteremia]]** that seeded the urine. Why this host? **Diabetes** markedly increases disseminated gram-positive infection (*S. aureus*, group B strep), by mechanisms **independent of the A1c**. He strengthens the case from a second angle: an unexplained high leukocytosis with prostatic **cystic lesions that could be abscesses**, and in a non-traveling US patient only a short list of organisms readily form abscesses — **[[Staphylococcus aureus Bacteremia|S. aureus]] and Streptococcus milleri (anginosus)**. He would **re-culture the blood**, treat for staph, and engage **urology** about the prostate, worrying about **MRSA** given the lack of response to ceftriaxone (though MSSA with undrained abscess would also "fail"). Reza distills the transferable rule: **"when you see staph in the urine, treat it like staph in the blood — unless there is a chronic indwelling Foley."**

**Aliquot 9 and resolution.** Vancomycin is started; susceptibilities return **oxacillin-sensitive (MSSA)**. Weighing the ~**20–25% mortality of *S. aureus* bacteremia**, Reza consults a colleague about de-escalation — and, catching his own **[[Availability Heuristic|availability bias]]** (a recent case of *Pseudomonas* that switched from levofloxacin-sensitive to -resistant had made him culture-conservative) — adds **cefazolin** while continuing vancomycin until blood cultures finalize, because **vancomycin is inferior to cefazolin for MSSA**. Blood cultures return **1 of 4 bottles positive for MSSA**; vancomycin is stopped. On cefazolin the count falls **18,000 → 10,000 (one day) → 8,000 (two days)**, and the **TTE is normal** (no valvular vegetation). ID is consulted. The likely **portal is the skin** (his chronic excoriation), the host factor diabetes; staph "went to" the urine (± prostate), sparing valves and bone. Interestingly, ID **did not pursue the pelvic MRI**, reasoning that a low-grade (1/4) bacteremia with such rapid clinical and laboratory response argues against a drainable prostatic abscess. Rabih's closing frame: the pivotal finding was the **persistent/rising neutrophilic leukocytosis**, which meant either a **source-control** problem or a **misidentified organism** — and both were true: the **base rate** said gram-negative, but the **negative nitrite** flagged a gram-positive, and the **prostate** was a possible unaddressed source. Reza's caution: the nitrite clue **deserved a conversation, not a leap** — you cannot elevate a single case into "nitrite-negative means Staph until proven otherwise."

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Base Rate|Base-rate]] set the wrong prior — and the UA corrected it.** Community UTIs are overwhelmingly gram-negative *Enterobacteriaceae*, so the empiric ceftriaxone was reasonable; the error would have been *staying* there. The **negative nitrite** was the data point that should downgrade the gram-negative prior and surface the nitrate-nonreducing organisms — a reminder that base rates are a starting point to be updated, not a destination.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Interpreting the trend, not the value]] — "respect the white count."** The governing move is treating a *rising* neutrophilic leukocytosis as a hypothesis-generating signal and refusing to read a within-noise dip (18,000 → 17,880) as response. The corollary is reading each test by what it can and cannot do: leukocyte esterase reports cells, nitrite reports specific bacteria, a negative urine culture can reflect prior antibiotics or an obstructed system, and a normal TTE lowers but never zeroes endocarditis.
- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|Schemas]] convert overwhelming findings into checkable lists.** Three carry the case: a **leukocytosis** differential (confirm neutrophilic → meds → occult source), a **sterile pyuria** schema (antibiotics / culture-negative organism / adjacent inflammation), and the **staph metastasis** schema ("where did it come from — the skin; where did it go — vessels, bone, urine").
- **[[Anatomical Approach|Anatomical reasoning]] links distant findings.** Batson's valveless plexus offered a route from a GU source to the spine (tested and rejected at the bedside), and the "inflammation lights up its neighbors" principle explains sterile pyuria from a colon or prostate abutting the bladder — anatomy doing diagnostic work in both directions.
- **[[Availability Heuristic|Availability bias]], caught in real time.** Reza names his own recent *Pseudomonas* case pushing him to distrust culture-guided de-escalation; recognizing it lets the evidence (MSSA superiority of cefazolin) win over the vivid anecdote — a live [[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive-autopsy]] move rather than a retrospective one.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Humility]] against over-generalizing a single case.** The disciplined endpoint is *not* concluding "nitrite-negative pyuria = Staph aureus until proven otherwise." The clue earned a **conversation** about non-gram-negative organisms, bounded by the many benign reasons a nitrite can be falsely negative (low urine pH — his was 5 — high specific gravity, short dwell time). Knowing when *not* to escalate a heuristic is itself expertise.

## Teaching Discussion

- Treat a rising neutrophilic leukocytosis as a diagnostic emergency of attention: a solitary high count is noise, but a count that climbs under treatment for your working diagnosis means either the source is not controlled or the organism is misidentified — and small down-ticks within assay variability are not improvement.
- When the bedside and the laboratory disagree, decide explicitly which governs; an unexplained, presumably neutrophilic leukocytosis can outweigh a reassuring exam and a well-appearing patient, especially when the historian is the patient's own family reporting a return to baseline.
- Read the urinalysis component by component: leukocyte esterase marks white cells, nitrite marks nitrate-reducing Enterobacteriaceae, and a nitrite-negative pyuria should prompt naming Staph aureus, Enterococcus, group B strep, and Pseudomonas — while remembering nitrite is falsely negative with low urine pH, high specific gravity, or short bladder dwell time, so the clue opens a conversation rather than dictating therapy.
- Work sterile pyuria through three doors — prior antibiotics, a culture-negative organism (gonorrhea, chlamydia, tuberculosis), or inflammation on the outer bladder wall from an adjacent process (appendicitis, colitis, prostatitis, even a gynecologic emergency) — because disease outside the bladder is frequently read through the urine.
- Interpret Staphylococcus aureus in the urine by catheter status: with a chronic Foley it is usually colonization, but without one it implies hematogenous seeding and should be pursued and treated as bacteremia, with attention to its favored metastatic sites — heart valves, bone, and the urinary tract — and a search for the portal, most often the skin.
- Remember that diabetes increases the risk of disseminated gram-positive infection independent of glycemic control, so a "controlled" A1c does not lower the index of suspicion for S. aureus or group B strep dissemination.
- De-escalate MSSA bacteremia to cefazolin or nafcillin once susceptibilities allow, since the targeted beta-lactam outperforms vancomycin; and let the therapeutic response inform the anatomic question — a low-grade bacteremia that clears rapidly with a falling white count argues against an undrained deep abscess, though cross-sectional or urologic follow-up remains reasonable when imaging cannot exclude one.
- Localize an "abnormal gait" before assuming the brain owns it: gait can fail from motor, sensory, or coordination causes, and even when a shared central lesion is likely, that assumption should not exonerate a treatable motor or sensory contributor.
