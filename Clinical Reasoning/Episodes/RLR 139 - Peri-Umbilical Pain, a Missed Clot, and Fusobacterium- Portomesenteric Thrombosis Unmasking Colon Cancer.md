## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 74-year-old man presents with four days of fever, chills, nausea, and diffuse peri-umbilical abdominal pain. A first pass through the CT abdomen/pelvis looks reassuringly normal — until Robbie spots a subtle filling defect that turns out to be a large superior mesenteric vein thrombosis, missed on first read because the scan wasn't timed for the venous phase. With no cirrhosis, no pancreatitis, and no other inflammatory source visible, Reza works through the differential for an unprovoked mesenteric venous clot — myeloproliferative neoplasm, antiphospholipid syndrome, paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria — methodically ruling each out before blood cultures return the real answer: Fusobacterium nucleatum bacteremia, a gut-derived organism with a well-described tropism for the portomesenteric venous system. A month later, prompted by new iron deficiency anemia and the known epidemiologic link between Fusobacterium and occult colorectal cancer, a colonoscopy finds early-stage colon cancer in the transverse colon — caught before it could spread.

## Pearls

- Fever, chills, and diffuse abdominal pain should be worked up like any inflammatory syndrome: localize by anatomy, but hold localizing symptoms loosely, since organs in the abdomen, pelvis, and lower thorax can all refer pain to similar regions — abdominal localization by symptom alone has real but limited discriminating value.
- Point tenderness on exam, even in a diffusely tender abdomen, meaningfully narrows the anatomic differential and should guide which structures get the closest look on imaging.
- A CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast should generally be pursued for suspected intra-abdominal inflammation even with unknown renal function — in an acutely ill patient, the diagnostic yield of contrast usually outweighs the risk of contrast-induced nephropathy.
- Peri-umbilical pain with a benign-appearing colon, appendix, and gallbladder on CT should prompt a second look specifically at the mesenteric and portal venous system — CT protocols not specifically timed for the venous phase can miss a real, clinically significant filling defect.
- Before accepting any radiologic finding as the explanation for a clinical syndrome, explicitly ask whether it actually accounts for the presentation — an incidental finding and a causal finding can look identical on the images themselves, and the absence of collateral vessels is a useful clue that a clot is acute rather than long-standing (and therefore more likely to be causally related to an acute presentation).
- Mesenteric (portal, splenic, or superior mesenteric) venous thrombosis matters clinically for two reasons: the risk of downstream portal hypertension and variceal hemorrhage, and — when severe or progressive — the risk of bowel ischemia analogous to phlegmasia cerulea dolens in the venous system of the leg.
- Anticoagulation, not thrombectomy, is the default treatment for mesenteric venous thrombosis unless there's evidence of bowel ischemia (an elevated lactate being one practical marker); a low-molecular-weight heparin can often be used, paralleling standard DVT management.
- The most common cause of mesenteric venous thrombosis is cirrhosis-related hypercoagulability, followed by intra-abdominal inflammation of any kind (pancreatitis, colitis, or other visceral inflammatory processes) creating a locally hypercoagulable milieu — splenectomy is another recognized but usually benign cause that often doesn't need intervention.
- When a mesenteric venous clot occurs with no cirrhosis and no visible intra-abdominal inflammation, move to occult causes: myeloproliferative neoplasms specifically favor unusual venous territories like the mesenteric/portal system, so a hematology workup (peripheral smear, MPN panel) is warranted even with an unremarkable CBC.
- Fusobacterium (nucleatum and necrophorum among the recognized species) is a gram-negative, fastidious, hard-to-culture organism associated with intra-abdominal and portomesenteric hypercoagulability — it can cause a picture resembling cholangitis or an occult visceral infarction, and blood cultures may only turn positive in a minority of bottles drawn.
- New iron deficiency anemia in an older adult without a prior colonoscopy is a strong independent signal to pursue colon cancer screening, and should be weighed alongside — not overshadowed by — an acute infectious or thrombotic presentation.
- The association between Fusobacterium (portomesenteric) infection and occult colorectal cancer is well described in the literature, with a reported odds ratio around 12 for colon cancer in patients found to have this specific infection — a single positive blood culture result can be the trigger for a cancer workup that might otherwise be deferred.
- A proximal finding (a clot) explains a symptom, but "why is the clot there" is a distinct and equally important question — a positive workup for the clot's mechanism (here, Fusobacterium bacteremia) is itself only a proximal answer until it, in turn, leads to the actual underlying driver (the colon cancer).

## Case Narrative

After the usual banter — cat commentary, a running-and-gym pep talk, and a detour through the economics of international soccer tournaments — Robbie introduced a case he said was worth far more attention than its outward simplicity suggested: a 74-year-old man, "Mr. N," who arrived in the emergency department with four days of fever, chills, nausea, and diffuse abdominal pain.

Reza's first pass, working from that summary alone, mapped fevers and chills onto inflammation, and reasoned that with nausea and abdominal pain the process was most likely intra-abdominal, while acknowledging that the chest and pelvis sit close enough to the abdomen that referred pain from either region couldn't be excluded outright. He noted, invoking a cardiology analogy about the limited localizing value of ST depressions, that abdominal pain localization is genuinely useful but imperfect — better than nothing, worse than a clean anatomic signal.

Vitals in triage showed a fever of 39.2°C and a heart rate of 110, with normal blood pressure; he looked unwell. Exam revealed diffuse abdominal tenderness, worst around the umbilicus, enough that the patient asked the examiner to stop because it was making him nauseated. With that added localization, Reza's differential sharpened: point tenderness at the umbilicus offered real anatomic value, enough that he recommended a contrast-enhanced CT abdomen/pelvis without waiting on renal function, reasoning that in an acutely ill patient with likely intra-abdominal inflammation, the risk of missing pathology outweighed the risk of contrast nephropathy. His initial order set: CBC, liver function tests, lipase, an EKG, and CT with contrast.

The first labs came back unrevealing in a telling way: normal point-of-care creatinine and electrolytes, a normal EKG showing only sinus tachycardia, a white count of 15, and entirely normal liver enzymes and lipase. Reza worked through an anatomic differential for peri-umbilical inflammatory pain — cholecystitis or cholangitis (right upper quadrant), diverticulitis (left lower quadrant), hepatitis or liver abscess, inflammatory bowel disease given the colon's broad anatomic reach, and appendicitis, which classically starts peri-umbilical before migrating to the right lower quadrant and could explain how sick the patient looked if perforation had occurred. He weighed pancreatitis but considered it a poor fit given the typical absence of high fevers in his clinical experience with the disease. His leading targets for the CT read: the appendix, the sigmoid/descending colon for diverticulitis, the gallbladder and biliary tree, and — given the specific peri-umbilical location — the stomach.

Robbie confirmed this mirrored his own initial read of the scan: he traced the bowel, the appendix, and the gallbladder and found nothing abnormal in any of them — a genuinely unrevealing scan on first pass, prompting real puzzlement about what could be driving such a convincing clinical picture.

The answer turned out to be a subtle one. Robbie directed Reza's attention to a specific finding he'd zoomed in on: a filling defect in a vessel that traced upward into the portal system — specifically the superior mesenteric vein. The scan, as it happened, hadn't been specifically timed to optimize the venous phase, and catching the clot required both an astute radiologist and some luck. The official read: a large thrombosis in the superior mesenteric vein, with no cirrhosis, no splenomegaly, no pancreatitis, and — notably — no other abnormal finding anywhere else on the scan, and no collateral vessels suggesting chronicity.

Reza's first move was to ask whether this finding actually explained the clinical syndrome — a discipline he singled out as something neurologists do particularly well, given how often MRI and CT findings in that specialty are incidental rather than causal. Here, he reasoned it did: a large enough mesenteric venous clot disrupts forward blood flow and circulation, the absence of collaterals supported an acute process, and there was no other candidate explanation anywhere on the scan. He then walked through why the clot mattered clinically: mesenteric/portal venous thrombosis threatens portal hypertension and, downstream, variceal hemorrhage — echoing the feared complication pathway in cirrhosis — and, if severe enough, threatens bowel ischemia, which he analogized (with an explicit disclaimer not to fact-check the terminology) to phlegmasia cerulea dolens in the venous system of the gut. With a normal lactate, active bowel ischemia wasn't currently a concern, which favored anticoagulation over more aggressive intervention (thrombectomy), and he floated using a low-molecular-weight heparin, mirroring standard DVT management, for simplicity.

But treating the clot was only half the problem — Reza reframed the clot itself as a proximal finding, not a terminal diagnosis, and turned to why it existed. The single most common cause of mesenteric venous thrombosis, he explained, is cirrhosis-driven hypercoagulability; the second is intra-abdominal inflammation of any kind creating a locally hypercoagulable environment. Neither fit this patient — no cirrhosis, no visible inflammation anywhere on the scan. He noted, as an aside, that splenectomy is a recognized cause that usually doesn't need intervention (referencing a prior RLR case). With those ruled out, his mind moved to occult causes, particularly malignancy, and within malignancy specifically to myeloproliferative neoplasms, which have a recognized predilection for unusual venous territories like the mesenteric and portal systems. The CBC, however, wasn't classically suggestive (white count 13, with normal hemoglobin and platelets), tempering but not eliminating that concern. His planned next steps: a peripheral smear, a broader infectious workup given the fever and leukocytosis (including blood cultures), and more history to see if anything preceded the GI symptoms — explicitly invoking a prior RLR case in which a patient presumed to have cholangitis actually had a portal venous clot infarction from Fusobacterium.

Robbie then revealed the actual workup that had been sent: an MPN panel (JAK2 and related mutations), antiphospholipid antibodies, a PNH panel, a CT chest, and blood cultures — with only one of the five returning positive. Reza worked through each in turn before guessing: the MPN panel, he predicted, would be negative given the unremarkable CBC (correct). Antiphospholipid syndrome he judged unlikely given the clot's atypical, non-classic location without other supporting features like thrombocytopenia or an elevated PTT, and the patient's age (correct — negative). PNH he considered a good theoretical fit for exotic clotting but ultimately too rare and unsupported by other clues like hemolysis (correct — negative). That left the CT chest and the blood cultures. Reza reasoned through what each result could mean: the CT chest could show either more clot burden without answering the underlying "why," or a mass driving a paraneoplastic hypercoagulable state; the blood cultures could return a specific organism directly implicated in this pattern of visceral hypercoagulability. Given the fever, chills, and leukocytosis, and recalling that a specific, hard-to-culture organism is classically linked to this exact presentation, Reza chose blood cultures as his answer — correctly. One of two blood culture bottles grew Fusobacterium nucleatum.

Robbie confirmed the patient was treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics, narrowed to Augmentin, and anticoagulated with enoxaparin. But the case had one more chapter: a month after discharge, a final diagnostic test was performed. Reza, recalling Robbie's own prior teaching on this exact organism, correctly predicted a colonoscopy — specifically to look for occult colon cancer. It found stage I colon cancer in the transverse colon, with no evidence of metastatic disease, subsequently fully addressed surgically. Robbie noted the odds ratio for colon cancer associated with this specific infectious finding is around 12, and that the patient — 74 years old — had never had a screening colonoscopy. He also revealed, deliberately withheld earlier to preserve the diagnostic arc, that the patient had new iron deficiency anemia at the time of presentation, an additional and independent signal that had reinforced the push for colonoscopy.

Robbie closed with a personal reflection, recalling a past instance of missing a case of gallstone pancreatitis after Reza had specifically taught him about the lipase-can-be-normal pitfall, only to catch this near-identical Fusobacterium/mesenteric-clot presentation in real life after having discussed the entity on the podcast previously. He framed the moment as a reminder that ongoing, repeated engagement with the same material — teaching it, then encountering it clinically — is what makes it truly stick, rather than a one-time exposure.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Effect on Leading Hypothesis |
|---|---|
| Point tenderness at the umbilicus, fever 39.2°C, HR 110, WBC 15 | Raises probability of a localized intra-abdominal inflammatory process |
| Normal LFTs, lipase, lactate, and creatinine; CT shows normal bowel, appendix, and gallbladder | Lowers probability of cholecystitis, appendicitis, diverticulitis, and pancreatitis |
| Filling defect traced to the superior mesenteric/portal vein, no collaterals, no other CT finding | Confirms an acute superior mesenteric vein thrombosis as the explanation for the abdominal pain |
| No cirrhosis, no splenomegaly, no pancreatitis or other visible inflammation on CT | Lowers probability of the two most common causes of mesenteric venous thrombosis (cirrhosis, local inflammation); raises probability of an occult/malignant cause |
| Normal CBC (no polycythemia, thrombocytosis, or leukocytosis pattern typical of MPN) | Lowers probability of a myeloproliferative neoplasm, though doesn't fully exclude it |
| Negative MPN panel, APLS antibodies, and PNH panel | Further lowers probability of the classic hematologic hypercoagulable syndromes |
| Blood culture positive for Fusobacterium nucleatum (1 of 2 bottles) | Confirms an infectious, gut-flora-derived cause of the portomesenteric thrombosis |
| New iron deficiency anemia, age 74, no prior colonoscopy, known Fusobacterium-colon cancer association | Strongly raises probability of occult colorectal malignancy, prompting colonoscopy |
| Colonoscopy shows stage I transverse colon cancer, no metastatic disease | Confirms colon cancer as the underlying driver of the entire presentation |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The case is a model of disciplined sequential rule-out under a "proximal versus terminal diagnosis" framework, applied twice in the same case: first to the abdominal pain (clot, not yet a full diagnosis) and then to the clot itself (Fusobacterium, still not the full diagnosis) — each answer immediately reframed as a new proximal finding demanding its own "why." The systematic elimination of MPN, APLS, and PNH before accepting an infectious explanation demonstrates resistance to anchoring on the most "interesting" or first-considered hypothesis (malignant hypercoagulability) in favor of following the data. Robbie's personal anecdote about catching this presentation in real life after previously teaching it — and about missing gallstone pancreatitis despite prior teaching on the same pitfall — models the value of spaced repetition and self-directed pattern recognition built through teaching, not just clinical volume.

## Teaching Discussion

The episode's central teaching point is a two-stage "why is this here" discipline: treating a positive finding (a clot) as only a proximal cause of symptoms, and treating the explanation for that finding (an infection) as, in turn, only a proximal cause requiring its own explanation (an underlying malignancy) — rather than stopping at the first satisfying answer. It also reinforces a specific, easy-to-miss radiologic pitfall: a CT abdomen/pelvis not specifically timed for the venous phase can miss a real and clinically significant mesenteric or portal venous clot, making a deliberate second look at that vascular territory worthwhile whenever the standard organ-by-organ read is unexpectedly unrevealing. Finally, the Fusobacterium-colon cancer association is presented as a concrete, actionable epidemiologic link — a specific microbiology result that should trigger colorectal cancer screening even in a patient who has just been through an unrelated acute infectious illness.
