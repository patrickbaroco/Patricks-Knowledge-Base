## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A healthy man in his early 30s with no prior surgeries presents to Robbie's ED with sudden severe abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting, and CT reveals a small bowel obstruction that turns out to be a jejunal intussusception — surgically explored twice over the following month with no anatomic lead point ever found. The case only resolves when Robbie finally asks about prior episodes and learns of a decade of brief nausea-and-vomiting spells relieved by hot showers, alongside two decades of heavy daily cannabis use: cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome. Seven months later, a second, unrelated patient with the same recurrent lead-point-negative intussusception and the same cannabis history sends Robbie to the surgical literature, where a case series confirms jejunal intussusception as a genuine, described complication of CHS — mechanistically explained by patchy bowel wall edema from CHS-related vascular dysregulation creating a functional, non-anatomic "lead point."

## Pearls

- The speed of symptom onset should calibrate the speed of your diagnostic workup, not just the perceived severity: sudden or hyperacute abdominal pain should trigger immediate consideration of obstruction, perforation, or a vascular ("electrical discharge") catastrophe of a vessel or organ, and prompt stat imaging rather than a stepwise workup.
- Absence of abdominal distension does not rule out bowel obstruction — a proximal (near-pyloric or gastric) obstruction can present with significant gastric distension and severe nausea/vomiting without any distension visible or palpable on abdominal exam.
- Young age and no cardiac history don't exclude a vascular catastrophe (acute coronary syndrome, aortic dissection) as a cause of abdominal pain with nausea and vomiting via referred pain — an EKG and troponin are reasonable even when abdominal tenderness makes a primary cardiac process less likely.
- The most common cause of small bowel obstruction overall is adhesions from prior abdominal surgery — a young patient with a truly obstructive picture and no surgical history should prompt a different differential: volvulus, a congenital remnant like a Meckel's diverticulum acting as a lead point, a strangulated hernia, or (rarely, at a young age) a mass lesion.
- Approach unfamiliar CT imaging the same way you'd approach an EKG in suspected ACS: generate a hypothesis-driven differential before looking, so you have a framework to interpret ambiguous findings against rather than searching for a pattern with no prior expectations.
- Two adjacent loops of small bowel that appear fused or "married" on imaging, one clearly telescoping into the other, is the signature finding of intussusception — distinct from, but reasonably grouped alongside, volvulus in an initial "bowel twisting on itself" differential.
- Intussusception in an adult almost always has a "lead point" — a focal lesion (benign polyp, Meckel's diverticulum, malignant mass, or in women, endometriosis) that the bowel telescopes around — and finding or ruling out that lead point is the actual diagnostic task once intussusception itself is identified.
- Whether an intussusception can be managed endoscopically (both diagnostically and therapeutically) versus requiring surgery depends heavily on anatomic accessibility — a distal jejunal intussusception, for example, may be too far from either endoscopic approach to reach, mandating surgical exploration even when endoscopy might otherwise be considered.
- In adults, surgery for intussusception serves two distinct purposes: reducing the acute obstruction, and identifying/addressing the lead point — because unlike some pediatric cases, an adult lead point won't spontaneously resolve, and without addressing it, recurrence is expected (an analogy was drawn to biliary colic: once you've had a true episode from gallstones, further episodes are expected without definitive treatment).
- Very few medical conditions genuinely improve with a hot shower — when a patient reports this pattern, it's a specific and useful clue. Recognized causes include cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (classic), pruritus relieved by heat in certain mast cell/myeloproliferative conditions, and — worth keeping in the back pocket as a mimicker of recurrent attacks — angioedema, which can occur anywhere in the body, not just the oropharynx.
- Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome should be actively screened for (duration and frequency of cannabis use, and specifically whether hot showers or baths relieve symptoms) in any patient with recurrent, unexplained nausea and vomiting, especially with a multi-year history of heavy daily use.
- When a hypothesis about a novel or unusual disease association feels compelling, deliberately check it against the literature rather than trusting the "hit" alone — case reports exist for nearly any claim, so distinguish a genuinely well-described phenomenon (ideally a case series with a proposed mechanism) from an isolated anecdote before treating an association as established.
- Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome is increasingly understood to involve vascular dysregulation with bowel wall vasodilation and edema — this edema is thought to produce transient, incomplete obstruction (explaining the nausea/vomiting episodes) and to be relieved by hot water exposure via a redistribution of blood flow, and — when the edema is patchy rather than uniform across adjacent bowel segments — can create a functional, non-anatomic lead point sufficient to trigger true intussusception, distinct from a discrete anatomic lesion.
- A negative surgical exploration for intussusception (no anatomic lead point found) should not be treated as a dead end — recognizing an underlying functional driver (like cannabis-related bowel edema) can convert "we have no idea why this keeps happening" into an actionable plan (cessation counseling) with real potential to prevent further recurrences.

## Problem Representation

A man in his early 30s with two decades of heavy daily cannabis use and a decade of unrecognized cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (brief spells of nausea and vomiting relieved by hot showers) presents with the first of three episodes of acute, lead-point-negative small bowel (jejunal) intussusception over roughly a month, ultimately understood as a described complication of CHS-related bowel wall edema rather than a discrete anatomic lesion.

## Case Narrative

Before the case, Reza recounted a recent morning report aside — a mishearing of "Eisenmenger syndrome" that briefly stumped him mid-text with Robbie — as an excuse to revisit the physiology: a long-standing left-to-right cardiac shunt (classically an ASD) eventually drives progressive pulmonary hypertension until the shunt reverses to right-to-left, and once that reversal (Eisenmenger syndrome) has occurred, the shunt should not be closed, since it now functions as a pressure "release valve" for a circuit that can no longer tolerate the alternative.

The actual case began with a patient Robbie described as alive and well now, but who hadn't been in that state when he first met him. A man in his early 30s (later confirmed as 34 on chart review) arrived in the ED with abrupt-onset severe abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting, roughly a day in duration. He was afebrile, tachycardic, normotensive, with a normal respiratory rate and oxygen saturation — acutely uncomfortable, tender diffusely but worst around the umbilicus, without distension.

Reza's first response emphasized a general principle: the more rapid the onset of symptoms, the more rapid the diagnosis needs to be — not because slower-developing problems are less serious, but because there's less time available to reach an answer. He invoked a framework attributed to Robbie: sudden-onset abdominal symptoms should prompt immediate consideration of obstruction, perforation, or a vascular ("electrical discharge") event affecting a vessel or organ. He argued the constellation here — sudden pain, nausea, and vomiting — pointed first toward intestinal obstruction. Asked whether the lack of distension argued against that, he said it lowered but didn't eliminate the probability, recalling a prior RLR case where a pyloric obstruction produced gastric distension and severe nausea/vomiting without any abdominal distension on exam — and that this picture warranted stat CT imaging, not plain films, given the acuity. He also didn't want to exclude a vascular catastrophe like acute coronary syndrome or aortic dissection despite the patient's young age, since either could produce referred abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting — tenderness on exam made this less likely but didn't eliminate it, and he recommended an EKG and troponin alongside imaging. His two starting questions: has this ever happened before, and get a stat CT plus basic labs, troponin, and EKG.

Robbie then walked through the real-time ED course. The patient was placed on a monitor with an EKG, IV access, and labs ordered immediately; he received a liter of IV fluid and 4mg of Zofran, with pain medication held pending imaging to avoid masking findings before the scan. Robbie noted the patient's chart was essentially empty — sparse, scattered VA notes, no substantial prior medical care. The EKG showed sinus tachycardia without ischemic changes; a rapid chemistry panel returned a normal creatinine (0.7), and the patient was sent to CT before the remaining labs resulted. The radiologist called immediately: a small bowel obstruction. Robbie posed the puzzle to Reza directly — why would someone with no prior surgical history have an SBO, since adhesions (the leading overall cause) require a surgical history this patient didn't have?

Reza worked through the differential for SBO without a surgical history in a young, healthy patient: malignancy (unlikely at this age, though he noted the well-publicized rise in early-onset colorectal cancer as a caveat), volvulus (bowel twisting on itself, possible at any age), a congenital remnant like a Meckel's diverticulum serving as a nidus for the bowel to twist around, and a strangulated abdominal hernia. Given the lack of distension and severe nausea/vomiting, he leaned toward a more proximal (near-pyloric) process.

Reviewing the CT together, Reza described a distended, air-filled stomach with thickening bowel walls, and dilated loops of bowel containing both air and fluid — consistent with obstruction, though he had trouble localizing the specific abnormality Robbie was pointing to. Once guided to the finding, he described what looked like a dense outpouching or "fingerprint" pattern, wondering aloud if it represented a diverticulum or a transition point. Robbie then named it directly: two loops of small bowel that were "married" together when they shouldn't be — one segment telescoping into the other — a small bowel intussusception.

Reza, folding this into his earlier "twisting" category, reasoned that intussusception in an adult typically has a lead point — a focal lesion, benign or malignant, that the bowel telescopes around. He again judged malignancy less likely given no family history of hereditary colorectal cancer, but raised a benign polyp or a diverticulum as plausible candidates, and noted that in women, endometriosis is a recognized cause of intussusception as well. Regardless of the specific driver, he emphasized the need for urgent decompression, and wondered whether this could be managed endoscopically — both diagnostically and therapeutically — rather than requiring surgery, given the small bowel location.

Robbie explained why that wasn't possible here: the intussusception was located in the distal jejunum, far outside the reach of either an upper or lower endoscopic approach, so surgery was mandatory. Once the surgical team saw the imaging, they moved immediately — an NG tube was placed and the patient was taken straight to the OR; Robbie never saw him again during that visit.

He continued following the chart out of curiosity, since he'd never encountered intussusception before. Surgery, to their surprise, found no lead point at all — nothing external, nothing palpable internally — and the patient was discharged without a clear cause identified. Two weeks later, the exact same presentation recurred: another episode of intussusception, another trip to the OR, again with no lead point found. By the time Robbie saw the patient a third time — one month after the first visit — he was looking at a 34-year-old man with recurrent, idiopathic intussusception and no answer in sight.

That third encounter was when Robbie finally circled back to the question Reza had asked at the very start: had anything like this ever happened before? The patient said this specific severe pain was new, but for roughly the past ten years he'd experienced brief, three-to-four-day spells of severe nausea and vomiting without pain, which reliably improved when he took a hot shower.

Reza, working through that detail live, said his first thought upon hearing about prior episodic symptoms had been angioedema — a "sneaky" cause of recurrent attacks that can occur in locations beyond the classic oropharyngeal presentation. But he noted that very few conditions genuinely improve with a hot shower: certain pruritic conditions, some cases relieved similarly to cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome, and — checking his own notes live on air — a recollection that mast cell disorders and myeloproliferative neoplasms can produce pruritus relieved by warm water. He proposed running the specific combination (young man, recurrent nausea/vomiting, now intussusception, symptom relief specifically with hot water) through an AI assistant or literature search to see what it surfaced.

Robbie revealed the answer directly: further questioning uncovered that the patient smoked cannabis heavily — three to four joints daily for the past twenty years. The diagnosis was cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (CHS). With the updated problem representation — a man in his 30s with two decades of heavy cannabis use complicated by CHS, now with three episodes of lead-point-negative small bowel intussusception — Robbie asked how Reza would try to connect the dots if he genuinely couldn't figure it out himself.

Reza reasoned that the honest next step would be checking whether this was a recognized phenomenon rather than assuming causation from a compelling but unproven hunch — cautioning that a case report can be found to support almost any hypothesis, so the bar should be a more substantial body of literature. He wondered whether cannabis itself might directly alter the intestinal lining, or whether the hyperemesis itself (via repeated vomiting and the pressure changes that come with it) could mechanically predispose to intussusception, while acknowledging the more sinister structural causes had already been reasonably excluded by two negative surgical explorations.

Robbie then described how the mystery actually resolved. He'd made nothing of the case in the moment and moved on — until, seven months later, he encountered an entirely different patient presenting with his own third episode of recurrent, lead-point-negative intussusception. Reviewing that second patient's chart, Robbie noticed a decade of urine toxicology screens all positive for cannabis, and confirmed on direct questioning that this patient also had classic CHS symptoms. Recognizing this couldn't be coincidence, he searched the surgical literature and found a case series specifically describing jejunal intussusception as a genuine, described complication of cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome.

The proposed mechanism, per that literature: CHS involves vascular dysregulation causing bowel wall vasodilation and edema. This edema produces a transient, incomplete obstructive picture — explaining the recurrent nausea and vomiting episodes over the preceding decade — and the relief patients experience with hot showers is thought to reflect a redistribution of blood flow away from the edematous, engorged bowel. Critically, this edema isn't uniform: because some segments of bowel become more edematous than immediately adjacent segments, the differential swelling itself can act as a mechanical catch point, allowing one segment to telescope into another — producing true intussusception without any discrete anatomic lesion. This explained why repeated surgical exploration in both patients found nothing: the "lead point" was a transient physiologic process, not a fixed structural abnormality.

Practically, sharing this literature with both patients' surgical teams shifted the outlook for both cases from "an unexplained process likely to keep recurring indefinitely" to a plan with real optimism — since the driver (ongoing heavy cannabis use) is addressable through cessation, unlike a fixed anatomic lesion that would require repeated intervention.

Robbie closed with a broader point about why surgery remains necessary even when a lead point can't be found: in adults, an intussusception's underlying driver won't spontaneously disappear, so surgery serves two purposes at once — reducing the acute event and identifying (or, when the driver is behavioral rather than anatomic, ruling out) a fixable lead point — since without addressing the underlying cause, recurrence should be expected, much as a first episode of true biliary colic from gallstones predicts further episodes without definitive treatment. Reza, struck by how common cannabis use is and how easily this connection could otherwise go unrecognized, committed to producing a dedicated teaching video on intussusception for the audience.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Effect on Leading Hypothesis |
|---|---|
| Abrupt-onset severe abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting; no distension | Raises probability of obstruction (proximal/gastric location specifically, given absent distension), perforation, or a vascular catastrophe; prompts stat CT over plain films |
| Normal EKG (sinus tachycardia only), normal creatinine | Lowers probability of ACS/dissection and of significant renal involvement, without fully excluding either |
| CT shows small bowel obstruction in a patient with no prior abdominal surgery | Lowers probability of adhesion-related SBO (the most common cause); raises probability of volvulus, a congenital lead point (e.g., Meckel's), hernia, or mass |
| CT shows two "married" small bowel loops telescoping into each other | Confirms small bowel intussusception as the specific mechanism of obstruction |
| Distal jejunal location | Confirms surgical (not endoscopic) management is required, given inaccessibility |
| First and second surgical explorations find no anatomic lead point | Lowers probability of a discrete structural lesion (polyp, Meckel's, mass, endometriosis); raises probability of a non-anatomic/functional cause |
| History of decade-long episodic nausea/vomiting relieved by hot showers | Raises probability of cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (or another heat-relieved condition, e.g., mast cell disease) as an underlying process |
| 20-year history of heavy daily cannabis use | Confirms cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome as the underlying diagnosis |
| A second, unrelated patient with the same recurrent lead-point-negative intussusception and a positive cannabis history | Raises probability that CHS-related bowel edema is a genuine, generalizable cause of intussusception rather than a coincidental single-case finding |
| Literature case series describing jejunal intussusception as a recognized CHS complication | Confirms the association as an established, mechanistically explained phenomenon, not an isolated coincidence |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The case is a clean illustration of a lead point search that correctly exhausts the anatomic differential (polyp, Meckel's, mass, endometriosis) before the answer turns out to be physiologic rather than structural — a reminder that "no lead point found" on surgical exploration should prompt a search for a functional cause rather than being treated as a dead end or written off as truly idiopathic. Reza's explicit caution about the seductiveness of a single compelling case-level "hit" — that you can always find a case report to validate almost any hypothesis — models healthy skepticism toward pattern-matching under uncertainty, while Robbie's independent confirmation with a second, unrelated patient seven months later demonstrates how recognizing recurring atypical presentations across otherwise unconnected cases is what actually converts a hunch into a validated clinical association.

## Teaching Discussion

This episode's central teaching point is that a negative anatomic workup for intussusception (no lead point found on surgical exploration) is not necessarily the end of the diagnostic story — cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome-related bowel wall edema can produce a genuine, mechanistically plausible, non-anatomic lead point, and recognizing that possibility changes both the explanation given to a frustrated patient and the actual, addressable treatment plan (cannabis cessation) available to prevent recurrence. The case also reinforces two generalizable habits: taking a rapid-onset abdominal complaint straight to advanced imaging rather than a stepwise workup, and treating "has this happened before" as an essential, sometimes underused question that can unlock a decade-old undiagnosed pattern — as it did here once finally asked in enough depth.
