## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A man in his 60s comes to the ED not because he's alarmed by his few days of exertional dyspnea and mild leg swelling, but because he doesn't want those symptoms to get his upcoming epidural steroid injection cancelled. He looks great, his exam is entirely normal, and a thorough cardiac and pulmonary workup — EKG, troponin, BNP, chest X-ray — comes back reassuringly negative. The case stalls until Rabih realizes, mid-discussion, that he never registered the patient's triage oxygen saturation of 88%, unresponsive to supplemental oxygen despite a completely normal chest X-ray and no increased work of breathing. That combination is the signature of shunt physiology, and a markedly low PaO2 on ABG (arguing against methemoglobinemia) sets up a bubble echocardiogram that finds a wide-open PFO — with the patient's wife revealing, almost as an afterthought, that his oxygen saturation has quietly been dropping when he sits up for six months. The episode is presented deliberately out of order, modeling the real, nonlinear, occasionally embarrassing texture of live diagnostic reasoning.

## Pearls

- Any non-emergent procedure should generally be deferred, rather than rushed through, when a patient presents with a new, unexplained, potentially active medical issue — even a "minor" or "minimally invasive" procedure is major from the patient's perspective, and there is little downside to taking the time to clarify what's going on first.
- Dyspnea on exertion accompanied by lower extremity edema should prioritize cardiac causes but should not exclude renal, hepatic, or simple venous stasis explanations for the edema — a directed exam (JVP, auscultation for rales) and an observed walk test (with pulse oximetry) add meaningfully more diagnostic information than history and a resting exam alone.
- A patient who is themselves privately worried about a specific finding (like leg swelling) but subsequently backs off that concern once examined ("maybe I'm overly vigilant") is offering a useful, if imperfect, calibration signal — but that self-correction should not substitute for objective exam and testing.
- Physical exam has real limits worth remembering even when reassuring: JVP assessment has meaningful interobserver unreliability, roughly a third of patients with decompensated heart failure lack lower extremity edema, and patients with chronic heart failure may lack pulmonary crackles due to adapted lymphatic drainage — a normal cardiopulmonary exam should lower but not eliminate concern for heart failure, and a BNP remains a reasonable next step even after a reassuring exam.
- Dyspnea can function as an anginal equivalent, particularly in a high cardiovascular risk patient (e.g., with a prior stent); even with a normal EKG, a high-sensitivity troponin is a reasonable next step in this context, though the high sensitivity of modern troponin assays has somewhat reduced the practical clinical relevance of "unstable angina" as a distinct diagnostic category.
- When a directed cardiopulmonary workup is unrevealing, it is useful to deliberately step back and enumerate causes of dyspnea that are inherently difficult or impossible to detect on a routine exam: anemia (pallor is an unreliable sign), deconditioning, depression, thyrotoxicosis, body habitus limiting ventilation, and occult processes like pulmonary embolism or early pulmonary hypertension.
- Before escalating to cross-sectional imaging in a diagnostically unclear case, a truly exhaustive physical exam (undressing the patient fully, examining between digits, the oral cavity, and the entire skin surface) can occasionally reveal the answer directly — exemplified by finding an apical tooth abscess as the source of an otherwise unexplained leukocytosis.
- Reflexively checking an oxygen saturation is easy to skip in a patient who appears comfortable and has a normal work of breathing, especially for an experienced clinician who has learned, through long practice, that this value is overwhelmingly normal in such patients — recognizing this as an efficient, generally rational Bayesian shortcut (rather than simple carelessness) does not eliminate the risk that it will occasionally cause a genuinely abnormal, diagnostically pivotal value to be missed.
- Not every missed data point deserves the same self-critical response: a miss reflecting an appropriately low pretest-probability shortcut (skipping a value that is abnormal in only a small fraction of similar patients) can reasonably be processed with less self-blame than a miss of a data point that should be checked in essentially every patient regardless of context (such as ambulating a fatigued patient, or examining for bladder distension in unexplained abdominal pain).
- The combination of hypoxemia that fails to improve with supplemental oxygen, a completely normal chest X-ray, and normal work of breathing is a strong, relatively specific signature of shunt physiology — since an alveolar filling or airspace process severe enough to cause this degree of hypoxemia would typically be audible on exam and radiographically visible, and would typically provoke a compensatory increase in respiratory rate that this specific physiology does not produce.
- Shunts causing refractory hypoxemia can be intracardiac (e.g., a patent foramen ovale or atrial septal defect with right-to-left flow) or intrapulmonary (e.g., an arteriovenous malformation, or diffuse intrapulmonary shunting as in hepatopulmonary syndrome); a bubble echocardiogram is the key test to identify and localize the shunt, with bubble transit within roughly three to four cardiac cycles suggesting an intracardiac shunt and delayed transit beyond that suggesting an extracardiac (intrapulmonary) shunt.
- In personal clinical experience, pulmonary embolism severe enough to cause marked hypoxemia is uncommon in isolation — PE more typically presents with tachycardia, dyspnea, and chest pain as the dominant drivers of presentation, with hypoxemia, when present, often comparatively mild.
- Methemoglobinemia is another cause of hypoxemia (low oxygen saturation) that plateaus and fails to correct with supplemental oxygen, classically producing a saturation that does not drop below roughly 85% and cyanotic-appearing but often non-dyspneic patients — a notable contrast with this patient's genuine dyspnea, which should lower (though not eliminate) suspicion for the diagnosis.
- Methemoglobinemia results from oxidation of hemoglobin's iron from the ferrous (Fe2+) to the ferric (Fe3+) state, which increases the hemoglobin molecule's affinity for oxygen and impairs its release to tissues — the physiologic problem is defective oxygen delivery/release rather than defective oxygen loading, meaning arterial PaO2 (dissolved plasma oxygen) is typically normal or even elevated in isolated methemoglobinemia, in contrast to a low PaO2 as seen with a true right-to-left shunt.
- A markedly low PaO2 on arterial blood gas (rather than a normal-to-high PaO2 with a low calculated/measured oxygen saturation) argues against methemoglobinemia as the primary process and supports a genuine right-to-left shunt or other cause of true hypoxemia, since methemoglobinemia does not impair the initial transfer of oxygen from alveolus to plasma.
- Orthodeoxia — a drop in oxygen saturation specifically when moving from supine to upright position — is a recognized feature of shunt physiology, classically associated with hepatopulmonary syndrome (gravity-dependent redistribution of blood through predominantly lower-lobe intrapulmonary shunts) but also seen with intracardiac shunts, where positional changes in blood flow (sometimes influenced by structures like a prominent Eustachian valve or an intracardiac mass) can preferentially direct flow across a defect; when accompanied by positional dyspnea as well, the combined syndrome is termed platypnea-orthodeoxia.
- A chronic, longstanding shunt that a patient has physiologically adapted to over time can produce marked hypoxemia without a compensatory increase in respiratory rate, since the patient's baseline work of breathing has already accommodated to the chronic condition — explaining why severe, chronic hypoxemia can coexist with an entirely unremarkable-appearing patient on exam.
- Deliberately reflecting on and sharing the true, nonlinear sequence of real-time clinical reasoning — including overlooked data, incorrect intermediate hypotheses, and self-corrected errors — offers different and arguably more valuable teaching than a cleanly reconstructed, textbook-style case presentation, since it more accurately reflects how diagnostic reasoning actually unfolds in practice.

## Problem Representation

A man in his 60s with a remote MI and stent, presenting with several days of exertional dyspnea and self-reported leg swelling (not corroborated on exam), whose fully reassuring initial cardiopulmonary workup (normal exam, EKG, troponin, BNP, chest X-ray) obscured a markedly abnormal, initially overlooked triage oxygen saturation of 88% refractory to supplemental oxygen — ultimately explained by a chronic, positionally worsening (orthodeoxic) right-to-left shunt from a large patent foramen ovale, scheduled for percutaneous closure.

## Case Narrative

Rabih presented a man in his 60s who walked into the ED himself, asking that his wife (arriving separately) be allowed in once she parked. His chief concern was three to four days of increasing shortness of breath, worse with exertion, along with some leg swelling — but his primary motivation for the visit was not alarm about these symptoms; he had chronic low back and neck pain and an upcoming scheduled epidural steroid injection, and wanted to preempt any chance that his cardiopulmonary symptoms might get the procedure cancelled. His wife, once present, corroborated the history but characterized the severity as mild — he had been getting around fine, just "a little winded" over the preceding few days.

Reza's first response addressed the procedural context directly: any non-urgent, non-emergent procedure should generally be deferred rather than rushed through in the presence of a new, active medical issue, regardless of how "minor" the procedure seems to the medical team — a minimally invasive procedure is not minor from the patient's perspective. Turning to the clinical picture, he noted that dyspnea on exertion with lower extremity edema prioritizes the cardiovascular system, while acknowledging the edema alone could also reflect renal or hepatic disease or simple venous stasis unrelated to the chief complaint. He recommended a directed exam (JVP, auscultation for rales) and, importantly, an observed walk test with pulse oximetry to obtain an objective correlate for the subjective complaint.

Rabih reported the exam was essentially entirely normal: no resting tachypnea, flat JVP, clear lungs, and — notably, in contrast to what he had expected given the presenting complaint — no lower extremity edema at all. When Rabih raised this discrepancy with the patient directly, the patient offered his own self-correction: perhaps he was being overly vigilant given his awareness that leg swelling can signal heart problems. Additional history revealed a prior MI in 2010 treated with a single stent at an outside health system (not reflected in the current chart), currently on aspirin alone (previously on two antiplatelet agents), with NSAIDs and gabapentin for chronic back pain as his only other medications. Concerned by the complete absence of any exam findings to explain the presenting symptoms — and specifically worried this could represent unstable angina rather than reassuring findings — Rabih moved the patient to a bed and obtained an EKG, which showed normal sinus rhythm without ischemic changes.

Reza acknowledged the reassurance of a normal exam but flagged its real limits: JVP assessment carries meaningful interobserver unreliability, roughly a third of decompensated heart failure patients lack lower extremity edema, and chronic heart failure patients may lack crackles due to adapted pulmonary lymphatic drainage — supporting a BNP even after a reassuring exam, with a low BNP essentially excluding heart failure as the explanation. He then addressed the cardiac ischemia question: given the patient's prior stent, dyspnea could represent an anginal equivalent, and even with a normal EKG, a high-sensitivity troponin was reasonable — while noting that the sensitivity of modern troponin assays has somewhat reduced "unstable angina" as a standalone diagnostic category in practice, since true acute plaque events typically leave some biomarker or angiographic signature. Stepping back further and deliberately setting aside the cardiac history, he enumerated a broader set of dyspnea causes that are inherently hard to detect on routine exam: anemia (pallor is unreliable), deconditioning, depression, thyrotoxicosis, body habitus limiting ventilation, and occult processes like pulmonary embolism or early pulmonary hypertension (which would still typically show some right heart strain signature). His recommendation: obtain the high-sensitivity troponin, BNP, and hemoglobin, then decide next steps based on those results.

All of the returning data was reassuring: normal hemoglobin, white count, and renal function; normal BNP; normal troponin; normal chest X-ray. Reza worked through each in turn — the normal BNP combined with the normal exam substantially lowered the post-test probability of heart failure; the normal high-sensitivity troponin, combined with several days of ongoing symptoms (atypical for an acute unstable plaque event), made ACS unlikely; the normal chest X-ray argued against a frank pleural or parenchymal process and against pulmonary hypertension (which would typically show a plumped pulmonary artery and right heart strain). Genuinely uncertain how to proceed, he shared an anecdote about a prior case of unexplained leukocytosis resolved only after a fully exhaustive physical exam (undressing the patient completely, examining between the toes, the oral cavity, and the entire skin) revealed an apical tooth abscess — illustrating the value of thorough examination before escalating to cross-sectional imaging — but ultimately admitted he was stumped and passed the case back to Rabih.

Rabih then revealed the missing piece: he had been "solving the wrong problem" because he had overlooked a key data point from triage — an oxygen saturation of 88% on room air that remained 88% despite four liters of supplemental oxygen, in a patient whose other triage vitals (heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature) were all normal. He invited reflection on why this value had likely been overlooked: the patient appeared comfortable and well, with no resting tachypnea, making it easy — in a busy, hypothesis-driven clinical workflow — to not reflexively check the oxygen saturation despite the chief complaint of dyspnea. He drew a parallel to an earlier personal miss (an overlooked distended bladder during a case that was instead worked up purely as abdominal pain) and framed both as examples of how team-based care and colleague catches, rather than individual vigilance alone, are what protect against this category of error — a reason for self-compassion rather than self-blame when such gaps are caught.

Reza reframed the case around this new finding as "chest-X-ray-negative hypoxemia not improving with supplemental oxygen," teaching that this specific combination strongly prioritizes shunt physiology — either intracardiac (e.g., a PFO or ASD with right-to-left flow) or intrapulmonary (e.g., an arteriovenous malformation, or hepatopulmonary syndrome) — since a process severe enough to explain this degree of hypoxemia would typically also be visible on chest X-ray and audible on exam if it were an alveolar filling process. He recommended a bubble echocardiogram to evaluate for and localize a shunt, noted that PE in his own experience is less often associated with marked isolated hypoxemia (more typically presenting with tachycardia, dyspnea, and chest pain as the dominant features), and raised methemoglobinemia as an additional cause of oxygen saturation that fails to correct with supplemental oxygen — classically producing a saturation that plateaus around 85% in patients who often appear cyanotic but not dyspneic, a notable contrast to this genuinely dyspneic patient, and recommended an ABG with co-oximetry to directly assess for it alongside calculating an A-a gradient.

The ABG (drawn without co-oximetry, a lab processing error) returned with a normal pH and PaCO2 but a markedly low PaO2 of 56. When the nurse asked whether to redraw with co-oximetry, Reza reviewed the pathophysiology of methemoglobinemia: oxidation of hemoglobin's iron from ferrous to ferric impairs oxygen release to tissue without impairing initial oxygen loading, meaning arterial PaO2 should typically be normal or elevated in isolated methemoglobinemia, with the abnormality confined to functional oxygen-carrying capacity; he reviewed the medication list for oxidizing agents, flagging allopurinol (also on NSAIDs) as a plausible culprit, and leaned toward recommending the repeat co-oximetry to confirm or exclude the diagnosis.

Rabih, deciding to "cowboy" the decision, declined the repeat co-oximetry: reasoning from the same pathophysiology Reza had just described, a markedly low PaO2 (rather than a normal-to-high PaO2 with an isolated low oxygen saturation) argued against methemoglobinemia and instead confirmed a genuine defect in oxygen transfer from alveolus to blood — consistent with true shunt physiology rather than a hemoglobin-level oxygen-release problem. He proceeded directly toward a bubble echocardiogram.

Before the echo could return, further history from the patient's wife reframed the entire case: using a home pulse oximeter purchased during the COVID pandemic, she had noticed his oxygen saturation had been low for the past six months — and specifically that it dropped further when he sat upright and improved when he lay down. Reza identified this as orthodeoxia (oxygen desaturation specifically with upright positioning; when accompanied by positional dyspnea as well, the combined syndrome is termed platypnea-orthodeoxia), a finding strongly reinforcing the shunt hypothesis. He explained this pattern is classically associated with hepatopulmonary syndrome (gravity-dependent redistribution of blood flow through predominantly lower-lobe intrapulmonary shunts in cirrhotic patients) but can also occur with intracardiac shunts, where positional changes in flow — sometimes influenced by structures like a prominent Eustachian valve or an adjacent intracardiac mass — preferentially direct blood across a septal defect; given this patient's lack of any cirrhosis history, a cardiac source was favored over hepatopulmonary syndrome.

The following day, a bubble echocardiogram confirmed intracardiac shunting through a wide-open patent foramen ovale, and the patient was scheduled for percutaneous PFO closure.

Reflecting on the case's arc, Rabih noted that although he had not been hard on himself for the initial oxygen saturation oversight (given the low pretest probability reasoning behind it), in retrospect "everything was there in the room" from the start — had he registered the abnormal saturation immediately, his wife's history of chronic, positional desaturation would likely have pointed toward the diagnosis far more directly, without the intervening detour through cardiac and pulmonary rule-outs. He deliberately framed the episode's circuitous, occasionally self-correcting structure — including his own admitted uncertainty and errors along the way — as more representative of real clinical reasoning than a cleanly reconstructed case, contrasting it with an unrelated prior instance (mentioned only briefly) where he had simply and unambiguously omitted mentioning a patient's chest pain, calling that specific omission not an authentic reasoning wrinkle but simply an error. Reza praised this transparency and the format itself, expressing interest in continuing to present cases this way.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Refractory shunt-physiology hypoxemia illness script: hypoxemia that fails to correct with supplemental oxygen, in the setting of a normal chest X-ray and normal work of breathing, should immediately prioritize shunt physiology (intracardiac or intrapulmonary) over parenchymal lung disease — a distinctive enough pattern that recognizing it can shortcut an otherwise long differential once the oxygen saturation is actually registered as abnormal.

The case is a direct, extended example of hypothesis-driven data omission: an entirely reasonable clinical gestalt (a comfortable-appearing, non-tachypneic patient) led to a reflexively low priority being placed on checking a specific vital sign, illustrating how efficient pattern-recognition shortcuts — generally rational and usually correct — can occasionally cause a diagnostically pivotal, atypical finding to be missed. The case also explicitly models graded, calibrated self-evaluation of diagnostic misses: distinguishing between a miss reflecting a reasonable low-pretest-probability shortcut (this episode's oxygen saturation) versus a miss of a universally-indicated checklist item (the previously discussed fatigue-patient ambulation and bladder-distension examples), with correspondingly different appropriate emotional and practice-change responses.

## Teaching Discussion

The central teaching point is the diagnostic signature of shunt physiology — hypoxemia refractory to supplemental oxygen, with a normal chest X-ray and normal work of breathing — and the corresponding workup (bubble echocardiogram, with attention to intracardiac versus intrapulmonary localization based on bubble transit timing).

A second teaching point is the differentiation of methemoglobinemia from true shunt-related hypoxemia using PaO2: since methemoglobinemia impairs oxygen release to tissue rather than oxygen loading into blood, PaO2 remains normal or elevated in methemoglobinemia, while a markedly low PaO2 (as in this case) points toward a genuine oxygen-transfer defect instead.

A third teaching point is orthodeoxia (and the related platypnea-orthodeoxia syndrome) as a positional clue to shunt physiology, with hepatopulmonary syndrome as the classic association and intracardiac shunts as an important alternative explanation in a patient without liver disease.

The overarching, explicitly named teaching point is methodological: presenting a case with its real, nonlinear reasoning sequence intact — including an overlooked vital sign, a subsequently well-calibrated (not overcorrected) response to that miss, and an in-the-moment clinical judgment call (declining repeat co-oximetry) — offers a more authentic and arguably more useful model of real-world diagnostic reasoning than a cleanly reconstructed, textbook-style case presentation.
