> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A man in his 70s — an unhoused veteran who distrusts medicine and takes no medications — returns to the ED for the third time in two weeks with **bilateral calf pain worse with walking**, greeted by "oh no, he's back." Rabih (presenting his own case) resists the [[frequent-flyer bias]], reproduces the pain by having him ambulate, and finds **absent pulses that won't even Doppler** with cool feet but *no* ulceration — pointing to severe [[peripheral arterial disease|PAD]]; a CTA shows **complete bilateral arterial occlusion at the same level**. Then the vascular surgery resident asks about the **[[lower-extremity edema]]** that Rabih and his colleague had both missed — a live-patient **[[inattentional blindness|"invisible gorilla"]]** — reframing the case toward **heart failure**. ProBNP is high, troponin 1.5, the heart is enlarged; an echo shows a **preserved EF, biatrial enlargement, and diffuse speckled thickening of all chambers** with an EKG whose **voltage is far too low for the wall thickness** — the signature of **infiltration**. Diuresis relieves the edema and **the pulses return**: the "occlusion" was **[[pseudo-claudication|pseudo-PAD]]** — severe **[[venous hypertension]]** so slowed the arterial contrast that the scan was captured before it reached the distal legs. A bilateral **carpal tunnel** history, negative light chains, and a positive **[[technetium pyrophosphate scan|Tc-pyrophosphate scan]]** clinch **wild-type (senile) [[ATTR cardiac amyloidosis]]**. The reasoning spine: **data acquisition, not interpretation, is where this case is won or lost** — and heart failure can masquerade as leg pain.

**Pearls**

- **"He's back" is a diagnosis-blocking prior.** [[Frequent-flyer bias|Frequent-flyer]] and social-complexity bias, plus the *absence* of documented chronic disease in someone who never gets worked up, must be held cautiously — not as reassurance.
- **[[Claudication]] splits into vascular and neurogenic**; non-dopplerable pulses with cool, *non-ulcerated* feet favor a vascular problem but sit on a spectrum — either true low flow **or** something interfering with the Doppler signal (e.g., overlying [[edema]]).
- **Seek one explanation, and respect the direction of causality.** You *can* explain arterial insufficiency through severe **[[venous hypertension]]**, but you cannot explain [[lower-extremity edema|leg edema]] through an arterial lesion — so the edema, once seen, becomes the organizing finding.
- **Bilateral, same-level "occlusion" with no collaterals is a red flag for artifact, not atherosclerosis.** True chronic PAD is rarely perfectly symmetric and recruits collaterals; here the CTA was a **timing artifact** of contrast slowed by venous hypertension — **[[pseudo-claudication|pseudo-PAD]]** that reversed with diuresis.
- **Low EKG voltage against a thick ventricle means infiltration.** Low voltage has a differential (effusion, air/COPD, obesity — things *between* leads and muscle), but low voltage with a *thick wall* means **substance replacing myocytes** — [[cardiac amyloidosis|amyloid]] until proven otherwise (speckled echo, biatrial enlargement).
- **The [[troponin pyramid]]:** start at ACS (highest stakes), then climb to **demand** — here a stretched-atria [[atrial flutter]] and a high **[[LVEDP]]** impairing subendocardial perfusion in diastole explain the troponin without an acute plaque event.
- **Amyloid worth knowing in three types:** AA (inflammation, spares the heart), **AL** (light chains, renal), **[[ATTR amyloidosis|ATTR]]** (age, carpal tunnel, tendon rupture) — and ATTR, unlike AL, is **treatable**, so the non-biopsy pathway (light chains → [[technetium pyrophosphate scan|Tc-PYP]]) matters.

**Problem Representation**
A 70s unhoused veteran with bilateral exertional calf pain and non-dopplerable pulses initially attributed to severe PAD, ultimately found to have wild-type ATTR cardiac amyloidosis causing heart failure and profound lower-extremity venous hypertension — a pseudo-arterial-occlusion that reversed with diuresis.

## Case Narrative

Rabih presents a case "set up for tremendous bias." The first words he hears are **"oh no, he's back."** Mr. P, a man in his 70s, has come to the ED three times in two weeks with the same complaint — **both calves hurt** — and prior colleagues, not from resentment but from a felt helplessness, dread the visit. The context is heavy: decades of distrust of medicine, refusal of all medications (including for a glaucoma that has cost most of his vision), a recent move to the Bay Area to be near family he hasn't located, and unstable housing. It is hard, Rabih admits, not to walk in projecting "what is the gain of this visit?"

The word that breaks the frame is **calves**. A normal CK and labs are already back, but "when somebody hurts in their calf and the blood work is normal, couldn't this be [[peripheral arterial disease|peripheral arterial disease]]?" He has the patient **walk**, and both calves hurt almost immediately — **[[claudication]]**. Off come the old shoes and torn socks: **no pulses palpable**, and — a Doppler quietly produced by the charge nurse — **no dopplerable DP or PT signal on either side**, confirmed by a colleague. The feet are cool, yet there is **no ulceration and no nail changes**.

Reza, setting the social context aside as a probable contributor rather than the medical answer, frames the problem as **bilateral exertional calf pain = claudication**, split into **vascular vs neurogenic**, and makes vascular the clear leading hypothesis given the non-dopplerable pulses and cool extremities. He flags one honest **disconnect**: severe true ischemia usually shows on the skin. He reaches back to the **[[RLR 043 - Cold Thumb and Dialysis Access Steal Syndrome|dialysis-steal case]]**, where a *dopplerable* (if weak) pulse coexisted with a gangrenous thumb — the inverse mismatch — and concludes the current picture means either genuinely low flow *or* something **interfering with the Doppler**. His plan: renal function, then a **CTA of the abdomen/pelvis/legs with runoff** (an ABI risks being falsely reassuring if vessels are calcified). Rabih adds the systems move: he **calls vascular surgery** early — the specialists who will interpret the CTA and act — "no different than calling neurology for brain ischemia or cardiology for heart ischemia."

The pivot is a question, not a test. Vascular surgery arrives before the CTA, intrigued by ischemia this severe without ulceration, and asks: **"What do you think about his edema?"** Rabih and his colleague had **noticed no edema**. He walks back, and thirty minutes later the same patient has **3+ edema** he simply had not seen — a live-patient **[[inattentional blindness|"invisible gorilla"]]**, his brain so locked on *arteries* that the edema fell into a blind spot. He broadens: EKG, chest x-ray, **BNP**, UA, albumin, LFTs — though the patient is whisked to CTA before the blood is drawn.

Reza reframes around the newly-seen edema, insisting on **one unifying explanation** and on the **direction of causality**: you can drive arterial insufficiency *from* severe **[[venous hypertension]]**, but you cannot drive leg edema *from* an arterial lesion — so the edema, at 3+, takes priority. He runs the bilateral-edema schema — **heart, liver, kidney** — checks the **JVP**, notes venostasis is usually unilateral and chronic (and this patient lacks varicosities, [[lipodermatosclerosis]], or hemosiderin staining), excludes cirrhosis clinically, and leans toward **cardiac** (with nephrotic syndrome a consideration given the prior normal creatinine).

The data arrive. The **CTA shows complete bilateral arterial occlusion just distal to the femoral, at the same level, with no distal flow.** Reza flags the amyloid-relevant point in passing (a CTA is venous contrast timed to the arterial phase, so contrast-nephropathy fears are overblown on the venous side) and starts building a vasculopath picture: PAD as a **[[coronary artery disease]] equivalent**, so think heart failure and CAD — while remembering non-atherosclerotic arterial disease ([[polyarteritis nodosa]] and other [[vasculitis]], [[fibromuscular dysplasia]]), where the *location* of blockage (e.g., bifurcations) can hint at vasculitis. Then the labs: **atrial flutter at 60**, **ProBNP ~2000**, **troponin 1.5**, clear chest x-ray but **cardiomegaly**, normal liver/renal/UA. Reza reads this as heart failure and works Rabih's **[[troponin pyramid]]**: not ACS (no chest pain, no ischemic rhythm) but **demand** — a stretched-atria flutter and a high **[[LVEDP]]** that impairs subendocardial perfusion during diastole. Absent metabolic risk factors, he predicts **HFrEF of ischemic origin** and asks for an echocardiogram.

The twist detonates. Aggressive **diuresis** improves the edema and **the pulses come back** — 2+ after several liters. The echo shows a **preserved EF**, **biatrial enlargement**, and **diffuse speckled thickening of all chambers**, prompting a look back at the EKG: **voltage far lower than the wall thickness predicts**. Vascular surgery declares there is **no PAD**: bilateral simultaneous occlusion at exactly the same level, with **no collaterals**, never made sense — the "occlusion" was a **contrast-timing artifact**, the flow so slowed by venous hypertension that the scan caught the contrast before it reached the distal legs. Diurese the venous hypertension, and arterial flow — and pulses — return. Reza closes the physiology loop: exactly the "edema → impaired arterial flow" direction he had insisted on.

The unifying diagnosis is **[[cardiac amyloidosis]]**. The **speckled echo + low-voltage EKG + biatrial enlargement** point straight to infiltration. Reza runs the amyloid triage: **AA** (inflammatory, spares the heart — absent here), **AL** (light chains, renal), and **[[ATTR amyloidosis|ATTR]]** (age, carpal tunnel, tendon rupture). He would send **SPEP/UPEP/serum free light chains** (and a spot protein-to-creatinine, since light chains can spill without frank albuminuria) to exclude AL, and — given age — pursue [[ATTR amyloidosis|ATTR]], asking specifically about **carpal tunnel** and tendon injury. The follow-up bears it out: **bilateral carpal tunnel surgery a decade earlier**, **negative SPEP and free light chains**, and a **positive [[technetium pyrophosphate scan|technetium-pyrophosphate scan]]** confirming **wild-type (senile) ATTR** — importantly, the **treatable** form. Rabih's closing line: *who thinks TB or syphilis is the great masquerader once you've seen heart failure present as leg pain?*

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The case is a clinic on **acquisition over interpretation**: the reasoning engine was sound, but a **missed physical finding** (3+ edema) — classic **[[inattentional blindness]]**, the invisible gorilla — nearly closed the case as PAD. The countermeasures modeled are structural, not exhortational: **team distributed cognition** (charge nurse's Doppler, the surgery resident's edema, the colleague's second look) repeatedly rescues the individual blind spot, and Reza's explicit lesson is to **judge the missing finding as something you too will miss**, not as another provider's failure. The **[[frequent-flyer bias]]** is named and actively resisted (curiosity re-opened by the word "calves"). Two physiologic principles do the diagnostic work: **direction of causality** (venous → arterial, not the reverse) and **artifact-awareness** (bilateral symmetric same-level "occlusion" with no collaterals is implausible for atherosclerosis). The **[[troponin pyramid]]** prevents anchoring on ACS. And the final leap is **pattern integration** — speckled echo *plus* discordantly low voltage *plus* biatrial enlargement — the constellation, not any single sign, that names amyloid.

## Teaching Discussion

**Bias against the "familiar" patient.** The most human teaching is that a returning, socially-complex patient generates a prior — "there's nothing to find" — that suppresses curiosity, and that the *absence* of documented chronic disease in someone who is never worked up is not reassurance but a data gap. The re-opening move here was concrete: a semantic qualifier ("calves") triggered a specific hypothesis ([[peripheral arterial disease|PAD]]) and a specific maneuver (watch him walk).

**Claudication and the pulse-skin disconnect.** [[Claudication]] divides into **vascular** and **neurogenic**; the vascular branch is supported by non-dopplerable pulses and cool extremities, but the *absence* of ulceration is a genuine disconnect worth naming. Everything is a spectrum: a non-dopplerable signal can reflect true low flow **or** an impediment to the Doppler — foreshadowing the overlying edema. The deliberate contrast with the **[[RLR 043 - Cold Thumb and Dialysis Access Steal Syndrome|dialysis-steal case]]** (dopplerable pulse, gangrenous digit) sharpens the point that pulse findings and tissue findings can dissociate in *either* direction.

**One explanation, and the arrow of causality.** The discipline of seeking a single unifying diagnosis is paired with a physiologic constraint: **severe [[venous hypertension]] can throttle arterial inflow, but an arterial lesion cannot produce bilateral leg edema.** That asymmetry tells you which finding is upstream — so the moment the edema is seen, it becomes the organizing problem, and the "arterial occlusion" becomes a phenomenon to *explain* rather than a diagnosis to treat.

**Pseudo-PAD as a contrast-timing artifact.** A CTA runoff captures contrast at a chosen arterial phase; if venous hypertension slows flow enough, the image is taken **before** contrast reaches the distal vessels, mimicking **occlusion**. The tells that this is artifact rather than atherosclerosis: **perfect bilateral symmetry, identical level, and no collaterals** (true chronic PAD recruits collaterals and is rarely so symmetric). The confirmatory experiment is therapeutic — **diuresis restores the pulses**. (En route, the [[contrast-induced nephropathy]] aside: the evidence is far stronger for arterial than venous contrast, and a "CTA" is venous contrast timed to the arterial phase.)

**Reading the infiltrated heart.** PAD is a **[[coronary artery disease]] equivalent**, so a vasculopath with leg edema invites a heart-failure hypothesis — but the *type* matters. Here the **echo** (preserved EF, biatrial enlargement, diffuse **speckled** thickening) and the **discordantly low EKG voltage** are the giveaways. Low voltage has a differential of things *between* the leads and the muscle (pericardial effusion/[[tamponade]], COPD air, obesity), but **low voltage with a thick wall** means **substance replacing myocytes** — the hallmark of **[[cardiac amyloidosis]]**. The **[[troponin pyramid]]** explains the enzyme leak without ACS: demand ischemia from a stretched-atria **[[atrial flutter]]** and a high **[[LVEDP]]** that compromises subendocardial perfusion in diastole.

**Amyloid triage and why the subtype matters.** Three types are worth reflex recall: **AA** (chronic inflammation, spares the heart), **AL** (plasma-cell light chains, renal-predominant), and **[[ATTR amyloidosis|ATTR]]** (age, **carpal tunnel**, tendon/biceps rupture, lumbar stenosis). The **non-biopsy pathway** is high-yield: exclude AL with **SPEP/UPEP and serum free light chains** (plus a spot protein/creatinine, since light chains may spill without albuminuria), then confirm ATTR with a **grade 2–3 [[technetium pyrophosphate scan|Tc-pyrophosphate scan]]**. ATTR further splits into **hereditary** and **wild-type (senile)** — and because ATTR is **treatable** (transthyretin stabilizers/silencers), distinguishing it from AL changes both prognosis and therapy.

**Acquisition is the whole game.** The meta-lesson Rabih and Reza return to: we test ourselves endlessly on *interpreting* data but rarely on *acquiring* it, yet the process begins with acquisition — "garbage in, garbage out, even in the best machine." A missed exam finding, not a reasoning error, is what nearly sent this patient home; the fix is humility, repeat examination, and a team that fills each other's blind spots.
