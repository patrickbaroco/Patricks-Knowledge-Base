## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A man with long-standing insulin-dependent diabetes and refractory peripheral arterial disease pain arrives by ambulance angry that he wasn't given his insulin and oxycodone on his "last visit" — a claim a colleague directly refutes. Rather than accepting either the patient's or the system's account at face value, Rabih investigates both, and a strange, disorganized conversation (wrong visit counts, unreachable family, a confused timeline) prompts a head-to-toe exam that reveals truncal ataxia, gaze-evoked nystagmus, and poor recent memory with preserved remote memory. Reza builds the case for a cerebellar (specifically vermian) process from the exam alone, and an MRI reveals a classic, symmetric pattern in the thalamus, hypothalamus, and mammillary bodies — Wernicke encephalopathy from non-alcoholic thiamine deficiency, traced back to an old, easily overlooked abdominal surgical scar from a distant pancreatectomy and gastrectomy, with a missed post-surgical vitamin regimen and a socially isolated patient nobody was positioned to catch.

## Pearls

- Diabetic emergencies exist on a spectrum with two true emergencies at the extremes — DKA and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS) — and a low bicarbonate in a hyperglycemic patient should not be assumed to mean DKA before considering osmotic diuresis, hypovolemia, lactic acidosis, and renal insufficiency as alternative, non-ketotic explanations.
- HHS technically requires altered mental status (coma is literally in its classical description); many patients with very high glucose values are not yet at that threshold when first encountered, and the label should be applied carefully rather than reflexively to any very elevated glucose.
- When a patient's account and the medical record directly conflict (here, "they didn't give me my insulin" versus a colleague's confident "we gave it to him"), investigate both sides rather than assuming either the patient is fabricating or the record is wrong — a discrepancy this clean and this repeated is itself a diagnostic clue.
- A sudden, out-of-character new pattern of frequent visits (a patient a staff member "knows by name" who is now unrecognizable, cognitively) should prompt a broader workup rather than a repeat of the same reflexive management (refilling the requested medication) that had been "working" on prior visits.
- Truncal ataxia without significant appendicular ataxia localizes preferentially to the cerebellar vermis, distinguishing it from hemispheric cerebellar lesions (which produce more limb/appendicular ataxia) and from sensory ataxia (which would typically be accompanied by other peripheral neuropathy findings).
- A remote, unmentioned surgical scar can be the single most important physical exam finding in an otherwise confusing case — patients do not reliably volunteer surgical history from a decade earlier, especially when cognitively impaired, and a scar that "doesn't fade" can be the thread that unravels the entire etiology.
- Roughly half of thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency cases occur in patients who do not use alcohol — cancer patients and those with poor nutritional access or malabsorption (including post-gastrectomy/pancreatectomy patients, whose prescribed post-surgical multivitamin regimen may quietly lapse) make up a substantial share, and alcohol use should not be a prerequisite for suspecting the diagnosis.
- Defining the clinical problem (turning a disorganized, seemingly psychiatric or behavioral presentation into a clean neurologic syndrome — "confabulation and ataxia in a nutritionally at-risk patient") is often far harder than solving it once properly defined; the diagnostic difficulty in cases like this lives almost entirely in the framing, not the final differential.
- Severe social isolation (no family, no community, an eviction notice, a lapsed utility bill) is itself a risk factor that should raise diagnostic suspicion for an unwitnessed cognitive or functional decline, since there is no one present in the patient's life to notice and report that "he's not acting like himself."

## Problem Representation

An insulin-dependent diabetic man with a remote gastrectomy/pancreatectomy history and no social support, presenting with a new pattern of frequent ED visits, disorganized recent memory, and truncal ataxia, ultimately diagnosed with Wernicke encephalopathy from unrecognized non-alcoholic thiamine deficiency.

## Case Narrative

The patient arrived by ambulance to a short-staffed, stretched emergency department, sitting comfortably with two canes, insisting he was there only because he had not received his insulin or oxycodone on a prior visit. His point-of-care glucose was 512 (EMS had recorded 497), with otherwise normal vital signs.

Before addressing management, the discussion built out the general framework for diabetic emergencies: DKA and HHS as the two true emergencies on a spectrum, with DKA typically presenting earlier (often with abdominal pain from ketosis, and glucose levels not always extremely elevated because normal kidney function is usually still intact at presentation) and HHS technically requiring altered mental status, meaning many patients with very high glucose values simply haven't yet reached that threshold when first seen. A key caution was raised: a low bicarbonate in a hyperglycemic patient should not be assumed to represent DKA before considering osmotic-diuresis-driven hypovolemia, lactic acidosis, and renal insufficiency as alternative explanations — a mistake the discussion noted has happened in real practice. For this patient specifically, with no abdominal pain and no tachypnea, fluids were prioritized over immediate insulin, with basic labs (a BMP with attention to the bicarbonate) to follow, and a note that even mild DKA is increasingly managed with subcutaneous insulin rather than an insulin drip, avoiding an ICU-level admission.

Before proceeding with a reflexive fluids-and-insulin plan, however, Rabih was struck by how angry and insistent the patient was about his medications, and paused to review the chart rather than simply refilling both prescriptions. This revealed a long history of poorly controlled insulin-dependent diabetes and severe, revascularization-refractory peripheral arterial disease explaining the chronic low-dose oxycodone. More strikingly, the notes tab showed eight ED visits in the preceding month — an unusual, new pattern — and the most recent visit's discharge summary explicitly stated that insulin had been refilled, while oxycodone had not (per an established pain contract), with a note that the patient's primary care provider had been alerted. This directly contradicted the patient's insistence that he had received neither medication. A colleague who had personally provided the insulin at that visit confirmed it directly.

Rather than assuming the patient was fabricating the claim (a common but, as the discussion noted, not obviously applicable assumption when the drug in question is insulin rather than an opioid, since there was no clear secondary-gain motive for misrepresenting an insulin refill), Rabih returned to talk with the patient further. The conversation became increasingly disorganized: the patient could not accurately recall how many times he had visited the ED (guessing two or three against an actual count of eight to ten), described himself as bedridden despite arriving with a cane-assisted gait, gave vague or evasive answers about family support, and reported having last seen his sister "a couple of years ago" in a way that did not track logically with the rest of his account. A separate colleague who knew the patient well by name confirmed this was an entirely new and unusual presentation for him.

This prompted a deliberate, thorough head-to-toe exam. The patient was alert and oriented to person, place, and time, with poor recall of recent events but well-preserved recall of distant events. He had bilateral gaze-evoked nystagmus and no appendicular ataxia, but severe truncal ataxia on standing and attempting to walk — explaining the two canes. He was not weak, and his cardiopulmonary exam was unremarkable. A surgical scar was noted across his abdomen; tracing back roughly a decade of chart notes revealed this was from a combined pancreatectomy and gastrectomy performed for a duodenal GIST tumor — the actual explanation for his insulin-dependent diabetes, distinct from typical type 1 or type 2 disease.

With this exam in hand, the ataxia was worked through systematically: the general differential for ataxia includes weakness, vestibular disorders, and normal pressure hydrocephalus (which can present insidiously with cognitive changes, gait abnormality, and urinary symptoms, though not all three need be present), but the specific finding of truncal — rather than appendicular — ataxia localizes preferentially to the cerebellar vermis rather than the cerebellar hemispheres or a peripheral sensory ataxia (which would typically come with other peripheral neuropathy findings). Given a time course better described as acute-to-subacute (unfolding over roughly a month) rather than hyperacute, a stroke code was not activated; instead, inflammatory, demyelinating, and infectious etiologies were prioritized, and an MRI of the brain with and without gadolinium was recommended as the single most informative next test — analogized to choosing a kidney biopsy over an exhaustive serologic panel in nephrotic syndrome, letting the pattern on imaging guide the next steps rather than testing broadly up front.

Before the imaging results were revealed, the discussion paused on the patient's social circumstances, uncovered while reviewing his chart: he had stopped paying his electricity bill and had his power cut off, had received an eviction notice for unpaid rent, and neighbors had reported him wandering the hallways of his building in vulnerable states. Despite having been seen personally by Rabih on two prior visits and by numerous other staff across ten total encounters, no one had connected these visits into a pattern until this one — a system explicitly built for acute diagnostic throughput, but poorly designed to catch a slow-motion decline in a patient with no family or community to flag that "he's not acting like himself."

The MRI, obtained within two hours with the aid of a small dose of lorazepam to help the patient tolerate the scan, showed no visible cerebellar lesion but did reveal symmetric abnormal signal in the thalamus, hypothalamus, and mammillary bodies bilaterally. Reasoning through what these structures do — thalamic and basal ganglia circuitry contributing to coordination (plausibly explaining the gait disturbance), a less certain link to the specific memory deficits, and the hypothalamus's roles in temperature regulation and vasopressin synthesis — the combined radiographic and clinical picture (acute-to-subacute ataxia with rapidly progressive cognitive impairment, and symmetric lesions in these specific structures) prompted Reza to state an explicit problem representation and, working purely from the pattern of structures involved, to independently arrive at thiamine deficiency as the leading hypothesis, given the classic association between mammillary body involvement and this diagnosis, with osmotic demyelination syndrome considered as an alternative pattern to rule out. He specifically raised the question of alcohol use, given the classic association, while noting that a socially isolated patient with unclear nutritional access should also raise this concern independent of alcohol history.

The formal read confirmed a symmetric pattern of abnormal signal in the thalamus, hypothalamus, and mammillary bodies, and the reading radiologist independently called it out as a classic pattern for thiamine deficiency. On further reflection, the discussion noted that thiamine deficiency occurs in non-alcohol-using patients in roughly half of cases, disproportionately in cancer patients and those with poor nutritional access — a category this patient fit precisely, given his gastrectomy and pancreatectomy. He had, in fact, been prescribed a post-surgical multivitamin regimen including thiamine after his original surgery years earlier, but had not been taking it, and this detail had never surfaced in his numerous recent ED visits because the surgical history itself required deliberately digging back roughly a decade into his chart to uncover — the patient did not volunteer it, and the only outward clue was the unexplained abdominal scar. Notably, Rabih candidly acknowledged that the actual reason he had ordered the MRI was to rule out a strategic stroke masquerading as diabetic confusion, not because he had independently constructed the nutritional-deficiency hypothesis Reza built purely from the imaging pattern and problem representation — and that he had, in fact, personally missed the same diagnosis on two prior visits with this same patient, treating the presentation each time as a straightforward request for insulin. The patient was admitted and started on high-dose IV thiamine (500mg), with a follow-up on his clinical course pending in a future episode.

The episode closed with a brief practice update on a prior RLR case (RLR 083, the Fusobacterium/hepatic infarct/portal vein thrombosis patient): repeat antiphospholipid serologies after discontinuing apixaban returned a persistently positive lupus anticoagulant, and given single-antibody positivity, hematology's guidance was that this represented a mild form of antiphospholipid syndrome not requiring conversion to warfarin — apixaban was deemed an acceptable (and even potentially dose-reducible) choice for a single-positive antiphospholipid patient, contrary to older teaching that anticoagulation in APLS requires warfarin.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| Patient's account directly contradicted by a colleague who personally administered the medication | Raises suspicion for a cognitive/memory problem rather than either dishonesty or a documentation error |
| Inaccurate visit count, illogical timeline, confused answers about family | Raises suspicion for a confabulation/amnestic syndrome |
| New, out-of-character pattern of frequent visits in a patient known well by staff | Raises suspicion for an unwitnessed, evolving process rather than baseline behavior |
| Truncal ataxia without appendicular ataxia | Localizes to cerebellar vermis over hemispheres or sensory ataxia |
| Bilateral gaze-evoked nystagmus + poor recent memory + ataxia | Builds toward a triad-pattern neurologic syndrome (ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus, ataxia, confusion) |
| Unexplained abdominal surgical scar | Prompts chart archaeology revealing remote gastrectomy/pancreatectomy |
| MRI: symmetric signal abnormality in thalamus, hypothalamus, mammillary bodies | Classic pattern for thiamine deficiency (Wernicke encephalopathy) |
| No alcohol use elicited; history of GI surgery and social/nutritional vulnerability | Supports non-alcoholic thiamine deficiency (malabsorption/malnutrition mechanism) |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Wernicke encephalopathy illness script: the classic triad of confusion, ataxia, and ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus (though all three need not be present simultaneously), with MRI showing symmetric T2/FLAIR signal change in the mammillary bodies, medial thalami, periaqueductal gray, and hypothalamus; classically associated with alcohol use disorder, but occurring in roughly half of cases from non-alcoholic causes such as malnutrition, malabsorption (including post-bariatric or post-gastrectomy states), and malignancy.

The case is an extended demonstration of the difference between solving a well-defined problem and defining an ambiguous one — Rabih explicitly frames his own repeated prior misses (attributing the presentation twice to a simple medication-refill request) as a failure of problem definition rather than problem solving, and credits Reza's independently constructed, exam-driven differential (arriving at thiamine deficiency purely from the structures involved on imaging, without yet knowing the surgical history) as the moment the "problem" became well-defined enough to solve. It is also an explicit lesson in decoupling social vulnerability from diagnostic reasoning as an afterthought — instead treating the patient's isolation and unraveling life circumstances as an active red flag that should raise, not lower, diagnostic suspicion.

## Teaching Discussion

The central teaching point is the discipline of investigating both sides of a stated discrepancy — patient account versus medical record — rather than resolving it by assumption in either direction, and using an unresolved, illogical discrepancy as an active diagnostic signal (here, a signal of an amnestic/confabulatory process) rather than an administrative annoyance to be smoothed over.

A second teaching point is the anatomic localization value of truncal versus appendicular ataxia (vermis versus hemispheres) as an efficient way to prioritize the differential and the correct next test (MRI) without needing to run an exhaustive battery of investigations first — explicitly likened to choosing a single high-yield test (a kidney biopsy, or here, an MRI) to let the pattern guide subsequent steps, rather than testing broadly and hoping for an answer.

The most memorable teaching point, explicitly named by both hosts, is the distinction between solving a well-defined clinical problem and doing the much harder work of defining it in the first place — most of the diagnostic difficulty in this case lived in recognizing that "insulin and oxycodone refill request" was the wrong problem representation, not in generating a differential once "confabulation and ataxia in a nutritionally at-risk patient" was correctly identified as the actual problem.
