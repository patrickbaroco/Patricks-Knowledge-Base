## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 62-year-old man presents to the ED with abdominal pain and [[Abdominal Distension|distension]], mildly tachycardic and *hypertensive*. Rabih treats the distension as low-yield on its own — a [[Solid Liquid Gas Framework|solid/liquid/gas]] problem that, like most abdominal pain, resolves only with a picture — while noting the hypertension argues *against* [[Cirrhosis]] (a vasodilatory, low-SVR state). CT reveals the real story: a markedly distended bladder ([[Urinary Retention]]) and hard stool in the rectum with [[Stercoral Colitis]]. The two feed a [[Constipation–Retention Cycle|pelvic-crowding vicious cycle]] — impacted colon compresses urinary outflow, the full bladder compresses the colon. History then supplies the root cause: ten days earlier the patient saw urology for incontinence, had a UTI, and was started on [[Oxybutynin]], an [[Anticholinergic]] that plausibly precipitated the retention. Management is decompression (foley, >1 L out) plus an escalating bowel regimen that ultimately breaks the impaction with [[Magnesium Citrate]] after enemas and [[Manual Disimpaction]] fail. The teaching heart of the episode is a medical student, Tyler, who kept [[Asking Why|asking "why"]] until he traced the whole cascade back to the oxybutynin.

## Pearls

- **Abdominal [[Abdominal Distension|distension]] is a [[Solid Liquid Gas Framework|solid/liquid/gas]] problem — but it adds little over abdominal pain.** Distension can be solid (adipose, pregnancy, organomegaly), liquid (ascites or blood), or gas (free peritoneal air, or bowel gas from ileus/obstruction), yet you usually can't tell which without imaging. Pain plus distension mostly just pushes the needle harder toward getting a picture.
- **Hypertension argues against [[Cirrhosis]].** Advanced cirrhosis is a vasodilatory, low-systemic-vascular-resistance state (failed hepatic clearance of nitric oxide and other mediators, manifest on the skin as spider angiomas); by the time ascites is present, patients are typically *hypo*tensive. A distended, hypertensive patient makes cirrhotic ascites unlikely.
- **Constipation and [[Urinary Retention]] can drive a self-reinforcing cycle in older adults.** Pelvic crowding from an impacted colon compresses the urinary outflow tract, and the resulting distended bladder compresses the colon — each worsening the other, much like a mediastinal mass pressing on both esophagus and bronchus.
- **[[Stercoral Colitis]] is pressure-necrosis colitis from hard stool, and its danger lies as much in the host as in the rectum.** Impacted stool causes local ischemia with a real risk of bacterial translocation and perforation; because it strikes frail, often cognitively impaired, comorbid patients, morbidity is high. Uncomplicated cases are managed like uncomplicated diverticulitis — internist's hands, aggressive disimpaction — with surgery reserved for perforation or other complication.
- **Refractory constipation has an escalation ladder.** Combine a bulking/osmotic agent ([[Polyethylene Glycol|Miralax]]) with a motility agent (senna) and work from below (soap-suds or mineral-oil enemas); when that fails, reach for an osmotic cathartic ([[Magnesium Citrate]] or milk of magnesia — the bowel-prep bottles) and consider [[Manual Disimpaction]] or GI consultation.
- **[[Asking Why|Ask "why"]] until you reach the root cause.** Treating the retention and constipation downstream misses that a single upstream event — the newly started [[Oxybutynin|anticholinergic]] — plausibly triggered the entire cascade. Fixing the root cause treats the disease; treating only its ramifications does not.

## Problem Representation

A 62-year-old man presents with abdominal pain and distension, mild tachycardia, and mild hypertension, found on CT to have marked urinary retention and hard stool impaction with stercoral colitis and an ascending UTI — a picture of coupled bowel-and-bladder outlet obstruction in an older man, temporally linked to an anticholinergic ([[Oxybutynin]]) started ten days earlier at a urology visit for urinary incontinence.

## Case Narrative

The case opens with a 62-year-old man in the ED with [[Abdominal Distension|abdominal pain and distension]]. Triage vitals show mild tachycardia and mild hypertension with a normal temperature, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation; the abdomen is distended and mildly tender, and the presenter (Reza) candidly withholds the percussion data because his percussion is unreliable. Rabih, discussing, immediately deflates distension as a high-value clue. He frames it as a question of *what phase of matter* is taking up the extra space — solid material (benign adipose or pregnancy, or organomegaly of spleen or liver), liquid ([[Ascites]] or, more ominously, blood), or gas (free intraperitoneal air, or gas trapped in a colon dilated by ileus or obstruction). His point is epistemic: the bedside exam, even robustly studied in ascites, rarely distinguishes these, so distension mostly reduces to "get imaging" — the same conclusion abdominal pain already forces for most patients outside a short list (zoster, hernia, pancreatitis). Distension simply pushes the needle further toward a picture.

Before imaging, Rabih extracts one rule-out from the vitals: the patient's hypertension is "markedly inconsistent" with [[Cirrhosis]]. Asked to explain, he lays out that cirrhosis is a vasodilatory state — the failing liver cannot clear mediators such as nitric oxide, producing a low-systemic-vascular-resistance physiology visible on the skin as spider angiomas — so patients with cirrhotic ascites are typically hypotensive by the time ascites appears. A hypertensive patient with distension is therefore unlikely to have cirrhosis.

The CT reframes the case. It shows significant [[Urinary Retention]] — a markedly distended bladder — plus solid stool in the rectum with [[Stercoral Colitis]]. Rabih is careful about inference: the picture proves the bladder is full, but not that the patient is *trying* to void, so retention must still be confirmed clinically (though a radiologist can reasonably infer it from sheer bladder volume). He builds the retention differential as either a blockage (usually [[Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia|BPH]]) or a failure of the neural signal to void (central, in the cord, or peripheral, as in diabetic [[Neurogenic Bladder]]). Then he connects the two findings: pelvic crowding from an impacted colon can compress the urinary outflow tract, and the resulting big bladder compresses the colon in return — a [[Constipation–Retention Cycle|vicious cycle]] common in older adults, which the hosts riff about wanting to give a name of its own. He situates stercoral colitis within a broader map of constipation's complications — common ones (hemorrhoids, anal fissure, urinary retention) and morbid ones ([[Sigmoid Volvulus]] and stercoral colitis) — and explains that stercoral colitis is a pressure-induced colitis whose morbidity and mortality reflect both local events (translocation, perforation) and the frail, comorbid, often cognitively impaired host who develops it. He would decompress both systems — confirm the patient can't void and place a foley, then attack the constipation — and, absent perforation, treat it like uncomplicated diverticulitis rather than reflexively calling surgery.

History fills in the arc. The patient has been constipated for about nine days with several days of decreased urine output; ten days ago — before the retention — he saw urology for urinary incontinence. An indwelling foley now drains over a liter. An initial aggressive regimen (Miralax plus mineral-oil enemas every three hours) fails to evacuate the rectum. The urology visit is the pivot: he had no retention then, only lower urinary tract symptoms and incontinence, a urinalysis growing >100,000 CFU of *E. coli*, and — critically — a new prescription for [[Oxybutynin]] for his symptoms; the same CT also noted an ascending UTI with perinephric changes. At the bedside, the medical student and then Reza attempt [[Manual Disimpaction]] before an audience of curious nurses; Reza is disarmingly honest about struggling to distinguish rectal folds from the stool ball, sweeping with a "helicopter" finger motion, and ultimately failing. A phoned friend suggests [[Magnesium Citrate]], which works. The next day the patient himself dictates a near-perfect plan: an abdominal x-ray to exclude obstruction, another dose of magnesium citrate, 24 more hours of mineral-oil enemas, discontinuation of the oxybutynin, and removal of the foley on the reasoning that his retention was driven by the constipation.

The pathophysiology capstone is the [[Anticholinergic]] paradox. Rabih walks through the three forms of [[Urinary Incontinence]] — [[Overflow Incontinence|overflow]] (storage capacity exceeded behind a tight prostate; most common in older men), [[Stress Incontinence|stress]] (weak pelvic floor, leak with raised abdominal pressure), and [[Overactive Bladder]] (detrusor overactivity, near-random voiding) — and explains why a urologist would start an anticholinergic like oxybutynin in a man with obstruction: chronic BPH can hypertrophy the bladder muscle and produce a *secondary* overactive bladder, which the anticholinergic is meant to quiet. In this patient the experiment backfired, plausibly tipping him into retention — though Rabih declines to brand it an error, having seen it help others. The episode closes on its real lesson: the student, Tyler, kept [[Asking Why|asking "why"]] — why constipation, why retention — until he tracked the cascade back to the oxybutynin started nine days earlier, a temporal match that most clinicians (Rabih admits, himself included) might have missed while busy decompressing bowel and bladder.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Data point | Hypothesis raised/adjusted | Direction of update |
|---|---|---|
| Abdominal pain + distension | Broad intra-abdominal process requiring imaging | Distension adds little over pain; both point to imaging |
| Mild tachycardia, mild **hypertension** | Cirrhotic ascites | Strong decrease (cirrhosis is a hypotensive, vasodilatory state) |
| CT: markedly distended bladder | Urinary retention | Raised strongly (still needs clinical confirmation of intent to void) |
| CT: hard stool in rectum + stercoral colitis | Severe impaction / stercoral colitis | Raised; flags perforation/translocation risk |
| Coexisting retention + impaction | Pelvic-crowding vicious cycle | Links the two problems mechanistically |
| Constipation 9 days; retention only several days | Constipation as the earlier/primary driver | Sequences the cascade (bowel before bladder) |
| Urology visit 10 days ago, no retention then | Iatrogenic trigger at that visit | Directs search upstream to the visit |
| Oxybutynin (anticholinergic) started at that visit | Drug-induced urinary retention | Large increase — candidate root cause |
| UA >100,000 CFU *E. coli*; perinephric changes on CT | UTI / ascending pyelonephritis | Raised as contributor/comorbidity |
| Enemas + manual disimpaction fail; mag citrate works | Refractory impaction needing osmotic cathartic | Guides escalation up the constipation ladder |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Distinguishing what is known from what is inferred.** Rabih repeatedly separates hard data from inference — the CT proves a full bladder but not the *desire* to void; percussion data is withheld because it's unreliable. Naming the limits of each datum prevents overreading the exam and the image.
- **A single vital sign used as a discriminator.** Hypertension is deployed to down-weight cirrhosis via a mechanistic link (vasodilatory, low-SVR physiology), illustrating how one reliable data point can prune a common cause rather than being averaged into a vague gestalt.
- **[[Root Cause Analysis|Root-cause reasoning]] versus treating downstream effects.** The student's iterative "why constipation? why retention?" is the episode's central cognitive move — it converts a two-problem symptomatic picture into a single upstream cause (oxybutynin), the counter-move to [[Diagnostic Momentum]] and to a management-only, symptom-chasing reflex.
- **Resisting [[Premature Closure]] on the obvious comorbidity.** With a documented UTI and impaction on the scan, it would be easy to stop at "treat the UTI and disimpact." Tracing the timeline instead surfaces the iatrogenic trigger that the salient findings would otherwise have masked.
- **Withholding the "error" label.** Rabih explicitly declines to code the oxybutynin as a mistake, noting it helps some patients with secondary overactive bladder — separating a *bad outcome* from a *bad decision* and avoiding hindsight bias in the debrief.
- **Framework-driven differentials.** Solid/liquid/gas for distension, blockage-versus-neural-signal for retention, and the three-type taxonomy of incontinence each show pre-built scripts organizing an otherwise sprawling space into a small, checkable set.

## Teaching Discussion

- **Distension rarely earns its keep as an independent clue.** Because bedside distinction of solid, liquid, and gas is unreliable, distension usually collapses into the same imaging recommendation that abdominal pain already generates; treat it as an amplifier of the pretest need for imaging, not a specific localizing sign.
- **Let a discriminating vital sign do work.** Hypertension is meaningful evidence against cirrhotic ascites given the vasodilatory, low-SVR physiology of advanced cirrhosis — use the mechanism, not just pattern-matching, to justify the update.
- **Model bowel and bladder as a coupled system in older adults.** Impaction and retention can each cause and worsen the other; decompress both and look for a single trigger rather than treating them as unrelated problems.
- **Know stercoral colitis and its dual source of risk.** It is pressure-necrosis colitis from impacted stool with meaningful perforation and translocation risk, but much of its morbidity rides on the frail host; manage uncomplicated disease like uncomplicated diverticulitis, escalating to surgery only for complications.
- **Have an escalation ladder for refractory constipation.** Bulking/osmotic agent plus a motility agent plus enemas from below, then osmotic cathartics (magnesium citrate, milk of magnesia), then manual disimpaction or GI — and be honest that manual disimpaction is technically hard and often incomplete.
- **Understand the anticholinergic-in-obstruction paradox.** Chronic BPH can cause secondary detrusor overactivity, which is why a urologist may prescribe oxybutynin despite outlet obstruction; the trade-off is a real risk of precipitating retention, as likely happened here.
- **The three incontinence types guide therapy.** Overflow (most common in older men, behind a tight prostate), stress (weak pelvic floor), and overactive bladder (detrusor overactivity) point to different mechanisms and treatments; misclassifying drives the wrong drug.
- **Asking "why" is a teachable, high-yield habit.** Root-cause reasoning turned a symptom-management case into an iatrogenic diagnosis; cultivate the discipline of tracing a timeline back to its trigger, because the trigger is what you can actually stop.
