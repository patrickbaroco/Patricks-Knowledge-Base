## Pearls

- **[[Dizziness|Dizziness]] localizes to one of three organs — heart, blood, or nervous system.** When a patient says "dizzy," the answer, if found, lies in the cardiovascular system (the causes of [[Presyncope|presyncope]]), the blood ([[Hypoglycemia|hypoglycemia]] or low hemoglobin, including [[Dyshemoglobinemia|dyshemoglobinemias]] such as carbon monoxide), or a neurologic disorder (usually [[Vertigo|vertigo]], occasionally non-vertiginous phenomena like [[CSF Hypotension|CSF hypotension]]). Anchoring straight onto vertigo skips this map.
- **Run a second, actionable schema: [[Triggered vs Continuous Dizziness|triggered versus continuous]].** Triggered dizziness starts with a provocation and stops when it stops (the model is presyncope on standing); continuous dizziness runs on an impulse that does not care whether the patient moves (stroke, vestibular neuritis). The caveat: *no one* feels perfect the instant a trigger is removed, so what you grade is the **delta** between provoked and unprovoked states, not its complete absence — and that delta can be genuinely hard to read.
- **Abrupt onset is the feature that pushes continuous vertigo toward central.** A vertigo syndrome that switches on suddenly and persists raises [[Posterior Circulation Stroke|posterior-circulation stroke]]; the bedside discriminator is the [[HINTS Exam|HINTS exam]], more sensitive than early MRI for posterior stroke — but only in expert hands, and worth naming out loud when it is beyond your own.
- **A negative CTA excludes large-vessel thrombus and profiles risk, but clean brain vessels do not exclude [[Cardioembolic Stroke|embolic]] stroke.** Brain infarction is as readily embolic as thrombotic, so pristine vessels still leave [[Atrial Fibrillation|cardioembolism]] on the table — in sharp contrast to clean coronaries, which meaningfully lower [[Acute Coronary Syndrome|ACS]] because coronary disease is an in-situ, local process and coronary embolism is rare.
- **An abrupt central-type deficit that fully resolves within 24 hours is, by definition, a [[Transient Ischemic Attack|TIA]] — believe it only if you find its fingerprint.** Look for the substrate: [[Atrial Fibrillation|atrial fibrillation]] on EKG, lower-extremity edema hinting at venous thrombus, any cardioembolic source. Absent a risk-factor fingerprint, the TIA hypothesis weakens and the case pivots to a peripheral vertigo or a severe [[Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo|episodic]] syndrome.
- **The reassuring evolution of a syndrome can outweigh fancy imaging — and severe-appearing vertigo skews peripheral.** The single most helpful datum here was that the patient got dramatically better; a patient who looks *very* sick from vertigo is, empirically, more likely peripheral than central. Getting back to work the next day is, as Rabih put it, the best prognostic sign.

## Problem Representation

A previously healthy 67-year-old man with hyperlipidemia on atorvastatin — no hypertension, no tobacco, no significant alcohol, and a reassuring family history — develops abrupt dizziness at 10 p.m. while rising to use the restroom, with a sensation of falling toward his right side, marked worsening on any head movement, nausea, and one episode of emesis. The course is continuous rather than clearly triggered, and an initial FaceTime neurologic screen shows intact extraocular pursuit without appreciable nystagmus. In a community emergency department a code stroke is activated within minutes: non-contrast head CT shows no hemorrhage, CTA of the head and neck shows no large-vessel thrombus and no atherosclerosis, and vital signs and laboratory studies (CBC, chemistries, liver panel, lipids, A1c) are normal. Admitted on aspirin for monitoring, he has roughly ten episodes of emesis overnight, improves by the early morning, and is entirely well the next day; a deliberately delayed MRI, an EKG, and overnight telemetry are all normal. The presentation is ultimately judged a peripheral vertigo syndrome — a severe BPPV, vestibular neuritis, or vestibular migraine — rather than a central or cardioembolic event, with the precise cause left undetermined.

## Case Narrative

After the signature palindrome banter and a warm aside — Reza recounts localizing a kind, 91-year-old inpatient as "a professor" from his manner of speaking, and both hosts pitch the forthcoming RLR CP Solvers website that will house schemas, illness scripts, and teaching videos beyond the podcast — Reza turns to a case that kept him up at night, promising to reveal at the end why it was so personal. He was at home eating a red-leaf salad when he took a phone call.

The caller's daughter relays that a 67-year-old man got up around 10 p.m. to use the restroom and felt acutely [[Dizziness|dizzy]], as though he would fall — specifically toward his right side — though he did not fall. The dizziness returned as he headed back to bed, prompting him to move slowly downstairs and sit motionless in a chair, because any movement of his head worsened it. He reported nausea followed immediately by an episode of emesis. Reza FaceTimed the family, saw a man too uncomfortable to move his head, ran a quick cranial-nerve screen (the patient followed his fingers), and did not appreciate [[Nystagmus|nystagmus]] over video.

Rabih declines to anchor on [[Vertigo|vertigo]] and offers instead a place to start that ignores specific diagnoses and maneuvers. When a patient says "dizzy," he teaches, the answer — if one is ever found — lies in one of three organs. The **heart** supplies the causes of [[Presyncope|presyncope]]. The **blood** supplies [[Hypoglycemia|low sugar]] and low hemoglobin, including [[Dyshemoglobinemia|dyshemoglobinemias]] such as carbon monoxide. The **nervous system** supplies the world of vertigo, plus a few non-vertiginous perceptions of dizziness tied to CSF pressure, such as [[CSF Hypotension|CSF hypotension]]. Onto this "lay of the land" he layers a second, actionable schema: is the dizziness **[[Triggered vs Continuous Dizziness|triggered]]** — present with a provocation and gone when it stops, as in presyncope on standing — or **continuous**, driven by an impulse indifferent to movement, as in a stroke or vestibular neuritis? He reads this man's abrupt, persistent, movement-sensitive syndrome as continuous vertigo, and flags that its abruptness is highly concerning for a central cause, namely a [[Posterior Circulation Stroke|stroke]]. He inserts the one major caveat: everyone with dizziness — cardiac, hematologic, or neurologic — feels imperfect even when the trigger is removed, so what matters is the *size of the delta* between triggered and untriggered states, not its total disappearance. In this patient there is little delta; he is simply, continuously dizzy.

The bedside tool to separate central from peripheral continuous vertigo is the [[HINTS Exam|HINTS exam]], which acutely is even more sensitive than MRI for a posterior stroke. Both hosts, with characteristic candor, dwell on how operator-dependent it is: Rabih likens his own HINTS to a non-contrast CT of the abdomen next to an expert's contrast-enhanced study, and both agree it is reliable only in trained hands — a David Newman-Toker or an Aaron Berkowitz — not in theirs. In Rabih's real ED, this presentation would trigger a code stroke and neurology would perform the HINTS; his own is "not good enough."

Reza then reveals the patient is his father, who consented to the story and who once asked why Reza had no video on vertigo. Reza notes the discipline he learned early: not to *care for* family, but to help guide them to the appropriate setting. He advised the emergency department, and his father went to a new community hospital in the suburbs of Wexford, where Reza was struck by the quality of bread-and-butter care. A code stroke fired within minutes. A non-contrast head CT showed no hemorrhage; a CTA of the head and neck showed no thrombus and no atherosclerosis. Vital signs (drawn before imaging) were normal, and the CBC, liver chemistries, renal panel, lipids, and A1c were all normal. He emerged from imaging feeling terrible, was admitted for monitoring, and was given aspirin. Overnight he had roughly ten episodes of emesis; by 4–5 a.m. he felt better, and by the next day he felt completely well.

Rabih reflects that the sequence of events is deeply reassuring, and that the syndrome's **evolution** — the patient getting much better — is more helpful than any of the "fancy, schmancy" imaging. Taking each datum in turn: a non-contrast CT is a fast first step but is not sensitive for posterior-fossa disease; a negative CTA is valuable because it excludes a large thrombosis (a medical emergency) and profiles a patient's risk factors, but — he cautions with emphasis — it does **not** exclude [[Cardioembolic Stroke|cardioembolic disease]], which can arise in [[Atrial Fibrillation|atrial fibrillation]]. He draws the contrast that clean coronaries can be used to argue down [[Acute Coronary Syndrome|acute coronary syndrome]] precisely because coronary disease is an in-situ, local process and coronary embolism is rare, whereas brain disease is as readily embolic as thrombotic — so clean brain vessels easily still permit an embolus. He then labels the increasingly reassuring picture: an abrupt central-type concern lasting under 24 hours is the definition of a [[Transient Ischemic Attack|TIA]]. A TIA would gain credence from a fingerprint of embolic risk — atrial fibrillation on EKG, lower-extremity edema suggesting a venous thrombus — and lose it if no such substrate appears, at which point the case pivots to peripheral continuous vertigo ([[Vestibular Neuritis|vestibular neuritis]], [[Meniere Disease|Meniere disease]], [[Vestibular Migraine|vestibular migraine]]) or a very severe episodic vertigo like a bad [[Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo|BPPV]] that lasted unusually long. He names the resulting **tension**: the most morbid diagnosis, stroke, has not been definitively excluded — even an MRI would not fully exclude it now — so pursuing MRI if feasible is reasonable while all hypotheses remain live.

The plan was an MRI the *next* day, on the logic that an early MRI can miss a stroke not yet radiographically apparent; the delayed MRI was normal. The EKG was normal, there was no lower-extremity edema, and overnight telemetry showed sinus rhythm. Reza had expected to add an echocardiogram and a Holter to hunt for occult atrial fibrillation, but the vertigo expert Aaron Berkowitz — reachable by text at 10 p.m. — and the hospital neurologist both reassured him: the way the syndrome unfolded, together with the exam, made a central, embolic process very unlikely, and a patient who looks *very* sick from vertigo is more often peripheral than central. Reza, moved by being on the receiving end of a clinician's phone call to family, reflects on how much that communication matters. The consensus settled on a peripheral cause, much more likely than a transient embolic phenomenon — though precisely which peripheral cause is unclear. Berkowitz noted he had never seen a BPPV severe enough to bring a patient to the ED (without excluding it); vestibular neuritis fit poorly given no sick contacts and overnight improvement; and vestibular migraine struck Reza as compelling. His father, back to managing his properties the next day, texted that it did not sound like vestibular neuritis or BPPV — "what's going on with you, Reza?" — to which the honest answer was "I don't know."

In a brief second segment, Rabih adds that BPPV is the overwhelming cause of vertigo he sees in the ED — perhaps nine of ten cases — but that the vast majority of those patients have had many prior stereotyped episodes, making a first presentation uncommon; neurology is essentially never consulted, so an emergency physician's lens on vertigo differs sharply from a neurologist's. Few of these patients are hospitalized. Reza mentions his mother has had BPPV too, teeing up a future case, and the two close on their running theme of being "the champions of I don't know."

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring]] deliberately refused at the front door.** Reza's explicit praise is for *not* anchoring on vertigo — Rabih steps back to ask whether the organ at fault is the heart, the blood, or the nervous system before entertaining any vestibular diagnosis. Naming the anchor and resisting it is the episode's opening reasoning move.
- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|Two stacked schemas]] structure an otherwise formless symptom.** The three-organ localization ("heart, blood, or brain") maps the terrain, while the [[Triggered vs Continuous Dizziness|triggered-versus-continuous]] axis is the actionable, real-time discriminator. Each new datum lands in a slot rather than drifting, and the hosts' honesty that even the triggered/continuous call is hard keeps the schema from being applied mechanically.
- **[[Illness Tempo|Tempo and evolution]] treated as primary data.** Rabih's central teaching is that the *evolution* of the syndrome — steady, dramatic improvement — is more informative than the CT, CTA, or MRI; the story getting better is itself the most reassuring test result. This mirrors the recurring RLR lesson that an unfolding illness must be read over time, not frozen at one frame.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Interpreting a negative test by disease mechanism]].** The set-piece insight is that a "clean" vascular study means different things in different beds: clean coronaries argue down [[Acute Coronary Syndrome|ACS]] because coronary disease is in-situ and local, whereas clean brain vessels do *not* argue down stroke because cerebral infarction is as embolic as it is thrombotic. The value of a negative result is conditioned on the pretest anatomy of the disease, not read off the report alone.
- **Calibrating to one's own [[HINTS Exam|operator skill]].** Both hosts refuse to claim a test they cannot reliably perform; the HINTS exam is sensitive *in expert hands*, and pretending otherwise would manufacture false reassurance. Knowing the ceiling of one's own bedside exam is itself a reasoning skill.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Humility]] and the discipline of not treating family.** The case closes on "I don't know" as an earned endpoint rather than a failure, and on the professional boundary of guiding — not directing — a family member's care. Reza's reflection on how much a clinician's phone call to an anxious family means is a parallel lesson in the relational side of reasoning.

## Teaching Discussion

- Meet the word "dizzy" with the three-organ map before any maneuver: the heart (the presyncope causes), the blood (hypoglycemia, low hemoglobin, dyshemoglobinemia), and the nervous system (vertigo, and rare non-vertiginous causes such as CSF hypotension) — then use triggered-versus-continuous as the real-time discriminator, remembering that the graded quantity is the delta between provoked and unprovoked states, not its complete absence.
- Let abrupt onset raise your concern for a central cause of continuous vertigo, and reach for HINTS to separate central from peripheral — but name honestly whether the exam is within your own competence, because its sensitivity lives in expert hands and a poorly performed HINTS offers false comfort.
- Read a negative CTA for exactly what it gives: exclusion of large-vessel thrombus and a picture of atherosclerotic risk, not exclusion of cardioembolism — clean brain vessels still permit an embolic stroke, unlike clean coronaries and ACS, because the brain is as embolic as it is thrombotic while the coronaries are a local, in-situ bed.
- Treat an abrupt central-type deficit that clears within 24 hours as a TIA only when you can find its fingerprint (atrial fibrillation, a venous source, lower-extremity edema); when no substrate appears despite EKG, telemetry, and exam, let the diagnosis migrate toward a peripheral vertigo or a severe episodic syndrome.
- Weigh the evolution of the syndrome as hard data — a patient who improves dramatically, and who looked very sick from vertigo, skews peripheral — and time the MRI to the disease, since an MRI obtained too early can miss a posterior stroke that only later becomes radiographically visible.
- Hold peripheral vertigo as a differential, not a single answer: severe BPPV can rarely be dramatic (though it seldom reaches the ED), vestibular neuritis is argued down by absent sick contacts and rapid resolution, and vestibular migraine may fit best — and "I don't know" is a legitimate, honest endpoint when the fingerprint never appears.
- Remember the relational layer: guide family to the right setting rather than treating them yourself, and recognize how much a brief, proactive phone call to an anxious family changes their experience of care.
