## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 74-year-old man presents with groin pain and is found to have right inguinal lymphadenopathy on ultrasound. A methodical lymph node exam finds nothing else palpable except a "bruise" on the right third toe, but a falling hemoglobin reframes the case as anemia with localized lymphadenopathy. Imaging reveals a large, incidental gastric mass (later an unrelated GIST) alongside retroperitoneal and mesenteric lymphadenopathy, pulling the differential toward gastric malignancy and lymphoma — until the "bruise" on the toe is re-examined, reclassified as hyperpigmentation with an elevated, oozing nail bed, and ultimately diagnosed as subungual melanoma.

## Pearls

- For groin pain with a normal or equivocal exam, don't stop at "no hernia" — think referred pain from adjacent structures (hip, spine, retroperitoneum/ureter) before concluding the exam is truly unrevealing.
- Palpable lymphadenopathy in one region should always prompt two questions: is this the tip of the iceberg (systemic) or truly local, and are the locally draining structures (genitals, leg, perianal area) and other lymph node basins also involved?
- A significant, unexplained hemoglobin drop in a patient with lymphadenopathy should be prioritized as its own problem — split the workup into bleeding versus hemolysis (peripheral smear, retic count, LDH, haptoglobin, liver chemistries) before assuming the anemia is simply "from" the lymph node process.
- Retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy is uncommon and should narrow, not broaden, the differential — think aggressive lymphoma, IgG4-related disease, or non-Langerhans cell histiocytosis, roughly in that order of likelihood.
- The word chosen to describe a finding shapes the diagnostic path: calling a nail-bed finding a "bruise" invites dismissal by trauma, while recognizing it as hyperpigmentation reframes it immediately toward melanoma — precise descriptive language is itself a diagnostic act.
- A large, dramatic incidental finding (here, an 8x7 cm gastric mass) can become a "massive distractor" that consumes the workup's attention even when it turns out to be unrelated to the actual answer.

## Problem Representation

A 74-year-old man presents with groin pain and is found on ultrasound to have right inguinal lymphadenopathy without other palpable adenopathy, a new hemoglobin drop from a normal baseline, and a chronic nail-bed finding on the right third toe initially dismissed as a bruise; cross-sectional imaging reveals an incidental large gastric mass alongside retroperitoneal and inguinal lymphadenopathy, but the gastric mass (a GIST) is ultimately unrelated, and re-examination of the toe — hyperpigmentation and nail bed elevation rather than a bruise — localizes the true diagnosis to subungual melanoma metastatic to the lymph nodes.

## Case Narrative

A 74-year-old man presents to the emergency department with inguinal and groin pain. Before any exam findings are known, the discussant reasons that groin pain should prompt a direct look at the groin first, since an inguinal hernia or muscle strain is common; a negative exam does not exclude a hernia (patients must sometimes be examined standing, since contents can retract into the scrotum) and should also prompt consideration of referred pain from the hip or from a retroperitoneal structure such as the ureter.

The exam is equivocal — the examiner suspects something is present but cannot characterize it — so an ultrasound is obtained directly from the ED without labs, showing three to four right-sided inguinal lymph nodes, each 2 cm or larger. With this finding, the discussant reasons that palpable inguinal lymphadenopathy should prompt examination of the structures that drain to that basin (the penis, the leg) for a local cause such as cellulitis, as well as a broader lymph node survey; any additional palpable adenopathy elsewhere would justify cross-sectional imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis with contrast.

A comprehensive re-examination follows: no other symptoms besides groin discomfort, and no lymphadenopathy anywhere else palpable — cervical, axillary, epitrochlear, or contralateral inguinal chains are all negative, as is a digital rectal exam. The only other finding is a bruise on the right third toe, which the patient does not recall causing. Labs are sent given the ED visit: all normal except hemoglobin, which has fallen from a baseline of 15 (six to seven months prior) to 11.

The discussant treats the bruised toe as low-yield (a common, easily explained finding — many people have unexplained bruised digits) but the hemoglobin drop reframes the case: the problem to solve is now anemia with localized lymphadenopathy, and the anemia is prioritized. A bleeding history (GI, GU, pulmonary, skin) is pursued, alongside consideration of hemolysis; normal vital signs argue for a subacute rather than acute process. The plan is a peripheral smear, reticulocyte count, LDH, haptoglobin, liver chemistries, and — once bleeding and hemolysis are addressed — a ferritin, transferrin, and TIBC to assess for iron deficiency as a major branch point.

Results: an inappropriately low reticulocyte count, normal ferritin and percent saturation, an LDH in the 500 range, and a normal peripheral smear with no abnormalities noted; liver enzymes are normal. CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast shows large right inguinal lymphadenopathy (>2 cm) plus scattered large retroperitoneal and mesenteric lymphadenopathy, and — the dominant new finding — a large gastric mass measuring roughly 8 by 7 cm, with no other abdominal or pelvic abnormality.

Faced with a large gastric mass, diffuse lymphadenopathy, and an elevated LDH, the discussant reasons that the elevated LDH reflects increased cell turnover from either malignancy (lymphoproliferative or solid) or hemolysis, and that hemolysis should still be actively excluded (haptoglobin) even as the lymph node findings make malignancy the leading explanation. The gastric mass, lymphadenopathy pattern, and elevated LDH are unified around a working hypothesis of gastric malignancy with nodal spread — approached by first asking whether it is a hematologic (lymphoma, including gastric MALT lymphoma from H. pylori) or solid (adenocarcinoma) process, with a leaning toward lymphoma given the retroperitoneal and inguinal nodal pattern and the high LDH, while acknowledging adenocarcinoma (including an aggressive mucinous subtype) remains fair game. An upper endoscopy with biopsy is planned.

The EGD reveals a gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) without evidence of invasion beyond the gastric wall, appearing longstanding and localized — explicitly not linked to the lymphadenopathy by the team. With the GIST excluded as the explanation for the LDH and lymphadenopathy, attention returns to the retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy itself: this pattern is uncommon and narrows toward lymphoma (favoring an aggressive subtype such as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma over an indolent process, given the degree of adenopathy and subacute anemia), with IgG4-related disease considered but felt unlikely absent typical involvement elsewhere (pancreas, orbit, lung), and non-Langerhans cell histiocytosis noted as a less likely alternative. A Coombs test is sent given the association between lymphoproliferative disease and autoimmune hemolysis; an excisional (not fine-needle or core) biopsy of the accessible inguinal node is planned specifically to preserve architecture for immunophenotyping and flow cytometry. Serologic testing (HIV, syphilis, EBV, CMV) is sent as a potential non-invasive path to diagnosis, with an explicit caution that a positive EBV result should not stop the workup, since EBV is itself associated with lymphoma.

During the admission awaiting excisional biopsy, the toe is re-examined and found to be worse: the nail is elevated and oozing. The discussant activates a bleeding schema, but normal PT, PTT, and platelet count argue against a coagulopathy, and no vasculitic pattern fits a single distal digit without a broader rash. On direct questioning of the finding's character, the team learns that the discoloration is not distal (as trauma-related bruising typically is) but proximal, at the margin of the nail — and is hyperpigmentation, not a bruise. This single reframing — from "bruise" to "hyperpigmentation" — immediately promotes melanoma to the leading diagnosis, distinguished from a splinter hemorrhage (which occurs at the distal nail) and explaining the nail bed elevation. The excisional inguinal lymph node biopsy confirms metastatic melanoma, staining well for PD-1 ligand (supporting immunotherapy candidacy), and the toe lesion is diagnosed as subungual melanoma — a notoriously easy-to-miss site that can metastasize before it is recognized.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Effect on leading hypothesis |
|---|---|
| Groin pain with an equivocal exam; ultrasound showing right inguinal lymphadenopathy | Raises concern for a local process (infection, drainage from the leg/genitals) versus a systemic process reflected in the groin nodes |
| Thorough lymph node survey negative everywhere else except a toe "bruise" | Argues (at this point) for a localized rather than systemic lymphadenopathy process; the toe finding is initially dismissed as incidental trauma |
| Hemoglobin fallen from 15 to 11 over roughly six months | Reframes the case around anemia; prioritizes a bleeding versus hemolysis workup over the lymphadenopathy alone |
| Inappropriately low retic count, normal ferritin/percent sat, elevated LDH, normal smear | Argues against simple iron deficiency or an ongoing hemolytic process as the sole explanation; elevated LDH raises concern for increased cell turnover from malignancy |
| CT: large gastric mass (8x7 cm) plus retroperitoneal, mesenteric, and inguinal lymphadenopathy | Strongly raises probability of gastric malignancy (lymphoma versus adenocarcinoma) with nodal spread as a unifying explanation |
| EGD: GIST without invasion, explicitly not linked to the lymphadenopathy | Removes the gastric mass as the explanation for the LDH and lymphadenopathy; redirects the primary-malignancy search elsewhere |
| Retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy pattern, high LDH, subacute anemia | Favors an aggressive lymphoma (e.g., diffuse large B-cell lymphoma) over an indolent lymphoproliferative process, IgG4-related disease, or histiocytosis |
| Toe lesion reclassified: proximal (not distal) hyperpigmentation, elevated and oozing nail bed | Shifts the leading hypothesis decisively toward subungual melanoma rather than trauma |
| Excisional inguinal node biopsy: metastatic melanoma, PD-L1 positive | Confirms subungual melanoma with nodal metastasis as the unifying diagnosis |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The initial characterization of the toe finding as a "bruise" functions as an anchor that persists for most of the case; only when the team is pressed to describe its location precisely (proximal versus distal) and its evolving character (elevation, oozing) does the label change to hyperpigmentation — illustrating how the word chosen for a finding can silently foreclose an entire branch of the differential until it is re-examined and re-described.

The large, dramatic gastric mass is explicitly named in retrospect as a "massive distractor": its size and incidental discovery pulled significant diagnostic energy toward gastric malignancy, even though it proved to be an unrelated GIST — a reminder that the most visually or numerically striking finding is not always the operative one.

The discussant models a structured self-critique after the diagnosis is confirmed: recognizing that the case's answer was available from early in the workup (the groin, correctly identified as the region draining the leg and genitals) but was not connected to melanoma until the qualitative reframing of the toe finding — and reflecting that the broader lesson is not to memorize exact metastatic patterns for every cancer, but to pause and ask how a given finding would explain the pattern observed (e.g., recognizing that liver involvement should be expected before other distant lymph nodes are involved via portal/systemic drainage, which was absent here, correctly de-prioritizing a GI-tract primary).

## Teaching Discussion

Palpable lymphadenopathy in one region should prompt exam of both the locally draining structures and other lymph node basins; if additional adenopathy is found, cross-sectional imaging (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis with contrast) is the appropriate next step, since many nodal basins (retroperitoneal, mediastinal) are inaccessible to palpation.

An elevated LDH in the context of lymphadenopathy reflects increased cell turnover and should prompt consideration of both malignancy (lymphoproliferative or solid) and hemolysis; a haptoglobin helps adjudicate between the two, and the two processes can coexist (e.g., autoimmune hemolysis complicating a lymphoproliferative disorder via a positive Coombs test).

When lymph node tissue is being sampled for a possible lymphoma, an excisional biopsy (rather than fine-needle aspiration or core biopsy) is preferred because architecture matters for accurate subtyping; immunophenotyping and flow cytometry on the excised tissue further refine the diagnosis.

Certain infectious causes of lymphadenopathy can be screened for serologically without biopsy — HIV, syphilis, and infectious mononucleosis (EBV, CMV) — but a positive result for a virus like EBV should not be treated as the final answer, since EBV is itself an established driver of lymphoma.

Subungual melanoma is a notoriously easy site to miss, often persisting for months to years before recognition; Hutchinson's sign — hyperpigmentation extending onto the proximal nail fold/matrix — is a described clue that should prompt concern, distinguishing melanoma-related pigmentation from ordinary trauma, where a bruise is typically distal and does resolve rather than progress or ooze.
