> [!summary] Summary
> A 55-year-old woman wakes up with acute binocular diplopia, confirmed by the classic cover-test/prism trick. Over the next two days she develops symmetric numbness and tingling in her hands, feet, mouth, and tongue, followed by incoordination of her arms and an unsteady gait. Exam reveals a partial right ophthalmoplegia (worst on left gaze), globally absent reflexes despite the patient's own baseline being brisk, distal lower-extremity sensory loss, and ataxia out of proportion to weakness (strength is full 5/5 throughout). The combination of ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, and areflexia is the classic triad of **[[Miller Fisher Syndrome]]**, a variant of [[Guillain-Barré Syndrome]]. CSF shows early albuminocytologic dissociation; the patient is treated empirically with IVIG before antibody testing (anti-GQ1b) returns.

## Pearls

- **Binocular vs. monocular diplopia** is decided at the bedside: cover one eye. If the double vision resolves, it's binocular (ocular misalignment — a neurologic/anatomic problem); if it persists with one eye covered, it's monocular (almost always an ophthalmologic/optical problem, e.g. a lens issue).
- **Cranial nerves III (except its pupillomotor/levator fibers), IV, and VI are peripheral nerves**, just like the nerves of the limbs — so a single global peripheral-nervous-system process (like Guillain-Barré/Miller Fisher syndrome) can cause both diplopia and limb numbness/incoordination at once. CN II (optic nerve) is the exception: it is true CNS tissue, which is why it (not III/IV/VI) is the cranial nerve classically affected in multiple sclerosis.
- **Supranuclear gaze-control lesions (frontal eye fields, PPRF) cause a conjugate gaze palsy, not diplopia** — because both eyes are still moving together, just failing to move as a pair in one direction. True diplopia requires a lesion of a nerve nucleus, nerve, neuromuscular junction, muscle, or the internuclear connection (MLF) between the nuclei.
- **Myasthenia gravis vs. peripheral neuropathy/Miller Fisher**, at the bedside: myasthenia should spare reflexes and sensation and should show fatigable weakness; a peripheral process should show reduced/absent reflexes and can involve sensation. This patient had absent reflexes and distal sensory loss — pointing away from myasthenia despite a red-herring history of "fatigue."
- **Miller Fisher syndrome** = ophthalmoplegia + ataxia + areflexia; add altered mental status and you have **[[Bickerstaff Brainstem Encephalitis|Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis]]** (see [[CPS 220 - Drowsiness and Bickerstaff Brainstem Encephalitis|CPS 220]], explicitly referenced as a companion case in this discussion).
- **Albuminocytologic dissociation** (elevated CSF protein, normal-to-near-normal cell count) is the classic Guillain-Barré/Miller Fisher CSF pattern; if the cell count is unexpectedly high (dozens of cells, not just a handful), reconsider Lyme radiculoneuritis, HIV seroconversion, or — rarely — neurolymphomatosis rather than idiopathic post-infectious GBS.

## Problem Representation

A 55-year-old woman with a history notable only for colonic polyps and two prior COVID-19 infections develops acute-onset binocular diplopia on waking, confirmed by resolution with monocular occlusion. Over the following two days she develops symmetric distal numbness and tingling (hands, feet, mouth, tongue) and incoordination of her arms with gait unsteadiness. Exam shows a partial right ophthalmoplegia worst on left gaze, globally absent reflexes (from a known-brisk baseline), full 5/5 strength, distal lower-extremity loss of pinprick/temperature/proprioception, and ataxia on finger-to-nose disproportionate to any weakness — the triad of ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, and areflexia localizing to the peripheral nervous system (cranial and limb nerves together) and diagnostic of Miller Fisher syndrome, a Guillain-Barré spectrum variant.

## Case Narrative

The case was presented by John and discussed by Valeria and Madalena, with Aaron Berkowitz guiding the discussion.

The chief concern — diplopia — was met with visible enthusiasm from both discussants, prompting Berkowitz to have Valeria walk through her framework first. She distinguished **monocular versus binocular diplopia**: binocular diplopia means the eyes are misaligned, and its localization runs the full length of the [[Diplopia]] pathway — the ocular motor cranial nerve nuclei in the brainstem, the nerves themselves (III, IV, VI), the neuromuscular junction (myasthenia gravis), and the extraocular muscles (thyroid eye disease, or a local orbital process like cellulitis or an abscess). Monocular diplopia, by contrast, persists even with one eye covered and is usually an ophthalmologic problem with the lens or refractive apparatus, outside the neurologic differential. Berkowitz demonstrated the bedside cover test: gently occluding one eye and moving the eyes into misalignment reproduces the double vision, because the brain is thrown off by images no longer landing on corresponding retinal points; if covering either eye in turn resolves the diplopia, both eyes are individually intact but misaligned — binocular diplopia.

Madalena added the complementary axis — **[[Illness Tempo|time course]]** — reminding the group that "the differential is the localization times the time course," and that a chronic diplopia raises different possibilities (e.g., a slowly compressive mass) than an acute one. Berkowitz built out the localization further: cranial nerve VI innervates the lateral rectus (abduction), cranial nerve IV innervates the superior oblique, and cranial nerve III innervates the remaining extraocular muscles (medial, superior, and inferior rectus; inferior oblique) plus the levator palpebrae and the parasympathetic pupillary fibers. Using the **rule of 4**, Valeria placed the nuclei: midbrain for cranial nerves 1–4 (relevant here: III and IV), pons for 5–8 (relevant here: VI), medulla for 9–12 — with standard exceptions (I and XI never enter the brainstem; V spans all three levels; VIII straddles pons and medulla).

Berkowitz then asked how the nuclei of III and VI communicate across the midline to produce conjugate horizontal gaze (e.g., left lateral rectus plus right medial rectus, coordinated, to look left). The answer is the **medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF)** — not to be confused with the medial lemniscus (the dorsal column pathway) — which is really a "highway" connecting the nuclei of III, IV, VI, and VIII (the last link explaining the vestibulo-ocular reflex). A lesion of the MLF produces **internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO)**. Above this level sits the supranuclear system: the **paramedian pontine reticular formation (PPRF)**, commanded by the contralateral **frontal eye fields (FEF)**, tells the eyes to move together in a given direction. Critically, a PPRF or FEF lesion produces a *conjugate gaze palsy*, not diplopia, because both eyes still move together — this is why a large MCA stroke classically produces eyes deviated toward the side of the lesion (away from the hemiparetic side), not double vision, since the corticospinal tract has not yet crossed at the level of the pons.

**History**: a 55-year-old woman with a history of COVID-19 (twice) and colonic polyps, otherwise unremarkable, presented after three days of acute-onset double vision that began on waking and had not resolved. An optometrist confirmed it was correctable with a prism and, at her request (her daughter's wedding was that weekend), taped one lens of her glasses as a temporary fix — a maneuver Berkowitz noted is functionally the same as the monocular-occlusion cover test, just repurposed cosmetically. The day after the optometrist visit she developed numbness and tingling in her hands, feet, mouth, and tongue. The following day she felt mildly unsteady on her feet and had new difficulty controlling her arms and hands — spilling tea, unable to twist a lamp switch — which prompted her ED visit.

Both discussants converged quickly on binocular diplopia (given the response to occlusion) and considered whether the new numbness/tingling and incoordination were part of the same process or a separate one. Valeria initially favored a neuropathy or mononeuritis multiplex, floating infectious causes with cranial nerve tropism (VZV, Lyme) and wondering about a metabolic cause like hypercalcemia (perioral numbness), while flagging that she might be "closing the differential too soon." Madalena raised the acute time course as evidence against a length-dependent process like diabetic peripheral neuropathy and, prompted by Berkowitz, the group discussed the entity of a **diabetic (microvascular) third, fourth, or sixth nerve palsy** as a distractor: these classically present with acute, sometimes painful, isolated ocular motor nerve palsies, and when CN III is affected, the pupil is characteristically spared because the pupillomotor fibers travel on the *exterior* of the nerve (vulnerable to compression) while a microvascular insult affects the nerve's *core* (vulnerable to ischemia) — a rule useful but imperfect, since some compressive lesions can also spare the pupil. This distractor was set aside here both because the patient had no known diabetes and because a length-dependent diabetic neuropathy could not plausibly progress this fast or affect the mouth and tongue simultaneously with the hands and feet.

Berkowitz pushed the group toward a **unifying localization**: cranial nerves III and below (excepting II, the optic nerve, which is true CNS tissue and the reason MS affects vision but not the other ocular motor nerves directly) are peripheral nerves. A global process affecting both cranial and limb peripheral nerves over a few days points toward infectious (VZV, Lyme — noting Lyme meningitis/radiculitis can cause cranial neuropathies because the cranial nerves traverse the subarachnoid space), toxic-metabolic (rare heavy-metal exposures), or post-infectious/inflammatory causes — **[[Guillain-Barré Syndrome]]** and its variants, prompted explicitly by chat participants and the discussants alike. Madalena specifically raised the possibility that the "difficulty controlling" the arms (rather than frank weakness) plus the ophthalmoplegia could represent a facial/oculobulbar or ataxic GBS variant.

Additional history: fatigue in the legs not worse by day's end; eyes "tired" but diplopia not worse late in the day (both arguing against a fatigability pattern); the patient's daughter noticed bilateral eyelid drooping the patient herself hadn't appreciated; no facial droop, dysarthria, dysphagia, voice change, or vision loss; a recent bout of congestion/postnasal drip seen by her PCP; a tick bite within the last month without a rash, notable given the group practices in a Lyme-endemic area; no history of stroke, cardiovascular disease, cancer, or autoimmune disease; family history notable for a son with Crohn's disease and a daughter with type 1 diabetes.

Berkowitz used the "fatigue" detail as a teaching moment about **anchoring on buzzwords**: Valeria admitted the word "fatigue" pulled her mind toward a neuromuscular junction disorder — "that may just be my brain trying to take a multiple-choice test" — but the group talked through why myasthenia didn't fit: diplopia not worse at end of day, and, decisively, **sensory symptoms**, which should not occur in a pure neuromuscular junction disorder. On the exam-based distinction between neuropathy and neuromuscular junction disease, Valeria correctly predicted that **reflexes** would be preserved in myasthenia (lost in neuropathy) and that a fatigability test (sustained upgaze, or repetitive strength testing — Berkowitz's "50 chicken-wing exercises" example) could unmask true NMJ fatigability, while Madalena identified sensory loss as the discriminating feature that shouldn't appear in isolated NMJ disease.

**Labs**: the peripheral neuropathy workup was reported as entirely negative except for a mildly elevated ESR of 35.

**Exam**: mental status normal. Pupils equal and reactive, visual fields full. In primary gaze the right eye was mildly deviated laterally off the midline (exotropic) — explained by either an overactive right lateral rectus or a weak right medial rectus. Extraocular movements were technically full range, but diplopia was markedly worse on far left gaze and only mild-to-absent in other directions of gaze, localizing the deficit to the right eye's adduction (right medial rectus/CN III) or, alternatively, a right INO — a possibility Valeria raised, since a right eye that fails to adduct while the left eye abducts (with nystagmus) on left gaze is the classic INO pattern. Given the broader peripheral neuropathy picture, the group favored a **partial right CN III (oculomotor) involvement** as part of a peripheral ophthalmoplegia rather than a discrete brainstem internuclear lesion — Miller Fisher's ophthalmoplegia is frequently partial rather than a complete external ophthalmoplegia. All other cranial nerves were intact. Motor exam: normal bulk and tone, no abnormal movements, full 5/5 strength throughout including neck flexion/extension. **Reflexes were zero throughout** — notably surprising to the patient herself, who knew her reflexes were normally brisk and was alarmed by their absence. Sensory exam: diminished pinprick and temperature in the bilateral lower extremities below the knee with intact light touch, and reduced proprioception at the toes; no extinction to double simultaneous stimulation. Coordination: ataxia on finger-to-nose testing throughout the movement (not just terminally, as in a cerebellar dysmetria pattern) in both hands; heel-to-shin intact; gait not frankly ataxic but with corrective steps on tandem/heel-to-toe walking.

With the full exam in hand, Berkowitz asked the group to localize and to state how they would confirm the diagnosis. Both discussants converged on a peripheral process — cranial and limb nerves together, with absent reflexes clinching the localization away from the neuromuscular junction — and named **[[Miller Fisher Syndrome]]**, proposing confirmatory anti-ganglioside antibody testing (anti-GQ1b, most associated with Miller Fisher) while acknowledging results can take weeks and are not useful for an acute treatment decision. Nerve conduction studies were floated as an alternative, with the caveat that very acute demyelinating changes may not yet be apparent. Madalena, prompted, correctly recalled that lumbar puncture would classically show **albuminocytologic dissociation** — elevated protein with a normal-to-near-normal white cell count. Berkowitz used this to teach a related pearl: if the CSF instead showed a substantial pleocytosis (dozens of cells, not just a handful) in an otherwise GBS-like picture, this should prompt reconsideration of an infectious mimic — Lyme radiculoneuritis, HIV at the time of seroconversion (which can also cause facial nerve palsy or meningitis), or, rarely, neurolymphomatosis.

**Treatment discussion**: IVIG and plasma exchange have been shown roughly equivalent in clinical trials, with different risk-benefit profiles — IVIG is given through a peripheral line but carries some hypercoagulability risk, while plasma exchange requires central venous access but avoids that particular risk. Combining or sequentially redosing the two has been studied without clear added benefit. Berkowitz emphasized that families should be counseled that recovery is not immediate: demyelination has already occurred, treatment "puts out the fire" but does not instantly repair the damaged myelin, which regrows slowly; patients often reach their nadir around two weeks into the illness and may appear to worsen even while being treated, but — including patients who become intubated — full recovery is possible, though not universal, and worse in genuinely axonal variants.

**Outcome**: The ED work-up included a CT head and MRI head, both negative. Given the strength of the clinical picture, the team offered to proceed directly to treatment, but the patient elected to wait for LP results first. The LP, resulted in real time during the discussion, showed elevated protein with a mild elevation in white cell count — still interpreted as consistent with early or atypical albuminocytologic dissociation for Guillain-Barré/Miller Fisher spectrum disease rather than an infectious mimic. Anti-GQ1b antibody was sent for academic confirmation (pending at the time of the discussion) but not awaited for treatment. The patient began a five-day course of IVIG the same day.

Berkowitz closed by connecting this case to **[[CPS 220 - Drowsiness and Bickerstaff Brainstem Encephalitis|CPS 220]]** ("Maria's case"), presented earlier in the series, which had the identical ophthalmoplegia-ataxia-areflexia triad plus altered mental status — Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis. He also noted ongoing debate (citing his mentor Allan Ropper, among others) about whether the altered mental status and other central features seen in severe Guillain-Barré/Bickerstaff cases reflect a truly central lesion or are a secondary phenomenon of critical illness, and shared an anecdote about a patient with an unusual facial-diplegia-with-paresthesias GBS variant who went on to develop full Miller Fisher syndrome at a different hospital — underscoring that these variants often overlap rather than presenting in pure, textbook form.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

**[[Miller Fisher Syndrome]]** — a variant of [[Guillain-Barré Syndrome]] presenting with the triad of ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, and areflexia, typically evolving over days, often associated with anti-GQ1b ganglioside antibodies, and treated identically to classic GBS (IVIG or plasma exchange). Add altered mental status and the diagnosis becomes [[Bickerstaff Brainstem Encephalitis]].

**[[Guillain-Barré Syndrome]]** (framework) — an acute (days, not sudden, not chronic), typically post-infectious/inflammatory polyradiculoneuropathy that can affect both cranial and limb peripheral nerves, since cranial nerves III and below are peripheral nerves; classic CSF finding is albuminocytologic dissociation; treated with IVIG or plasma exchange (shown equivalent in trials); recovery can be prolonged and non-linear, with a nadir around two weeks, but even severe cases can fully recover.

**[[Anchoring Bias]] (exemplar)** — Valeria's explicit, self-caught anchor on the word "fatigue" pulling her toward a neuromuscular junction/myasthenia diagnosis ("that may just be my brain trying to take a multiple-choice test"), quickly corrected by returning to the fuller clinical picture (sensory loss, diplopia not worse at day's end, absent reflexes) that argued against it.

**[[Unifying Diagnosis]] (exemplar)** — the discussion is explicitly organized around the question "is there a way to unify [the diplopia] and [the numbness/tingling] together?", resolved by recognizing that cranial nerves III and below are peripheral nerves just like limb nerves, allowing one diagnosis (Miller Fisher/GBS) to explain findings that initially seemed to belong to two separate problems.

**[[Diagnostic Humility]]** — Valeria's mid-discussion self-check ("I don't know if I've been too... closing my DDX too soon") about prematurely narrowing to a peripheral neuropathy before the exam; and the group's choice to obtain LP and send confirmatory antibody testing even once the clinical diagnosis felt essentially certain, rather than relying on pattern recognition alone.

## Teaching Discussion

- **Bedside binocular-vs-monocular diplopia test**: cover one eye — resolution means binocular (misalignment); persistence means monocular (optical/lens problem, refer to ophthalmology). This patient's optometrist-prescribed taped glasses were functionally the same maneuver, repurposed as a temporary fix.
- **Extraocular muscle innervation**: CN VI → lateral rectus (abduction); CN IV → superior oblique; CN III → medial/superior/inferior rectus, inferior oblique, levator palpebrae, and pupillary parasympathetics.
- **Rule of 4 / brainstem cranial nerve nuclei**: midbrain (1–4), pons (5–8), medulla (9–12), with exceptions (I and XI never enter the brainstem; V spans all three levels; VIII straddles pons/medulla).
- **MLF and INO**: the medial longitudinal fasciculus links the nuclei of III, IV, VI, and VIII (not just III and VI) — a "highway" underlying both conjugate horizontal gaze and the vestibulo-ocular reflex. A lesion causes internuclear ophthalmoplegia: failure of adduction on the affected side with abducting nystagmus in the contralateral eye.
- **Supranuclear vs. infranuclear diplopia**: PPRF and frontal eye field lesions cause a *conjugate gaze palsy* (both eyes move together but fail to move in one direction) rather than diplopia — the anatomic explanation for eyes deviating toward the lesion (away from the hemiparesis) in a large MCA stroke, since the corticospinal tract hasn't yet crossed at the pontine level.
- **Diabetic (microvascular) ocular motor nerve palsy**: acute, sometimes painful, isolated CN III/IV/VI palsy with pupil sparing when CN III is involved (pupillomotor fibers run peripherally in the nerve, spared by an ischemic insult to the nerve's core) — a useful but imperfect rule, since atypical compressive and microvascular presentations both exist.
- **Cranial nerves as peripheral nerves**: CN III and below are peripheral nerves (unlike CN II, true CNS tissue) — the anatomic basis for a single peripheral process like GBS/Miller Fisher producing both cranial and limb findings.
- **Myasthenia gravis vs. peripheral neuropathy** at the bedside: reflexes preserved (MG) vs. lost (neuropathy); fatigable weakness testable via sustained upgaze or repetitive strength testing (MG) vs. not present in pure neuropathy; sensory symptoms absent (pure NMJ disease) vs. present (neuropathy). (Lambert-Eaton, a rare presynaptic NMJ disorder, is noted to show reflex *facilitation* after exercise rather than fatigability, the opposite pattern from myasthenia.)
- **Albuminocytologic dissociation** and its exceptions: a GBS-like picture with a markedly elevated CSF cell count should prompt consideration of Lyme radiculoneuritis, HIV seroconversion, or (rarely) neurolymphomatosis rather than idiopathic post-infectious GBS.
- **IVIG vs. plasma exchange**: trial-equivalent efficacy; differing risk profiles (IVIG's hypercoagulability risk vs. PLEX's need for central access); no proven benefit to combining or sequentially redosing.
- **Recovery counseling**: nadir around two weeks; myelin repair is slow, so patients can appear to worsen during treatment; full recovery is possible even after severe, intubated GBS, though axonal variants tend to fare worse.
- **Variant overlap**: Guillain-Barré/Miller Fisher variants are not always textbook-pure and can evolve or overlap (illustrated by a facial-diplegia-with-paresthesias variant that progressed to full Miller Fisher syndrome).
