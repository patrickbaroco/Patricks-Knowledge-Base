## Pearls

- A casually mentioned symptom like "I get short of breath sometimes" in a patient admitted for an unrelated chief concern deserves the same systematic workup as a primary complaint — vitals, a quick lung exam, a chest X-ray, and an EKG — since it may be the only clue to a serious, slowly progressive process.
- Approaching a chest X-ray by first noticing what stands out (rather than a rigid systematic scan) and only then working back through the rest of the film systematically is a reasonable and common expert reading style; mediastinal fullness should trigger a specific three-way differential: lymph nodes, a mass, or an enlarged vascular structure (most often the pulmonary artery).
- On EKG, a tall P wave (right atrial enlargement) combined with a right bundle branch block pattern and near-equal R and S waves in V1 (borderline right ventricular hypertrophy) should raise concern for right heart strain from pulmonary vascular or parenchymal lung disease.
- On CT or echocardiogram, ventricular septal bowing toward the left ventricle (rather than the normal, subtler bowing into the right ventricle) indicates that right-sided pressures have exceeded left-sided pressures — a sign of severe, hemodynamically significant pulmonary hypertension; right ventricular wall thickening (rather than just dilation) suggests the process is chronic, since the thin-walled RV dilates acutely but only thickens over time, unlike the LV, whose dilation alone implies chronicity.
- Localizing the cause of pulmonary hypertension can be approached anatomically by asking where the obstruction to flow begins: left heart disease (elevated filling pressures transmitted backward), lung parenchymal disease with reactive capillary vasoconstriction, or intrinsic pulmonary artery/pulmonary vascular disease — echocardiography and the absence of pulmonary edema can exclude left heart disease, while a clean CT chest (together with a normal BMI, to exclude obesity hypoventilation/OSA) can exclude most parenchymal lung disease.
- A history of substance use, even a strong and well-documented one, should not by itself close the diagnostic loop on a finding like pulmonary hypertension; a patient with a substance use disorder retains the same baseline risk of an unrelated or co-existing diagnosis, and anchoring on the substance can mean missing a treatable connective tissue disease.
- Reflexively attributing puffy or edematous hands in a person who uses methamphetamine to venous stasis from prolonged dependent positioning ("puffy hand syndrome") should not override a compelling independent clue — like a clear history of episodic color change with cold exposure (Raynaud phenomenon) — that points toward a distinct diagnosis such as an underlying connective tissue disease.

## Problem Representation

A man in his 60s with a history of daily methamphetamine use, admitted for suicidal ideation, is found on routine evaluation to have intermittent exertional dyspnea; a chest X-ray showing mediastinal fullness, an EKG showing right atrial enlargement and right bundle branch block, and a CT and echocardiogram demonstrating an enlarged pulmonary artery with right ventricular hypertrophy, dilation, and septal bowing toward the left ventricle establish chronic, severe pulmonary arterial hypertension; a subtle rash over the metacarpophalangeal joints and a history of cold-induced color change in the fingers raise concern for an underlying connective tissue disease as a contributing or alternative cause, rather than methamphetamine use alone.

## Case Narrative

A man in his 60s with a history of daily methamphetamine use and no other documented medical history presents to the emergency department with active suicidal ideation, including a specific plan, and is evaluated by psychiatry for admission. Almost as an aside, he mentions to the ED provider that he occasionally gets short of breath. His vitals — blood pressure, oxygenation, respiratory rate — are all normal, and a quick lung exam is unremarkable. Given this history, a chest X-ray and EKG are obtained.

Reviewing the chest X-ray, the discussant describes his typical reading style: noticing whatever stands out first, then working back through the film systematically. Here, what stands out is fullness in the mediastinum, which he frames as a three-way differential — lymph nodes, a mass, or an enlarged vascular structure. Because the heart itself appears normal in size and shape (arguing against significant left heart disease as the driver of an enlarged pulmonary artery), he leans toward lymphadenopathy, though an enlarged pulmonary artery remains possible. The rest of the film — lung fields, costophrenic angles, bones, trachea — is largely unremarkable, without clear evidence of an interstitial infiltrate. He recommends comparison with prior imaging if available, and, if unavailable or unhelpful, a CT scan; this matches the radiologist's own differential on the official read.

An EKG is obtained next. The discussant notes a very tall P wave (over 2.5 mm) and a right bundle branch block pattern on first glance. Systematically, the rate is borderline tachycardic around 100, and the rhythm is sinus (with flutter waves specifically excluded). He explains that P wave duration reflects the time for atrial depolarization to travel from the right atrium to the left, so a prolonged P wave suggests left atrial enlargement, while a tall P wave reflects increased right atrial muscle mass — right atrial enlargement. Combined with a right bundle branch block pattern, this is highly suggestive of right-sided heart disease, usually secondary to pulmonary disease or pulmonary vascular disease. He also looks for right ventricular hypertrophy by comparing the R and S wave amplitudes in V1 — here nearly equal, a borderline finding — and notes subtle anterior ST depressions that are an expected accompaniment of a right bundle branch block but could also reflect RV overload. This pushes his differential toward an enlarged pulmonary artery over isolated lymphadenopathy.

A CT scan (timed for the pulmonary artery) is reviewed next. There is no pulmonary embolism, but the pulmonary artery itself is enlarged, as is the right ventricle — the most anterior cardiac structure, abutting the chest wall, normally smaller than the left ventricle. The discussant explains that because the left ventricle operates at much higher pressure than the right, the interventricular septum normally bows slightly into the right ventricle; here, the septum is flattened and bowing slightly toward the left ventricle instead — a compelling sign of pulmonary arterial hypertension with significant RV overload. He further notes that the RV wall appears mildly thickened, suggesting the process is chronic, since the RV (unlike the thick, pressure-adapted LV) usually dilates quickly under acute pressure but only thickens over a more prolonged, chronic course; RV dilation alone, therefore, cannot reliably distinguish acute from chronic pulmonary hypertension the way LV dilation can for left heart disease.

The only abnormality on physical exam is in the hands, which are shown next. The discussant describes a discrete, erythematous-to-violaceous rash centered over the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints, extending into the spaces between the MCP and PIP and between the PIP and DIP joints — a distribution he finds highly evocative of Gottron's papules. He also notes a possible early ulceration over the right fourth PIP joint and the left wrist, cautioning that such lesions are often traumatic and inconsequential, particularly in someone who uses methamphetamine (where itching-related excoriation is common). Reza adds an examination detail: when asked to make a fist, the patient could not do so fully, and when asked whether his hands change color in the cold, he reported they go from white to blue to red — a history strongly suggestive of Raynaud phenomenon, which the discussant notes as powerful given the episodic, resolving nature of the color change (distinct from the fixed skin changes of scleroderma, which he'd otherwise expect to leave a lasting rather than episodic imprint).

Asked to comment further on a differential for possible skin thickening of the hands, the discussant acknowledges the temptation to attribute this to a high base rate of manual labor and substance use in this patient population — an instinct he flags as dangerous to let go unexamined. He walks through a location-based differential for conditions mimicking scleroderma: true systemic sclerosis begins acrally (face and hands) and can remain limited or progress proximally beyond the elbows (diffuse cutaneous disease); mimickers that do involve the hands include diabetic stiff hand syndrome, scleromyxedema, and nephrogenic systemic fibrosis (historically linked to gadolinium contrast in patients with end-stage renal disease, though a prior exposure — even without current renal disease — should still be considered); eosinophilic fasciitis and scleredema, by contrast, characteristically spare the hands. Reza separately notes a subjectively small oral aperture on exam, acknowledging his own limited experience assessing this finding.

A point-of-care ultrasound of the heart performed overnight by a resident is shared; the discussant is candid that he cannot meaningfully interpret it. The formal echocardiogram read is then provided: severely enlarged right ventricle with moderately reduced RV systolic function, severely dilated right atrium, RV volume and pressure overload with a normal LV ejection fraction and size, an elevated right atrial pressure of 15, an RVSP (via tricuspid regurgitant velocity) of 111, an estimated pulmonary vascular resistance greater than 6 Wood units, and a dilated IVC with reduced respiratory variation; a bubble study shows no evidence of intracardiac shunting.

The discussant asks for the patient's BMI (normal, not overweight) and whether there are any parenchymal findings on the CT chest (none), explaining that these two pieces of information let him bypass the standard five-group pulmonary hypertension classification in favor of an anatomic localization approach: is the pressure elevation confined to the pulmonary artery and lungs (left heart spared), driven by lung parenchymal disease causing reactive capillary vasoconstriction, or driven by elevated left-sided filling pressures transmitted backward? A right heart catheterization (the gold standard) can distinguish left heart disease from non-left-heart disease by measuring the wedge pressure, but cannot itself distinguish pulmonary artery disease from lung parenchymal disease — that distinction requires CT. Here, the echocardiogram and absence of pulmonary edema argue against significant left heart disease; a clear CT chest argues against most parenchymal lung disease, with one important CT-silent exception — obesity hypoventilation syndrome or obstructive sleep apnea, causing intermittent nocturnal upper airway obstruction without any radiographic signature (hence the BMI question). With a normal BMI and clean CT, the discussant lands on primary pulmonary arterial hypertension, while emphasizing humility, since up to 40% of cases have no identifiable cause and may be idiopathic or an unrecognized genetic disease.

Within pulmonary arterial hypertension, the discussant organizes causes into inflammatory and non-inflammatory categories. The patient's daily methamphetamine use places him at risk for substance-related pulmonary hypertension, and associated nutritional deficiencies (B12 deficiency, scurvy) are also worth considering. But the hand rash and Raynaud-type history raise real concern for an unrecognized connective tissue disease, with the leading candidates being scleroderma (the connective tissue disease most strongly associated with pulmonary arterial hypertension, though rarer overall than lupus), lupus (more common by base rate despite a lower per-case association with PAH), and the inflammatory myopathies — which the discussant flags as the most likely to present with isolated, disproportionate pulmonary artery involvement without other obvious systemic clues, since scleroderma and lupus both tend to leave more visible fingerprints elsewhere in the body or chart. The recommended next step is an ANA and myositis-specific subserologies.

The patient is transferred to the cardiology service for a right heart catheterization, which he declines due to his mental health condition; he is empirically started on a phosphodiesterase inhibitor for presumed pulmonary arterial hypertension based on the available (non-catheterization) data. Separately, a cardiology resident raises "puffy hand syndrome," a recognized entity in people who inject methamphetamine into the dorsum of the hand, causing venous sclerosis and superficial edema that can mimic scleroderma, nephrogenic systemic fibrosis, or RS3PE (remitting seronegative symmetrical synovitis with pitting edema) — with no true joint involvement and no x-ray abnormality. On further questioning, however, the patient reports that he smokes rather than injects methamphetamine, making puffy hand syndrome's proposed mechanism (dependent positioning and injection-site venous stasis, or possibly a heat-related phenomenon similar to erythema ab igne from holding heated methamphetamine before smoking it) a less clean fit. Despite recommending an ANA, CK, and LDH, the primary team elects not to send them, attributing the entire presentation to methamphetamine use.

In discussion, both physicians emphasize that a history of Raynaud phenomenon is not explained by methamphetamine use and should not be dismissed simply because it is difficult to confirm objectively (a cold-water or ice-cup provocation test is suggested as one way to try to reproduce it at the bedside). They estimate roughly a 90% probability that the presentation is methamphetamine-related, given how "sneaky" a cause pulmonary arterial hypertension can be and the absence of other systemic connective tissue disease clues at this patient's age — but argue that the remaining 10% probability of a treatable connective tissue disease is significant enough to warrant two simple, low-cost tests (ANA and CK), especially since a high BMI or a benign appearance should never be taken as proof of adequate nutritional or physiologic reserve (referencing a recent teaching point about bariatric surgery patients with normal BMI but significant micronutrient deficiency). They close by noting that HIV testing is also indicated in the pulmonary hypertension workup, and that base-rate reasoning about substance use disorders should never fully substitute for ruling out coexisting, unrelated diagnoses — a patient can simultaneously have methamphetamine use disorder and an autoimmune disease.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The case is built around anchoring bias: a patient with daily methamphetamine use and an incidental finding of severe pulmonary hypertension is an easy fit for a single, substance-related explanation, and the treating team's own point-of-care reasoning as well as a colleague's specific "puffy hand syndrome" hypothesis both risk closing the loop prematurely on that explanation.

The discussants explicitly reference base-rate reasoning from a preceding episode: even in a population with a very high prevalence of substance use, the base rate of a substance use disorder does not eliminate the independent, ongoing risk of an unrelated or coexisting diagnosis (here, a connective tissue disease) — patients with methamphetamine use disorder are not protected from also having lupus or dermatomyositis.

A clinical finding that is genuinely difficult for a patient to demonstrate or for a clinician to confirm at the bedside (Raynaud phenomenon, reported only as a history) is treated as carrying real diagnostic weight specifically because it is not explained by the leading, substance-related hypothesis — illustrating that an unexplained finding should raise rather than lower suspicion for an alternative or additional process, rather than being discounted because it's hard to verify.

## Teaching Discussion

A symptom mentioned only in passing by a patient admitted for an unrelated primary complaint still deserves a basic, systematic workup — vitals, exam, chest X-ray, and EKG — since it may be the only surface clue to a serious underlying process.

Mediastinal fullness on chest X-ray has a compact differential (lymph nodes, mass, or vascular structure) that can often be further narrowed using heart size and shape on the same film; EKG findings of a tall P wave and right bundle branch block pattern should raise concern for right heart strain from pulmonary vascular or parenchymal disease.

Ventricular septal position and RV wall thickness on CT or echocardiogram provide complementary information: septal bowing toward the left ventricle indicates that right-sided pressures have surpassed left-sided pressures (severe pulmonary hypertension), while RV wall thickening (rather than dilation alone) is the more reliable marker of chronicity, since the thin-walled RV dilates quickly under acute pressure but thickens only over a more prolonged course.

Pulmonary hypertension can be localized anatomically (left heart, lung parenchyma/vasculature, or pulmonary artery) using a combination of echocardiography (to assess left-sided filling pressures), CT chest (to assess for parenchymal lung disease), and BMI (to flag CT-silent causes such as obesity hypoventilation syndrome or OSA) — narrowing the differential before resorting to the full five-group classification system.

Within pulmonary arterial hypertension, inflammatory (connective tissue disease, HIV, portal hypertension/schistosomiasis) and non-inflammatory (substance-related, nutritional) causes should both be systematically screened for, since a compelling explanation in one category (such as methamphetamine use) does not exclude a coexisting cause in the other.

A patient's substance use history, however extensive and well-documented, should never be allowed to fully substitute for ruling out an independent or coexisting diagnosis; unexplained findings (such as a history of Raynaud phenomenon that the leading hypothesis cannot account for) should be actively pursued with simple, low-cost testing (ANA, CK) rather than dismissed as difficult to verify or as "probably related" to the more obvious diagnosis.
