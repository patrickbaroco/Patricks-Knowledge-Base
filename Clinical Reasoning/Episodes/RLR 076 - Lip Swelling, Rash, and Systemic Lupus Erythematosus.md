## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Three weeks after a root canal and drained dental abscess, a young woman develops persistent lip swelling and, later, a rash. The ED presumes [[Angioedema]], but the discussant's "tip of the iceberg" approach to the rash — judging its width, depth, and rate of evolution — favors a primary dermatologic process while low complements and a mild leukopenia keep the door open for something systemic. The true diagnosis, systemic lupus erythematosus, is nearly missed while the team debates histamine- versus bradykinin-mediated angioedema; this is explicitly framed, echoing [[RLR 074 - Bilateral Facial Swelling and Pacemaker Lead-Induced SVC Syndrome]], as solving the wrong problem.

## Pearls

- Any complaint of facial or lip swelling that is unilateral or localized should first prompt a search for a local cause (dental infection, cellulitis, salivary gland disease) before invoking a systemic process — contrast with bilateral facial swelling, which points more directly to a systemic or vascular cause.
- Approach an unfamiliar rash by judging its three dimensions — how wide, how deep, and how quickly it is evolving — to decide whether it is likely the "tip of the iceberg" of a visceral process or an isolated primary dermatologic condition.
- Angioedema has causes well beyond ACE inhibitors: ARBs, opiates, antibiotics, topical anesthetics, and OCPs/progesterone should all be on the medication review.
- Persistent swelling lasting well beyond the typical three-day course of ordinary angioedema, without lip predominance, should prompt reconsideration of the working diagnosis rather than simply extending the angioedema timeline.
- Complement levels are most useful in three clinical situations: glomerulonephritis, urticarial vasculitis, and angioedema; an isolated low C4 with a positive rheumatoid factor should raise concern for cryoglobulinemic vasculitis.
- Never let leukopenia pass unremarked in a young woman with a new inflammatory syndrome — it is a powerful, easily overlooked clue toward [[Systemic Lupus Erythematosus]].

## Problem Representation

A young woman develops persistent, initially unilateral facial and lip swelling in the weeks following a dental root canal and drained abscess, followed by a subacute, non-necrotic, non-purpuric rash involving the face, back, and upper extremities together with fatigue; despite an ED presumption of angioedema, the swelling's persistence and an underlying leukopenia ultimately localize the process to systemic lupus erythematosus rather than to a primary allergic or vascular disorder.

## Case Narrative

Three weeks before presentation, the patient had a root canal with initial pain relief, then recurrent tooth and mouth pain from a dental abscess that was drained and treated with antibiotics. Facial swelling persisted despite the antibiotics, and four days before presentation her lip became swollen along with fatigue and malaise. In the ED, clinicians attribute this to angioedema, possibly with anaphylaxis, and treat with IM epinephrine, diphenhydramine, and steroids; her airway remains secure and she is discharged. She returns because the lip swelling persists and she has now developed a rash.

The discussant frames this as round two of facial swelling but notes the pattern is different from a prior bilateral case: here the swelling is unilateral/localized (left side, matching the side of the dental procedure), which favors a local process — dental infection, cellulitis, or salivary gland disease — over a systemic one, though localized or asymmetric angioedema remains possible and can persist up to roughly three days. Because the patient is now four days out with persistent swelling, that duration argues somewhat against ordinary angioedema. Before pursuing the swelling further, the discussant prioritizes assessing for deep airway involvement and characterizing the newly reported rash, reasoning that solving the mucocutaneous syndrome requires understanding the rash first rather than pressing forward on an incomplete framework.

Vitals are normal. The face is swollen more on the left, matching the side of the dental procedure; the airway is clear with no stridor, difficulty breathing, or drooling. The rash is itchy, with a faint, lace-like pattern on the upper extremities, hyperpigmented macules about one centimeter in diameter on the upper back, and non-pustular papules around the face.

Applying the "tip of the iceberg" approach — judging a rash's width, depth, and rate of evolution — this rash is not wide, not deep (no necrosis, purpura, ulceration, or pain), and not rapidly evolving, favoring a primary dermatologic condition on first pass, though facial papules in a young woman with fatigue are enough to justify pursuing labs to distinguish an isolated cutaneous disease from a systemic syndrome with cutaneous manifestations. Acne and rosacea are considered for the facial component but felt unlikely given the absence of pustules.

Labs: hemoglobin 10.2 (long-standing, unexplained anemia), platelets 378,000, WBC 6,000 with 0% eosinophils and neutrophil predominance, and a mild leukopenia; chem-7 and liver chemistries normal (creatinine 0.7); CT face shows edema without abscess; C3 and C4 both low; ESR 92; C1 inhibitor level normal; tryptase normal; elevated IgG; HIV, syphilis, and hepatitis serologies negative.

The CT frames the swelling as edema without a focal collection of blood or pus, consistent with an operating hypothesis of persistent angioedema. Medication review broadens beyond ACE inhibitors to ARBs, opiates, antibiotics, topical anesthetics (received during the dental procedure), and OCPs (progesterone as a trigger). The presence of a rash pulls toward a histamine-mediated mechanism, but the lack of improvement with antihistamines/epinephrine and the fact that some hereditary forms of angioedema can have a mild rash keeps bradykinin-mediated angioedema on the table. Complement interpretation adds tension: a low C4 suggests C1-esterase-related consumption, but the C1 inhibitor level is normal and C3 is also low — a normal C1 inhibitor level does not exclude a qualitative or functional C1-esterase inhibitor defect, and the coexisting leukopenia and anemia raise the possibility of an acquired (rather than genetic) C1-esterase abnormality, classically associated with lupus.

Overnight, allergy and immunology consultation concludes this is not angioedema and, without explaining the lip/facial swelling (attributed to mucosal injury from the dental procedures), recommends against antihistamines and steroids and pursues an ANA. Re-examination of the facial rash — previously interpreted only through an angioedema lens — reveals a malar-appearing rash with nasolabial sparing; the lace-like pattern on the arms is considered as a possible, though uncertain, livedo reticularis. ANA returns floridly positive, and double-stranded DNA is also floridly positive. Rheumatology confirms systemic lupus erythematosus and favors serum sickness, rather than pure angioedema, as the explanation for the rash and swelling — an illness script the discussant does not have a strong working command of, though it does not cleanly match the classic serum sickness triad of fever, joint pain, and systemic illness with diarrhea.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The episode is explicitly framed by the discussants as an example of solving the wrong problem: forty-five minutes are spent on the mechanics of angioedema — histamine versus bradykinin, complement pathways, C1-esterase interpretation — while the underlying disease (lupus) hides behind an easily overlooked leukopenia. This directly echoes the problem-representation lesson from [[RLR 074 - Bilateral Facial Swelling and Pacemaker Lead-Induced SVC Syndrome]], where the chief concern (facial swelling) was likewise a distractor from the true process.

The discussant names a general rule for getting stuck in a case: either a key piece of knowledge is missing, or the wrong problem is being solved — and states plainly that both applied here, since acquired angioedema from lupus was an illness script he did not have.

Diagnostic humility is modeled explicitly and repeatedly ("this is above my pay grade... but those are the tension points") rather than forcing the ambiguous complement and serology data into a single clean mechanism.

The allergy and immunology team's insistence that "this is not angioedema" — without initially offering a positive alternative — illustrates productive skepticism: naming what a presentation is *not* consistent with, even without yet knowing the answer, can be as valuable as naming a leading hypothesis, and it was this skepticism that ultimately redirected the workup toward ANA testing.

## Teaching Discussion

Complement physiology: C3 and C4 are synthesized in the liver as inactive precursors and released into circulation; the classical pathway consumes both C3 and C4 to reach the terminal membrane-attack-complex pathway, while the alternative pathway does not consume C4 — a discordance between C3 and C4 levels can help identify which pathway is driving consumption.

Complement levels are most clinically useful in three settings: glomerulonephritis, urticarial vasculitis, and angioedema; an isolated low C4 with a positive rheumatoid factor is a recognized association with cryoglobulinemic vasculitis. Roughly half of lupus patients have normal complement levels and half have low complement, and C4 tends to be consumed disproportionately relative to C3 when the classical pathway predominates.

A normal C1 inhibitor level is not sufficient to exclude C1-esterase system dysfunction, because quantitative assays can miss qualitative or functional defects in the C1-esterase inhibitor pathway; acquired forms of C1-esterase dysfunction (as opposed to hereditary angioedema) can arise from autoimmune disease, with lupus being a notable trigger.

Never let unexplained leukopenia in a young woman pass without consideration of lupus — leukopenia predates and can be an early, easily dismissed clue to systemic autoimmune disease well before more classic lupus features (malar rash, oral ulcers, alopecia) are appreciated.
