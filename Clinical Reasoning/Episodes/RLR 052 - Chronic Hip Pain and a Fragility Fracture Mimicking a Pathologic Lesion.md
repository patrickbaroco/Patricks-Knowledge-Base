> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Reza presents — and titles the episode **"The Alternate Diagnosis,"** borrowing from a Gurpreet Dhaliwal *NEJM* exercise — a **70-year-old man** who **fell** when his foot caught under an object, landing on his **right hip and knee**. There was no syncope, no ataxia, no sensory loss: a purely **mechanical fall**. ED **x-rays of the hip and knee showed no fracture**, he could bear weight (painfully), and he was discharged. Rabih uses the opening to separate a fall's **cause from its consequence** and to ask whether **plain film is a sufficient test** — good for **bony** injury, blind to **soft-tissue/intra-articular** injury (hemarthrosis, ligament or tendon rupture) — and to insist that **watching the patient walk** is the only real arbiter. But the **course deviates**: **three months** later the man still has groin pain, now uses a **walker**, is **bed-bound and losing weight** (he avoids eating so he won't have to walk to the bathroom). A **CT** for persistent pain (and possible hernia) shows an **impacted right femoral-neck fracture** with **osteolysis** "concerning for an underlying lesion," plus a second **iliac lucency**. Rabih flags the pivotal move — **check your assumptions**: the problem may **predate the fall**, reframing this as a **[[pathologic fracture]]** and making the whole case "**what is that bone lesion?**" He deploys the **[[Five Ps of Bone Lesions|five P's]]** (PTH, PSA, Paraprotein, (R)PR/syphilis, Primary bone tumor), asks whether there's a **systemic fingerprint** vs isolated bone disease, and keeps **avascular necrosis** in view given remote alcohol use — while refusing to fully trust the radiograph. Reza teaches a clean **[[preoperative risk assessment]]** (urgency → active cardiac disease → optimize chronic disease → **functional capacity/METs** → RCRI/Gupta; "you risk-stratify, you don't clear"). The workup: **PTH normal, PSA 1, calcium normal, SPEP normal, vitamin D 11**, **PET-CT** lighting up **only** the hip lesion, **DEXA T-score −2.4**, and **surgical pathology showing only a subacute fracture — no malignancy**. The unlock comes from the **MSK radiologist**: a fracture that is **borne weight on for months without repair** develops **osteolytic remodeling that mimics a pathologic lesion**. Final diagnosis: **age- and vitamin-D-related osteoporosis → fragility fracture from standing height**, whose **weight-bearing osteolysis impersonated malignancy** — a triumph of **base rate** and of distrusting even "the radiology." Treatment: vitamin D repletion and, per endocrine, the anabolic **teriparatide**.

**Pearls**

- **Separate a fall's *cause* from its *consequence*.** Ask *why did they fall* (syncope, arrhythmia, ataxia, mechanical) and *what did the fall do* — two different problems with two different workups. A purely mechanical trip with x-ray-negative pain is usually a soft-tissue contusion; **watching the patient walk** is the decisive test, not more imaging.
- **Plain film answers a *bony* question only.** X-ray is superb for fractures but blind to **intra-articular and soft-tissue** injury — hemarthrosis, quadriceps/patellar/hamstring tendon rupture, ligamentous tears. Persistent pain and poor weight-bearing after a normal film is a **non-bony orthopedic** question until proven otherwise.
- **When the course deviates from your script, check your assumptions — out loud.** The anchor here was "healthy man, mechanical fall, will improve." Three months of *worsening* forces the realization that **the problem may predate the fall** — reframing an injury as a **[[pathologic fracture]]** through pre-existing bone.
- **A fracture from a standing-height fall is abnormal** — it means **thin bone** (osteoporosis/osteomalacia) *or* a **pathologic lesion**. Distinguishing those two is the whole case.
- **The [[Five Ps of Bone Lesions|five P's]] of a bone lesion:** **PTH** (primary hyperparathyroidism), **PSA** (prostate cancer), **Paraprotein** (myeloma/plasmacytoma), **(R)PR** (syphilis — the one infection that seeds bone with *no* systemic fingerprint), and **Primary bone tumor** (e.g., Paget disease, osteosarcoma). Then ask the meta-question: **isolated bone disease, or systemic disease with bony involvement?**
- **A *solitary* bone lesion argues against metastatic cancer.** Prostate and myeloma are the champions of bone tropism, but they usually paint **many** lesions; **one** lucency, "so bad but only the hip, and nothing anywhere else, for months," *doesn't add up* for malignancy — and that discordance is detectable **without** being a radiologist.
- **"Pseudo-radiology": trust imaging, but not blindly.** Radiology and pathology are powerful and **flawed** (sampling error, misleading remodeling). A fracture **borne weight on for months** develops **osteolysis that mimics a pathologic lesion** — the single most valuable move here was the primary team **calling the MSK radiologist**.
- **Preoperative "clearance" is a myth — you risk-*stratify*.** Ask: **how urgent/serious is the surgery**, is there **active cardiac disease** (HF, arrhythmia, ACS), any **chronic condition to optimize**, and what is the **functional capacity** — ≥**4–5 METs** (a flight of stairs) means **no further cardiac testing**; then quote the **RCRI/Gupta** risk and let the surgeon and patient share the decision.

**Problem Representation**
A 70-year-old man with a mechanical fall and x-ray-negative hip pain that progressed over three months, whose CT showed an impacted femoral-neck fracture with osteolysis mimicking a pathologic lesion — ultimately diagnosed as an osteoporotic fragility fracture (aging + vitamin D deficiency) whose sustained weight-bearing produced malignancy-mimicking bony remodeling, with no cancer found.

## Case Narrative

Reza opens with a nod to a **Gurpreet Dhaliwal** *NEJM* clinical-reasoning exercise titled *An Alternate Diagnosis* and, with permission, borrows the frame — "because in reality, anything can be an alternate diagnosis." The patient is a **70-year-old man** whose **foot got stuck under an object**; he **fell onto his right hip and knee**. Crucially there was **no loss of consciousness, no ataxia, no sensory abnormality** — a **mechanical fall**. In the ED he had **pain with weight-bearing** in the hip, knee, and groin; **x-rays of the hip and knee showed no fracture**; he ambulated (painfully) and was **discharged**.

Rabih uses the opening to model two disciplines. First, **cause vs consequence**: every fall poses *why did they fall* and *what did the fall do*. Here the cause is a trip "that would have caused anybody to fall," and the consequence is an **x-ray-negative** injury. Second, **is the test sufficient for the question?** Plain film is a **fantastic test for bony complications** but says nothing about **non-bony** injury — a **hemarthrosis** of the hip, a **quadriceps/patellar/hamstring tendon rupture**, an intra-articular or ligamentous problem. He is deliberately **authentic**: most x-ray-negative contusions simply limp and recover, so **seeing the patient walk** — not reflexively escalating to MRI — is what should drive the decision. If there is a "second aliquot," it is either a soft-tissue injury (and the story ends) or a question of whether the **bone was imaged well enough** or the problem is **non-bony orthopedic**.

Then the **course deviates**. **Three months** later the man tells his PCP the **groin pain persists**; he now uses a **walker**, avoids weight-bearing, spends much of the day in **bed**, and has **lost significant weight** — a poignant mechanism: he **won't eat** because eating means a trip to the bathroom, and walking hurts. (An aside the hosts admire: he is **45 years sober**, an AA leader.) A **CT of the pelvis/lumbar spine** (also for possible hernia) reveals an **impacted right femoral-neck fracture with varus deformity**, **osteolysis at the fracture site "raising concern for an underlying lesion,"** and a second **lucent lesion in the posterior right ilium** — the radiologist recommends **MRI**. He is admitted, and Reza is consulted — on the **medicine consult service** — for a **preoperative risk stratification** before repair.

Rabih makes the pivotal move: **check your assumptions**. The working story assumed a **previously healthy** man with a mechanical fall who should have **improved**; instead there is **radiographic evidence the problem predated the fall**. That reframes the event as a **[[pathologic fracture]]** — a **subclinical lesion** that **broke under minimal trauma** — and makes the case entirely about **"what is that bone lesion?"** He reaches for the **[[Five Ps of Bone Lesions|five P's]]**: **PTH** (primary hyperparathyroidism), **PSA** (prostate cancer), **Paraprotein** (myeloma/plasmacytoma), **(R)PR** (syphilis — uniquely able to cause bone lesions with **no** systemic fingerprint), and **Primary bone tumor** (Paget disease as the classic; osteosarcoma and a long tail of rarities). Over the top of the mnemonic he asks the organizing question — **isolated bone disease vs a systemic disease with bony involvement** — and, noting the **remote but extensive alcohol history**, keeps **avascular necrosis of the hip** in view as a competing cause of subacute hip pain, refusing to assume "the radiographic analysis is perfect."

Reza then teaches the **[[preoperative risk assessment]]** he was actually consulted for. The spine: (1) **urgency/seriousness of surgery** — a life-saving operation proceeds regardless; here the fracture is months old and behaves almost **electively**; (2) **active cardiac disease** — heart failure, arrhythmia, or ACS (this patient has none); (3) **other chronic disease to optimize** before an elective procedure (e.g., hypercalcemia, thyrotoxicosis); (4) **functional capacity in METs** — the ability to **climb a flight of stairs (~4–5 METs)** means **no further cardiac testing is needed**; and then quoting a **calculator** (**RCRI** or **Gupta**) for the actual event rate. His refrain: **you do not "clear" anyone — you risk-stratify**, and the surgeon and patient make a **shared decision**. This man was **low risk (<1%)** for a low-to-intermediate-risk repair; the only wrinkle was a **chronic superficial clot** (no DVT), for which Reza recommended **post-op enoxaparin** prophylaxis (the team used aspirin — and the patient later clotted).

The **five P's come back normal**: **PTH normal**, **calcium normal**, **PSA 1**, **SPEP** (checked) **without a monoclonal spike**, syphilis testing negative. **Vitamin D is low at 11.** A **PET-CT** shows uptake **only** at the hip lesion — no second site. Rabih reads this as **isolated bone disease**: *if* malignant, the champions of bone affinity are **prostate** (PSA argues against) and **myeloma** (but a **solitary** lesion wouldn't meet myeloma criteria — at most a **[[solitary plasmacytoma]]**, which is uncommon in the **pelvis** and favors the spine), leaving a **primary bone tumor** or the possibility that **the radiology is misleading**. The strongest reason for doubt is precisely the **solitary-ness**: one lesion, so severe, only the hip, nothing elsewhere, for months — that pattern "**doesn't add up**" for cancer. He pivots to the **mimicker** — **thin bone** — and works the **vitamin D** systematically: the commonest cause is **dietary/sun** insufficiency, but one must also consider **absorption** (gut), **storage** (liver), **conversion** (kidney), and **drug interference**; here liver and kidney are fine and there are no offending drugs.

The **resolution** arrives in two steps. **Surgical pathology** of the fracture site shows **only a subacute fracture — no malignancy, no atypia, no neoplasm**. And the primary team (Reza credits them, not himself) **calls the musculoskeletal radiologist**, who delivers the unlock: when a fracture is **borne weight on for months without healing**, the site develops **osteolytic remodeling that mimics a pathologic lesion**. The "lesion" was a **consequence of the un-repaired fracture**, not its cause. A **DEXA** returns a **T-score of −2.4** (just shy of the −2.5 threshold). The **final diagnosis**: **osteoporosis** (aging plus **vitamin D deficiency**) → a **fragility fracture from standing height** → **weight-bearing osteolysis impersonating malignancy** — **no cancer at all**. It is, as titled, an **alternate diagnosis** driven by **base rate**. Treatment: **vitamin D repletion** and, per endocrinology, the **PTH analog [[teriparatide]]** (anabolic — it *builds* bone short-term, in contrast to the bone-weakening effect of chronic secondary/tertiary hyperparathyroidism), to be paired later with an anti-resorptive. Matt closes the episode.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The episode is a study in **assumption hygiene**. The seductive first script — *healthy man, mechanical fall, x-ray negative, will heal* — is entirely reasonable **until the trajectory contradicts it**, and Rabih's discipline is to treat the **deviation itself as data** and re-open the timeline ("the problem probably didn't begin when he fell"). The **five P's** convert an intimidating "bone lesion" into a **finite, checkable list**, while the **systemic-fingerprint** question guards against tunneling on the local abnormality. The subtler bias managed here is **automation/authority bias toward imaging and pathology** — what Rabih calls **"pseudo-radiology."** Both hosts explicitly *worship* radiology and pathology for their power and then **refuse to worship them blindly**, recalling a prior case of phantom "liver lesions." The clue that licenses the doubt is **base rate + pattern**: a **solitary**, severe, months-long lesion with **no disease elsewhere** is far more consistent with a **local mechanical process** than with a cancer that politely restricted itself to one hip — and that inference is available to any clinician, radiologist or not. Finally, the **pre-op consult frame** is a lesson in **role discipline without abdicating thought**: Reza answers the narrow question asked (risk stratification) *and* reasons deeply about the medicine, but keeps the two **appropriately separated** in the chart.

## Teaching Discussion

**The two questions every fall asks.** *Why did they fall* (syncopal, arrhythmic, neurologic, or mechanical) and *what did the fall injure* are separate problems. A witnessed **mechanical trip** with preserved consciousness and no focal deficits points the workup toward **injury**, not toward a cardiac or neurologic cause — and there, the key is matching the **test to the tissue**.

**Match the test to the tissue.** **Plain radiographs** interrogate **bone**; they are near-blind to **intra-articular and soft-tissue** injury (**hemarthrosis**, **tendon rupture** — quadriceps, patellar, hamstring — ligamentous tears, occult stress fractures). When x-rays are negative but the patient **can't bear weight or won't improve**, the question is no longer "is there a fracture?" but "is this a **non-bony orthopedic** problem, or have I **under-imaged** the bone?" The **bedside gait exam** — not reflexive advanced imaging — is the highest-yield next step.

**Let a deviating course rewrite the problem.** Clinical stories carry **embedded assumptions** (here: previously healthy, injury began at the fall). When the **trajectory contradicts** the script — progressive pain, functional decline, weight loss over months — the highest-value cognitive act is to **name and re-test the assumptions**. Reframing the event as a **[[pathologic fracture]]** (bone that failed under trivial load) is what turns a "contusion follow-up" into an oncologic/metabolic workup.

**The five P's of a bone lesion.** A structured screen for a lytic/suspicious bone lesion: **P**TH (primary hyperparathyroidism / brown tumors), **P**SA (prostate cancer, the great bone-seeker), **P**araprotein (**SPEP/UPEP/free light chains** for myeloma or **[[solitary plasmacytoma]]**), **(R)P**R (syphilis — the infection that can seed bone with **no systemic fingerprint**), and **P**rimary bone tumor (**Paget disease** as the everyday example; osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, and rarer entities). Layer on the organizing question: **isolated bone disease vs systemic disease with skeletal involvement** — and, per host, keep mimics like **avascular necrosis** (relevant with alcohol or steroid exposure) on the list.

**Solitary lesions bias away from metastasis.** Metastatic prostate cancer and myeloma usually produce **multiple** lesions; a **single** focus that lights up on PET with **nothing elsewhere** is statistically **unusual for cancer** and should raise **solitary plasmacytoma**, **primary bone tumor**, or a **non-neoplastic mimic**. **[[solitary plasmacytoma|Solitary plasmacytoma of bone]]** — a localized clonal plasma-cell tumor that **progresses to myeloma** in a large fraction over years — is classically **vertebral**, so a **pelvic** location is itself a soft argument against it.

**"Pseudo-radiology": powerful tests, humbly used.** Imaging and pathology are indispensable and **fallible** — pathology to **sampling error**, imaging to **misleading remodeling**. A fracture that is **borne weight on for months without union** develops **reactive osteolysis** that can **mimic a pathologic/malignant lesion**. The move that cracked this case — the primary team **phoning the MSK radiologist** — is a reminder that a **targeted conversation with the imager** often outperforms another scan.

**Preoperative evaluation is risk-stratification, not clearance.** A compact framework: (1) **urgency** — emergent/life-saving surgery proceeds regardless of risk; (2) **active cardiac conditions** — unstable coronary syndromes, decompensated **heart failure**, significant **arrhythmias**, severe valvular disease — which demand attention first; (3) **optimize modifiable chronic disease** before elective surgery; (4) **functional capacity** — self-reported ability to achieve **~4–5 METs** (climb a flight of stairs, walk up a hill) identifies patients who need **no further cardiac testing**; and (5) quote an **objective estimate** (**RCRI**, **Gupta/NSQIP**) so the surgeon and patient can make a **shared decision**. The consultant **stratifies and optimizes**; they do not "clear."

**Osteoporosis, fragility fracture, and the anabolic option.** A **fall from standing height that fractures** is, by definition, a **fragility fracture** and diagnoses osteoporosis clinically regardless of the T-score (here **−2.4**, near the **−2.5** densitometric cutoff). **Vitamin D deficiency** (level 11) should be **worked up systematically** — intake/sun, malabsorption, hepatic storage, renal 1-α-hydroxylation, drug interference — and repleted. For high fracture risk, **anabolic** agents like **[[teriparatide]]** (recombinant PTH 1-34) **build** bone and are typically **followed by an anti-resorptive** to preserve gains — the physiologic irony being that **chronic** PTH excess weakens bone while **intermittent** PTH strengthens it.
