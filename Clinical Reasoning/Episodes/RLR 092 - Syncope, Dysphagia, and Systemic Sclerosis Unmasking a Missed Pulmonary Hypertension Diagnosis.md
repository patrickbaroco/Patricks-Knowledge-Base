## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A man in his early 60s had an ED visit for exertional syncope three years ago that led to a diagnosis of pulmonary hypertension, attributed at the time entirely to his methamphetamine use — a conversation that damaged the therapeutic relationship and led him to disengage from care. He returns three years later with progressive dysphagia to solids and liquids; a workup for extrinsic esophageal compression incidentally reveals severe, apparently untreated pulmonary hypertension, and a re-examined ANA (positive at a significant titer, never previously followed up with subserologies) ultimately points to limited systemic sclerosis as the unifying cause of both his pulmonary hypertension and his esophageal dysmotility. The presenting discussant explicitly reconstructs the case out of its true chronological order for teaching purposes, revealing the actual, less tidy sequence of events at the end.

## Pearls

- When a patient presents with syncope, first reframe the label as "transient loss of consciousness" to avoid prematurely excluding mimics such as seizure or hypoglycemia; once true syncope is established, exertional syncope specifically points toward a cardiac cause, since orthostatic and reflex-mediated syncope do not typically manifest with exercise intolerance.
- Progressive, gradually worsening exercise intolerance culminating in syncope favors a structural cardiac cause (valvular, pericardial, pulmonary vascular, or cardiomyopathic) over a primary arrhythmia, though the two are not mutually exclusive — structural heart disease can itself progress to an arrhythmic event.
- Esophageal dysphagia should be organized into structural versus motility causes, and the tempo of onset and pattern (solids progressing to liquids favors a structural lesion; simultaneous difficulty with both liquids and solids from the outset favors a motility disorder) helps prioritize between them; a hyperacute presentation should always raise concern for an impacted foreign body or bolus, a true emergency given the risk of esophageal perforation.
- A simple bedside maneuver — having the patient sip water, then attempt to swallow a whole ice cube (which will melt rather than cause true obstruction if lodged) — can help characterize dysphagia severity at the bedside before endoscopy or imaging is available.
- Severe pulmonary hypertension can cause syncope through two compounding mechanical effects on the left ventricle: reduced RV output into the left heart (decreasing LV preload and stroke volume) and, once septal bowing reverses toward the left ventricle, a secondary outflow tract obstruction analogous to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.
- An ANA result — especially a meaningfully elevated titer — should never be left unfollowed simply because a patient has a well-documented substance use disorder that could plausibly explain the same presentation; failing to send subserologies after a positive ANA can delay recognition of a distinct, treatable autoimmune disease for years.
- When a patient with substance use disorder is given a diagnosis attributed solely to that substance, the conversation itself can rupture the therapeutic relationship and cause disengagement from follow-up care — an outcome that compounds the harm of an incomplete workup with a lost opportunity for early treatment.

## Problem Representation

A man in his early-to-mid 60s with daily methamphetamine use had an ED visit three years prior for progressive exertional intolerance culminating in syncope, at which time pulmonary hypertension was identified on outpatient echocardiogram and attributed entirely to methamphetamine use without subserology follow-up on a significantly positive ANA; he returns three years later, having disengaged from care in the interim, with progressive dysphagia to solids and liquids, found on workup to have severe, apparently unaddressed pulmonary hypertension and, ultimately, limited systemic sclerosis (confirmed by anti-SCL-70 positivity, telangiectasias, sclerodactyly, and esophageal manometry showing hypoperistalsis with reduced lower esophageal sphincter tone) as the unifying diagnosis linking both his pulmonary vascular disease and his esophageal dysmotility.

## Case Narrative

*Note: the presenting discussant explicitly reconstructs this case out of its true chronological order — intentionally showing the diagnostic reasoning as if unfolding stepwise across the syncope workup and the dysphagia workup together — and reveals at the end that the real sequence of events was considerably less tidy and educational than presented here.*

Before seeing a new patient checking in, the presenting discussant (unusually, since he prefers going into a room "blind") reviews the chart and finds the patient hasn't been seen since 2020. An ED note from roughly three years earlier describes a then-61-year-old man with progressive exertional intolerance — his exercise capacity gradually narrowing from unlimited to half a mile to a few blocks — culminating in an episode of syncope while walking, which brought him to the ED.

Given only this history, the responding discussant reframes syncope as "transient loss of consciousness" to keep seizure and hypoglycemia in view as mimics, then works through the classic syncope framework: cardiogenic, orthostatic, and reflex-mediated causes. Progressive exercise intolerance argues against orthostatic hypotension and reflex-mediated (vasovagal/situational) syncope, neither of which typically produces exertional limitation, and points toward a cardiac cause. Within cardiac causes, the gradual, progressive tempo of the exercise intolerance (rather than a sudden, discrete event) favors a structural etiology over a primary arrhythmia, though he notes the classic pathway of structural heart disease (such as heart failure with reduced ejection fraction) eventually producing an arrhythmic event. Approaching structural heart disease anatomically, he lists aortic stenosis, pericardial disease, pulmonary hypertension, and cardiomyopathy (such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy causing outflow obstruction) as leading candidates to prioritize ruling out.

Returning to the present day, the presenting discussant is interrupted while reading the old note and doesn't finish it before walking into the room. He finds a pleasant, non-distressed-appearing man whose chief complaint today is progressive difficulty swallowing over the past couple of months, affecting both solids and liquids from the outset, localizing to just below the sternal notch, without cough, regurgitation, or dyspnea associated with swallowing. At the bedside, he has the patient sip water (tolerated, though uncomfortable) and then attempt to swallow a whole ice cube (which causes visible discomfort) — a maneuver chosen because the ice cube will simply melt rather than cause a true obstruction if it becomes lodged.

The responding discussant deliberately avoids linking this new complaint to the syncope history from three years earlier, framing the current problem purely as dysphagia. He distinguishes oropharyngeal from esophageal dysphagia (acknowledging the history is an imperfect localizer, though the patient's pointing to his sternum favors an esophageal process) and further divides esophageal dysphagia into structural and motility categories, using tempo to help prioritize: a hyperacute presentation should raise concern for an impacted food bolus or foreign body (a true emergency given perforation risk) or, separately, an acute stroke. He lists his diagnostic toolkit up front — upper endoscopy, barium swallow, cross-sectional imaging (for extrinsic compression), and manometry (for motility) — acknowledging he lacks a firm framework for choosing endoscopy versus barium swallow first. Structural causes include esophageal webs (with Plummer-Vinson syndrome and iron deficiency), strictures, esophagitis, intraluminal mass or cancer, and extrinsic compression (a mediastinal mass, left atrial enlargement, or — recalling a prior personal case — an aortic aneurysm compressing the esophagus). Motility causes include diffuse esophageal spasm and connective tissue disease-related dysmotility (systemic sclerosis/CREST, and other autoimmune processes such as dermatomyositis); simultaneous difficulty with both liquids and solids from the outset is noted as a soft clue favoring a motility disorder over a structural lesion.

In real time, given it is after hours (barium swallow unavailable) and the ED is busy, the presenting discussant orders a CT scan with contrast — partly to evaluate for extrinsic esophageal compression, and partly because of the patient's known cardiac history — along with basic labs screening for malnutrition (BUN, creatinine, liver enzymes, CBC), all of which return normal. The CT shows no intraluminal esophageal mass and no extrinsic compression, but incidentally reveals a dilated pulmonary artery, right ventricular hypertrophy, and septal bowing. Reassured that the dysphagia is not from malignancy or malnutrition, he refers the patient to GI and to speech and swallow for a modified barium swallow, while remaining intrigued by the incidental cardiac findings.

With more time afterward, he reviews the old chart further: the ED note from three years prior documented right axis deviation on EKG without ischemic changes; the patient was discharged at the time with an outpatient echocardiogram and an ambulatory rhythm monitor (per patient preference over admission). The rhythm monitor was normal. The outpatient echocardiogram showed pulmonary hypertension without evidence of intrinsic lung disease or left heart disease.

The responding discussant notes that pulmonary hypertension is an easy diagnosis to miss as a cause of syncope, given its relatively low prevalence compared to more common cardiac and pulmonary causes, but concludes that this patient's syncope three years ago was very likely due to previously undiagnosed pulmonary hypertension. He laments that the patient was lost to follow-up, drawing an analogy to iron deficiency anemia — labeling a diagnosis without treating it accomplishes little, whereas early diagnosis and treatment of pulmonary hypertension can slow its progression — and predicts a repeat echocardiogram will show meaningfully worse numbers than three years ago, given the intervening years without treatment. Having already excluded left heart disease (group 2) and intrinsic lung disease (group 3) from the prior workup, he walks through the remaining categories: group 4 (chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, uncommon here but important because it is potentially curable by clot removal and requires a V/Q scan rather than CTA to diagnose), group 1 (pulmonary arterial hypertension, from inflammatory/autoimmune causes such as lupus or systemic sclerosis, infectious causes such as schistosomiasis-driven portopulmonary hypertension or HIV, or non-inflammatory causes such as scurvy or methamphetamine use), and group 5 (miscellaneous causes such as hemolytic disorders like sickle cell disease, or sarcoidosis).

He explicitly wonders whether the dysphagia and the pulmonary hypertension are linked — either mechanically (uncertain whether pulmonary artery or RV enlargement can directly compress the esophagus, noting that extrinsic esophageal compression is usually attributed to the left atrium or the aorta rather than the pulmonary vasculature) or through a shared systemic driver, such as an autoimmune process (systemic sclerosis, dermatomyositis) capable of affecting both the esophagus and the pulmonary vasculature simultaneously. He explains the mechanism by which severe pulmonary hypertension can itself produce syncope: septal bowing toward the left ventricle both reduces RV output delivered to the left heart (lowering LV preload and stroke volume) and creates a secondary, hypertrophic-cardiomyopathy-like outflow tract obstruction as the bowed septum encroaches on the LV cavity — a "two-hit" mechanism for exertional syncope. His plan is to repeat the echocardiogram and to send a panel that deliberately overlaps the causes of pulmonary hypertension and the causes of dysphagia: ANA, CK, HIV, ESR, and CRP.

The presenting discussant then reveals what the old chart actually showed: HIV negative, no culprit medications, no suspected nutritional deficiency, CK normal, and ANA positive at a significant titer of 1:640 — but subserologies were never sent, because a urine toxicology screen was positive for methamphetamine and the patient disclosed daily use three to four times a day. The prior provider attributed the entire presentation to methamphetamine-associated pulmonary arterial hypertension and had a cessation conversation that went poorly, damaging the therapeutic relationship and leading the patient to disengage from care until this dysphagia visit three years later.

The responding discussant draws an explicit parallel to the diagnostic lesson from the immediately preceding case (methamphetamine use does not exclude an independent or coexisting diagnosis): a significantly elevated ANA titer should never have gone unfollowed simply because methamphetamine use offered a plausible alternative explanation. In real time, he texts the inpatient psychiatry team currently caring for the patient (having since been transferred there) to send a CK and repeat ANA; both return — ANA remains elevated, CK normal — reinforcing that the picture is not purely methamphetamine-induced. With pulmonary hypertension, esophageal dysphagia, and a persistently positive ANA now converging, his leading concern becomes limited systemic sclerosis (CREST syndrome) as the unifying diagnosis, while explicitly flagging that autoimmune diseases overlap significantly (mixed connective tissue disease and overlap syndromes are common) and that determining the specific subserology matters greatly for management — for example, misattributing the picture to lupus or dermatomyositis and treating empirically with high-dose steroids could precipitate scleroderma renal crisis if systemic sclerosis is the true, unrecognized diagnosis.

The presenting discussant then reveals the resolution: barium swallow and EGD were both consistent with esophageal dysmotility; manometry showed hypoperistalsis/aperistalsis of the esophageal body with reduced lower esophageal sphincter tone; ANA subserologies returned positive for anti-SCL-70 (Scl-70), and rheumatology exam identified telangiectasias, skin tightening, and sclerodactyly — establishing a diagnosis of systemic sclerosis. He then discloses that the entire case, as presented, was a deliberately reconstructed and reordered teaching narrative built from a real morning report case he did not personally treat, adjusted specifically to make the diagnostic reasoning more educational than the true, messier sequence of events that actually occurred.

In closing, the responding discussant thanks the presenter for the structure of the case and emphasizes the broader lesson: clinicians can carry unconscious bias against patients who use substances, attributing new findings to the substance without proving that alternative causes have been excluded, and that this bias, once recognized, should be actively corrected — echoing the explicit teaching point from the prior episode's methamphetamine-and-pulmonary-hypertension case.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The case is structured around anchoring and diagnostic momentum: a significantly positive ANA was never pursued three years earlier because methamphetamine use offered an immediately available, socially reinforced explanation for the pulmonary hypertension — and the conversation built on that incomplete workup actively damaged the patient's trust in the medical system, delaying diagnosis of a treatable connective tissue disease by years.

The presenting discussant's explicit, disclosed reconstruction of the case — reordering real events into a more pedagogically clean narrative — is itself a teaching device about the gap between how diagnoses are taught (a clean, escalating logical sequence) and how they actually unfold in practice (fragmented across encounters, interrupted, revisited later with more context).

The responding discussant explicitly reinforces the immediately preceding episode's lesson on substance-use bias, treating consistent application of a corrective principle across cases as itself a marker of genuine practice change rather than a one-off observation.

## Teaching Discussion

Syncope should be reframed as "transient loss of consciousness" to keep seizure and hypoglycemia in view as mimics; once true syncope is established, exertional syncope specifically favors a cardiac cause, and the tempo of any preceding exercise intolerance (gradual versus sudden) helps distinguish a structural from a primarily arrhythmic driver.

Esophageal dysphagia should be organized into structural and motility categories, using onset pattern (progressive solid-to-liquid dysphagia favoring structural disease; simultaneous solid-and-liquid dysphagia favoring motility disease) to prioritize the workup, while always screening first for the acute emergencies (impacted bolus, stroke).

Severe pulmonary hypertension can cause syncope through a compounding two-hit mechanism: reduced RV output to the left heart lowering LV preload, and septal bowing toward the LV creating a secondary, hypertrophic-cardiomyopathy-like outflow obstruction.

A meaningfully elevated ANA titer should always be followed with subserologies, regardless of how compelling an alternative (such as substance use) explanation may seem — an isolated positive ANA left unexplained can represent years of delayed diagnosis for a treatable autoimmune disease.

Overlapping presentations (pulmonary hypertension plus esophageal dysmotility) should prompt a search for a single unifying systemic diagnosis (such as limited systemic sclerosis) rather than treating each abnormal finding as independent, since autoimmune diseases frequently affect multiple organ systems through a shared mechanism.

Determining the specific autoimmune diagnosis (rather than treating empirically) matters for safety as well as diagnostic precision — for example, unrecognized systemic sclerosis treated with high-dose steroids (as might be reasonable for lupus or dermatomyositis) carries a real risk of precipitating scleroderma renal crisis.

Clinicians should actively guard against unconscious bias toward patients with substance use disorders, in which new findings are reflexively attributed to the substance without first excluding — or actively pursuing — a coexisting, unrelated, and potentially treatable diagnosis; recognizing and correcting this bias should be treated as an ongoing practice, not a single lesson learned.
