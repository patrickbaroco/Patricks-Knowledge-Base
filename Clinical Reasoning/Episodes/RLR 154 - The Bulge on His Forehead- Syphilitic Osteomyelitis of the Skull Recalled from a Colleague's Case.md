## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Robbie presents his own ED case: a man with a slowly enlarging, painful bulge on his frontal skull, first noticed as a headache about a month prior. A layered anatomical work-up (skin, soft tissue, bone) plus a "pain implies rapid stretch" heuristic drives imaging, which reveals focal frontal bone destruction resembling osteomyelitis without contiguous sinus disease. Reza refuses to accept "osteomyelitis" as a settled read from CT alone, invoking a Lawrence Tierney teaching pearl about disc-space involvement discriminating infection from malignancy in the spine and applying the same anatomic logic to an isolated skull lesion. A fully negative standard work-up (PTH, SPEP, free light chains, PSA, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis) forces a pivot to atypical or immune-privileged processes, at which point Reza recalls a colleague's prior case of syphilis of the skull — and Robbie, prompted by the recollection, elicits a previously unmentioned history of well-controlled HIV and male sexual partners. Serologies confirm secondary syphilis with syphilitic osteomyelitis of the skull.

## Pearls

- Rapid stretch of tissue — whether from a fast-growing benign or malignant process — activates nociceptors and causes pain; this is the same logic behind the teaching pearl that tender, rapidly enlarging lymph nodes (from infection stretching the capsule quickly) are often more reassuring than painless, slowly enlarging ones, while acknowledging that rapidly growing lymphoma can also be painful.
- A reported headache overlying a scalp/skull mass should not be assumed to originate from the mass itself — an intracranial process extending outward must be considered and reasonably excluded (typically via contrast imaging) before attributing head pain to a superficial or bony lesion.
- Contrast CT alone cannot reliably distinguish osteomyelitis from a bone-invading malignancy or other destructive process based on appearance; radiographic resemblance to osteomyelitis is a description of morphology, not a confirmed diagnosis.
- Lawrence Tierney's disc-space teaching pearl, generalized: in vertebral disease, involvement of the avascular disc space favors infection over malignancy, because infection can cross avascular tissue while malignancy requires a blood supply to invade — the same anatomic-vascularity logic can be extended to other bones to help discriminate infectious from neoplastic bone destruction.
- Absence of contiguous sinus or soft-tissue disease adjacent to a focal skull lesion argues against simple direct extension as the source and should raise suspicion for a distant primary process seeding the bone (hematogenous metastasis or an atypical/opportunistic infection).
- A single, uniformly negative broad screen (CBC, renal function, LFTs, PTH, SPEP, serum free light chain ratio, PSA) meaningfully lowers the probability of the major "usual suspect" causes of an isolated bone lesion (hyperparathyroid bone disease, myeloma, metastatic prostate cancer) and should prompt escalation to rarer or more specific diagnoses rather than repeating the same category of testing.
- The CNS is an immune-privileged space behind the blood-brain barrier and can sustain isolated inflammation without producing systemic inflammatory markers — a useful explanation for why a destructive process can look alarming locally while systemic labs remain reassuringly normal; bone itself, however, is not part of the CNS, and this logic must be applied carefully to the specific tissue in question.
- "Vanishing bone syndrome" (a rare, distinct entity from vanishing bile duct syndrome) illustrates a broader category of isolated-bone-process differentials that are diagnosable only by biopsy, underscoring that a bone lesion refractory to noninvasive work-up ultimately needs tissue.
- A previously heard case — even one presented by a colleague on an entirely different segment, for a superficially unrelated presentation — can function as a stored illness script that enables near-instant pattern recognition when a new case shares a deep structural similarity (an isolated destructive bone lesion with an otherwise unremarkable work-up), even when the anatomic location differs.
- Sexual history (partners, HIV status) is easily deferred or omitted in an initial ED history, but can be the single history element that reframes an entire differential — its absence from an initial presentation does not mean it is not present, only that it has not yet been asked.
- Secondary syphilis classically features a rash in roughly 95% of patients, but its absence on a documented exam should not be over-interpreted as true absence — it may simply reflect an incomplete or non-targeted skin exam rather than a genuinely rash-free presentation.
- Syphilitic osteomyelitis classically shows tropism for the skull base, but this case is a reminder that the frontal calvarium (a non-classic site) can also be involved, and that classic tropism patterns describe typical rather than exclusive distributions.
- Treponemal-first serologic testing algorithms (treponemal antibody, reflexing to RPR) take several days to result, meaning empiric management decisions in an alarming-appearing bone lesion often must proceed on clinical suspicion before serologic confirmation returns.

## Problem Representation

A man presented with roughly a month of frontal headache evolving into a progressively enlarging, painless-to-palpation-unclear bulge on the top of his head, without fever, chills, or weight loss but with mild fatigue, initially reassured by his PCP, ultimately found on contrast CT to have focal frontal bone destruction without contiguous sinus disease or intracranial extension, and — after a completely unremarkable standard metabolic, myeloma, and prostate-cancer screen plus a negative CT chest/abdomen/pelvis — diagnosed via a colleague's-case-triggered recollection and a subsequently elicited history of well-controlled HIV with male sexual partners as secondary syphilis with syphilitic osteomyelitis of the skull.

## Case Narrative

Robbie presents a case from his own ED practice: a patient presents with a bulge on the top of his head, in the frontal region, first noticed about a month prior as a headache in that same area. Fullness began roughly three weeks ago and has been progressively enlarging, with the growing prominence over the past two weeks prompting the ED visit. He denies fevers, chills, or weight loss, though he reports mild fatigue and feeling generally run down. He had seen his primary care physician around the two-week mark, who was not confident anything serious was going on and recommended watchful monitoring.

Working from a shared clinical photograph, Reza describes the appearance as resembling "two golf balls" sitting under the skin of the frontal forehead, not reaching the eyebrow. He notes no ecchymosis or discoloration, which argues against a simple traumatic etiology. He organizes his thinking using a layered anatomical framework — skin, subcutaneous fat, vessels, and bone/skull — and considers a lipoma (though an unusual location for one), a mass arising off the skull itself, or a venous dilatation. He emphasizes that palpation on exam is essential: assessing the consistency of the mass and its relationship to the underlying skull would substantially narrow the differential. He asks for more history — onset, growth trajectory, any trauma, and associated symptoms — and, if a skilled colleague is available, would want an ultrasound first to distinguish fluid or blood from fatty or solid/tumorous tissue; otherwise, a contrast CT to assess bony involvement and enhancement. He also wants basic labs screening for markers of an inflammatory or infiltrative process — elevated platelets, low albumin, or low hemoglobin.

The reported headache becomes a specific focus. Reza flags it as a red flag rather than an incidental complaint: rapid stretch of tissue, whether from a fast-growing benign or malignant process, activates nociceptors and produces pain. He draws an analogy to the teaching pearl that tender, rapidly enlarging lymph nodes are often more reassuring than painless, slowly enlarging ones — because rapid infectious enlargement stretches the nodal capsule quickly, causing pain — while acknowledging that rapidly growing lymphoma can also be painful, so pain alone does not exclude malignancy. Critically, he cautions against simply assuming the mass itself is the source of the headache; an intracranial process extending outward through the skull must be considered and reasonably excluded first, which argues for obtaining a CT scan with IV contrast rather than relying on the external appearance alone.

Imaging obtained in the ED shows a focal area of bony destruction and erosion in the frontal bone, radiographically resembling osteomyelitis. There is no surrounding sinus disease and no intracranial extension; the overlying soft tissue swelling is explained as edema associated with the underlying bony process.

Reza pushes back on simply accepting "osteomyelitis" as a settled diagnosis based on the CT appearance alone, noting that contrast CT cannot definitively distinguish osteomyelitis from another bone-invading process purely by morphology. He recalls a teaching pearl from Lawrence Tierney ("LT") about vertebral disease: involvement of the intervertebral disc space favors an infectious over a malignant cause, because infection — unlike cancer, which requires a blood supply to invade tissue — can cross the avascular disc space, while malignancy generally cannot. He extends this same anatomic-vascularity logic to the skull lesion in front of them as a general discriminating principle. He notes he has personally never encountered osteomyelitis of the frontal bone specifically, and that the absence of contiguous sinus disease argues against simple direct extension as the source — raising suspicion that this may not be straightforward primary osteomyelitis, and prompting consideration of a distant primary process seeding the skull: a rapidly metastasizing malignancy (such as small cell carcinoma) or an atypical infection (such as Nocardia) originating from the lungs. He recommends a low threshold for CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis alongside the basic labs already planned.

The labs return uniformly, strikingly normal: CBC, renal function, and liver function tests are all normal; parathyroid hormone (PTH) is normal; SPEP and the serum free light chain ratio are normal, arguing against myeloma; PSA is normal, arguing against metastatic prostate cancer. Reza reacts with genuine surprise at how completely unremarkable the panel is, given the alarming appearance of the lesion.

This uniformly negative screen prompts Reza to recall a case presented by a colleague, Gabby — a neurologist, trained in part alongside a colleague referred to as "Tiago," with a background touching UCSF and a possible move toward further training at UCLA — on a sister segment of the show (referred to inconsistently in conversation as "RLR," "BMR," and "VMR," apparently a distinct recurring offering rather than the main podcast). In that prior case, the final diagnosis was syphilis involving the skull. This recollection reframes Reza's thinking toward an isolated-bone-process category: he raises "vanishing bone syndrome" (distinct from vanishing bile duct syndrome) as an example of a rare entity in this category that is diagnosable only by biopsy, and recommends obtaining a sexual history, RPR testing, consideration of HIV testing, further imaging to exclude disease elsewhere, and ultimately a bone biopsy to establish the underlying process if serologic and screening tests remain unrevealing. He notes that the CNS, as an immune-privileged space behind the blood-brain barrier, can sustain isolated inflammation without producing systemic markers — while explicitly clarifying that bone itself is not technically part of the CNS, so this logic must be applied carefully rather than assumed to cover the lesion directly.

CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis is obtained and returns negative for disease elsewhere, eliminating an occult visceral primary as the source. At this point, Robbie reveals a history detail not previously shared with the audience: the patient has well-controlled HIV and is sexually active with men. This lands as his own "aha" moment, mirroring Reza's recollection of Gabby's case in real time. Syphilis serologies are sent using a treponemal-first algorithm — treponemal antibody testing, reflexing to RPR — which takes two to three days to result. The final diagnosis is secondary syphilis with syphilitic osteomyelitis of the skull; syphilitic bone involvement classically shows tropism for the skull base, though in this patient the lesion was frontal, a non-classic site. The patient's fatigue and malaise are retrospectively explained as manifestations of secondary syphilis.

Robbie admits he did not carefully examine the patient's skin for a subtle rash, noting the textbook teaching that roughly 95% of secondary syphilis patients have one, and speculates that its absence from his documentation likely reflects an incomplete exam rather than a truly rash-free presentation. He updated the patient by phone (he has not seen him again in person) and referred him to infectious disease for outpatient treatment, with follow-up anticipated the following Tuesday. He speculates that a single dose of intramuscular penicillin might be the standard treatment but wonders whether ID will be more conservative given how alarming the presentation appeared radiographically.

Reza closes by reflecting on how directly a previously-heard case — Gabby's syphilis-of-the-skull presentation — enabled rapid pattern recognition in this new, superficially very different case, reinforcing the show's recurring theme that case-based recall is often the actual trigger for catching a rare diagnosis, more than any systematic differential-generation process. Reza also reflects, somewhat wistfully, on having been "preoccupied by golf" lately while still following the show and Robbie's work from a distance.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Data Point | Effect on Differential |
|---|---|
| Slowly enlarging frontal scalp/skull bulge over ~1 month, no ecchymosis | Lowers probability of simple trauma; keeps lipoma, vascular lesion, and bone-origin mass on the differential |
| Associated headache in the same region | Raises concern for rapid tissue stretch (benign or malignant) and for a possible intracranial process extending outward, rather than a purely superficial process |
| Contrast CT: focal frontal bone destruction, no contiguous sinus disease, no intracranial extension | Raises probability of osteomyelitis by morphology alone, but the absence of contiguous sinus disease lowers probability of simple direct extension and raises probability of a distant/hematogenous source |
| Fully normal CBC, renal function, LFTs, PTH, SPEP, free light chain ratio, PSA | Substantially lowers probability of hyperparathyroid bone disease, myeloma, and metastatic prostate cancer; narrows the field toward atypical infection or a rarer process |
| Negative CT chest/abdomen/pelvis | Lowers probability of an occult visceral malignancy or infectious source seeding the skull |
| Recalled colleague case of skull syphilis (Gabby's case) | Reprioritizes syphilis/atypical infection within the differential before any new history is obtained |
| Newly elicited history: well-controlled HIV, sexually active with men | Sharply raises pretest probability of syphilis and prompts confirmatory serologic testing |
| Positive treponemal antibody / RPR (secondary syphilis) | Confirms syphilitic osteomyelitis of the skull as the unifying diagnosis, explaining the bone lesion and the associated fatigue/malaise |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Pain-as-tissue-stretch heuristic**: rapid capsular or periosteal stretch from either infectious or neoplastic processes produces pain, reused from the lymph-node teaching pearl and applied here to a growing bony/soft-tissue mass.
- **Imaging-morphology-is-not-diagnosis discipline**: refusing to accept a CT read of "resembles osteomyelitis" as a confirmed diagnosis, and instead using anatomic reasoning (contiguous sinus disease vs. not) to weigh direct extension against a distant seeding source.
- **Disc-space infection-versus-malignancy pearl (LT), generalized beyond the spine**: an avascular-tissue-crossing heuristic originally taught for vertebral disease, deliberately extended here to reasoning about a skull lesion.
- **Immune-privilege reasoning, applied cautiously**: invoking the CNS's capacity for isolated inflammation without systemic markers to explain a normal systemic screen, while explicitly flagging that bone is not CNS tissue and the analogy has limits.
- **Case-based recall as the diagnostic trigger**: a colleague's previously heard case (skull syphilis) — encountered on an entirely different segment — functions as the actual mechanism that reprioritizes syphilis on the differential, ahead of and independent from any new patient-specific history.
- **Deferred/incomplete sexual history as a diagnostic blind spot**: a standard component of history-taking that was not obtained on initial presentation becomes the single most consequential missing data point once revisited.

## Teaching Discussion

The case is built around a discipline of refusing premature diagnostic closure at each successive step: refusing to attribute a headache automatically to an overlying mass without excluding an intracranial process, refusing to accept a CT's morphologic resemblance to osteomyelitis as a settled diagnosis, and refusing to stop investigating once the first round of "usual suspect" labs returned normal. At each junction, an explicit anatomic or physiologic principle — tissue-stretch-causes-pain, avascular-tissue-crossing infection-versus-malignancy logic, immune privilege — is used to keep reasoning honest rather than defaulting to the most convenient label.

The pivotal teaching moment, though, is not analytic but associative: Reza's recollection of a colleague's syphilis-of-the-skull case, heard on an unrelated segment, is what actually reprioritizes syphilis on the differential — before any new history had been obtained from the patient. This is a clean illustration of the show's recurring "case-based recall as the trigger for catching a rare diagnosis" theme, and it also models productive cross-pollination between colleagues' individual case experiences and a shared institutional illness-script library.

The final piece — Robbie's own elicitation of a previously unmentioned HIV/MSM history once syphilis was already on his mind — models how a differential-driven return to history-taking (rather than a rote, front-loaded social history checklist) can surface the single decisive data point. Robbie's candid admission that he never carefully examined for a rash reinforces a broader theme of diagnostic humility: acknowledging gaps in one's own exam rather than assuming an unremarked finding was truly absent.
