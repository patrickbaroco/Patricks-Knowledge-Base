## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A young man with a kidney transplant, Crohn's disease, and prior splenectomy develops sudden severe abdominal pain and nausea while driving. What follows is a genuine diagnostic tug-of-war between the abdomen and the chest: is his cardiopulmonary decompensation caused by an intra-abdominal catastrophe, or was the abdominal pain itself always a red herring — referred pain from a heart under attack? A subtle but critical EKG finding (ST elevation in aVR) is initially missed, and the working diagnosis drifts toward aspiration pneumonia, delaying recognition of an evolving myocardial infarction in a 30-year-old with no classic cardiac risk factors beyond chronic inflammatory disease and renal impairment. Reclaiming the case requires shedding "contrast phobia," reading the room-temperature EKG with real suspicion, and ultimately a coronary catheterization that finds and treats the true culprit: an acute plaque rupture.

## Pearls

- Truly sudden-onset abdominal pain in an otherwise healthy patient has a relatively short differential — something ruptured (e.g., a AAA), something got mechanically obstructed (e.g., a bowel obstruction), or there was a sudden discharge of electrical activity (more classically a seizure or arrhythmia than an abdominal process) — with rapid-acting molecular triggers (anaphylaxis, hypoglycemia) as less common alternatives.
- In a patient with a complicated medical history (prior abdominal surgery, chronic immunosuppression, inflammatory bowel disease), "sudden onset" symptoms should be interpreted with an open mind toward "sudden recognition" of a process that has actually been gradually brewing — analogous to a patient with longstanding but unrecognized aortic stenosis presenting with sudden syncope.
- Biologic immunosuppressants — TNF-alpha blockers, JAK inhibitors, IL inhibitors, and other immunotherapies — are all associated with an increased risk of bowel perforation, since they fundamentally weaken both systemic immune surveillance and the integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier.
- Fever with neutrophilic leukocytosis should not be reflexively equated with infection — tissue ischemia and necrosis can produce an essentially identical inflammatory laboratory picture, and both possibilities deserve consideration before anchoring on an infectious explanation.
- When a patient presents with abdominal pain alongside signs of cardiac strain (hypertension, elevated troponin, elevated BNP), a normal EKG can be used to reasonably localize the process to the abdomen, while a grossly abnormal EKG (e.g., diffuse ST elevation) raises strong concern the process is primarily cardiac — genuinely equivocal EKG findings (e.g., isolated ST depressions) leave the directionality of the problem unresolved and warrant further testing on both fronts.
- A practice pattern of avoiding IV contrast out of fear of contrast-induced nephropathy ("contrast phobia") can meaningfully delay diagnosis in a critically ill patient — the underlying disease process being investigated is typically a far greater threat to renal (and overall) survival than a single dose of contrast, and a genuine contrast allergy is more common than clinically significant contrast-induced nephropathy.
- An upright KUB has a sensitivity no higher than roughly 50% for bowel obstruction or perforation, making it an inadequate stand-alone test to exclude these diagnoses when clinical suspicion is significant.
- When an abdominal process appears to be causing new cardiopulmonary decompensation, there are three classic anatomic routes to consider: aspiration via the esophagus (vomitus reaching the airway), transdiaphragmatic spread (e.g., large-volume ascites tracking into the right pleural space), and a vascular/sympathetic route (severe abdominal pain driving a sympathetic surge that can trigger hypertensive flash pulmonary edema, Takotsubo physiology, or plaque rupture and ACS) — but the reverse causal direction (a primary cardiac event manifesting as abdominal pain, including via congestive hepatopathy or bowel wall edema) must always be considered and actively ruled out before accepting the abdomen as the primary source.
- ST elevation in lead aVR is an important and easily overlooked marker of diffuse subendocardial ischemia or left main coronary disease, and should be specifically sought whenever diffuse ST depressions are seen across the precordial and limb leads, rather than being dismissed as a nonspecific or secondary finding.
- The differential for ST elevation extends beyond acute coronary syndrome to include Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, pericarditis with effusion, and pulmonary embolism — each has distinguishing features (reciprocal ST depression pattern, QTc behavior, associated inferior lead changes) worth actively considering rather than anchoring immediately on ACS.
- Activating a formal STEMI response pathway is meant to be imperfect by design — being wrong a meaningful fraction of the time when activating it is an acceptable and expected cost of a system built to catch true STEMIs quickly; when the clinical picture is atypical or confusing, directly engaging a cardiology fellow for urgent, focused input is a reasonable alternative to full activation.
- A dramatic drop in ejection fraction that is disproportionate to a comparatively modest troponin elevation (a low "troponin x EF product") favors Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, whereas a troponin elevation proportionate to the degree of EF depression (a preserved or high troponin x EF product) favors true acute coronary syndrome — a useful heuristic when a patient shows apical akinesis and elevated biomarkers but the underlying etiology (infarct versus stress cardiomyopathy) remains unclear.
- Reperfusion-pattern T-wave changes (deep, symmetric T-wave inversions, an isoelectric ST segment, and absence of Q waves — of which the classic "Wellens'" pattern is one recognizable subtype) reflect myocardial edema after acute coronary occlusion followed by reperfusion, and should be recognized as a marker of a genuinely ischemic process requiring further workup, not merely a resolving or benign EKG evolution.
- QTc prolongation is a feature that can raise suspicion for Takotsubo cardiomyopathy over straightforward acute coronary syndrome, but must be interpreted alongside the patient's medication list — QTc-prolonging drugs (such as citalopram) are a confounder that can independently explain the finding and must be actively considered before attributing it to the underlying cardiac process.
- True acute coronary disease and Takotsubo cardiomyopathy can coexist or exist on a spectrum in some patients (with case reports describing synchronous Takotsubo and LAD vasospasm or dissection), meaning that identifying apical akinesis on echocardiogram does not exclude concurrent obstructive coronary disease — a coronary catheterization is generally required to distinguish (or identify overlap between) the two.
- Patients with chronic systemic inflammatory diseases (such as Crohn's disease) carry meaningfully elevated cardiovascular risk independent of traditional risk factors, and this risk should inform the threshold for cardiac workup even in young patients who would not otherwise be considered at high risk for coronary disease.
- Obtaining an EKG should be considered routine for essentially any patient with upper abdominal pain regardless of age, and for any patient over roughly age 50 with abdominal pain of any location — a young age alone should not be used to exclude acute coronary syndrome from the differential for atypical abdominal presentations, especially in patients with elevated baseline cardiovascular risk.

## Problem Representation

A young man with end-stage renal disease status post kidney transplant, Crohn's disease, and prior splenectomy for ITP who develops sudden severe periumbilical abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting while driving, followed by evolving hypoxemic respiratory failure initially attributed to aspiration pneumonia — ultimately found, via serial EKGs, troponin trends, echocardiography, and coronary catheterization, to represent an acute plaque rupture and myocardial infarction, with the abdominal pain retrospectively determined to have been entirely referred cardiac pain.

## Case Narrative

Reza presented a case from his institution's Apex conference, originally managed by chief residents he had mentored: a young man who developed sudden, severe abdominal pain with nausea and vomiting while driving, forcing him to pull over; the pain partially improved and he drove himself to the emergency department. His past medical history included end-stage renal disease from focal segmental glomerulosclerosis with a kidney transplant in 2010, hypertension, Crohn's disease, and a history of ITP status post splenectomy. His medications included adalimumab (a TNF-alpha inhibitor), citalopram, pantoprazole, prednisone, tacrolimus, and sodium bicarbonate.

Rabih began by reasoning through the significance of a truly sudden symptom onset: a short list of mechanisms typically produce genuinely abrupt symptoms — rupture (e.g., a AAA), mechanical obstruction (e.g., bowel obstruction), or a sudden electrical discharge (more classically relevant to the brain or heart than the abdomen) — with rapid molecular triggers like anaphylaxis or hypoglycemia as less common alternatives. However, given this patient's extensive medical history, he flagged an important alternative interpretation: "sudden recognition" of a process that had actually been gradually brewing, using aortic stenosis presenting with sudden syncope as an illustrative analogy. He noted that the patient's prior abdominal surgery raised risk for bowel obstruction, his Crohn's disease raised risk for bowel perforation, and — as a broader teaching point — biologic immunosuppressants (TNF-alpha blockers, JAK inhibitors, IL inhibitors, and other immunotherapies) all carry an association with bowel perforation risk, given their combined effect of weakening systemic immunity and the intestinal mucosal barrier. He concluded that the severity of this presentation demanded a thorough, full workup regardless of which specific mechanism was ultimately responsible.

The exam and initial data: temperature 38.1°C, blood pressure 190/128, heart rate 101, oxygen saturation 96% on room air with normal work of breathing; tachycardic heart sounds without murmurs; diffuse periumbilical abdominal tenderness without distension, with normal bowel sounds and a soft abdomen. Labs showed hemoglobin 7.7 and platelets 51 (both near his post-splenectomy baseline), white count 15,000 with neutrophil predominance, potassium 5.5 (expected in a dialysis-dependent patient), otherwise unremarkable electrolytes, alkaline phosphatase 306, a normal lactate, high-sensitivity troponin 506, BNP approximately 3,000, a normal chest X-ray, pending blood cultures, and a negative COVID test.

Rabih synthesized this as two overlapping "threads": an inflammatory thread (fever, neutrophilic leukocytosis) and a cardiovascular thread (hypertension, elevated troponin and BNP). He cautioned against reflexively equating fever and neutrophilia with infection alone, since tissue ischemia and necrosis can produce an essentially identical inflammatory picture. He then worked through the central diagnostic tension of the case: was this a primary intra-abdominal catastrophe severe enough to cause marked hypertension and secondary cardiac strain (particularly given the patient's already-impaired renal function and presumed longstanding hypertension), a primary cardiac event with abdominal pain either referred or arising from cardiac ischemia's downstream effects, or an abdominal process transmitting its effects to the heart through a hypertensive/sympathetic surge? He proposed the EKG as the single most powerful discriminating tool: a completely normal EKG would support confidently localizing the process to the abdomen, while a clearly abnormal EKG (e.g., diffuse ST elevation) would strongly implicate the heart — while acknowledging a normal-appearing EKG doesn't fully exclude Takotsubo triggered by an abdominal event, and other patterns would remain genuinely equivocal. He also recommended an urgent CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast, explicitly defending this choice against contrast-avoidance concerns: in his practice, the underlying disease process (particularly when bowel ischemia, perforation, or infection is on the differential) poses far greater risk than a limited dose of IV contrast, and an upright KUB — sensitivity capped around 50% for obstruction or perforation — would be inadequate on its own.

Before this plan could be executed, the patient developed acute respiratory distress in the emergency department, desaturating to 84% on room air and requiring 40 liters of high-flow nasal cannula at 50% FiO2 to reach 96%, with new crackles on repeat exam. Rabih worked through the three classic anatomic routes by which an abdominal process can produce cardiopulmonary decompensation: aspiration via the esophagus (vomitus reaching the airway), transdiaphragmatic spread (e.g., large ascites tracking preferentially into the right pleural space), and a vascular/sympathetic route, wherein a severe abdominal event drives a sympathetic surge capable of triggering hypertension-induced flash pulmonary edema, Takotsubo physiology, or — as is well recognized as a risk during abdominal surgery — plaque rupture and acute coronary syndrome. He was equally explicit about the reverse possibility: that the disease had actually begun in the chest, either as an atypically localized (periumbilical) inferior myocardial infarction, or as rapid-onset heart failure producing congestive hepatopathy or bowel wall edema — the latter a particularly good anatomic fit for periumbilical pain, given the bowel's proximity. He concluded that, regardless of directionality, a CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast remained the single most informative next test: a completely normal result would confidently redirect the entire workup to the chest.

An initial EKG was obtained and shared. Rabih's reflexive read immediately flagged ST elevation in lead aVR — a marker of diffuse subendocardial ischemia or left main coronary disease — along with diffuse ST changes across V4-V6, I, aVL, and V2, and possible (submillimeter, uncertain) ST elevation in V1-V2. He reviewed the broader differential for ST elevation beyond ACS — Takotsubo, pericarditis with effusion, and pulmonary embolism — noting ST depressions in the inferior leads (compatible with PE) and a prolonged QTc (a feature he associated with Takotsubo, while acknowledging uncertainty about aVR's specific behavior in that condition). He recommended point-of-care echocardiography to assess for focal wall motion abnormalities, apical ballooning, pericardial effusion, or right ventricular strain (McConnell sign), anticipated resistance from cardiology to catheterizing a patient with severe undifferentiated abdominal pain before abdominal imaging, and outlined immediately actionable ED steps: aspirin 325mg (contingent on a reassuring point-of-care hemoglobin, given the patient's baseline anemia/thrombocytopenia) and a statin, with heparin prepared but not yet given. He also discussed the judgment call inherent in activating a formal STEMI response versus directly engaging a cardiology fellow for urgent, focused input given the atypical clinical context — noting that being wrong a meaningful fraction of the time when activating a STEMI pathway is an acceptable, expected cost of that system.

The patient's actual course deviated from this plan. A chest X-ray during the respiratory decompensation showed bilateral opacifications, and — without contrast imaging of the abdomen and without a witnessed aspiration event — the working diagnosis became aspiration pneumonia; the patient was started on vancomycin and cefepime, given furosemide given uncertainty between pneumonitis and pulmonary edema, and a non-contrast CT chest (showing nodular and confluent consolidations) and lower-extremity ultrasound (negative for DVT) were obtained; he was admitted to the MICU on high-flow nasal cannula.

Rabih responded to this trajectory with pointed but explicitly non-judgmental pushback, framing his own past management errors as context for why he felt comfortable raising the concern. He argued the aspiration-pneumonia framing left a major evidentiary gap — no witnessed aspiration event, an unexamined abdomen, and objective markers of cardiac strain (aVR elevation, elevated troponin and BNP) not yet meaningfully addressed. He attributed the deviation to contrast phobia, arguing that a normal creatinine would very likely have already prompted a contrast-enhanced CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, and that his own practice had shed this reflex over time: contrast allergy is more common than clinically significant contrast-induced nephropathy, and a well-justified indication for scanning outweighs CIN risk. He also flagged the absence of a plausible mechanism for aspiration in an otherwise neurologically intact 30-year-old without dementia or another neurodegenerative process, cautioning against treating "aspiration" as a default, unexamined explanation reserved more appropriately for older or cognitively impaired patients.

Serial EKGs and troponins followed the patient's course: four hours later, troponin 761, with more pronounced T-wave inversion in the lateral leads (notably deeper in V4) but an otherwise similar EKG; six hours later, troponin 1,380, with a new pattern of isoelectric ST segments, no Q waves, and inverted T waves across V2-V5 — a "reperfusion pattern" (of which the classically described Wellens' pattern is one recognizable variant), reflecting myocardial edema following acute occlusion and reperfusion. Rabih flagged the QTc prolongation as somewhat atypical for straightforward ACS (usually associated with a relatively normal QTc) and raised Takotsubo as a competing or coexisting possibility, introducing a heuristic — the "troponin x EF product" — in which Takotsubo tends to show a relatively low troponin alongside a low EF (a low product), while ACS tends to show a proportionately high troponin when EF has dropped significantly (a preserved or high product). Citalopram's known QTc-prolonging effect was raised as a confounder for the QTc finding, momentarily overlooked by both hosts before being explicitly acknowledged.

The patient remained chest-pain-free throughout. His oxygen requirement improved with diuresis (furosemide), and a formal echocardiogram showed moderate concentric LVH, an entirely akinetic apex, an LVEF of 55-60%, and a small pericardial effusion. Rabih taught that heart failure is most confidently diagnosed retrospectively by its response to diuresis, since few other processes improve with diuretics in this way. He acknowledged the apical akinesis was suggestive of apical ballooning (Takotsubo), but emphasized that distinguishing this from a true LAD infarct requires real nuance — citing literature describing LAD disease and Takotsubo as existing on a spectrum in some patients, including case reports of synchronous Takotsubo with LAD vasospasm or dissection, and drawing a parallel to a previously discussed RLR case of Kounis syndrome. He concluded that a coronary catheterization was necessary to distinguish between (or identify overlap between) these possibilities, and separately noted that roughly 10% of Takotsubo patients develop LV outflow tract obstruction from basal hyperkinesis, a detail relevant to hemodynamic management (favoring caution with inotropes and preferential use of alpha agonists if this complication is suspected).

Coronary catheterization revealed diffuse, unexpectedly severe stenotic coronary disease for a 30-year-old, with the dominant lesion located in the proximal-to-mid LAD, and confirmed an acute plaque rupture as the cause of true acute coronary syndrome (rather than isolated Takotsubo). The patient received a stent and recovered well. His abdominal pain never recurred after the index event, retrospectively confirming that his initial nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain had been entirely referred cardiac pain from the onset of his coronary event. Notably, the original interpreting team had not identified the aVR elevation on the initial EKG, instead framing the diffuse ST depressions as a nonspecific sequela of demand physiology — a framing error that, in retrospect, was shaped by the team's prevailing working hypothesis (aspiration pneumonia) rather than an unbiased read of the tracing.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| Truly sudden-onset abdominal pain, but in a patient with extensive relevant PMH (transplant, Crohn's, prior surgery) | Leaves open both a genuinely sudden mechanical event and "sudden recognition" of a gradually brewing process |
| Fever + neutrophilic leukocytosis | Should not be assumed infectious; ischemia/necrosis can produce an identical laboratory picture |
| Elevated troponin and BNP alongside marked hypertension and abdominal pain | Raises a genuine three-way question of primary abdominal vs. primary cardiac vs. bidirectional process |
| Initial EKG: ST elevation in aVR, diffuse ST depressions, subtle STE in V1-V2 | Strong, easily overlooked marker of diffuse subendocardial ischemia/left main disease, favoring a primary cardiac process |
| New hypoxemic respiratory failure without a witnessed aspiration event | Weakens the aspiration-pneumonia hypothesis; should prompt reconsideration of cardiac and other abdominal-to-chest mechanisms |
| Bilateral opacifications on CXR/CT chest, no contrast-enhanced abdominal imaging obtained | Consistent with several competing hypotheses (aspiration, flash pulmonary edema, evolving heart failure); does not itself discriminate between them |
| Rising troponin (506 → 761 → 1,380) with an evolving reperfusion T-wave pattern | Strongly supports an ongoing/evolved ischemic coronary event over a purely stress-mediated (Takotsubo) or non-cardiac process |
| QTc prolongation | Raises consideration of Takotsubo, but is confounded by citalopram, a known QTc-prolonging medication on this patient's list |
| Echo: apical akinesis, EF 55-60%, small pericardial effusion, improvement with diuresis | Confirms a cardiac process (heart failure) but does not by itself distinguish Takotsubo from a true LAD territory infarct |
| Coronary catheterization: diffuse stenotic disease, dominant lesion in proximal/mid LAD, acute plaque rupture | Confirms true ACS as the diagnosis, effectively excluding isolated Takotsubo as the sole explanation |
| Abdominal pain never recurs after treatment of the coronary event | Retrospectively confirms the original abdominal pain was entirely referred cardiac pain |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Referred cardiac pain illness script: sudden abdominal pain — even periumbilical, atypical for classic cardiac referral patterns — combined with objective markers of cardiac strain (troponin, BNP, and especially an easily overlooked aVR elevation) should keep a primary cardiac event on the differential, particularly in patients with elevated baseline cardiovascular risk (chronic inflammatory disease, transplant-related risk factors) regardless of young age.

The case is an explicit teaching example of framing-driven anchoring: once the working diagnosis became "aspiration pneumonia," the same EKG that Rabih read with alarm was interpreted by the original team as a nonspecific sequela of demand physiology — illustrating how the hypothesis a clinician enters a case with shapes which findings register as significant versus dismissible. Rabih also explicitly named "contrast phobia" as a systemic bias pattern that can delay diagnosis in critically ill patients, and modeled non-judgmental peer correction by grounding his critique in his own past management errors rather than positioning himself as immune to the same failure mode.

## Teaching Discussion

The central teaching point is the reciprocal, bidirectional relationship between abdominal and cardiac pathology: an abdominal catastrophe can produce cardiopulmonary decompensation via aspiration, transdiaphragmatic spread, or a sympathetic/vascular surge, while a primary cardiac event can equally produce abdominal symptoms via referred pain or downstream congestive effects — and both directions must be actively considered rather than assumed from the initial presentation.

A second teaching point is the diagnostic value of lead aVR, an underappreciated lead whose ST elevation can be the single most important clue to diffuse subendocardial ischemia or left main disease, particularly when other leads show only nonspecific-appearing ST depressions.

A third teaching point is the practical case against reflexive "contrast phobia" in critically ill patients — the risk calculus overwhelmingly favors obtaining necessary contrast-enhanced imaging when the alternative is a delayed or incorrect diagnosis in an unstable patient.

The final, practice-changing teaching point, articulated explicitly by Rabih, is to obtain an EKG in any patient with upper abdominal pain regardless of age, and in any patient over roughly age 50 with abdominal pain of any location — reflecting the case's core lesson that a young patient's age should not be used to prematurely exclude acute coronary syndrome, especially when elevated baseline cardiovascular risk factors (like chronic inflammatory disease) are present.
