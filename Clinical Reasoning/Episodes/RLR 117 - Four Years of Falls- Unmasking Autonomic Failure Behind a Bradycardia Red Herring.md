A "company the disease keeps" framework, a careful search for red-flag mimics (amyloidosis, Sjögren's), and a genuinely practical management pearl about nocturnal pressure natriuresis round out a case that ultimately settles on isolated (or early neurodegenerative) autonomic failure, managed empirically and effectively in parallel with an unresolved diagnostic workup.

## Pearls

- Symptomatic bradycardia is not fundamentally about the absolute heart rate — it is about the loss of atrioventricular synchrony; pure sinus bradycardia, in the absence of any conduction disease, essentially cannot cause symptomatic bradycardia unless the rate falls below roughly 20 beats per minute, because complete AV synchrony means every atrial contraction still fills and ejects effectively regardless of how slow the rate is.
- Bradyarrhythmias can be stratified by degree of AV synchrony: complete synchrony (sinus bradycardia, first-degree AV block) essentially never warrants a pacemaker; complete asynchrony (third-degree/complete AV block, sinus arrest) almost always warrants one; and intermediate, partial-synchrony rhythms (Mobitz type I, "predictable" asynchrony, versus Mobitz type II, "unpredictable" asynchrony) fall in between and require individualized judgment.
- Third-degree (complete) AV block is dangerous not because of its measured rate but because its *effective* heart rate can be functionally zero: without coordination between atrial and ventricular contraction, some or all ventricular "beats" may eject no blood at all, unlike sinus bradycardia at the same measured rate, where every beat is a fully effective, synchronized contraction.
- A finding that has been ruled out as the direct cause of a patient's symptoms (here, sinus bradycardia with confirmed intact AV synchrony) can still be a meaningful clue rather than a dead end — it may mark an underlying, more diffusely diseased heart (e.g., a restrictive cardiomyopathy like ATTR amyloidosis) that contributes to symptoms through a different mechanism entirely, so a technically-exonerated finding should still prompt the question "what is this a marker of?"
- The most valuable physical exam maneuver for an intermittent, currently-absent symptom is to actively try to reproduce it ("reenact the crime") rather than only searching for static fingerprints of disease left behind — orthostatic vital signs performed personally at the bedside, rather than delegated or read off a flowsheet, can reveal a dramatic and diagnostic finding that would otherwise be missed or underweighted.
- Hypotension can be organized into a central category (a blockage at or near the heart — cardiac, pulmonary arterial, or pericardial) versus a vascular category (empty vessels from hypovolemia, or inadequate vasoconstrictive "clamping"); orthostatic hypotension is overwhelmingly a vascular-category phenomenon, but analyzing *why* the orthostatic reflex itself is failing is a distinct and important further question.
- In a normal orthostatic reflex, standing triggers autonomic-driven peripheral vasoconstriction and a compensatory rise in heart rate to offset the blood pressure drop from venous pooling; when blood pressure falls sharply on standing but the heart rate fails to rise appropriately, the lesion is localized to the efferent arm of the autonomic (baroreceptor) reflex — a failure of protective compensation, not merely evidence that hypotension is present.
- A described delta-heart-rate-over-delta-systolic-blood-pressure ratio below roughly 0.5 on standing localizes to autonomic (efferent) failure even in less dramatic, non-"slam dunk" cases than a flat heart rate with a large pressure drop — with the explicit caveat that intrinsic AV conduction disease is a confounding alternative explanation for a blunted heart rate response that this formula does not, by itself, distinguish from autonomic failure.
- Before attributing a blunted heart rate response to autonomic disease, the medication list should be scrutinized for drugs that suppress the reflex directly (beta-blockers, alpha-blockers) and intrinsic AV conduction disease should be excluded (e.g., via a loop recorder or similar); once both are excluded, the finding localizes definitively to a primary autonomic nervous system disorder.
- A useful framework for autonomic dysfunction is to look for "the company the disease keeps": autonomic dysfunction accompanied by CNS signs (e.g., tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia pointing to Parkinson's disease or a Parkinson-plus syndrome with dysautonomia), accompanied by peripheral nervous system signs (e.g., sensory neuropathy and reduced reflexes pointing to diabetic autonomic neuropathy), or isolated (with autoimmune, neurodegenerative, and paraneoplastic subtypes) — isolated autonomic dysfunction should always prompt consideration of amyloidosis (both AL and ATTR subtypes) and Sjögren's syndrome, since both are capable of producing disproportionate dysautonomia with only very subtle accompanying CNS or PNS findings.
- Amyloid light-chain (AL) amyloidosis in particular deserves urgent testing (serum free light chains, SPEP, UPEP) whenever isolated autonomic failure is being worked up, because it can be a genuinely time-sensitive, "sneaky" diagnosis, whereas a slowly evolving neurodegenerative process like early Parkinson's disease may not become clinically apparent, even in retrospect, for years.
- In a patient with both supine hypertension and orthostatic hypotension, aggressive nocturnal pressure natriuresis and diuresis (driven by the elevated supine blood pressure perfusing the kidneys overnight) can cause the patient to become progressively hypovolemic overnight, worsening morning orthostatic hypotension — a pattern that argues for moving antihypertensive medications from daytime to nighttime dosing, so blood pressure is controlled while supine and volume is preserved for the following day's upright activity.
- Midodrine can be used cautiously to treat orthostatic hypotension even in a patient with underlying hypertension and cardiovascular risk factors, given morning-only dosing timed to when the patient is upright and active, and after multidisciplinary buy-in (here, cardiology clearance) given its theoretical risk of worsening supine hypertension and cardiovascular strain.
- Knowing one's limitations and proactively involving subspecialty consultants — not as a last resort, but as a routine part of a thorough workup — reflects good clinical judgment rather than a lack of independence; asking neurology to specifically evaluate for a neurodegenerative cause of autonomic failure, and ID to weigh in on HIV/antiretroviral neuropathy and possible syphilis reinfection, exemplifies targeted, hypothesis-driven consultation rather than a broad, undirected request.

## Problem Representation

A 72-year-old man with a four-year history of recurrent falls and presyncope, previously anchored on a diagnosis of "symptomatic bradycardia" based on a baseline heart rate of 52, whose loop recorder confirms intact AV synchrony (excluding bradycardia as the cause) and whose bedside-reproduced orthostatic vitals (a dramatic blood pressure drop with a flat heart rate response) localize the problem to failure of the efferent arm of the autonomic reflex, ultimately best explained by isolated (versus early neurodegenerative) autonomic failure, managed empirically with nocturnal antihypertensive dosing, morning midodrine, and compression while diagnostic workup for amyloidosis, Sjögren's, and Parkinsonism continues.

## Case Narrative

Reza presented a 72-year-old man coming to the emergency department after several episodes of standing up, feeling lightheaded, and falling, with uncertain loss of consciousness but a clear mind and no head trauma once he regained awareness on the ground. He had carried this same complaint for four years, followed by both cardiology and his primary care doctor, who had settled on a working diagnosis of "symptomatic bradycardia" based on his baseline heart rate of 52; his baseline blood pressure was hypertensive at roughly 150 systolic, with normal temperature, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation.

Rabih began by noting that falls represent an extremely broad differential — spanning the heart, blood vessels, brain, spinal cord, peripheral nerves, neuromuscular junction, joints, and sensory system — but that the positional trigger (falls specifically on standing) pointed toward a cerebral perfusion problem, a hypothesis he said would be reinforced if the patient's gait were normal once he equilibrated after standing. He then used the working "symptomatic bradycardia" diagnosis as a launching point for an extended teaching segment: symptomatic bradycardia, he explained, is not fundamentally a problem of absolute heart rate but of atrioventricular synchrony. If heart rate alone slows with intact AV synchrony, the atria simply have more time to fill the ventricles, which then eject normally — true symptomatic bradycardia from pure sinus bradycardia essentially cannot occur unless the rate falls below roughly 20. He illustrated why third-degree AV block is uniquely dangerous using two patients with an identical measured heart rate of 31: in sinus bradycardia, all 31 beats are effective, synchronized contractions, while in complete heart block, the atria and ventricles are uncoordinated, so some or all of those 31 ventricular contractions may eject no blood at all — the *effective* heart rate can be functionally zero despite an unchanged measured rate. He organized bradyarrhythmias along a synchrony spectrum: complete synchrony (sinus bradycardia, first-degree AV block) essentially never warrants a pacemaker; complete asynchrony (third-degree AV block, sinus arrest) almost always does; and intermediate, partial-synchrony rhythms (Mobitz type I, predictably intermittent asynchrony, versus Mobitz type II, unpredictably intermittent asynchrony) fall in between. He concluded that the first priority was not the patient's heart rate itself, but his degree of AV synchrony.

Reza then revealed that an implantable loop recorder placed roughly a month earlier showed only sinus bradycardia with a brief, asymptomatic run of SVT and no evidence of AV asynchrony — even during a device-triggered symptomatic episode. Rabih reasoned that this confirmed sinus bradycardia was not the direct cause of the patient's symptoms, but cautioned against dismissing it entirely: it could still be a marker of an underlying, more diffusely diseased heart — for example, restrictive cardiomyopathies like ATTR amyloidosis, where a slow heart rate in a poorly compliant, restrictive ventricle further limits an already-inadequate stroke volume — contributing to symptoms through a mechanism distinct from the low rate itself.

Turning to the physical exam, Rabih described his approach as trying to "reproduce the history and see it with your eyes instead of your ears" — actively attempting to reenact the patient's symptoms rather than only searching for static residual findings, illustrating the principle with a recent, unrelated case of dysphagia that only revealed abnormal findings once the patient was actually given an ice chip to swallow. Applying this to the current patient, he proposed having the patient stand and observing directly whether blood pressure dropped in parallel with the reported lightheadedness.

Reza described doing exactly this at the bedside himself, along with his team, rather than delegating the task: with the patient supine, heart rate ran 50-52 and systolic blood pressure was approximately 160-164; on standing, at one minute the heart rate remained 50-51 while systolic blood pressure fell to roughly 80, without the patient reporting significant lightheadedness; the drop persisted at three minutes, and the finding was reproduced on repeat testing. A chart review by the team's intern turned up a similar, smaller-magnitude orthostatic drop (a roughly 40 mmHg systolic decline with a modest heart rate rise from 60 to 68) documented four years earlier, suggesting the pattern had been present, if underappreciated, from early in the patient's four-year course.

Rabih praised the decision to perform the test personally rather than delegate it, and reasoned through the finding's significance: this reframed the falls as concerning orthostatic hypotension. He distinguished the label "orthostatic hypotension" from hypotension in general using the RLR hypotension schema (central versus vascular causes), noting that orthostasis is overwhelmingly a vascular-category phenomenon — but pointed out that the deeper question here was not simply that hypotension existed, but why the body's normal protective orthostatic reflex had failed to compensate for it. In a healthy reflex, standing triggers autonomic-driven peripheral vasoconstriction and a compensatory heart rate increase to offset venous pooling; this patient's heart rate failed to rise at all despite a dramatic pressure drop, localizing the lesion to the efferent arm of that autonomic (baroreceptor) reflex — a failure either of the autonomic nervous system itself, of the heart's ability to respond (intrinsic AV conduction disease), or of medications suppressing the reflex.

Reza confirmed no autonomic-suppressing medications (beta-blockers, alpha-blockers) were on the patient's list, and that the loop recorder had already excluded AV desynchrony, leaving the autonomic nervous system as the primary suspect. He introduced a formula from a NEJM review on autonomic failure: a delta-heart-rate-over-delta-systolic-blood-pressure ratio below roughly 0.5 on standing localizes the problem to the efferent arm of the baroreceptor reflex, useful for confirming autonomic failure even in less dramatic cases than this one — with the caveat, not addressed in that article, that intrinsic AV conduction disease is a confounder the formula alone cannot rule out.

With the lesion localized to the autonomic nervous system, Rabih proposed a "company the disease keeps" framework: autonomic dysfunction accompanied by CNS findings (tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, suggesting Parkinson's disease or a Parkinson-plus syndrome with dysautonomia), accompanied by peripheral nervous system findings (sensory neuropathy, reduced reflexes, suggesting diabetic autonomic neuropathy), or isolated — with isolated autonomic dysfunction warranting careful consideration of autoimmune, neurodegenerative, and paraneoplastic causes, and an explicit "back pocket" warning to always keep amyloidosis (AL and ATTR) and Sjögren's syndrome in mind, since both can produce disproportionate dysautonomia with only very subtle accompanying findings.

Reza reported that the patient denied other autonomic symptoms (bowel, bladder, sweating, pupillary changes) but had severe restless leg syndrome, treated with ropinirole and gabapentin and followed by neurology; his past medical history also included well-controlled HIV on antiretroviral therapy with a fully suppressed viral load and excellent CD4 count, a remote history of treated secondary syphilis with low RPR titers, and no diabetes (normal A1c). Serum free light chains, SPEP, and UPEP were sent and returned negative. Rabih used the restless leg syndrome as an entry point into the world of REM sleep behavior disorders and Parkinson's-spectrum disease, recommending close examination for subtle bradykinesia or rigidity even though the patient was already followed by neurology, since such findings can be easy to miss.

Reza described consulting neurology specifically to evaluate for a neurodegenerative cause of the autonomic failure; they commended the workup and reported no signal for Parkinson's disease or a Parkinson-plus syndrome like multiple system atrophy. Rabih responded that, in his view, the amyloid testing was the only genuinely time-sensitive test in this workup — amyloidosis can be "sneaky" — while a slowly evolving neurodegenerative process like early Parkinson's disease might not declare itself, even in retrospect, for years; he referenced a personal case from his training at Johns Hopkins in which orthostatic hypotension was the presenting manifestation of AL amyloidosis as the origin of this pattern recognition. Reza also described consulting infectious disease colleague Kevin Okuda about whether HIV, antiretroviral therapy, or syphilis reinfection (tabes dorsalis) could explain the autonomic findings; the answer was that a link was plausible in principle but poorly supported by the literature and would remain a diagnosis of exclusion.

Rather than waiting for the full diagnostic picture to resolve, management proceeded in parallel. Reza described a "bombshell pearl" driving the medication changes: patients with both orthostatic hypotension and supine hypertension often experience aggressive nocturnal pressure natriuresis and diuresis, becoming progressively hypovolemic overnight and thereby worsening their orthostatic hypotension the following morning. Based on this, the patient's losartan and tamsulosin were both moved from daytime to nighttime dosing, and midodrine was added in the morning, timed to when the patient was upright and active; cardiology was specifically consulted and cleared this despite theoretical concerns about worsening supine hypertension and cardiovascular risk given his HIV and hypertension history. Waist-high compression stockings and an abdominal binder, as tolerated, were added as well. The combined regimen dramatically improved the orthostatic blood pressure delta from roughly 80-90 mmHg down to 20-40 mmHg. Rabih connected this new understanding directly to a longstanding personal clinical puzzle — why some of his own Parkinson's patients remain orthostatic despite aggressive oral fluid intake — recognizing nocturnal pressure natriuresis as a likely explanation he had not previously considered.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Symptomatic bradycardia anchoring bias: a low resting heart rate (52) drove a years-long working diagnosis without confirming the actual mechanistic requirement for symptomatic bradycardia — loss of AV synchrony — allowing the true cause (autonomic failure) to go undiagnosed for four years until a loop recorder excluded AV asynchrony and bedside orthostatic testing was actually performed.

Isolated autonomic failure illness script: falls or presyncope on standing, in the absence of AV conduction disease or autonomic-suppressing medications, with a blunted or absent compensatory heart-rate response to a significant orthostatic blood pressure drop, localizes to the efferent arm of the autonomic reflex — the differential then organizes around "company the disease keeps" (CNS-associated, PNS-associated, or isolated), with amyloidosis and Sjögren's syndrome as high-yield, easy-to-miss causes of isolated autonomic failure that warrant proactive testing given their potential severity and subtlety.

The case also models a bias-correction habit: Rabih's own retrospective realization — that nocturnal pressure natriuresis explains a pattern he had puzzled over for years in his own Parkinson's patients — shows a clinician updating a long-held, unresolved clinical question in real time based on a colleague's case, rather than treating existing practice patterns as settled.

## Teaching Discussion

The central teaching point is that symptomatic bradycardia should be understood through the lens of atrioventricular synchrony rather than absolute heart rate — a low heart rate with preserved AV synchrony is very unlikely to be symptomatic, while a "normal-looking" heart rate with complete AV asynchrony (third-degree block) can be functionally near-zero and dangerous.

A second teaching point is the diagnostic power of personally reproducing a patient's symptoms at the bedside rather than relying on documented history or delegated exams — orthostatic vitals performed by the treating physician directly revealed a dramatic, previously underappreciated finding that had likely been present for years.

A third teaching point is the "company the disease keeps" framework for autonomic dysfunction (CNS-associated, PNS-associated, or isolated), paired with an explicit reminder to keep amyloidosis and Sjögren's syndrome in mind for isolated autonomic failure given how subtly and dangerously they can present.

The final, most practically applicable teaching point is the nocturnal-pressure-natriuresis mechanism connecting supine hypertension to worsening morning orthostatic hypotension, and the resulting management strategy of moving antihypertensives to nighttime dosing while adding morning midodrine and compression — a pattern explicitly framed as broadly applicable well beyond this specific patient.
