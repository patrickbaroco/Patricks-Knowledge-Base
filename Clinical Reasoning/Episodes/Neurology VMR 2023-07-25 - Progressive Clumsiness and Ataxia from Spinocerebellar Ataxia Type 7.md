## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> An informal, monthly-format **Neurology VMR**, taught by **Aaron Berkowitz** after a travel hiatus, presented by Maria with discussants Andrea (a neurology residency applicant) and Sridhara (a stroke neurologist). A 72-year-old woman presents with a 20-year history of progressive gait clumsiness (cane, then wheelchair), hand incoordination, and more recent dysarthria, with reduced strength and length-dependent sensory loss on exam alongside prominent limb and gait ataxia, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, and scanning speech — but no nystagmus. A striking multigenerational family history, including a five-year-old great-grand-nephew already wheelchair-bound, points to an autosomal dominant hereditary cerebellar ataxia despite the very slow adult-onset course. Genetic testing identifies 39 CAG repeats in *ATXN7*, confirming **spinocerebellar ataxia type 7 (SCA7)** — a trinucleotide-repeat disorder classically associated with retinal degeneration, here presenting in its milder, later-onset, ocular-sparing adult phenotype, with genetic anticipation explaining the much earlier, more severe presentation in a young relative.

## Pearls

- **"Clumsiness" is a patient-generated word that needs decoding into a specific neurologic function** — it most often means weakness, numbness/sensory loss, or ataxia/incoordination (and occasionally apraxia), and structured follow-up questions (does the hand feel weak, numb, or is it just uncoordinated?) are needed to figure out which.
- **Apraxia is usually reported by a spouse or caregiver, not the patient** — patients themselves tend to describe the deficit vaguely ("it's not working right") rather than naming the specific loss of learned, skilled movement that apraxia represents.
- **Gait involves nearly every neurologic system at once** (strength, sensation/proprioception, coordination, vestibular function, vision, and higher-order gait initiation/patterning circuits), so gait disorders in older patients are usually multifactorial — the diagnostic task is often to identify which "slice of the pie" is large enough, and reversible enough, to be worth treating.
- **The "shower sign"** — worsening imbalance with eyes closed, in the dark, on a slippery surface — classically points to sensory ataxia (Rombergism, from impaired proprioception) rather than cerebellar ataxia, which worsens only modestly with eye closure.
- **A slowly progressive, decades-long neurologic syndrome can still be genetic, even without an obvious childhood-onset presentation** — repeat-expansion disorders in particular can present very late in life, sometimes unmasked by an incidental medication effect (e.g., statin-induced myopathy revealing an underlying genetic muscle disease).
- **A pedigree that doesn't skip generations, with both sexes affected, suggests autosomal dominant inheritance** — and in trinucleotide-repeat disorders, look for genetic anticipation: earlier and more severe onset in successive generations, as seen here with a five-year-old already in a wheelchair.
- **Whole exome sequencing can miss trinucleotide/nucleotide repeat-expansion disorders** — when a repeat disorder (like a spinocerebellar ataxia) is specifically suspected, a targeted repeat-expansion panel is needed rather than relying on exome sequencing alone.

## Problem Representation

A 72-year-old woman with a 20-year history of progressive gait dysfunction (initially subtle unsteadiness and falls, progressing from cane to wheelchair use), more recent hand incoordination and dysarthria over the last five years, and a striking multigenerational family history of a similar wheelchair-bound course (including a five-year-old great-grand-nephew already affected) presents with generalized reduced muscle bulk, mild weakness, normal reflexes, length-dependent sensory loss to light touch and pinprick, and prominent bilateral limb and gait ataxia with dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, intention tremor, and scanning dysarthria — without nystagmus. Prior MRI (15 years old) showed only mild cerebellar atrophy. A hereditary ataxia gene panel identifies 39 CAG repeats in *ATXN7*, confirming spinocerebellar ataxia type 7, an autosomal dominant trinucleotide-repeat disorder classically associated with retinal degeneration that, at this repeat length and adult onset, has so far spared her vision and eye movements while producing a slowly progressive pancerebellar syndrome.

## Case Narrative

This is an informal, monthly-format **Neurology VMR**, resuming after a travel-related hiatus, hosted by Aaron Berkowitz — introducing himself as a general neurologist and neurohospitalist at UCSF — with Maria presenting a case she and colleagues managed, and two volunteer discussants: Sridhara, a stroke neurologist from Jefferson in Philadelphia, and Andrea, an international medical graduate from Venezuela currently doing a white-matter-imaging research fellowship in Colorado and applying to neurology residency alongside Maria.

**Chief concern (one word): clumsiness.** Berkowitz begins by asking Andrea what "clumsiness" could mean to a patient before any further history, and she immediately identifies the ambiguity — does it mean trouble walking, disorientation, spinning, or something else — before landing on impaired motor function or dexterity as her leading interpretation. Sridhara adds ataxia to the list, framing clumsiness conceptually as a mismatch between an intended motor action and its execution, arising from either the corticospinal tract (weakness) or the spinocerebellar tract and its connections (ataxia). Berkowitz consolidates this into three main interpretations patients may mean by "clumsy": weakness (a motor problem anywhere from brain to muscle), numbness (a sensory problem depriving the cerebellum and motor system of the feedback needed to guide movement, as classically seen with carpal tunnel syndrome causing "clumsy" dropped objects from sensory loss alone, not weakness), or ataxia/incoordination (classically cerebellar, though not all ataxia is cerebellar — sensory ataxia from impaired proprioception is a key mimic). He adds apraxia as a fourth, subtler possibility, noting it is usually reported by a caregiver rather than the patient, who instead describes the deficit vaguely. The initial branch point, then, is: is this motor, sensory, or cerebellar (or apraxic) — and, as always, what is the time course?

**History: a 20-year, slowly progressive course.** The patient is a 72-year-old woman with a 20-year history of gait difficulty, initially manifesting as unexplained clumsiness and falls while walking normally (with others remarking on her tripping before she herself recognized a problem), progressing over the last decade from cane to wheelchair dependence. She also reports hand clumsiness — particularly washing dishes and dressing — and, more recently (over the last five years), difficulty with speech, alongside a need for assistance with most activities of daily living; she denies sensory abnormalities or dysphagia. Andrea reasons toward a degenerative cerebellar disorder given the chronic, progressive course and the combination of gait and hand clumsiness, while also considering peripheral neuropathy, motor neuron disease, or a primary muscle disorder, and asks about pain and an inciting trigger (raising, by contrast, the more relapsing-remitting course typical of multiple sclerosis). Berkowitz uses this to teach a broader lesson on gait disorders: gait requires the integration of strength, sensation/proprioception, coordination, vestibular function, vision, and higher-order gait-initiation circuitry (e.g., basal ganglia/frontal circuits, as in Parkinsonism or normal-pressure-hydrocephalus-type frontal gait disorder), so elderly patients typically have multifactorial gait disorders — a "pie chart" of small, often irreversible contributors (mild neuropathy, spine disease, visual loss, some Parkinsonism) — and the diagnostic task is identifying whether any single slice is large enough, and reversible enough, to be worth intervening on. Asked what the single most common "mundane" cause of a progressive gait disorder with new hand involvement would be in this age group, the discussants suggest Parkinson's disease and cervical spine disease (cervical myelopathy); Berkowitz notes that cervical disease often evolves more subacutely once it reaches a tipping point and should produce dorsal-column sensory symptoms, and that the emerging dysarthria and complete absence of sensory complaints argue against it here, even as "common things being common" would normally favor combinations of spine disease, neuropathy, and mild Parkinsonism.

**Building toward the cerebellum, with sensory ataxia as an active mimic to rule out.** Both discussants gravitate toward a cerebellar process, reinforced by the emerging dysarthria (Sridhara notes dysarthria specifically pulled his intuition toward the cerebellar vermis). Sridhara raises a clinical heuristic — the **"shower sign"**: patients who are dramatically worse with their eyes closed (as in a dim, wet shower) are more likely to have a sensory ataxia (Rombergism, from loss of large-fiber proprioceptive feedback via the peripheral nerve, dorsal root ganglia, or dorsal columns) than a cerebellar ataxia, which worsens with eye closure only modestly. Berkowitz elaborates with a story from a late mentor, Marty Samuels, about "bathroom neurology" — the shower as a natural Romberg test — and notes this patient hasn't explicitly reported sensory symptoms, though that doesn't rule them out. He also flags that genetic diseases, though usually thought of as presenting in childhood, can present very late in life due to variable penetrance or germline mutation, sometimes unmasked by an incidental exposure (e.g., a statin unmasking a previously subclinical genetic myopathy).

**A striking family history reframes the case.** Past medical history is notable only for hypertension and degenerative disc disease, with no relevant surgeries; medications are labetalol and atorvastatin. Family history, however, is dramatic: multiple family members have required wheelchairs, including a five-year-old great-grand-nephew, and possibly her own grandfather, who was "clumsy" and used a walker. Both discussants immediately favor a hereditary cerebellar ataxia (spinocerebellar ataxia), with Andrea also raising hereditary spastic paraplegia as an alternative hereditary gait disorder (typically affecting the corticospinal pathways in the cord to produce a pure or complex spastic paraparesis, sometimes with additional cerebellar features in complex subtypes). Reviewing a family pedigree (both sexes affected, disease present in multiple generations without skipping), the discussants reason through inheritance patterns — Sridhara initially favoring autosomal recessive from the number of people affected, then correcting to autosomal dominant once Andrea points out the disease does not skip generations, which Berkowitz, candidly deferring to their more recent genetics training, accepts.

**Exam: a pancerebellar syndrome, but weakness and sensory loss too.** Berkowitz first has the discussants predict expected cerebellar findings before the exam is revealed: central (direction-changing) nystagmus, scanning dysarthria, and both truncal and limb ataxia, with additional features depending on the specific genetic etiology. On exam, the patient is wheelchair-bound with leftward drift requiring repositioning; mental status, language, and cranial nerves are largely intact, though speech is slow with unusual syllable separation and variable intonation, and no nystagmus is present. Reduced muscle bulk and mild (3/5) weakness are noted diffusely, with normal, symmetric reflexes (only ankle jerks slightly reduced) and downgoing toes. There is length-dependent sensory loss to light touch and pinprick in the lower extremities (foot to mid-shin) with normal vibration, proprioception, and temperature. Cerebellar testing shows dysmetria and intention tremor on finger-to-nose (side-to-side oscillation and overshoot worsening as the target is approached, unaffected by eye closure), dysdiadochokinesia on rapid alternating movements, and fragmentation of heel-to-shin testing; gait and Romberg could not be formally assessed given her wheelchair dependence. Berkowitz walks through why this is unambiguously cerebellar — the intention tremor (an action tremor that specifically worsens with precision demands, distinct from ordinary action or physiologic tremor), the described "finger-chase" and air-square-drawing bedside tests for dysmetria, and dysdiadochokinesia — while explicitly naming what doesn't fit and needs separate explanation: the absent nystagmus (possibly a subtle/early finding, or reflecting relative sparing of the vestibulocerebellar flocculonodular lobe in this specific genetic disease), the weakness and reduced muscle bulk (attributed to disuse and deconditioning after years of wheelchair dependence rather than a primary motor problem, since reflexes remain normal), and the length-dependent sensory loss (possibly an early spinocerebellar-tract component of the underlying genetic disease, or simply a separate, common, age-related peripheral neuropathy). Berkowitz explicitly flags the risk in this reasoning: "I was also anchored on the cerebellum and tossing away the things that don't fit... though that can be a dangerous exercise" — naming the practice of explaining away non-fitting findings as something to do carefully, not reflexively.

**A brief detour: what if this weren't genetic?** Berkowitz pauses to ask what a similar pancerebellar syndrome evolving over months, rather than 20 years, without a family history, would suggest — surfacing paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration (anti-Yo, anti-Ri, anti-Ma2 antibodies, associated with ovarian/breast cancer in women and testicular cancer in men), non-paraneoplastic autoimmune causes (gluten ataxia, anti-GAD antibodies, the latter prone to low-titer false positives), nutritional causes (vitamin B12 or vitamin E deficiency, nitrous oxide/"whippet" toxicity producing a functional B12 deficiency and subacute combined degeneration), and JC virus granule cell neuronopathy (a rare, atrophy-predominant cerebellar JC virus variant). Sridhara offers a personal heuristic: ataxia plus neuropathy tends to suggest recessive ataxia syndromes, while ataxia plus spasticity or hyperreflexia tends to suggest dominant ataxia syndromes — a pattern he notes is imperfect given the constantly expanding list of both categories.

**Imaging and genetic confirmation.** Predicting the neuroimaging before it's described, the group expects cerebellar atrophy (best appreciated on sagittal MRI, showing prominent superior cerebellar folia) with incidental, age-related chronic microvascular white matter changes not to be mistaken for a separate disease process. The patient's only available MRI, performed 15 years earlier after she grew frustrated with repeated normal exams and stopped seeking further testing, showed only mild cerebellar atrophy. She eventually returns solely for genetic testing once multiple family members were affected; a hereditary ataxia panel identifies **39 CAG repeats in the *ATXN7* gene**, confirming **spinocerebellar ataxia type 7 (SCA7)**. Maria explains that SCA7 is one of the rarer spinocerebellar ataxias and is classically associated with retinal or macular degeneration in addition to cerebellar dysfunction, but with a repeat count in the smaller pathogenic range (as here), the phenotype tends to be milder, later-onset, and slower-progressing, with ocular/retinal findings — and the nystagmus Berkowitz had expected — often not yet manifest, in contrast to the larger-repeat, pediatric-onset phenotype, which presents more abruptly with retinal disease often prominent enough to be caught by an ophthalmologist first. Berkowitz notes he wouldn't have known to specifically request repeat-expansion testing without this family history, and Sridhara adds an important caveat: whole exome sequencing, while cost-effective for identifying causes of hereditary ataxia broadly, characteristically misses trinucleotide/nucleotide repeat-expansion disorders, which require dedicated repeat-expansion panels — a lesson Sridhara notes he learned from being "burned" by a missed diagnosis previously. Berkowitz recounts a similar personal case of adrenomyeloneuropathy (a variant of adrenoleukodystrophy) eventually diagnosed by whole exome sequencing after years of unrevealing targeted workup, illustrating the value of an unbiased genetic approach once a genetic cause is suspected but the specific gene is not obvious.

**Anticipation and geography.** The five-year-old great-grand-nephew already using a wheelchair is explained by **genetic anticipation** — the CAG repeat tends to expand further in successive generations of trinucleotide-repeat disorders, producing earlier and more severe disease in children than in their affected parents or grandparents. Asked about the family's ancestry, Maria reports the family is African American, based in the US, noting that SCA7 has been more classically reported in Northern European populations — an observation offered as an interesting epidemiologic aside rather than a diagnostic anchor, underscoring that rare genetic diseases are not confined to their most classically reported ancestral populations.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Explaining away exceptions, named as a risk rather than hidden.** Berkowitz explicitly states, "I was also anchored on the cerebellum and tossing away the things that don't fit... though that can be a dangerous exercise" — candid **[[Diagnostic Humility|diagnostic humility]]** about a reasoning move (explaining away non-fitting findings) that is often necessary but can just as easily paper over a second, coexisting diagnosis.
- **"Predict before you look," reused twice in one case.** Berkowitz has the discussants predict both the expected exam findings (central nystagmus, scanning dysarthria, limb/truncal ataxia) and the expected imaging (cerebellar atrophy pattern, incidental white matter change) before either is revealed — the same **[[Test Interpretation|pretest-calibration]]** habit recurring across all seven prior built episodes.
- **A heuristic offered, then immediately hedged by its own author.** Sridhara's "ataxia plus neuropathy suggests recessive; ataxia plus spasticity suggests dominant" rule of thumb is presented candidly alongside its own limitation (a constantly expanding list of both categories) — modeling how useful clinical heuristics should be held loosely rather than applied as fixed rules.
- **Correcting an initial answer in real time, without defensiveness.** Sridhara initially favors autosomal recessive inheritance from the pedigree, then updates to autosomal dominant once Andrea points out the disease doesn't skip generations — an unguarded example of belief revision happening live, modeled without any face-saving hedge.
- **A negative test result reframed rather than treated as fully reassuring.** The absence of nystagmus initially seems to argue against the group's cerebellar leading hypothesis, but is subsequently explained by the specific genetic phenotype (smaller repeat count, milder adult-onset SCA7) rather than being allowed to silently undermine the diagnosis — an example of actively reconciling a discordant finding rather than ignoring it.
- **A testing-modality blind spot named from firsthand experience.** Sridhara's point that whole exome sequencing can miss trinucleotide repeat expansions, learned from being "burned" previously, is a concrete instance of **[[Test Interpretation|test characteristics]]** mattering as much as test selection — the right gene can still be missed by the wrong assay.

## Teaching Discussion

- Decode "clumsiness" into weakness, sensory loss, ataxia, or (less often reported directly by the patient) apraxia before building a differential.
- Recognize gait as an integration of strength, sensation, coordination, vestibular function, vision, and higher-order gait-initiation circuitry, and expect multifactorial gait disorders in older patients rather than a single unifying cause.
- Use the "shower sign" (marked worsening with eyes closed, in the dark, on a wet surface) to favor sensory ataxia (Rombergism) over cerebellar ataxia, which worsens only modestly with eye closure.
- Don't exclude a genetic disease based on very late-life onset alone — repeat-expansion and other genetic disorders can present in the 60s-70s, sometimes unmasked by an incidental medication effect.
- Use pedigree analysis (skipped vs. non-skipped generations, sex distribution) to distinguish autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, and X-linked inheritance patterns, and recognize genetic anticipation (earlier, more severe disease in successive generations) as a hallmark of trinucleotide-repeat disorders.
- Know the core cerebellar exam findings (central/direction-changing nystagmus, scanning/ataxic dysarthria, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, intention tremor, gait and truncal ataxia, and — more subtly — reduced tone with increased limb swing) and localize limb-predominant vs. gait/truncal-predominant findings to hemispheric vs. vermian cerebellar involvement respectively.
- Build the subacute pancerebellar differential (paraneoplastic antibody syndromes, non-paraneoplastic autoimmune causes like gluten or anti-GAD ataxia, B12/vitamin E deficiency, nitrous oxide toxicity, JC virus granule cell neuronopathy) as the contrast case to a genetic/degenerative chronic cerebellar syndrome.
- Remember that whole exome sequencing, despite its efficiency for broad genetic ataxia workups, can miss trinucleotide/nucleotide repeat-expansion disorders — order a dedicated repeat-expansion panel when a repeat disorder is specifically suspected.
- Recognize spinocerebellar ataxia type 7 specifically for its classic retinal/macular degeneration association, its phenotype-repeat-length correlation (smaller repeats: milder, later-onset, more ocular-sparing course; larger repeats: severe, pediatric-onset, retinal-disease-prominent course), and that it is not confined to its most classically reported (Northern European) ancestral populations.
