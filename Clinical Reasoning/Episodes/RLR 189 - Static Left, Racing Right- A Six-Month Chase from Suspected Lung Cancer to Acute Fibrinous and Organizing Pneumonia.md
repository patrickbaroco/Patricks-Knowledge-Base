## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Reza presents a case he managed as a hospitalist that unfolded over roughly six months, opening with a nod to the show's modest download numbers and a callback bit about "Profrez" leaving things for "after tomorrow." A 70-year-old man with CKD and a heavy occupational asbestos/diesel exposure history presents with subacute, well-localized pleuritic chest pain and is found on CT to have two mass-like pulmonary consolidations — one in each lower lobe. He nearly faints on hearing the word "cancer," having watched former railroad colleagues die of it. An efficient VA outpatient oncology pathway (biopsy in two weeks, PET in three, tumor board in four) returns 47 negative biopsies from both lesions, only to reveal a puzzle: on repeat imaging the left lesion has stayed put while the right has grown dramatically and cavitated. That divergence forces Robbie to reject a single "bilateral mass-like consolidation" problem representation in favor of two distinct processes — a static left-sided finding and a rapidly evolving, necrotic right-sided one. A relapse with fever, weight loss, and rising inflammatory markers eventually yields a working diagnosis of acute fibrinous and organizing pneumonia (AFOP), an aggressive, cavitating variant of cryptogenic organizing pneumonia, confirmed by a brisk and sustained response to prednisone. Reza closes with a reflection on treating clinicians' own diagnostic misses with self-compassion, and Robbie draws a parallel to a documentary about Liverpool's 2005 Champions League comeback and the maxim that you learn more from losing than winning.

## Pearls

- Focal, reproducible pleuritic chest pain is usually reassuring because most serious causes of chest pain (ACS, aortic dissection, pericarditis) can't be pointed to — but pleuritis itself is the exception, since it is a genuinely localizing finding and often signals real pathology abutting the pleura.
- A textbook-sounding "pneumonia" story loses diagnostic traction fast when the tempo is wrong: ten days of symptoms and a normal white count are inconsistent with typical bacterial pneumonia and should lower the threshold to move straight to a contrasted CT rather than anchoring on a chest x-ray.
- When breaking news of a possible malignancy, resist applying a single universal script (e.g., "treat others as you'd want to be treated"); the more advanced move is to let the patient's own reaction and questions set the pace and depth of the conversation.
- A well-resourced outpatient system can make admission unnecessary even for a patient with concerning imaging: the decision to admit should hinge on whether the patient can reliably execute the outpatient workup, not on how worrisome the imaging looks.
- A single abnormal finding is never proof there isn't a second, silent one elsewhere — always take an "organ inventory" survey before locking onto a unifying diagnosis, since the body's failure to report symptoms from every affected site doesn't mean every site is spared.
- Extensive tissue sampling (47 biopsies here) that repeatedly comes back negative should shift your thinking away from "we must have missed it" (sampling bias) and toward "this mass may genuinely be composed of inflammation rather than malignant cells."
- Two lesions that look similar on a single scan can behave completely differently over time; don't collapse a static, unchanging lesion and a rapidly growing, cavitating one into a single problem representation — their divergent trajectories are themselves diagnostic information and usually mean two different processes are at play.
- Counterintuitively, the more aggressively a lesion grows, the easier it often becomes to diagnose — rapid, dramatic change forces the process to declare itself, whereas indolent processes can stay cryptic indefinitely.
- Inflammation found on pathology without malignant cells splits into two broad buckets worth checking systematically: physiologic (the body walling off an infection — regionally, coccidioidomycosis deserves the same "can't let the sun set on it" urgency that tuberculosis gets elsewhere) versus pathologic (autoimmune, such as GPA, or a malignancy hiding behind its own reactive inflammatory infiltrate, as lymphoma can do).
- When a presumed inflammatory lung process behaves atypically (rapid growth, cavitation) for its presumed diagnosis, consider that it may represent a more aggressive variant on the same disease spectrum rather than a wrong diagnosis altogether — and a brisk, sustained radiographic and symptomatic response to steroids can retrospectively serve as confirmatory evidence.

## Problem Representation

A 70-year-old man with stage 3A CKD, hypertension, and a 30-year occupational history of asbestos and diesel exhaust exposure presented with ten days of dyspnea, diaphoresis, and well-localized right-sided pleuritic chest pain with pink-tinged sputum, in the setting of a normal exam, oxygen saturation, CBC, and CMP, and was found on CT to have two mass-like pulmonary consolidations — a right lower lobe pleural-based lesion with surrounding ground-glass opacity and a left lower lobe lesion — that on serial imaging over the following weeks diverged sharply, with the left lesion remaining stable while the right lesion grew substantially and cavitated, ultimately proving, after 47 biopsies and extensive infectious and autoimmune workup were unrevealing, to represent acute fibrinous and organizing pneumonia that responded to corticosteroids.

## Case Narrative

The patient, a 70-year-old man with stage 3A chronic kidney disease and hypertension, presented to the emergency department with ten days of dyspnea and diaphoresis, along with right-sided pleuritic chest pain and a cough productive of pink-tinged sputum. His physical exam was unremarkable, his oxygen saturation was normal, and both his CBC and CMP were normal.

Robbie's initial framing centered on the localizing power of pleuritic pain: unlike most serious causes of chest pain, which patients typically cannot point to with a finger, pleuritis is genuinely focal, and its presence usually signals real inflammation abutting the pleura — often from an infectious process such as pneumonia. A chest x-ray would ordinarily be a reasonable first test, and a consolidation on x-ray might have been sufficient to close the loop if the story matched typical pneumonia. But the ten-day duration and the normal white count didn't fit that pattern, weakening confidence in a straightforward infectious pneumonia and raising the concern that any consolidation seen might actually represent something else, such as a pulmonary embolism with infarction. That mismatch between tempo and inflammatory markers argued for moving to a contrasted CT rather than stopping at a plain film — a decision reinforced by the fact that the patient had already had a chest x-ray a week earlier that had prompted this evaluation.

CT imaging revealed two abnormalities: a large, pleural-based, mass-like consolidation in the right lower lobe with surrounding ground-glass opacity, and a separate large mass-like consolidation in the left lower lobe, without pleural effusion or significant lymphadenopathy on initial review. Asked about admission, Robbie reasoned that admission is warranted primarily to deliver urgent, inpatient-only treatment, and this patient had no such acute need — the priority was expedited diagnostic workup, which could reasonably happen as an outpatient if the patient had the social support and system resources to complete it. Because a mass-like consolidation raises concern for malignancy, but can equally reflect infection, the plan was to broaden the workup with further history, labs, and imaging, understanding that the patient's own symptoms had only revealed one side of the problem (he had no idea the left-sided lesion existed) — underscoring the need for a fuller "organ inventory" before committing to a single diagnosis, and that in the absence of a microbiological explanation, tissue sampling would likely be required.

The patient's social history added weight to the malignancy concern: he had never smoked tobacco but used marijuana regularly in the past and currently used THC gummies; more strikingly, he had spent thirty years in the railroad industry as a conductor and in maintenance and brake service, with substantial unprotected exposure to asbestos and diesel exhaust, and he reported that several former colleagues had gone on to develop lung cancer. When the team raised the possibility that the imaging findings could represent cancer, the patient fainted, and on waking, explained his fear by referencing his colleagues' history. Robbie reflected that this reaction — far more dramatic than the more blunted responses often seen in older, more "hardened" patients — was itself informative, suggesting a patient who perceived himself as fundamentally healthy and was catching the news off guard, which made a metastatic malignancy diagnosis feel slightly discordant with his overall picture. Reza used the moment to make a broader point about communication: rather than applying a single default approach to breaking difficult news, it's more effective to let the patient's own reaction and follow-up questions guide how much information to share and how directly.

Given reassuring vitals, negative troponins, a normal EKG, and no acute indications, the emergency department discharged the patient with close outpatient follow-up through shared decision-making. What followed impressed both hosts: an e-consult to pulmonology led to a bronchoscopic biopsy within two weeks, a PET-CT within three weeks, and a multidisciplinary pulmonary-thoracic oncology conference within four weeks — a pace Robbie and Reza held up as an example of what a well-resourced outpatient system can accomplish for a patient who doesn't strictly need to be hospitalized to get answers quickly.

Comparing the PET-CT (and a subsequent CTA) to the original CT proved pivotal. The left lower lobe lesion looked essentially unchanged. The right lower lobe lesion, however, had grown substantially and now showed cavitation. Robbie's read of this divergence was that it fundamentally changed the problem: rather than a single "bilateral mass-like consolidation," the case now needed to be understood as two distinct processes — a static, unchanging left-sided lesion, and a dynamic, rapidly progressive, necrotic right-sided one — and it would be an "unfair" oversimplification to keep lumping them together in a single problem representation. He entertained the possibility that the right-sided lesion might represent a missed, evolving pulmonary infarct given its pleural-based, "wet" appearance, while wondering whether the left-sided lesion might be a more indolent, localized malignancy. He also offered a broader heuristic: the more aggressively a lesion behaves, the faster it typically declares itself — and, perversely, the easier it often becomes to diagnose.

At two weeks, the patient underwent robotic bronchoscopy with extensive sampling — 47 biopsies of the right middle lobe and left lower lobe lesions, plus endobronchial ultrasound-guided sampling of nearby lymph nodes. Every sample was negative for malignancy, and fungal stains and cultures were negative as well. Pathology described an area of organizing pneumonia with fibroblast foci and evidence of acute and chronic inflammation. HIV testing was negative, as were fungal antigen studies. Robbie's interpretation was that with a sample size this large, the usual worry about sampling bias largely fell away — this really did appear to be a mass composed predominantly of inflammation rather than an accumulation of a specific malignant cell population. That reframing prompted a systematic look at why the tissue might be so inflamed: physiologic inflammation, meant to contain an occult infection (with coccidioidomycosis flagged as a must-not-miss possibility given the patient's regional exposure risk, deserving the same urgency as tuberculosis would carry elsewhere), versus pathologic inflammation, either autoimmune (with granulomatosis with polyangiitis specifically named as capable of presenting this indolently in the lungs while staying silent systemically) or from a "liquid" malignancy such as lymphoma recruiting a dense inflammatory infiltrate around itself. Additional labs at this stage — ANA, ANCA, LDH, CBC, creatinine, and liver chemistries — were all normal or at the patient's baseline, and fungal studies remained negative, narrowing but not resolving the differential. Robbie suggested that, absent a clear answer, the "test of time" together with a full organ survey would likely be the most informative next step.

About one month after the initial presentation, and just days after the multidisciplinary tumor board had met (without the patient present) to formulate a plan, the patient was admitted with worsening respiratory symptoms: persistent cough, small-volume hemoptysis, right-sided pleuritic chest pain, new dyspnea, night sweats, and home-measured fevers to 101°F, alongside a twenty-pound weight loss over the preceding two months. Labs now showed a neutrophil-predominant leukocytosis of roughly 13,000, an ESR of 69, and a CRP of 135 — with repeat imaging matching what the PET-CT had already shown: continued growth and cavitation on the right, stability on the left. Robbie's read was that this now looked ominous enough to warrant treating empirically for a superimposed community-acquired pneumonia layered on already-vulnerable tissue, with the understanding that failure to improve would increase the likelihood that another tissue diagnosis was needed and support the idea that the underlying process really was pathologic inflammation.

During that admission, an extensive infectious workup was repeated, including a tuberculosis rule-out with negative MTB PCR and AFB stains. The patient completed seven days of ceftriaxone and doxycycline for presumed community-acquired pneumonia, after which his white count normalized and his hemoptysis resolved. At that point, an empiric diagnosis was made — one the team was cautious about given two atypical features: the unusually rapid radiographic growth and the cavitation. Robbie, working through the differential blind to the actual answer, converged on an isolated pulmonary autoimmune process, most likely a variant of organizing pneumonia, given the bilateral, plural-based, multifocal consolidative pattern; he flagged as atypical that the process had not migrated (as cryptogenic organizing pneumonia classically does) and that cavitation, while described in the literature, is uncommon for that diagnosis, also recalling (without being able to name precisely) the reversed halo/atoll sign associated with organizing pneumonia on imaging.

The actual diagnosis, once the multidisciplinary pulmonology team weighed in, was a more aggressive variant on that same spectrum: acute fibrinous and organizing pneumonia (AFOP), a pattern distinguished from classic cryptogenic organizing pneumonia by its more rapid radiographic progression and its capacity to cavitate, but similarly steroid-responsive. Tuberculosis having been excluded, the patient was started on prednisone 60 mg daily along with Bactrim for pneumocystis prophylaxis and pantoprazole for gastric protection; his course was complicated by steroid-induced hyperglycemia, which was managed, and his respiratory symptoms improved enough for discharge.

At the first pulmonology follow-up, roughly nine weeks after the initial presentation, his night sweats had resolved, though he continued to produce reddish-brown sputum, and he remained on treatment for steroid-induced hyperglycemia. Repeat CT showed real radiographic improvement — a decreased solid component within the cavitary right middle lobe lesion, interval reduction of the left lower lobe nodule, and no new pulmonary lesions — allowing prednisone to be tapered from 60 mg to 40 mg. A subsequent CT at four months showed continued improvement, with resolution of night sweats, hemoptysis, and dyspnea, though fatigue and weight loss persisted; prednisone was tapered further to 20 mg, with ongoing pulmonology follow-up and further imaging planned.

Reflecting on the case, Reza singled out how much diagnostic effort — from the emergency department through pulmonology and the multidisciplinary tumor board — went into methodically excluding cancer and uncommon infections before treating this as an inflammatory process, and how closely the patient was monitored even as an outpatient once treatment began. Both hosts closed with reflections on humility in practice: Reza described having grown more tolerant of his own diagnostic misses over time, framing them not as failures of caring but as an inescapable part of practicing medicine, which paradoxically makes it easier to keep learning from them rather than becoming discouraged; Robbie connected this to a documentary about Liverpool's 2005 Champions League final comeback and coach Rafa Benítez's father's maxim that you learn far more from losing than from winning.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Illness script — mass-like consolidation as a diagnostic Rorschach test.** A pleural-based, mass-like consolidation on CT sits at the intersection of at least three illness scripts — malignancy, infection, and organizing pneumonia/autoimmune inflammation — and this case shows the deliberate, sequential process of testing each: imaging characterization, extensive tissue sampling to rule out malignancy, infectious stains/cultures/serologies, and finally treatment response to confirm an inflammatory diagnosis.
- **Illness tempo as a discriminating variable.** The mismatch between a "pneumonia-sounding" story and its actual ten-day tempo (too slow for typical CAP) was the first tempo-based recalibration; the later divergence between a static left lesion and a rapidly cavitating right lesion was tempo used a second time, this time to split rather than merge the problem representation.
- **Cognitive bias avoided — premature unification.** Robbie explicitly resisted anchoring on a single "bilateral mass-like consolidation" framing once the two lesions began behaving differently, modeling how problem representations must be revised, not just added to, as new data arrives.
- **Cognitive bias avoided — sampling-bias overcorrection.** With only one or two negative biopsies, "we probably just missed it" would be the appropriate caution; with 47 negative biopsies, continuing to invoke sampling bias becomes its own error, and the more defensible read becomes "the mass really is inflammation."
- **Physiologic vs. pathologic inflammation as an organizing schema.** Robbie's framework — inflammation is either the body containing something (infection) or inflammation gone wrong (autoimmune disease or malignancy-associated reactive infiltrate) — gave the workup a systematic structure once malignancy and clear infection were both weakened as explanations.
- **Regional epidemiology shaping the differential.** Coccidioidomycosis was elevated specifically because of the patient's presumed geographic/occupational exposure, echoing the same "can't let the sun set on it" urgency Robbie has previously applied to tuberculosis in endemic settings — a reminder that illness scripts are calibrated by geography as much as by pathophysiology.
- **Treatment as a diagnostic test.** The eventual AFOP diagnosis was not proven by a single confirmatory test but was substantiated retrospectively by a brisk, sustained response to corticosteroids — a pattern the hosts have returned to before when a presumptive inflammatory diagnosis is confirmed by watching the disease recede on the appropriate treatment rather than by histology alone.
- **Communication as a calibrated skill, not a fixed rule.** Reza's evolution from "treat patients as you'd want to be treated" toward "let the patient's reaction guide the conversation" is itself a kind of illness script correction applied to communication rather than pathophysiology — recognizing that a single default approach doesn't fit every patient.

## Teaching Discussion

This episode is less about a single elusive diagnosis and more about the discipline of watching a case evolve honestly over six months without forcing premature closure. The earliest teaching point — that focal pleuritic chest pain is one of the few chest-pain presentations patients actually can localize, and that this localization itself signals real pleural pathology — sets up a recurring theme: precision in the problem representation drives the diagnostic conversation. That same discipline resurfaces powerfully at the midpoint of the case, when Robbie refuses to let a single "bilateral mass-like consolidation" framing survive contact with serial imaging that showed one lesion holding still while the other grew and cavitated. Treating radiographically discordant findings as a single problem is a subtle trap; recognizing that divergence forces you to entertain two separate causal stories is a transferable skill well beyond pulmonology.

The 47-biopsy negative result is a valuable teaching moment about the epistemics of tissue sampling: a small number of negative biopsies should make you worry about missing the target, but an exhaustive, EBUS-augmented sampling effort that consistently returns "just inflammation" should instead update you toward believing the mass really is what it says it is. That, in turn, licenses the physiologic-versus-pathologic-inflammation framework the team leaned on next — infection walling itself off versus autoimmune disease versus a malignancy hiding behind its own reactive infiltrate — which is a generalizable way to organize a differential whenever biopsy returns "inflammation, no malignancy" as its answer.

The eventual diagnosis, acute fibrinous and organizing pneumonia, is new to this podcast and worth flagging as a distinct clinical entity: a more aggressive, cavitating cousin of cryptogenic organizing pneumonia that still responds to corticosteroids, distinguished less by its pathology (which can look similar on limited sampling) than by its unusually rapid radiographic behavior. The episode also reinforces two systems-of-care points worth remembering — that a well-organized outpatient pathway (this VA's two-week biopsy, three-week PET, four-week tumor board turnaround) can make hospitalization unnecessary even for concerning imaging, and that admission decisions should hinge on whether a patient can actually execute an outpatient workup rather than on how alarming the imaging looks in isolation.

Finally, the episode's humanistic closing notes — Reza's reflection on extending himself the same grace he'd extend a colleague after a diagnostic miss, and the patient's visceral, informative reaction to hearing "cancer" — are reminders that the emotional and interpersonal dimensions of a case carry real diagnostic and professional weight, not just bedside-manner value.
