## Pearls

- **[[Orthostatic Hypotension|Orthostatic hypotension]] has a compact three-cause schema — aging (autonomic decline), medications, and [[Hypovolemia|hypovolemia]] — grounded in one physiologic fact.** Standing pools blood by gravity and drops venous return; within ~30 seconds a sympathetic surge (tachycardia plus vasoconstriction) should hold blood pressure dead steady. Any break in that reflex arc — a failing autonomic nervous system ([[Autonomic Insufficiency|Parkinson's, diabetes, amyloidosis]]), a beta-blocker or antihypertensive, or too little stroke volume — reproduces the same lightheadedness.
- **Convert "[[Syncope|syncope]]" to transient loss of consciousness, run the S's, then guard the cardiac branch as the do-not-miss.** The S's are seizure, sugar (hypoglycemia), and stroke/TIA; the malignant branch is cardiac, split into arrhythmia (usually no prodrome) and structural ([[Aortic Stenosis|aortic stenosis]], [[Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy|HCM]], [[Pulmonary Embolism|PE]], [[Pericardial Effusion|pericardial effusion]]). Most syncope is benign (vasovagal, orthostatic); the point of the schema is to not miss the few that are not.
- **The most ominous rhythm in medicine is [[Sinus Tachycardia|sinus tachycardia]].** In a previously robust patient it is the body announcing a compensatory struggle — reading blood pressure as cardiac output × SVR and cardiac output as heart rate × stroke volume, a tachycardic-and-soft patient is defending a failing equation, and the differential must include occult volume loss and PE, not just anxiety.
- **A fluid bolus is both therapy and a diagnostic test.** When 500 cc normalizes heart rate, blood pressure, and symptoms, the response retrospectively confirms a volume-deficient state and converts "why syncope?" into the sharper "why [[Hypovolemia|hypovolemic]]?" ([[Treatment as a Diagnostic Test|diagnosis by response]]).
- **A [[BUN-to-Creatinine Ratio|BUN out of proportion to creatinine]] has a short, high-yield differential — and an [[Upper Gastrointestinal Bleed|upper GI bleed]] sits at the top.** Digested hemoglobin is a protein load fed into the hepatic urea cycle, raising BUN; the other classic cause is the cachectic elder whose low muscle mass makes creatinine underread true renal function, and simple [[Prerenal Azotemia|prerenal azotemia]] from any hypovolemia. In this case the ratio was the only clue pointing at a bleed the patient had not yet declared.
- **[[Melena|Melena]] is ~75% upper and [[Hematochezia|hematochezia]] ~75% lower, but the tiebreaker for a patient with both is that upper sources cause hematochezia more readily than lower sources cause melena** — so a brisk upper GI bleed becomes the most morbid unifying hypothesis and is pursued first.

## Problem Representation

A previously highly functional, athletic 73–74-year-old man with hypertension and hyperlipidemia and no pertinent medications presents with one day of positional lightheadedness and a single witnessed syncopal episode on rising from his sofa, resolving as soon as he was supine. He arrives asymptomatic with a heart rate of 110 and a blood pressure softer than his usual 130s–140s (now ~100s), a normal cardiopulmonary exam, and confirmed orthostatic vital signs. A 500 cc crystalloid bolus normalizes his hemodynamics and symptoms, establishing hypovolemia as the mechanism of his orthostatic hypotension and syncope. Labs show only mild hyponatremia (sodium 130) with a normal creatinine (0.9) but a disproportionately elevated BUN (31), and two hours into the ED stay he develops large-volume hematochezia followed by melena — reframing the entire presentation as syncope from an evolving, initially occult gastrointestinal hemorrhage, most likely a brisk upper GI bleed, with EGD and colonoscopy pending.

## Case Narrative

After the signature palindrome banter and a Whole Foods riff on palm-print payment, Rabih proposes a new segment: a timed, three-minute "expert witness" exercise before the case. He casts Reza as a defendant and asks him to define [[Cushing Syndrome]] and how it presents.

Reza frames Cushing syndrome as a state of cortisol excess and is careful to separate the umbrella *syndrome* from [[Cushing Disease|Cushing disease]], the specific pituitary-adenoma cause in which excess ACTH drives the adrenal cortex. He teaches the phenotype by asking what too much prednisone does: fat redistribution (dorsocervical and supraclavicular pads), osteoporosis, thin skin with easy bruising and striae, neuropsychiatric change from depression to psychosis, and — because cortisol is counter-regulatory — new hyperglycemia. He then walks the causal branches: [[Iatrogenic Cushing Syndrome|exogenous glucocorticoids]] (among the most common causes, often unintentional in patients kept on steroids for sarcoid or rheumatologic disease), and [[Ectopic ACTH Syndrome|ectopic ACTH]], which carries a unique signature — ACTH so high that cortisol overwhelms the renal [[11-Beta-Hydroxysteroid Dehydrogenase|11-beta-HSD]] enzyme and spills onto the mineralocorticoid receptor, producing sodium retention, potassium and bicarbonate wasting (hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis), eosinopenia, and a robust onset without the long-standing skin and bruising changes.

Rabih then poses the cliffhanger. A patient has every feature of steroid excess — weight gain, new diabetes and hypertension, striae, leukocytosis, hypokalemia — yet a 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test, a midnight salivary cortisol, and a 24-hour urine cortisol are all reassuringly normal, and an incidentally ordered AM cortisol comes back *undetectable, zero*. He frames the paradox — a body that looks physiologically full of steroids while the laboratory says the adrenal glands are insufficient — and defers the answer to a short explanatory video, noting the pearl was gifted to him rather than deduced. (The recognized endocrine explanation for a Cushingoid patient with a suppressed HPA axis and undetectable measured cortisol is an *exogenous synthetic glucocorticoid* that produces the phenotype and suppresses endogenous cortisol but is not cross-detected by the cortisol assay — a mechanism the note flags below as a handbook and evergreen opportunity, distinct from whatever specific case the hosts will present.)

The main case then begins. A 73-year-old man comes to the ED after a day of lightheadedness specifically on standing; that morning, rising from his sofa, he had complete [[Syncope|loss of consciousness]], falling back onto the couch and recovering the instant he was flat. On arrival he is asymptomatic, with a heart rate of 110 and a blood pressure softer than his baseline.

Reza names two possibly-linked problems — [[Orthostatic Hypotension|orthostatic hypotension]] and [[Syncope]] — and deliberately declines to fuse them yet. He unpacks the orthostatic schema with its physiology: moving supine-to-upright, gravity pools blood and cuts venous return, and a normal sympathetic surge (tachycardia plus peripheral vasoconstriction) should hold blood pressure unchanged within 30 seconds; the schema's three buckets are aging (with [[Autonomic Insufficiency|autonomic causes]] including Parkinson's, diabetes, and amyloidosis), medications (beta-blockers, antihypertensives, alpha-blockers), and [[Hypovolemia|hypovolemia]], anchored to blood pressure as cardiac output × SVR and cardiac output as heart rate × stroke volume. For syncope he converts the event to transient loss of consciousness and runs the S's — seizure, sugar, stroke/TIA — before insisting the do-not-miss branch is cardiac, split into arrhythmia (typically no prodrome) and structural disease ([[Aortic Stenosis|aortic stenosis]], [[Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy|hypertrophic cardiomyopathy]], [[Pulmonary Embolism|pulmonary embolism]], [[Pericardial Effusion|pericardial effusion]]). He plans a thorough cardiac exam and an electrocardiogram, while noting that the preceding orthostatic symptoms are what let him pick syncope out of the S's — looking to the patient's recent past to interpret the present.

Rabih gives a reassuring foreground — well-appearing, normal cardiac and pulmonary exam, no murmurs — against a striking background: ridiculously healthy, athletic, only hypertension and hyperlipidemia, no pertinent medications. Reza says this makes him *more* worried than for a typical comorbid elder, because there is no easy explanation, and he fixes on the vitals: a [[Sinus Tachycardia|sinus tachycardia]] he calls the most ominous rhythm in medicine, plus relative hypotension, in a body clearly trying and failing to defend its blood pressure. He raises PE explicitly as an alternative reading of "tachycardic and soft." His first move is orthostatic vital signs (supine, then standing at one minute — the "Uncle Bob" one-minute rule), noting that a flat heart-rate response with a falling pressure argues for autonomic insufficiency, though a normal heart-rate response rules autonomic disease *in* poorly and does not rule it out. He orders a BMP, CBC with differential, liver chemistries, a lactate, and an ECG, and pointedly *holds* the echocardiogram, reasoning that without jugular venous distention or a murmur the yield approaches zero.

Rabih reports the orthostatics are positive and returns the data authentically, unbiased: hemoglobin 13.1, platelets 248, WBC 5.5 with a normal differential, sodium 130, potassium 4.8, chloride 110, bicarbonate 24, BUN 31, creatinine 0.9, normal glucose, normal lactate, normal BNP, no anion gap. The patient had received 500 cc of fluid before the ECG, which normalized his heart rate to the 80s and pressure to 120, so the tracing read as normal sinus rhythm; telemetry over ~90 minutes showed no events.

Reza reads the fluid response as diagnostic: treatment became the test, and the normalization confirms a volume-deficient state ([[Treatment as a Diagnostic Test|diagnosis by response]]). He would not even bother with an extended monitor given how cleanly the story fits orthostatic hypotension, and the still-elevated arrival heart rate reassures him the autonomic nervous system is intact, lowering diabetes/Parkinson's-type autonomic failure. The case now advances to "why [[Hypovolemia|hypovolemic]]?", which he says is usually answered by history: non-hemorrhagic losses (a heat wave, a long walk without water, diarrhea) versus hemorrhagic. He also reads the sodium of 130 as hypovolemic hyponatremia with ADH activation, and flags that this same pattern can signal [[Primary Adrenal Insufficiency|primary adrenal insufficiency]] — which itself causes natriuresis and volume loss — though autoimmune adrenalitis would be unusual at this age and the patient is not from a TB-endemic area (part of why he asked about eosinophilia on the differential).

Rabih then performs the artificial reasoning exercise of asking Reza to predict, re-reading the labs and drawing attention to the [[BUN-to-Creatinine Ratio|BUN-to-creatinine ratio]]. Two hours later, in the ED, the patient has a large-volume [[Hematochezia|hematochezia]]. Reza recalls "that Sarah Meller case" — a patient he once presented with unilateral cerebellar ataxia so early in its course that it declared bilaterally only on day two — and reasons by analogy that this man was having a very early GI bleed whose blood had not yet fully transited, later declaring more overtly with repeated hematochezia and [[Melena|melena]].

Reza explains the urea-ratio physiology: hemoglobin is protein, and an upper GI bleed feeds its amino acids through the hepatic urea cycle, driving BUN up out of proportion to creatinine. He is careful that the discordant ratio is not specific — it also appears with cachexia/low muscle mass in the elderly (where creatinine underestimates renal function) and with plain [[Prerenal Azotemia|prerenal azotemia]] — and invites the audience to build that differential. The mixed picture of both hematochezia and melena worries him for a brisk upper source (peptic ulcer, a Dieulafoy lesion) rather than a purely lower bleed (diverticular, AVM, cancer); he notes the absence of cirrhosis, distension, or caput medusae argues against variceal/portal-hypertensive bleeding.

Rabih closes with the real disposition: this happened on his shift, GI judged it a brisk upper GI bleed, and the plan is EGD plus colonoscopy, likely the next day unless the patient destabilizes. He reasons the tiebreaker between the two bleeding patterns: melena confers ~75% odds of an upper source and hematochezia ~75% odds of a lower source, but an upper bleed produces hematochezia more readily than a lower bleed produces melena, so the most morbid unifying hypothesis is a brisk upper GI bleed. His stated takeaways: scrutinize the labs beyond creatinine when a patient is hypovolemic, and — the central lesson — an early history of an evolving disease is an incomplete history, "no different than a non-contrast CT," with a rectal exam that would have found the melena that "was there the whole time." He reflects that his diagnostic errors cluster in exactly these not-yet-declared presentations, where subtle clues must substitute for the story that has not finished telling itself.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|Schema over free association]] carries the whole case.** Rather than react to "syncope," Reza deploys three interlocking schemas — the orthostatic triad (aging/meds/hypovolemia), the S's of transient loss of consciousness, and the hemorrhagic-versus-non-hemorrhagic split of hypovolemia — so that each new datum lands in a structured slot instead of drifting. The BUN:creatinine clue is meaningful only because a schema was already waiting to receive it.
- **[[Treatment as a Diagnostic Test|Treatment as a diagnostic maneuver]].** The 500 cc bolus is given therapeutically but read diagnostically: its success is what licenses the move from "orthostatic hypotension" to "hypovolemic orthostatic hypotension," narrowing the field before any source is known.
- **[[Premature Closure|Premature closure]] resisted by keeping the mechanism, not just the diagnosis, in view.** Reza repeatedly reaches a satisfying stopping point (orthostatic hypotension; then hypovolemia) and each time pushes one question deeper ("why hypovolemic?"), which is exactly what leaves the differential open enough to absorb the hematochezia when it arrives rather than being blindsided by it.
- **[[Analogical Reasoning|Analogical reasoning]] from a stored case.** The "Sarah Meller" unilateral-then-bilateral cerebellar case is retrieved as a structural analogy — a disease caught too early to have fully declared — and mapped onto a GI bleed whose blood has not yet transited, generating the correct reframe before the melena is documented.
- **[[Illness Tempo|Tempo]] as the hidden variable — an evolving illness is an incomplete history.** Rabih's central teaching is that a day-one or day-two history of an acute, still-declaring disease is structurally partial, "like a non-contrast CT," and that the reasoner must either wait for the story to complete or lean hard on subtle clues (the urea ratio, a rectal exam) that would otherwise be overlooked. This episode-coined framing is a candidate evergreen ([[Evolving Illness]]).
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Metacognitive humility]] as the closing reflection.** Rabih names not-yet-declared presentations as the specific terrain where his own [[Diagnostic Error|diagnostic errors]] (defined as a mistaken *or* inefficient diagnosis) cluster, and commits to priming his mind to detect the BUN:creatinine ratio and to perform the rectal exam next time — locating his growth edge rather than papering over the near-miss. Reza balances this by cautioning against over-learning the lesson (the ratio can reflect any prerenal state, not always a bleed).

## Teaching Discussion

- Open positional lightheadedness with the orthostatic-hypotension schema — aging/autonomic decline, medications, and hypovolemia — and remember the one-minute orthostatic vital sign: a flat heart rate with a falling pressure argues for autonomic failure, while a brisk reflex tachycardia rules autonomic disease *in* poorly and cannot exclude it.
- Convert syncope to transient loss of consciousness, run the S's (seizure, sugar, stroke/TIA), and treat the cardiac branch — arrhythmic and structural — as the do-not-miss; most syncope is benign, so the schema exists to catch the minority that is not.
- Read a sinus tachycardia in a previously robust patient as the body's alarm, not a nuisance: blood pressure is cardiac output × SVR and cardiac output is heart rate × stroke volume, so a tachycardic, soft patient is defending a failing equation, and PE and occult volume loss belong on that list.
- Let a fluid bolus double as a diagnostic test — normalization of heart rate, blood pressure, and symptoms after 500 cc retroactively confirms hypovolemia and reframes the question from "why syncope?" to "why volume-deficient?".
- Interrogate a hypovolemic patient's history for the source (heat, exertion, diarrhea, hemorrhage) and read the electrolytes as clues — hypovolemic hyponatremia with ADH activation can also hint at primary adrenal insufficiency, worth a differential glance even when unlikely by age and geography.
- Keep a short differential for a BUN elevated out of proportion to creatinine: an upper GI bleed (digested hemoglobin as a protein load through the urea cycle), the cachectic elder whose low muscle mass makes creatinine underread renal function, and ordinary prerenal azotemia — and do not overfit the ratio to a bleed.
- When a patient has both melena and hematochezia, weigh that melena is ~75% upper and hematochezia ~75% lower, but that upper sources produce hematochezia more readily than lower sources produce melena, so a brisk upper GI bleed is the most morbid unifying hypothesis and is pursued first (EGD then colonoscopy).
- Treat an early history of an acute, evolving disease as inherently incomplete — like a non-contrast CT that needs contrast — and respond by either allowing the story time to declare or scrutinizing subtle clues (the urea ratio) and doing the exam that closes the gap (a rectal exam for occult melena).
