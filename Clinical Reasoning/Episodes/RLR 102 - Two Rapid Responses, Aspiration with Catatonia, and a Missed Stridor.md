## Pearls

- When encountering an extreme, sudden vital sign abnormality, briefly verify it is real and deliberately pause before acting — the pause exists specifically to prevent tunnel vision on the most obvious problem (here, hypoxemia) before checking the handful of other systems (neurologic, cardiac, renal) whose status changes the management plan.
- The oxygen-delivery escalation ladder runs nasal cannula (effective up to about 6L) → face mask (up to about 10L) → then a branch point: high-flow nasal cannula for most patients, or BiPAP specifically for patients with heart failure/flash pulmonary edema or hypercarbic/ventilatory failure → mechanical intubation once that is maxed out.
- Empiric, low-evidence-cost interventions for undifferentiated hypoxemic respiratory failure include high-dose diuresis and blood pressure control (if the blood pressure supports it) and needle decompression for a suspected pneumothorax — useful bridges while a definitive cause is worked out.
- The "sudden onset" framework for an acute process (rupture, obstruction, electrical activity, or a molecule) applied to hypoxemia narrows quickly to rupture (pneumothorax, though this usually causes hemodynamic compromise more than isolated hypoxemia) or obstruction (aspiration or flash pulmonary edema, both representing fluid forced into the alveolar space by a functional or mechanical block to its normal outflow).
- Tachypnea despite significant supplemental oxygen argues against sedation/respiratory depression as the primary driver of a patient's unresponsiveness — a purely sedated patient without a separate oxygenation problem should improve with even modest supplemental oxygen, whereas a true alveolar-filling process will not.
- In a patient whose baseline mental status is already abnormal from an underlying condition (here, catatonia), intubation carries a hidden cost beyond the usual risks: it may become difficult or impossible to later determine whether the patient has "woken up" enough to extubate, since their baseline exam was never normal to begin with — this can justify a more conservative initial approach (suctioning, antibiotics, non-invasive support) if the patient can tolerate it.
- Sudden complete opacification of a hemithorax should be worked through as either a large pleural effusion or lung collapse (atelectasis/mucus plug); tracheal deviation toward or away from the opacified side, plus the presence of an air bronchogram, are the discriminating features — an air bronchogram argues for collapse around retained secretions rather than a fluid-filled pleural space.
- Wheeze localizes to the airways broadly, but the timing of the wheeze localizes further: expiratory-predominant wheeze suggests lower/intrathoracic airway obstruction (since intrathoracic airways narrow relatively more on expiration), while inspiratory-predominant wheeze (stridor) suggests an extrathoracic/tracheal-level obstruction (since extrathoracic airways narrow relatively more on inspiration).
- Missing a diagnosis on a brief, high-volume, low-context "spot" evaluation (a hallway triage encounter) is a different failure mode than missing it on a full dedicated workup, and should be examined with that context in mind — self-compassion paired with honest reflection on the contributing factors (inexperience with a specific exam finding, anchoring on a common diagnosis given the base rate, time pressure) is more constructive than harsh self-judgment, and is also more accurate than judging colleagues who miss findings under the same constraints.

## Problem Representation

Case 1: A catatonic patient on escalating diazepam and newly started tube feeds develops sudden hypoxemic respiratory failure, reasoned through as combined benzodiazepine effect and aspiration, ultimately confirmed as aspirated tube feeds requiring bronchoscopic suction. Case 2: A demented patient with new wheezing is presumptively treated as a COPD flare on a brief hallway evaluation; a subsequent thorough exam detects previously undetected stridor, revealing an upper airway mass.

## Case Narrative

### Case 1: The Rapid Response

Reza, covering the code pager over a weekend, was called to a rapid response and arrived to find a patient on a maxed non-rebreather with an oxygen saturation of 88%, previously normal. Before framing any diagnosis, the recommended approach was to briefly verify the number was real, then deliberately pause for a second or two — not to delay action, but to avoid immediately narrowing onto "hypoxemic respiratory failure" and missing a different or additional problem. That pause is used to quickly check three things: neurologic status (could this be a brain catastrophe with secondary aspiration), cardiovascular status (could this be flash pulmonary edema from a cardiac event), and renal function (because empiric diuresis, one of the few evidence-light bridging therapies available, is useless in a patient with end-stage renal disease and a creatinine of 7). In a patient this hypoxemic on a non-rebreather, the trajectory points toward likely intubation, so preparing the room and personnel for that possibility was emphasized alongside interim bridging measures: bag-mask ventilation as needed, empiric high-dose diuresis and blood pressure control if supported by the numbers, and a low threshold to consider needle decompression if a pneumothorax was suspected (while noting pneumothorax more classically causes hemodynamic compromise rather than isolated hypoxemia).

A general oxygen-escalation ladder was reviewed: nasal cannula loses efficacy around 6 liters; face masks can deliver up to roughly 10 liters; beyond that, the choice is between high-flow nasal cannula (the default for most patients) and BiPAP (reserved primarily for patients with heart failure/flash pulmonary edema, where its preload- and afterload-reducing effects help move fluid back into the interstitium, or for hypercarbic/ventilatory failure); mechanical intubation follows once these are maximized.

On arrival, the patient's vitals showed a normal, slightly elevated blood pressure, mild tachycardia, and a fever of 101°F, and the patient was found completely unresponsive. The one-liner: a patient with catatonia for more than a week, on diazepam that had just been increased from 15mg to 20mg every 4 hours per psychiatry's recommendation roughly 20 minutes prior; the patient had also had a witnessed aspiration event and fever the night before and had been started on piperacillin-tazobactam for a presumed hospital-associated pneumonia; and tube feeds had been started just 5 minutes before the event.

Rabih reasoned that hyperacute hypoxemic respiratory failure is overwhelmingly caused by either vascular leak (flash pulmonary edema) or aspiration, and that a normal blood pressure argued against flash pulmonary edema (absent a separate cardiovascular catastrophe like acute mitral regurgitation), leaving aspiration as the working hypothesis. Immediate priorities were suctioning the airway (given the significant contribution atelectasis/V-Q mismatch can make to hypoxemia), stopping the tube feeds, high-flow nasal cannula (BiPAP being poorly tolerated in an obtunded patient), and a discussion with psychiatry about whether the diazepam effect could be reversed with flumazenil, weighed against its seizure-precipitation risk (judged low here given no strong underlying seizure risk factors) — alongside a low threshold for intubation given the trajectory, and broad-spectrum antibiotic coverage (including staph and Pseudomonas coverage) given how sick a patient this aspirated typically is.

A key diagnostic pivot point was distinguishing benzodiazepine oversedation from aspiration as the dominant process. The patient's respiratory rate of 33 was the deciding clue: a purely sedated patient would not be expected to be this tachypneic, and a sedated-but-not-truly-hypoxemic patient would typically improve significantly with even modest supplemental oxygen — this patient did not, despite maximal non-invasive support, pointing toward a true alveolar-filling process (fluid in the lungs) rather than pure CNS depression. The conclusion reached was that both processes were likely operative simultaneously: the patient's catatonia (present even before the diazepam was started) explained the baseline unresponsiveness, while a genuine aspiration event explained the hypoxemia, since aspiration alone does not typically cause unresponsiveness and unresponsiveness alone does not typically cause this degree of hypoxemia.

A chest x-ray showed complete opacification of the left hemithorax. Working through the differential for hemithorax opacification (fluid in the pleural space versus lung collapse), the trachea was not deviated away from the opacified side (arguing against a large effusion pushing it) and an air bronchogram was visible extending toward the left main stem bronchus — both findings favoring collapse (atelectasis, likely from a mucus/tube-feed plug) over a pleural effusion. This was corroborated by decreased breath sounds and asymmetric chest expansion on physical exam.

A key management nuance was then raised: the ICU fellow explicitly wanted to give the patient a chance to recover from the acute aspiration event without intubating, specifically because of the underlying catatonia — if the patient were intubated, there would be no reliable way to assess neurologic readiness for extubation later, since the patient's baseline exam was never "awake" to begin with. This reasoning was highlighted as a sophisticated, real-time management consideration beyond the usual physiologic criteria for intubation. The patient was managed on high-flow support with close monitoring, but roughly ten minutes later became apneic and required intubation; bronchoscopy at that time suctioned a substantial amount of aspirated tube feed material directly from the bronchus, after which the previously collapsed lung re-expanded.

A brief coda applied the "sudden onset" framework (rupture, obstruction, electrical activity, or a molecule) specifically to hypoxemia: with no relevant molecule or electrical event in play, sudden severe hypoxemia narrows to rupture (pneumothorax, though this usually presents with hemodynamic compromise as much as hypoxemia) or obstruction (aspiration or flash pulmonary edema, both representing fluid forced into the alveoli by a block to its normal downstream flow). A related point was made that severe hypoxemia from pulmonary embolism requiring high-flow oxygen support, in the absence of hemodynamic compromise, is exceedingly rare in clinical experience.

### Case 2: The Missed Stridor

The second case was framed by Reza as a personal account of a diagnostic miss. A nurse called about a patient with advanced dementia on a locked dementia unit who had been wheezing audibly for about a week; the patient had no prior diagnosis of COPD but did have a 20-pack-year smoking history. On a brief hallway evaluation, the patient appeared comfortable, was conversing normally, had normal vitals (oxygen saturation 97% on room air, normal blood pressure, heart rate 90), and had diffuse expiratory wheezing on auscultation, having already received a dose of albuterol via metered-dose inhaler.

Working through the differential for new wheezing, the leading considerations were a flare of previously unrecognized chronic airways disease (COPD or asthma — with the observation that a substantial proportion of patients with these conditions have never been formally diagnosed with pulmonary function testing) or acute infectious bronchitis, with pulmonary edema/fluid-related airway edema flagged as an important mimicker to actively exclude, especially in an older patient. Given the smoking history, possible undiagnosed underlying lung disease, and the risk of an infectious process being transmitted to others on a shared unit, transfer for a more thorough medical evaluation was recommended, along with a nebulizer, steroids, and antibiotics as an empiric COPD-flare-type regimen; the referring clinician noted this encounter took only a few minutes as part of a broader, time-pressured triage role.

The following day, a colleague performing a more detailed physical exam — including a closer assessment of the inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio — detected stridor in addition to the previously noted wheezing. The physiologic distinction was reviewed: because extrathoracic (tracheal) airways narrow relatively more during inspiration and intrathoracic (lower) airways narrow relatively more during expiration, an inspiratory-predominant abnormal sound localizes to the trachea/upper airway (stridor), while an expiratory-predominant sound localizes to the lower airways (wheeze) — a distinction that is easy to overlook, especially when a stridorous component develops gradually over days rather than presenting acutely and dramatically.

With stridor identified, the anatomic approach to tracheal-level obstruction was applied: external compression (commonly benign causes like goiter or lymphadenopathy in older adults, evaluable by careful neck exam, palpation, and review of prior imaging), tracheal wall disease (a range of rare, often idiopathic or inflammatory conditions), or an intraluminal process (foreign body — an important consideration in a patient with dementia who may swallow or aspirate foreign material — or an intraluminal mass). CT imaging was recommended, specifically a CT chest without contrast extending up through the hyoid bone, or a dedicated CT neck; a dynamic inspiratory/expiratory CT was noted as useful for tracheal wall pathology such as tracheobronchomalacia, since it can directly demonstrate tracheal collapse on inspiration correlating with the exam finding.

In this patient, ENT was consulted for suspected vocal cord paralysis or upper airway obstruction, but the patient refused laryngoscopic evaluation; a CT neck (recommended by ENT beyond the CT chest, which had not shown a mass) revealed a mass in the upper airway concerning for malignancy. The patient was transferred to the ICU, and given the inability to obtain adequate laryngoscopic visualization, underwent a tracheostomy with biopsy of the mass, along with dexamethasone.

Reza then reflected candidly on the experience of learning about the missed stridor, describing an initial visceral, self-critical reaction, followed by a structured attempt to understand why it had happened: limited personal experience discerning subtle stridor (as opposed to acute, dramatic stridor, which he had recognized correctly in a prior case); anchoring on the higher base-rate diagnosis of a COPD flare given the smoking history; the practical constraints of a brief, hallway-based triage encounter rather than a full dedicated exam; and a specific, telling detail — his documentation had explicitly stated "no stridor," a note he later recognized as reflecting an unconscious awareness that the sound was ambiguous enough to require actively ruling it out, despite ultimately reaching the wrong conclusion. The discussion closed on the broader theme of processing diagnostic error: acknowledging the discomfort as appropriate evidence of caring, while resisting excessive or prolonged self-punishment, and extending the same graciousness to colleagues who miss findings under similar time and information constraints rather than judging them from the comfort of retrospective certainty.

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding (Case 1) | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| Normal blood pressure in hyperacute hypoxemia | Lowers probability of flash pulmonary edema; raises relative probability of aspiration |
| Respiratory rate 33 despite sedative dose increase | Lowers probability that sedation alone explains the presentation; raises probability of a true alveolar process |
| Failure to improve with non-rebreather despite high FiO2 | Raises probability of significant V/Q mismatch or shunt (aspiration) over pure hypoventilation |
| Chest x-ray: complete opacification, midline/right-shifted trachea, air bronchogram | Favors atelectasis/collapse from mucus or aspirated material over pleural effusion |
| Bronchoscopy: tube feed material suctioned, lung re-expands | Confirms aspiration of tube feeds as the mechanism |

| Finding (Case 2) | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| New wheezing over a week in a patient with smoking history | Raises probability of undiagnosed COPD/asthma flare or infectious bronchitis |
| Normal vitals, comfortable appearance | Lowers immediate acuity concern; contributes to a brief, lower-intensity evaluation |
| Inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio abnormality noted on repeat exam | Prompts closer auscultation, revealing previously undetected stridor |
| Stridor present in addition to wheeze | Localizes an additional problem to the trachea/upper airway, distinct from the lower-airway wheeze |
| CT neck showing an upper airway mass | Confirms a structural, potentially malignant cause of the stridor |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Case 1 illness script (aspiration in a sedated/catatonic patient): a patient with impaired baseline mental status and/or recent sedative escalation, in the setting of recently initiated enteral feeding, develops acute hypoxemia with tachypnea unresponsive to supplemental oxygen; imaging shows lobar/hemithoracic collapse with an air bronchogram; bronchoscopy confirms and treats aspirated material.

Case 2 illness script (upper airway mass presenting as refractory wheeze): an older patient with a smoking history and new wheezing that does not resolve with standard COPD-flare treatment, in whom a careful exam reveals an inspiratory component (stridor) alongside the expiratory wheeze, localizing an additional, more concerning process to the trachea rather than the lower airways.

Case 2 is an explicit, worked example of anchoring bias (COPD flare, the higher base-rate diagnosis, capturing the initial impression) combined with availability/experience bias (limited personal exposure to subtle stridor made it harder to recognize) and a documentation artifact (writing "no stridor" as an unconscious signal that the finding had been actively, if unsuccessfully, considered) — together illustrating how a diagnosis can be missed even by an attentive, experienced clinician operating under real time constraints.

## Teaching Discussion

Case 1's core teaching point is the deliberate pause-and-triage habit for any hyperacute vital sign abnormality: verify the number, then check neurologic, cardiac, and renal status before locking onto the most obvious diagnosis, since these three systems determine both what else might be happening and which empiric therapies are safe or futile. A second teaching point is the sophisticated management consideration that a patient's baseline neurologic status (here, catatonia) can and should influence the threshold for intubation, since intubating a patient whose baseline exam is already abnormal removes any decent way to gauge later readiness for extubation.

Case 2's core teaching point is the physiologic distinction between inspiratory-predominant (extrathoracic/tracheal) and expiratory-predominant (intrathoracic/lower airway) abnormal breath sounds, and the discipline of listening specifically for the timing of an abnormal sound rather than simply noting its presence. Equally important is the meta-teaching point about processing diagnostic error constructively: naming the specific, ordinary factors that contribute to a miss (inexperience with a finding, anchoring on base rate, time-constrained encounters) turns a diffuse feeling of failure into an actionable understanding of what to do differently, while modeling self-compassion as a professional skill rather than a lapse in accountability.
