## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> An 80s man is transferred to the VA "for surgery" with presumed acute [[Cholecystitis|cholecystitis]] — four days of RUQ pain, nausea, vomiting, leukocytosis to 17, mild transaminitis, [[Pyuria|pyuria]], and gallbladder-wall edema with fat stranding but *no stone* on CT or ultrasound. In a role-reversed inpatient case (Rabih presenting to Reza, with a live audience), Reza declines to accept the [[Diagnostic Momentum|handed-down label]] and lists the features that don't fit a "run-of-the-mill" cholecystitis: the transaminase elevation (which in a stone disease should make you fear [[Choledocholithiasis|choledocholithiasis]]/[[Cholangitis|cholangitis]]), and then, on repeat data, an *indirect* hyperbilirubinemia, a right [[Pleural Effusion|pleural effusion]], and 2+ lower-extremity edema — none of which a gallbladder alone produces. He walks the [[Anatomical Approach|anatomy]] from biliary tree to liver and lands on a suspicion that the liver, or the heart behind it, is the real site. A [[Point-of-Care Ultrasound|POCUS]] settles it: a globally poor EF, pulmonary edema with Kerley B-lines, a plethoric IVC, BNP 2500, and a 30-lb weight gain — new-onset systolic [[Heart Failure|heart failure]], with the gallbladder edema, congestive hepatopathy, indirect hyperbilirubinemia, effusion, and edema all downstream of venous congestion. Refusing to assume a single diagnosis, the team diureses hard (3 L/24 h → complete resolution of pain, transaminases, and bilirubin) while *keeping antibiotics* to cover a possible superimposed cholecystitis. The rapid response to diuresis becomes its own [[Treatment as a Diagnostic Test|diagnostic test]]; echo shows segmental dysfunction pointing to presumed coronary disease. Rabih names the coined lesson — **[[Cholecystalgia|cholecystalgia]]**, congestion masquerading as gallbladder disease — and the broader truth that "[[Congestive Organopathy|congestion-anything-opathy]]" is real.

## Pearls

- **A diagnosis handed over at transfer is a hypothesis, not a fact — re-derive it from the data before you operate on the label.** The features that "don't fit" ([[Diagnostic Momentum|momentum-carried]] transaminases, an indirect hyperbilirubinemia, a [[Pleural Effusion|pleural effusion]], leg edema) were exactly the ones that reopened the case.
- **In gallstone disease the *ALT* is the first thing to move when a stone obstructs the duct — never wave off a transaminase bump as incidental.** A truly isolated [[Cholecystitis|cholecystitis]] should barely touch liver chemistries; when they rise, think complication ([[Choledocholithiasis|choledocholithiasis]], [[Cholangitis|cholangitis]]) or a different diagnosis entirely.
- **Bilirubin fractionation redirects the whole workup: *direct* points to cholestasis, *indirect* points to hemolysis or impaired conjugation — including the impaired conjugation of hepatic congestion.** Here an indirect-predominant bilirubin beside a pleural effusion and leg edema pointed away from the biliary tree and toward the [[Heart Failure|heart]].
- **The liver sits between the gallbladder and the right pleural space, so hepatic congestion can produce gallbladder-wall edema *and* a right effusion at once — one upstream cause, three "organ" findings.** Reasoning [[Anatomical Approach|anatomically]] from a downstream finding back to its shared source beats cataloguing each abnormality separately.
- **Refuse the single-diagnosis reflex when one candidate is morbid: diurese for the heart *and* keep antibiotics for a possible cholecystitis until each is settled.** [[Dual Diagnosis|Two processes]] can coexist, and decompensated heart failure may itself be what an acute infection tipped over.
- **A brisk response to therapy is diagnostic data.** Complete resolution of pain, transaminases, and bilirubin after 3 L of diuresis in 24 hours is faster than treated cholecystitis could plausibly resolve — the [[Treatment as a Diagnostic Test|therapeutic response]] retrospectively confirmed a congested, volume-overloaded state.

## Problem Representation

An man in his 80s with cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes) is transferred for presumed acute cholecystitis after four days of RUQ pain, nausea, and vomiting, with tachycardia without fever, leukocytosis to 17, mild AST/ALT elevation, pyuria, and gallbladder-wall edema with fat stranding but no identifiable stone on CT or ultrasound; on repatriation his leukocytosis and pyuria resolve after ceftriaxone and fluids, but he is found to have an indirect-predominant hyperbilirubinemia (~3), ALT>AST transaminitis, a right pleural effusion, reduced right-base breath sounds, RUQ tenderness, and 2+ lower-extremity edema — ultimately diagnosed as new-onset systolic heart failure (globally reduced EF, pulmonary edema, plethoric IVC, BNP 2500, 30-lb weight gain) with congestive hepatopathy and gallbladder-wall edema ("cholecystalgia"), presumed ischemic, that resolves completely with aggressive diuresis while antibiotics are continued to cover a possible superimposed cholecystitis.

## Case Narrative

In a departure from his usual triage-room format, Rabih presents an inpatient case to Reza before a live audience — a role reversal in which Rabih plays the hospital attending signing out a patient his team found "too confusing." A man in his 80s was transferred from an outside facility for presumed acute cholecystitis and possible surgery. He had four days of worsening right-upper-quadrant pain, nausea, and vomiting; the outside workup showed RUQ tenderness, tachycardia without fever, a leukocytosis to 17, normal renal function, mild AST/ALT elevation, and pyuria with pending cultures. CT and ultrasound both showed gallbladder-wall edema and possible fat stranding but *no definitive stone*, and he was sent on for surgical evaluation of presumed cholecystitis.

Reza begins by sharing his illness script for **[[Cholecystitis|acute cholecystitis]]**: an acute inflammatory state of RUQ pain plus inflammatory signs (fever, leukocytosis) and consistent imaging. He then asks whether there is any reason to pause — and finds two. First, the AST/ALT elevation. Reasoning through the **[[Anatomical Approach|anatomy]]** (gallbladder → cystic duct → hepatic ducts → common bile duct → ampulla of Vater), he notes that most cholecystitis is calculous, with acalculous disease reserved for the critically ill (not this previously healthy man), so the *absence of a stone* already gives pause. He distinguishes [[Biliary Colic|biliary colic]] (non-inflammatory cystic-duct obstruction, post-prandial pain) from cholecystitis (inflammation of the gallbladder), and stresses that neither should perturb liver chemistries *unless there is a complication* — a stone passing into the biliary system to cause [[Choledocholithiasis|choledocholithiasis]] and potentially life-threatening [[Cholangitis|cholangitis]], or a gallstone eroding into bowel. So the transaminitis makes him ask: cholecystitis *plus* a complication, or the wrong diagnosis altogether, with something else in the abdomen causing RUQ pain and nonspecific gallbladder-wall swelling? Second, the **[[Pyuria|pyuria]]**: nonspecific and possibly incidental, but if tied to intra-abdominal inflammation it could reflect a GI process irritating the urinary tract (as inflammatory bowel disease can irritate the bladder). He parks the case as *conceivably* cholecystitis, but with the transaminases and pyuria flagging either a complication or a separate entity.

Rabih loads the repeat data. Repatriated to the VA (which lacks the outside records), the patient had already received ceftriaxone and 2 L of fluid; his white count is down to 13, the pyuria has resolved, and the UA is now negative. AST is in the 70s, ALT in the 80s, alkaline phosphatase normal, total bilirubin ~3 and *mostly indirect*, creatinine normal, and a chest x-ray shows a right-sided pleural effusion. On exam he has a BMI over 30, reduced breath sounds at the right base, a normal cardiac exam, RUQ tenderness, and 2+ lower-extremity edema.

Reza notes this has clearly deviated from run-of-the-mill cholecystitis. He offers a teaching point — unlike acute pancreatitis, cholecystitis *is* treated with antibiotics (gram-negatives plus anaerobes: ceftriaxone/metronidazole or ertapenem) — then lists what simple cholecystitis should *not* do: perturb liver chemistries, produce a pleural effusion, or cause leg edema. He fractionates the bilirubin: he had expected a *direct* elevation reflecting cholestasis, so an *indirect* one instead raises hemolysis (he wants the rest of the CBC) or impaired conjugation (Gilbert's, physiologic stress) — and, importantly, the impaired conjugation of hepatic congestion. The pleural effusion and leg edema make him wonder about undiagnosed **[[Heart Failure|heart failure]]** with congestive impaired conjugation. He delivers a key pearl — in choledocholithiasis and gallstone pancreatitis, the *ALT* is the first marker of obstruction, so an ALT rise must never be dismissed — and an anatomical insight: a gallbladder alone cannot cause a pleural effusion, but the *liver*, sitting between the right pleural space and the gallbladder, can, so hepatic inflammation or congestion could simultaneously produce gallbladder-wall edema and a right effusion. His interest swings to the liver. Most worried about missing cholangitis, he would review the images and consider an [[MRCP|MRCP]] (CT and ultrasound being insufficiently sensitive to exclude biliary obstruction); if negative, he would prioritize an inflammatory hepatic process, most likely a virus causing nonspecific gallbladder-wall edema and right pleural irritation. He frames the exercise as pinpointing the **[[Center of Gravity|site of pathology]]** — pleural space, liver, or a heart backing pressure into all of it and explaining the leg edema.

Rabih adds a metacognitive aside about his team: they sensed something was wrong without being able to articulate what, and he praises that instinct — "knowing something is not right" is a more developed and trustworthy skill than "knowing exactly what is wrong." Acting on it, they placed a **[[Point-of-Care Ultrasound|POCUS]]** probe and saw a globally poorly functioning heart with reduced EF, pulmonary edema with Kerley B-lines, a right-greater-than-left pleural effusion, and a dilated IVC over 3 cm; a BNP came back at 2500, and the chart showed a 30-lb weight gain over six or seven months.

Reza performs a real-time **[[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive autopsy]]**: the elegant unifying story is heart failure driving hepatic congestion — indirect hyperbilirubinemia, right pleural effusion, reduced breath sounds, and leg edema — and had he been forced to prioritize one organ, it should have been the heart. He sets the pyuria aside as nonspecific for now. The syndrome is **new-onset systolic heart failure**, and he chooses to work it as *acute* new-onset (more morbid, more often reversible) rather than long-standing decompensation only now recognized. Noting most HFrEF is ischemic, he again reasons anatomically through the heart — endocardium/valves, myocardium, rhythm, pericardium — de-prioritizing tamponade (no pericardial signal) and arrhythmia (no rhythm clue), acknowledging valvular disease needs a careful exam, and settling on the myocardium as the likely seat, pending a formal echocardiogram to exclude the others.

Rabih surfaces the management tension the team called him about: is this heart failure with congestive hepatopathy and gallbladder congestion (diurese alone), or chronic heart failure plus a genuine gallbladder infection (antibiotics and fluid resuscitation), or a middle path? Reza's answer models **[[Dual Diagnosis|dual-diagnosis]] discipline**: his instinct says the heart is primary, but the risk of missing an acute infection is too great, so he would continue antibiotics for possible cholecystitis — which may even be what tipped the patient into decompensation — while explicitly *not* fluid-resuscitating, and would consider a HIDA scan to interrogate the gallbladder.

The team commits: they diurese aggressively rather than resuscitate — about 3 L in 24 hours — and his symptoms resolve completely, along with his transaminases, indirect hyperbilirubinemia, and RUQ pain; the leukocytosis also resolves the next day (possibly the antibiotics). They go "all in" for heart failure while keeping antibiotics as insurance. Echocardiography confirms newly reduced EF without valvular disease, with segmental changes; with risk factors including hypertension and diabetes, he is presumed to have coronary artery disease and is pending an outpatient catheterization. Reza reflects that the rapid response is itself informative — "sometimes our treatment is our next step in diagnosis," and no cholecystitis (or pneumonia, or strep throat) would resolve that fast — supporting a purely volume-overloaded state.

Rabih closes with the coined lesson from his own five-to-six-hour deep dive: heart failure presenting as gallbladder-wall edema is a real entity he calls **[[Cholecystalgia|cholecystalgia]]**, and it prompted a broader question — why do we speak only of *congestive hepatopathy* and *congestive nephropathy* when in truth **[[Congestive Organopathy|congestion of any organ]]** can occur? His teaser for the case had been "when avocado toast is really just bread and butter." (A short bonus teaching case was recorded separately and is not part of this note.)

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Diagnostic Momentum|Diagnostic momentum]] / [[Framing Effect|framing]] from the transfer label.** The patient arrived pre-packaged "for cholecystitis and surgery," and Reza's central discipline is to treat that handed-down diagnosis as a hypothesis to be re-derived, not a settled fact. Naming the features that *don't* fit the label (transaminases, indirect bilirubin, effusion, leg edema) is what dissolves the momentum and reopens the differential.
- **Resisting the [[Anchoring Bias|anchor]] while still respecting the base rate.** Reza concedes the presentation is "overall consistent" with cholecystitis before pausing — he does not overcorrect into contrarianism, but lets specific discordant data (not a vibe) move him off the anchor. The stone's *absence* and the liver-chemistry abnormalities are the levers.
- **[[Dual Diagnosis|Occam vs Hickam]] and the morbidity-weighted differential.** Rather than force a single unifier, Reza deliberately holds two live diagnoses — decompensated heart failure (near-certain) and possible cholecystitis (morbid if missed) — and lets the *cost of being wrong* dictate management: diurese for the heart, keep antibiotics for the gallbladder. He explicitly warns against assuming one diagnosis when one candidate is dangerous.
- **[[Anatomical Approach|Anatomical reasoning]] as the engine, twice.** He walks the biliary tree to interpret the transaminases and the liver's position to unify the effusion with the gallbladder edema, then applies the identical method to the heart (endocardium/valves, myocardium, rhythm, pericardium) to localize the failure to the myocardium. The same systematic move localizes a "down" organ regardless of which organ it is.
- **[[Treatment as a Diagnostic Test|Therapeutic response as data]].** The completeness and *speed* of resolution after diuresis is read as confirmatory — faster than treated infection could resolve — an explicit instance of treatment doubling as the next diagnostic step.
- **Real-time [[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive autopsy]] and metacognition on instinct.** Reza narrates, mid-case, that the heart should have been prioritized once the effusion and edema appeared; Rabih separately valorizes the team's ability to sense "something is wrong" before they could name it, framing that pre-articulate alarm as a distinct, trainable, trustworthy skill.

## Teaching Discussion

- A diagnosis attached at transfer carries momentum that must be actively countered; the discordant data (transaminitis, indirect hyperbilirubinemia, pleural effusion, leg edema) are precisely what a busy team can smooth over under the inherited label.
- Liver chemistries discipline the biliary differential: an isolated cholecystitis should barely move them, the ALT is the earliest marker when a stone obstructs, and bilirubin fractionation reroutes the workup — direct toward cholestasis, indirect toward hemolysis or impaired conjugation (including congestive impaired conjugation).
- The liver's anatomic position between the right pleural space and the gallbladder lets one upstream cause — congestion — generate gallbladder-wall edema, a right pleural effusion, and transaminitis simultaneously; reasoning backward from a downstream finding to a shared source is more powerful than tallying abnormalities one by one.
- New-onset systolic heart failure deserves the same anatomical decomposition as any organ (valves, myocardium, rhythm, pericardium); most HFrEF is ischemic, and segmental echo changes plus cardiac risk factors point there, but valvular, rhythm, and pericardial causes are excludable with echo, EKG, and telemetry before committing to the myocardium.
- When a near-certain diagnosis (heart failure) coexists with a possibly-present morbid one (cholecystitis), manage both until each is resolved: diurese while continuing antibiotics, and let the safest path — not parsimony — govern. Note the physiologic loop: an acute illness can tip chronic cardiac disease into decompensation.
- Therapeutic response is legitimate diagnostic evidence: resolution of pain, transaminases, and bilirubin within a day of aggressive diuresis is too fast for treated infection and confirms a volume-overloaded state.
- The coined framing — *cholecystalgia*, and more broadly "congestion-anything-opathy" — is a reminder that congestive physiology is not confined to the liver and kidney; any organ's wall or parenchyma can edematize under venous back-pressure.
