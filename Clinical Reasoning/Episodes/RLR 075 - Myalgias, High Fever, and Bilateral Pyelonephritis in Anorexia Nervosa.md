## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A young woman with anorexia nervosa presents with five days of fever, myalgias, nausea, and vomiting. An initial anchor toward a viral syndrome dissolves when COVID/flu testing is negative, and a [[Cognitive Autopsy]] flags that her degree of cachexia cannot be explained by five days of illness alone. Escalating tachycardia and a temperature of 105°F, followed by asymptomatic pyuria with nitrites, ultimately localize — via [[Base Rate]] reasoning — to bacteremic bilateral pyelonephritis from Klebsiella pneumoniae.

## Pearls

- In young patients, favor the "bad luck / bad genetics / risky behavior" framework over an age-adjusted, comorbidity-driven differential — reserve genetic causes for presentations that are truly unusual once bad luck and behavior are excluded.
- A [[Cognitive Autopsy]] applied early — "no one loses that much muscle mass in five days" — can flag a chronic process hiding behind an acute presentation and reframe risk (here, immunocompromise from anorexia nervosa) before further workup.
- Trend data outperforms a single snapshot: tachycardia and fever that are actively worsening on repeat vitals argue against a benign etiology and away from diagnoses (like pulmonary embolism) that a single static reading might suggest.
- True defervescence after an NSAID supports an inflammatory, cytokine-mediated fever over a non-inflammatory cause of temperature elevation, such as a toxidrome, thyrotoxicosis, or drug fever.
- The diagnostic weight of pyuria and bacteriuria depends on the population: unlike the common "noise" of asymptomatic bacteriuria in older adults, pyuria with nitrites in a young, otherwise healthy patient is much more likely to reflect actionable infection.
- When a common diagnosis (pyelonephritis) presents atypically — bilateral, without flank pain or dysuria, over five nonspecific days — [[Base Rate]] reasoning should still favor it over an exotic explanation.

## Case Narrative

A woman in her twenties with a known diagnosis of anorexia nervosa presents in a wheelchair, frail and ill-appearing, with five days of severe myalgias, high fevers, nausea, and vomiting; there are no upper respiratory, chest, or abdominal symptoms beyond the nausea and vomiting, and specifically no diarrhea, dysuria, flank pain, or rash.

The initial framing centers on an inflammatory muscle process: fever plus diffuse myalgias at rest (not exertional claudication) raises concern for myositis, with polymyositis and dermatomyositis considered, while anticipating that CK may be falsely reassuring if muscle mass is already depleted — LDH and aldolase are sent alongside CK and inflammatory markers. A cognitive autopsy on the five-day history immediately flags that no one can lose this much muscle mass that quickly, pointing to a chronic process — her known anorexia nervosa — that predisposes to nutritional and immune deficiency, raising the risk of infection even though the tempo of the current illness is only acute.

Vitals: temperature 101.5°F, heart rate 120, normal respiratory rate and oxygen saturation, estimated BMI approximately 17. Exam is unremarkable apart from cachexia — clear lungs, soft nontender abdomen, no flank pain, no rash, no neck stiffness, no pharyngeal erythema.

Initial workup: CBC, renal panel, LFTs, lactate all normal except a white count of 12 with a mild neutrophil predominance; CK normal (arguing against significant myositis or rhabdomyolysis, albeit with the caveat that reduced muscle mass could still be masking damage); COVID, RSV, and influenza all negative, prompting a comprehensive respiratory viral panel per protocol.

The patient then develops a heart rate of 150 with an EKG showing sinus tachycardia. Sinus tachycardia is treated as a physiologic response requiring an explanation rather than beta-blockade, and the differential splits between an ongoing inflammatory/infectious fever and a non-inflammatory cause such as sympathomimetic toxicity, substance withdrawal, or thyrotoxicosis (features that would support the latter: elevated blood pressure, elevated lactate from increased metabolic rate, and hypokalemia from catecholamine-driven potassium shift). Before a CT scan can be ordered to evaluate for PE, the nursing team reports a temperature of 105°F; the CT order is cancelled in favor of empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics plus a TSH. She receives IV toradol (unable to tolerate oral Tylenol due to nausea) and defervesces to 100°F within one to two hours — true defervescence with an NSAID, favoring an inflammatory rather than non-inflammatory fever. TSH and free T4 return normal.

A urinalysis, which takes over three hours to obtain, ultimately shows greater than 180 white blood cells with no squamous epithelial cells and positive nitrites, in a patient with no dysuria or flank pain. This asymptomatic pyuria is initially treated with skepticism — in an asymptomatic patient, pyuria and bacteriuria are often "noise" not warranting treatment — but her youth changes that calculus: unlike in older adults, where asymptomatic bacteriuria/pyuria is common and nonspecific, pyuria in a young, otherwise healthy patient is much more likely to be meaningful. A CT abdomen and pelvis is obtained (deferred until after a negative pregnancy test) and shows bilateral pyelonephritis. Urine culture and two of two blood cultures grow Klebsiella pneumoniae. She receives broad-spectrum antibiotics narrowed to ceftriaxone and is discharged four days later.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

|---|---|
| Five days of fever, myalgias, nausea/vomiting in a cachectic young woman with known anorexia nervosa | A cognitive autopsy on the tempo (too little time to explain the degree of muscle loss) suggests a chronic process underlies the acute presentation, raising infection risk from malnutrition-driven immunocompromise |
| Diffuse myalgias without weakness, later a normal CK | Down-weights inflammatory myopathy/myositis as the primary process |
| Negative COVID, RSV, and influenza | Down-weights a common viral syndrome as the sole explanation, despite it being the initial working assumption |

| Heart rate rises from 120 to 150 with repeat vitals; temperature climbs to 105°F | Raises concern for a worsening process and moves away from a diagnosis (e.g., PE) that a single snapshot alone might have suggested |
| True defervescence after an NSAID | Favors inflammatory/cytokine-mediated fever over a non-inflammatory cause (toxidrome, thyrotoxicosis, drug fever) |
| Normal TSH and free T4 | Down-weights thyrotoxicosis |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The default illness script for a young, previously healthy-appearing patient with fever and myalgias is a viral syndrome, and the discussant states this anchor explicitly ("I was so confident she had COVID or the flu... what else could this be?"). The anchor is only overturned by negative testing, illustrating both the pull of a demographic-driven [[Representativeness Heuristic]] and the discipline required to keep testing an assumption rather than stopping once it feels satisfying.

A [[Cognitive Autopsy]] is explicitly named and used as a structured backward-reasoning tool: rather than pattern-matching the presentation to a single illness script, the discussant reasons from the degree of cachexia backward to the required tempo of disease, correctly inferring a chronic process before any chronic diagnosis is confirmed.

[[Diagnostic Humility]] is repeatedly voiced rather than premature closure — "I don't have an answer yet," "I can't blame the urine yet" — modeling comfort holding multiple live hypotheses (myositis, viral syndrome, thyrotoxicosis, sympathomimetic toxicity, pyelonephritis) simultaneously rather than committing early.

The pyuria interpretation is itself a [[Base Rate]] and test-interpretation lesson: a clinician who reflexively applies the "don't treat asymptomatic bacteriuria" heuristic learned from older-adult populations risks under-weighting a genuinely actionable finding in a young patient, where the pretest probability that pyuria reflects real infection is much higher. Recognizing that test characteristics are population-specific — not just diagnosis-specific — is itself a subtle bias to guard against.

## Teaching Discussion

Bilateral pyelonephritis can occur without localizing urinary symptoms in a meaningful minority of cases (roughly 15% by the discussant's estimate) and can present as an unexplained high fever in a young patient; imaging (CT abdomen/pelvis) is often what secures the diagnosis rather than symptoms alone.

In malnourished patients, a "normal" creatinine can still represent meaningful renal impairment, because reduced muscle mass lowers the expected baseline creatinine — creatinine must be interpreted relative to the patient's muscle mass, not against a fixed population reference range.

Distinguishing inflammatory fever from non-inflammatory temperature elevation draws on a cluster of findings: blood pressure (elevated favors sympathomimetic toxicity), lactate (elevated favors an increased metabolic rate from a toxidrome), potassium (low favors a catecholamine-driven intracellular shift), and response to antipyretics (true defervescence favors an inflammatory, cytokine-mediated fever).

A positive nitrite test together with pyuria and the absence of squamous epithelial cells suggests a well-collected sample and an Enterobacteriaceae organism (capable of converting nitrate to nitrite), which is more specific than leukocyte esterase alone.

Interpreting a positive urinalysis in a patient without urinary symptoms requires weighing alternative sources of pyuria — spillover irritation of the urinary tract from an adjacent process such as severe appendicitis or diverticulitis — before settling on a primary urinary source; here, the combination of very high pyuria, nitrite positivity, and a subsequently confirmatory CT scan resolved that ambiguity.
