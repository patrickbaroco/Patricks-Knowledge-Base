## Pearls

- The differential for a new cavitary lung lesion should be worked through roughly in order: infection first, then cancer (classically squamous cell carcinoma), then autoimmune disease (granulomatosis with polyangiitis) — with ischemia/pulmonary infarction from a PE added to the front of the list specifically in patients who are hypercoagulable, such as those with underlying glomerulonephritis or vasculitis.
- The type of immunosuppression a patient is on predicts what they are vulnerable to: cell-mediated immunodeficiency (from agents like prednisone and rituximab that deplete lymphocytes) raises risk for intracellular organisms such as mycobacteria and endemic mycoses, while neutropenia raises risk for molds and bacteria.
- Exposure history for a cavitary lesion should focus on country of origin/travel and latent TB testing (for mycobacteria), geographic location such as travel to a fungal-endemic region (for endemic mycoses like coccidioidomycosis), and outdoor/soil activities (for environmental molds like Aspergillus and Mucor). A positive exposure raises suspicion; a negative one does not meaningfully lower it, since exposure is common and under-recognized.
- The most common cause of a cavitary lesion overall is a polymicrobial lung abscess, which is a clinical diagnosis made by response to prolonged (6-8 week) antibiotics rather than by direct sampling — sicker or more immunosuppressed patients warrant a more aggressive upfront workup (sputum AFB/quantiferon, serologic panels, possibly bronchoscopy) rather than the empiric-antibiotics-and-reimage approach appropriate for a lower-risk host.
- Aspergillus serologic tests are not interchangeable: BAL galactomannan is the most reliable, serum galactomannan is a reasonable second, and antibody-class tests (IgG/IgE) are a distant third — an antibody test only proves prior exposure and is required for diagnosing ABPA, but it does not confirm invasive disease.
- ANCA titers correlate poorly with vasculitis disease activity; watching for other organ manifestations of a flare (hematuria, RBC casts, scleritis, nasal ulcers) and tracking B-cell markers as a surrogate for whether immunosuppression (e.g., rituximab) is actually working are more useful than repeating ANCA serologies.
- Rapid interval growth of a cavitary lesion over days narrows the differential meaningfully — it argues against slow-growing organisms (mycobacteria, most endemic mycoses) and toward either a fast-growing bacterial or angioinvasive fungal process, or a vasculitic flare causing rapid ischemic tissue loss.
- "Green herring syndrome" describes cavitary pulmonary mucormycosis occurring as an occult co-infection underneath what initially looks like a straightforward bacterial lung abscess — a variation on the "red herring" concept, distinguished by the greenish sputum some patients produce; it is a much less common presentation than the classic nodular, angioinvasive mucormycosis seen in neutropenic hosts.

## Problem Representation

A 73-year-old man on multi-agent immunosuppression for pauci-immune glomerulonephritis, presenting with a rapidly growing cavitary lung lesion initially attributed to invasive aspergillosis on serology, ultimately diagnosed by biopsy as cavitary pulmonary mucormycosis (green herring syndrome) superimposed on a bacterial process.

## Case Narrative

The patient was a 73-year-old man with a history of pauci-immune glomerulonephritis maintained on prednisone, rituximab, and mycophenolate, who presented with cough, fever, leukocytosis, and an elevated ESR and CRP. Rather than starting with a plain chest x-ray, the discussion went directly to CT chest, reasoning that the patient's presentation did not fit any of the three things a chest x-ray is genuinely good for (heart failure, pneumothorax, or an acute community-acquired pneumonia syndrome), and that in a markedly immunosuppressed, high-risk host, the greater diagnostic yield and clarity of CT justified skipping straight to it. The CT showed a left upper lobe cavitary lesion.

The general approach to a cavitary lung lesion was laid out as a journey: infection first (because a cavity represents necrotic lung tissue, and infection is the most common cause of that necrosis), then cancer if infection is excluded (with squamous cell carcinoma as the leading cell type), then autoimmune disease, specifically granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA), if both infection and cancer are excluded. An additional consideration was flagged for patients like this one who carry an underlying vasculitis or glomerulonephritis: because such patients are hypercoagulable, a pulmonary embolism with subsequent infarction can itself produce a cavitary lesion, and should be kept near the front of the list rather than reached only after infection and cancer are exhausted. The patient's underlying disease also meant that a cavitary lesion should raise earlier suspicion for GPA itself than it would in a patient without a known vasculitis.

To make progress on the infection category specifically, a framework based on the source of the organism was used: infections arising from the patient's own oropharyngeal flora (aspiration-type, polymicrobial lung abscess) versus infections arising from the environment (mycobacteria, endemic mycoses, and molds like Aspergillus and Mucor). The type of immunosuppression the patient was on was used to weight this differential — prednisone and rituximab, both of which deplete lymphocytes, create cell-mediated immunodeficiency, raising particular concern for intracellular organisms like mycobacteria and endemic fungi, as opposed to neutropenia, which would raise concern more specifically for molds and bacteria. An exposure history was recommended focusing on country of origin and travel (for TB risk and latent TB testing history), geographic location (for endemic mycoses — coccidioidomycosis was specifically flagged given travel to California's Central Valley as an example), and outdoor activity (for environmental molds and less common organisms like Nocardia and Actinomyces). It was emphasized that a positive exposure history raises suspicion for a given organism, but a negative history does not meaningfully lower it, since most patients cannot reliably characterize or recall all of their past exposures.

On the question of how aggressively to work up a cavitary lesion, the default in a lower-risk host was described as presumptive treatment for lung abscess (the most common cause overall), diagnosed retrospectively by clinical and radiographic response to 6-8 weeks of antibiotics rather than by direct sampling, with sputum AFB/culture sent given that roughly 5% of TB cases have no identifiable exposure risk factors. In a markedly immunosuppressed host like this patient, however, a more aggressive initial approach was favored: sending a broad panel of serologic markers (endemic mycosis serologies, cryptococcal antigen, beta-D-glucan, galactomannan) alongside sputum studies, and considering bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) despite its procedural risk, plus ANCA-associated markers, specifically B-cell counts, to assess whether the patient's rituximab was still adequately suppressing the underlying vasculitis.

In this patient, an aggressive initial workup was pursued, including a BAL that was negative for any organism on culture or targeted testing, but a serologic Aspergillus test came back positive. The patient was started on voriconazole and discharged. Because the specific serologic assay was not clearly an antigen-based test (like BAL or serum galactomannan) but appeared instead to be an antibody-class test, this was flagged as a weak basis for the diagnosis — an antibody test only demonstrates prior exposure to Aspergillus (and is the test required to diagnose allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis), it does not confirm active invasive disease, particularly when the more sensitive and specific BAL galactomannan had already come back negative.

Within days, the patient developed worsening shortness of breath, cough, and more persistent fever, and repeat CT showed the lesion had roughly tripled in size. This rapid interval growth was used to substantially reframe the differential: it argued against slow-growing organisms like mycobacteria and most endemic mycoses, and against Aspergillus specifically given that the patient was already on voriconazole (though resistance was acknowledged as a possibility). It raised concern for either an acute bacterial superinfection (the patient had not been started on any antibacterial coverage alongside the voriconazole) or a vasculitic flare causing rapid ischemic tissue loss, given the patient's underlying ANCA-associated disease. Mucor was also raised despite the patient lacking the more classic mucor risk factor profile (steroids in a post-COVID patient, or neutropenia with the more typical nodular/angioinvasive radiographic pattern) — introducing the entity of "green herring syndrome," a form of cavitary mucormycosis that occurs as an occult co-infection underneath an apparent bacterial lung abscess, distinguished from the more common nodular mucormycosis seen in neutropenic hosts, and named for the greenish sputum color some patients produce (an allusion to "red herring," since the bacteria found on culture can distract from the co-infecting mold hiding beneath it).

The patient was started on broad antibacterial coverage (ampicillin-sulbactam, with anaerobic coverage emphasized as essential for a presumed lung abscess) but did not improve, prompting a CT-guided percutaneous biopsy, which revealed mucormycosis. There are no reliable non-invasive serologic markers for mucor (a blood-based PCR exists but has limited real-world availability and performance), and tissue diagnosis is required in most cases; the disease can behave subacutely or can progress to death within hours to days once it accelerates. The patient tragically passed away. Voriconazole, effective against Aspergillus, does not cover Mucor species, which was noted as a key pharmacologic gap explaining why the patient continued to deteriorate on that regimen. The episode was dedicated to the memory of Dr. Francisco Marty, an infectious disease physician who first described and publicized this entity.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| Subacute cough/fever/leukocytosis in a heavily immunosuppressed host | Lowers probability of typical CAP; raises probability of an atypical/opportunistic infectious or autoimmune process |
| Left upper lobe cavitary lesion on CT | Triggers infection → cancer → autoimmune → (ischemia, given hypercoagulable vasculitis) framework |
| Negative BAL for organisms | Lowers confidence in common bacterial/fungal/mycobacterial causes, but does not exclude them |
| Positive Aspergillus antibody-class serology with negative BAL galactomannan | Weak/tentative evidence for aspergillosis — antibody alone proves exposure, not invasive disease |
| Lesion triples in size within days despite voriconazole | Strongly lowers probability of Aspergillus (or argues for resistance); raises probability of bacterial superinfection, vasculitic flare, or a mold not covered by voriconazole (Mucor) |
| No antibacterial coverage given alongside voriconazole | Leaves a bacterial superinfection unaddressed, consistent with continued deterioration |
| Biopsy showing mucormycosis | Confirms green herring syndrome (mucor + bacterial co-infection) as the actual diagnosis |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Green herring syndrome illness script: a patient with a presumed bacterial lung abscess (often on steroids, sometimes with other immunosuppression) who fails to improve on adequate antibacterial therapy, ultimately found on biopsy to have co-infecting Mucor beneath the bacterial process; distinct from classic angioinvasive pulmonary mucormycosis, which occurs in neutropenic hosts and produces large nodules rather than cavitary lesions.

The case illustrates a form of anchoring bias around serologic test results: a positive but low-specificity antibody test was allowed to anchor management (voriconazole, discharge) despite a negative, more specific BAL galactomannan pointing the other way. The rapid clinical deterioration is what forced re-evaluation of that anchor — a reminder that treatment response (or the lack of it) is itself diagnostic information that should be actively monitored and acted upon, not just awaited passively.

## Teaching Discussion

The episode's structural teaching point is the layered approach to a cavitary lung lesion — infection, then cancer, then autoimmune disease, with ischemia/infarction folded in early for hypercoagulable patients — paired with an immunosuppression-specific and exposure-specific approach to narrowing the infectious differential before reaching for invasive testing.

A second major teaching point concerns test performance characteristics: not all "positive Aspergillus serology" results carry the same diagnostic weight, and clinicians should know which assay was actually sent (BAL galactomannan > serum galactomannan > antibody) before anchoring management decisions on it. This generalizes to any serologic test with a range of possible assay types and performance characteristics.

The final and most consequential teaching point is that a lack of clinical improvement — or in this case, active worsening — on a seemingly appropriate empiric regimen is itself diagnostic data, and should trigger active reconsideration of the working diagnosis (including entities not fully covered by the current regimen) rather than simply extending the same treatment and waiting longer.
