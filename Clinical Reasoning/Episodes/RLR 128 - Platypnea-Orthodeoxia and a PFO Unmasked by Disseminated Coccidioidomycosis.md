## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Robbie returns from illness to deliver the long-promised "part two" of a case first presented in RLR 123 (Case 1): an older man with a bioprosthetic aortic valve, presumed culture-negative endocarditis, and an in-hospital MCA stroke, now awaiting TEE. A single ICU nursing observation — the patient's oxygenation and breathing are fine supine but collapse when he sits up — reroutes the entire diagnosis. Reza immediately recognizes platypnea-orthodeoxia (an explicit callback to the wide-open PFO case in RLR 125) and pivots the workup away from valvular endocarditis toward intracardiac shunting. The TEE with bubble study confirms a hemodynamically significant PFO with no vegetation; a CT chest reveals a right middle lobe mass with diffuse pulmonary nodules. After a detour into the physiology of stress leukograms and a live differential for concurrent neutrophilia-and-eosinophilia (deployed as a "friend's text" that turns out to be this same patient's own overlooked labs), the case resolves as disseminated miliary coccidioidomycosis — fungal microemboli raising pulmonary vascular pressures enough to pop open a previously silent PFO, producing both the positional hypoxemia and, via paradoxical embolism or coccidioidal meningeal vasculitis, the stroke.

## Pearls

- The most common cause of culture-negative endocarditis is antibiotics administered before blood cultures were drawn — always confirm the sequence explicitly before assuming true culture-negative disease.
- TEE is substantially more sensitive than TTE for endocarditis, but PET-CT can be even more sensitive still in select cases — some vegetations missed on TEE have been picked up by PET-CT.
- Orthopnea is technically named backwards from how it's colloquially used (ortho = "upright," pnea = "breathing" — literally "breathing fine upright"), but is conventionally understood to mean dyspnea while supine; a fun etymology aside with no real clinical import.
- Platypnea (subjective breathlessness worse when upright) and orthodeoxia (objective hypoxemia worse when upright) do not have to occur together, though both point to the same narrow differential: shunt physiology, either intracardiac (e.g., a PFO with right-to-left shunting) or extracardiac/intrapulmonary (e.g., hepatopulmonary syndrome, pulmonary AVMs).
- A subtle but important detail commonly missed on a busy service: this phenomenon is rarely caught not because of negligence, but because early workup is appropriately focused on more urgent problems — a reminder that "the last doctor to see the patient is often the smartest," since they benefit from more time and more accumulated data (an "Uncle Bob" teaching).
- On bubble study timing: rapid (near-immediate) appearance of bubbles on the left side indicates an intracardiac shunt (e.g., PFO); a delayed appearance (roughly 3-6 cardiac cycles) points to an extracardiac/intrapulmonary shunt instead.
- A PFO is present in roughly 1 in 5 people and is usually an incidental finding — the literature on whether/when to close an incidentally found PFO after stroke (particularly without a demonstrated DVT) remains genuinely unsettled. What elevates a PFO from "incidental" to "the lesion" is objective evidence of clinically significant shunting (rapid bubble transit) plus a compatible clinical syndrome (platypnea-orthodeoxia) — not the mere presence of the PFO on imaging.
- Anatomic reasoning for embolic source and septic pulmonary findings: an arterial (e.g., MCA) stroke from an intracardiac source generally implicates the left heart (aortic or mitral valve, left atrial/ventricular mass) unless there is a right-to-left shunt allowing right-sided material to cross over. Septic pulmonary emboli, by contrast, implicate a right-sided vegination (classically tricuspid), since right-sided material naturally embolizes forward into the pulmonary circulation.
- Radiographic pattern can help distinguish septic pulmonary emboli (more likely cavitary lesions) from vegetation-associated heart-failure infiltrates (more likely fluffy/alveolar opacities) — useful early framing, though not definitive on its own.
- Right atrial pressure is normally lower than left atrial pressure, so an incidental PFO typically shunts left-to-right (relatively benign — oxygenated blood mixing into the right side). Right-to-left shunting through a previously silent PFO requires something to raise right-sided pressures above left-sided pressures — classically an acute process like a large pulmonary embolus causing acute pulmonary hypertension, but any cause of pulmonary hypertension can do this.
- When a patient develops an unexplained new physiologic derangement, it can be useful to mentally walk through the WHO pulmonary hypertension groups (1 through 5) and ask which of them could plausibly evolve fast enough to explain an acute presentation — acute PE stands out as the group most compatible with a rapid timeline.
- Classic stress-response leukogram physiology: endogenous corticosteroids released during physiologic stress cause eosinopenia (eosinophils are steroid-sensitive) and neutrophilia (via neutrophil demargination) — so the "expected" pattern in any acutely stressed/ill patient is neutrophilia paired with a low eosinophil count. Concurrent neutrophilia AND eosinophilia together is an atypical combination that should prompt a specific search for a process that can override the expected eosinopenic stress response.
- When working up concurrent significant neutrophilia and eosinophilia, anchor the differential on the eosinophilia side (a shorter, more specific list) rather than the neutrophilia side (far too broad to be useful), then ask which eosinophilia-driving processes can also independently drive neutrophilia. Primary causes include leukemia and hypereosinophilic syndrome; secondary causes include adrenal insufficiency, allergy, autoimmune disease (EGPA notably, though its illness script of lung/kidney/nerve/small-vessel involvement often fits poorly with a lung mass and embolic stroke), neoplasm, and infection (particularly fungal and parasitic).
- Endemic mycoses — coccidioidomycosis prominently, but also historically described with tuberculosis — can cause a strikingly wide range of CBC abnormalities: pancytopenia from bone marrow infiltration, severe neutrophilia, or marked eosinophilia, depending on the case. This range is easy to forget and worth deliberately keeping on the differential when a hematologic picture doesn't fit the presumed primary diagnosis.
- A mass-with-eosinophilia-and-neutrophilia "Venn diagram" is a useful way to triangulate a differential: fungal infection (with cavitary "fungus ball" physiology, analogous to a TB ball), autoimmune disease, and malignancy are the major buckets, with the neutrophilia and hypercoagulable/embolic context helping to weigh which bucket is most likely.
- Solid tumor-associated eosinophilia is a real phenomenon (especially with mucosal-surface cancers like lung and GI malignancies, and more classically with lymphomas/cutaneous T-cell lymphoma) but is usually more modest than a magnitude in the low thousands — a very high eosinophil count should raise the bar for reconsidering infection or a primary hematologic process even after cancer starts to feel likely.
- Coccidioidomycosis incidence has been rising sharply in California (roughly fivefold in San Francisco per this discussion), and it can mimic other classic syndromes (e.g., Löfgren syndrome — erythema nodosum, arthritis, hilar adenopathy) — a reminder to keep cocci higher on the differential in endemic regions given rising local prevalence.
- Disseminated/miliary fungal infection can cause pulmonary hypertension through hematogenous microembolic plugging of small pulmonary vessels, and this mechanism can be severe enough to reverse flow across a previously silent PFO, producing both positional hypoxemia and a pathway for paradoxical embolization.
- When a diagnosis resolves cleanly in retrospect, it's still important to acknowledge the limits of clinical reasoning: had the biopsy instead shown, say, a lung cancer with paraneoplastic eosinophilia and miliary-pattern metastatic spread, the same reasoning process could not have reliably distinguished that from the actual fungal diagnosis in advance — some diagnoses are only confirmable, not reliably predictable, from the bedside.

## Problem Representation

An older man with a bioprosthetic aortic valve, presumed culture-negative infective endocarditis, and an in-hospital MCA stroke develops positional hypoxemia (platypnea-orthodeoxia) that redirects the workup away from valvular endocarditis and toward a hemodynamically significant PFO with right-to-left shunting, ultimately explained by disseminated miliary pulmonary coccidioidomycosis producing pulmonary hypertension, marked neutrophilia and eosinophilia, and a paradoxical (or coccidioidal-meningitis-associated) embolic stroke.

## Case Narrative

This episode opens as an explicit continuation of the first case from RLR 123, in which Robbie had presented a patient with a bioprosthetic aortic valve and three weeks of malaise, prominent at rest, who was found to have a white count of 29,000, an elevated BNP, peripheral opacities on chest x-ray, and a new MCA stroke syndrome that developed during hospitalization. TPA had been withheld given a high pretest probability of an infected valve and the associated risk of hemorrhagic transformation. A CT of the abdomen and pelvis showed no infectious focus, blood cultures were negative, and the TTE was negative; the patient was started empirically on vancomycin and ceftriaxone for endocarditis and scheduled for a TEE.

Reza opened by teaching that the most common cause of culture-negative endocarditis is antibiotics given before blood cultures were collected, and confirmed directly with Robbie — who, as the treating emergency physician, could answer definitively — that cultures had in fact been drawn first. With that possibility set aside, Reza's recommended next steps were either the pending TEE (more sensitive than TTE) or a PET-CT, which he noted can occasionally catch vegetations that a TEE misses.

The case took its pivotal turn once the patient was moved to the ICU for hourly neurochecks after the stroke. There, the team noticed that his oxygen saturation and breathing were excellent while supine but that he developed severe hypoxemia and tachypnea when sitting upright. Reza recognized this immediately as reminiscent of another recent case (an explicit callback to the wide-open PFO case discussed in RLR 125), and worked through the terminology: platypnea (subjective breathlessness worse upright) and orthodeoxia (objective hypoxemia worse upright), which needn't occur together but which both point to a narrow differential of shunt physiology — intracardiac (e.g., a PFO) or extracardiac/intrapulmonary (e.g., hepatopulmonary syndrome). He noted this finding wasn't missed by the primary team due to any error — early on, the clinical picture was moving too fast, from fatigue to stroke to a white count of 29,000, to prioritize positional vital sign testing — invoking the teaching that the last physician to see a patient often appears the smartest, simply by virtue of more time and accumulated data. Reza recommended a TTE with bubble study to look for an extracardiac shunt, speculating (without confirming the exact mechanism) that gravity likely explains the positional pattern, and connected the physiology back to the stroke: if a mass or thrombus (referencing Robbie's prior teaching on bland, non-infective thrombus) were redirecting flow across a defect depending on body position, it could account for both the paradoxical embolic-type stroke and the positional hypoxemia.

The team ordered a CT chest with contrast and upgraded the already-planned TEE to include a bubble study. Before results returned, Reza reasoned through the anatomy: an arterial stroke from an intracardiac source should implicate the left heart directly, unless a right-to-left shunt were allowing material from the right side to cross over. Pulmonary opacities, meanwhile, could reflect either septic emboli (implicating a right-sided vegetation, classically tricuspid, since right-sided material embolizes forward into the lungs) or heart-failure-pattern infiltrates from a valve-obstructing vegetation, distinguishable radiographically as cavitary (septic) versus fluffy/alveolar (heart failure). Given the platypnea-orthodeoxia finding, Reza speculated the pathology might in fact be right-sided after all — potentially explaining both the shunting and a pathway for paradoxical embolization to the brain.

The results returned: the TEE with bubble study showed no vegetation on the left side and a normal, well-seated bioprosthetic aortic valve, but did show a PFO with right-to-left intracardiac shunting, with bubbles appearing rapidly — confirming an intracardiac (not extracardiac) shunt. The CT chest with contrast revealed a large consolidative mass in the right middle lobe with diffuse nodules scattered throughout both lung fields.

Reza first addressed anatomy and PFO epidemiology: while the right ventricle, being the more anterior cardiac structure, is often better visualized on TTE, TEE remains superior overall due to less signal attenuation from bone and muscle. He reminded the audience that a PFO is present in roughly one in five people and is usually incidental — the literature on closing an incidentally discovered PFO after stroke without a demonstrated DVT remains genuinely unsettled — but that this patient's objective, rapidly-transiting shunt plus his platypnea-orthodeoxia syndrome moved the PFO well beyond an incidental finding into the actual lesion driving his physiology.

He then reasoned through the mass and nodules: could there be a missed right-sided vegetation causing septic emboli to the lungs, with the PFO separately explaining the stroke? He judged this unlikely, since neither the TTE nor the TEE had found any cardiac mass on either side. Instead, he built out the physiology of PFO pressure dynamics: left atrial pressure normally exceeds right atrial pressure, so an incidental PFO typically shunts left-to-right and is hemodynamically inconsequential. Right-to-left shunting requires something to raise right-sided pressures above left-sided pressures — classically an acute process like a large PE causing acute pulmonary hypertension. Using the lung mass and nodules as the entry point, and mentally working through the WHO pulmonary hypertension groups to identify which could evolve rapidly enough to fit this timeline, Reza landed on a working hypothesis: metastatic lung cancer producing a hypercoagulable state, triggering a pulmonary embolus, causing acute pulmonary hypertension, which reversed flow across the PFO — with a fragment of that same embolic process crossing paradoxically to cause the stroke.

At this point Robbie introduced what he framed as an unrelated question from a colleague texting him mid-recording: a patient with significant, simultaneous neutrophilia and eosinophilia — a combination he said he'd never seen described together. Reza worked through the physiology live: physiologic stress triggers endogenous corticosteroid release, which causes eosinopenia (eosinophils are steroid-sensitive) and neutrophilia (via demargination) — so the expected pattern in any stressed or acutely ill patient is neutrophilia with a low eosinophil count, making true concurrent neutrophilia and eosinophilia atypical and diagnostically meaningful. Reza organized his differential around the eosinophilia (judging the neutrophilia list alone too broad to be useful), considering primary causes (leukemia, hypereosinophilic syndrome) and secondary causes (adrenal insufficiency, allergy, autoimmune disease — with EGPA raised but felt to fit poorly given its classic lung/kidney/nerve/vessel illness script — neoplasm, and infection, particularly fungal and parasitic). Balancing the mass, nodules, embolic phenomenon, and neutrophilia, he judged autoimmune disease unlikely and leaned toward malignancy — specifically lung cancer with an associated hypercoagulable state — while flagging that a solid tumor typically produces a more modest degree of eosinophilia than what was being described, and that hypereosinophilic syndrome remained a formal consideration but was hard to reconcile with the PFO and embolic stroke storyline.

Robbie then revealed the framing device: there was no colleague's text — the neutrophilia-and-eosinophilia question was about this same patient's own admission labs, which had never been previously flagged in the chart. Of the original white count of 29,000, 23,000 were neutrophils and 4,500 were eosinophils, a detail that had gone unnoticed amid the fast-moving progression from fatigue to stroke to presumed endocarditis. The team proceeded to close the PFO and performed a bronchoscopy. The diagnostic bronchoalveolar lavage fungal culture grew Coccidioides immitis. With azole antifungal therapy and PFO closure, the patient's neutrophilia and eosinophilia resolved, and he was recovering well toward discharge.

The unifying explanation: disseminated, miliary-pattern pulmonary coccidioidomycosis, with hematogenous fungal spread producing innumerable small nodules that plugged small pulmonary vessels, raising pulmonary vascular pressures enough to reverse flow across the patient's previously silent PFO — producing both the platypnea-orthodeoxia and a pathway for the stroke. Robbie noted two competing (and not definitively distinguished) explanations for the stroke itself: paradoxical embolization of small clot fragments across the now chronically shunting PFO, given the lungs' impaired filtering capacity, or coccidioidal meningitis with an associated cerebral vasculitis, a possibility ID had raised but could not confirm. The cardiologists ultimately closed the PFO based on the degree of shunting demonstrated, independent of which stroke mechanism was correct. Robbie added that he had never previously encountered neutrophilia associated with coccidioidomycosis, but learned in researching the case that endemic mycoses generally (as with tuberculosis) can produce strikingly variable CBC findings — from pancytopenia with marrow infiltration to severe neutrophilia to marked eosinophilia. He closed by noting the sharply rising incidence of coccidioidomycosis in California (roughly fivefold in San Francisco) and a concurrent case that week mimicking Löfgren syndrome, arguing for keeping cocci higher on the differential given how quickly its local prevalence is climbing.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Impact |
|---|---|
| Blood cultures confirmed drawn before antibiotics | Rules out the most common cause of culture-negative endocarditis; keeps true infective endocarditis on the table pending TEE/PET-CT |
| Positional hypoxemia/tachypnea (supine well, upright hypoxemic) | Pivots the differential toward shunt physiology (platypnea-orthodeoxia) and away from valvular endocarditis as the sole driver |
| TEE with bubble study: no vegetation, normal bioprosthetic valve, rapid-transit right-to-left PFO shunt | Effectively rules out endocarditis as the source of the stroke; confirms a hemodynamically significant (not incidental) intracardiac shunt |
| CT chest: right middle lobe consolidative mass + diffuse nodules | Raises the differential toward either infection (septic emboli, fungal), malignancy, or an inflammatory process capable of elevating pulmonary vascular pressure |
| No mass found on either TTE or TEE | Lowers likelihood of septic emboli from an occult cardiac vegetation; redirects attention toward a primary pulmonary process |
| Neutrophilia (23,000) with concurrent significant eosinophilia (4,500) | Atypical combination for a routine stress response; narrows toward leukemia, hypereosinophilic syndrome, select autoimmune disease, malignancy, or fungal/parasitic infection |
| EGPA's classic lung/kidney/nerve/vessel illness script vs. this presentation | Lowers likelihood of autoimmune vasculitis as the unifying diagnosis |
| BAL fungal culture positive for Coccidioides | Confirms disseminated miliary coccidioidomycosis as the unifying diagnosis, explaining the mass, nodules, neutrophilia, eosinophilia, and (via pulmonary hypertension) the PFO shunting |
| Resolution of neutrophilia/eosinophilia with azole therapy and PFO closure | Retrospectively confirms the fungal/hemodynamic mechanism as the correct unifying explanation |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Anchoring on the established working diagnosis (endocarditis)**: the entire team's framework was built around infective endocarditis until a single nursing observation (positional hypoxemia) forced a re-evaluation — a reminder that new, seemingly minor findings deserve active pursuit even when a diagnosis already feels settled.
- **"Last doctor to see the patient is often the smartest" (Uncle Bob teaching)**: explicitly used to explain, without blame, why the platypnea-orthodeoxia phenomenon wasn't caught by the primary team during the fast-moving initial presentation.
- **Anchoring on neutrophilia as the more "obvious" abnormality**: Reza explicitly reasoned that starting the differential from neutrophilia (extremely broad) would be unproductive, and instead anchored on the rarer, more specific eosinophilia — a deliberate strategy for narrowing an overwhelming differential.
- **The "friend's text" framing device**: Robbie's fabricated framing (later revealed as this same patient's own overlooked labs) functioned as a structured teaching tool to isolate and test pure hematologic reasoning before revealing how directly it applied to the case at hand — worth noting as a case-presentation technique in itself.
- **Calibrated humility about predictive limits**: both hosts explicitly acknowledged that had the biopsy shown paraneoplastic eosinophilia from a lung cancer with miliary metastatic spread instead of coccidioidomycosis, their reasoning process could not have reliably distinguished that possibility in advance — some diagnoses are confirmable only in retrospect.

## Teaching Discussion

This episode is a study in how a single new physical exam or monitoring finding — positional hypoxemia — can override an entrenched working diagnosis, and in how structuring an overwhelming differential (concurrent neutrophilia and eosinophilia) around the rarer, more specific abnormality rather than the broader one can make an intractable problem tractable. It also models diagnostic humility explicitly: both hosts acknowledge that the eventual diagnosis (disseminated coccidioidomycosis) could not have been reliably predicted over an equally plausible alternative (paraneoplastic malignancy) purely from the bedside reasoning available at the time — the case had to be confirmed, not just reasoned to.
