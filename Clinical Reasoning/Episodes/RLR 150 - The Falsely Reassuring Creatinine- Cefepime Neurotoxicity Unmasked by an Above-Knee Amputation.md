## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 71-year-old man with critical limb ischemia and calcaneal osteomyelitis, admitted for revascularization and debridement, develops progressive encephalopathy with visual hallucinations over several days despite an unremarkable initial toxic-metabolic workup and a normal head CT. Robbie, presenting the case as it evolved on the wards, withholds two key details from Reza as a teaching exercise: the patient has a pre-existing contralateral above-knee amputation, and he is on cefepime. The reveal ties together a single teaching point — a falsely reassuring creatinine in a patient with reduced muscle mass masked significant renal impairment (confirmed by cystatin C), leading to cefepime accumulation and neurotoxic encephalopathy that resolved fully once the dose was corrected.

## Pearls

- The three most common causes of skin ulcers are venous, neuropathic, and arterial — together accounting for roughly 95% of cases, analogous to how heart and lung disease account for about 95% of causes of dyspnea.
- An ulcer's infection is a consequence of the ulcer, not its cause; the diagnostic and therapeutic priority is understanding why the ulcer exists in the first place (most often compromised blood flow).
- Limb-loss risk escalates with proximity: losing a toe carries some downstream risk, losing a foot more, and losing a limb carries a meaningfully higher associated mortality risk.
- The most common cause of altered mental status in a hospitalized patient is often something done to the patient — a medication effect — making iatrogenic causes the first branch to check.
- First-pass bedside workup for acute hospital-onset confusion: point-of-care glucose, a clear sense of the patient's baseline mental status and functional level from nursing, and a review of any new sedating or opiate/neuropathic-pain medications.
- Once immediately reversible and truly life-threatening causes of encephalopathy have been excluded (hypoglycemia, an evolving bleed, an overt toxic ingestion), a clinician can appropriately slow down — not every case needs to be fully solved in the first hour.
- Non-convulsive seizure should stay on the differential for unexplained encephalopathy once toxic-metabolic and structural causes have been reasonably excluded, even without overt convulsive activity.
- Serum creatinine is a marker of muscle mass as much as it is a marker of renal filtration — in a patient with significantly reduced muscle mass (e.g., after limb amputation), creatinine can substantially underestimate true renal impairment.
- Cystatin C is a more reliable marker of GFR than creatinine in patients with low or atypical muscle mass, including amputees.
- Renally-dosed medications (antibiotics, gabapentinoids, opioids) should be reconsidered in any patient whose creatinine may be an unreliable proxy for true renal function.
- Cefepime, a fourth-generation cephalosporin, is a recognized cause of neurotoxicity — encephalopathy, and sometimes myoclonus — especially when it accumulates due to renal impairment.
- When a hospitalized patient's condition doesn't fit the working plan, a full physical re-exam (not just chart review) can surface findings — such as an unnoticed amputation — that immediately reframe the entire case.
- Being kind to oneself after a diagnostic delay or near-miss is part of good practice — caring enough to worry is itself evidence of good clinical instincts, not a reason for self-punishment.

## Problem Representation

A 71-year-old man with hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and longstanding peripheral arterial disease, admitted to vascular surgery for critical limb ischemia with calcaneal osteomyelitis status post revascularization and debridement, developed a gradually progressive encephalopathy with visual and auditory hallucinations over several hospital days despite an unremarkable toxic-metabolic screen and a normal head CT, ultimately explained by cefepime neurotoxicity driven by a falsely reassuring creatinine that failed to reflect his true degree of renal impairment in the setting of a pre-existing contralateral above-knee amputation and correspondingly reduced muscle mass.

## Case Narrative

Robbie presents this case from his own ward service, narrating it as it evolved organically in real time. The patient, in his 70s, was admitted to the vascular surgery service for critical limb ischemia complicated by calcaneal wounds and subsequent osteomyelitis. Before diving into the encephalopathy that would become the heart of the case, Robbie invites Reza to teach on the underlying vascular presentation itself: the patient underwent successful revascularization via angioplasty and stent placement, and simultaneously underwent wound debridement with podiatry, which revealed bony involvement with a polymicrobial infection on histopathology. He was started on dual antiplatelet therapy and IV antibiotics and admitted for post-procedural monitoring.

Reza uses the case to review a bread-and-butter teaching point: the three most common causes of skin ulcers are venous, neuropathic, and arterial disease, together accounting for roughly 95% of cases — paralleling how heart and lung disease account for the large majority of causes of dyspnea. He stresses that the infection is a consequence of the ulcer, not its root cause; the real diagnostic question is why the ulcer exists at all, which in this case points toward compromised blood flow from atherosclerosis, whether driven by genetics, cholesterol, or other cardiometabolic risk factors. He flags that despite successful revascularization and debridement, limb salvage is not guaranteed, and that the risk of future complications escalates with the level of tissue lost — a lost toe carries some risk, a lost foot more, and a lost limb a meaningfully higher associated mortality.

Robbie rounds out the history: the patient is 71, with hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and longstanding peripheral arterial disease, who presented with progressive left lower extremity pain and a skin ulcer that culminated in the diagnoses of critical limb ischemia and contiguous osteomyelitis. On hospital day three or four, he began showing signs of mild confusion, prompting a hospitalist consult. On exam, his vital signs were normal; he was alert but had oscillating attention, was disoriented to the date and time, and had no focal neurologic deficits or other abnormalities beyond expected post-surgical findings.

Reza works through the case using a toxic-metabolic-structural-infectious framework, but treats his own instinct to reach for that framework as something running in the background rather than an immediate leap. His first concrete steps: asking for a point-of-care glucose (given the patient's diabetes and unknown medication regimen — insulin overdose being a common cause of confusion), and asking the bedside nurse for the patient's last known normal mental status and functional baseline. He also asks whether the patient has received any opiates or gabapentin, reasoning that the most common cause of altered mental status in a hospitalized patient is often something the medical team has done to the patient. He plans that if glucose and vitals are normal and no sedating medication has been given, he would next prioritize a basic metabolic panel (sodium, glucose, BUN) as key contributors to confusion, while keeping other labs and imaging in reserve.

Robbie fills in the additional data: the patient is functionally independent at baseline, living in the community without assistance. His medications include oxycodone and gabapentin (both newly started for pain control), aspirin (chronic), and rivaroxaban (newly started for the peripheral arterial disease post-stenting). Initial labs showed a normal white count, mild anemia at his baseline, normal platelets, normal electrolytes, a BUN of 24, and a creatinine of 1.3 — both at his baseline. Point-of-care glucose was normal.

Reza follows up by asking about timing — when the patient was last seen at baseline. The nurse reports the change was gradual and gentle, occurring over roughly the prior twelve hours overnight; the patient is now mildly encephalopathic — he knows his name and knows he had surgery, but has lost orientation to time.

With this timeline established, Reza works through his top differential for acute hospital-onset altered mental status, leading with toxic-metabolic causes. He flags that the patient's creatinine may not accurately reflect his GFR if his muscle mass is reduced, and that gabapentin and oxycodone can both accumulate and cause confusion in the setting of even mild chronic kidney disease. Given the new rivaroxaban and chronic aspirin, he also raises concern for an occult intracranial bleed — even without focal neurologic findings, since a small focus of hemorrhage can cause diffuse encephalopathy — and orders a CT of the head. In the back of his mind, he also holds non-convulsive seizure as a possibility if the toxic-metabolic and structural workups come back negative, and flags standard hospital-associated delirium as a plausible explanation given the patient's age, hospitalization, and recent procedures. He mentions he would also turn to GPT to check for described complications of revascularization presenting with delayed encephalopathy, given his own uncertainty about that specific association.

The CT head returns normal. The nurse clarifies the medication list further: aspirin was chronic, rivaroxaban was newly started for the stent, and both oxycodone and gabapentin were entirely new medications the patient had never taken before. Reza recommends stopping both, given their potential for causing confusion, while cautioning that discontinuing high-dose gabapentin abruptly can theoretically risk withdrawal seizures — though he's not concerned about that at this particular dose. He reflects that once immediately life-threatening and reversible causes have been reasonably excluded — glucose is fine, no bleed on CT, no signs of opiate overdose — a clinician doesn't need to solve everything at that exact moment; some diagnostic patience is appropriate.

The vascular team acts on this plan, stopping both gabapentin and oxycodone in hopes of improvement, and continues the patient's anticoagulation and antibiotics given the negative head CT. Instead of improving, however, the patient progressively worsens over the following days. He is eventually transferred to the medicine service, by which point he is completely confused with prominent visual and, to a lesser extent, auditory hallucinations, but still without other focal neurologic abnormalities.

At this point, Robbie reveals that he had deliberately withheld two pieces of information from Reza throughout the entire discussion, explaining that the team originally caring for the patient didn't have the medical knowledge to recognize their significance — "your eyes cannot see what your brain does not know." He frames it as a structured guessing exercise: he reveals the first concealed detail — the patient has severe, longstanding peripheral arterial disease in his right leg as well, resulting in a pre-existing right above-knee amputation — and asks Reza to guess the second. Reza immediately and correctly guesses: the antibiotic.

Reza explains the connection, crediting a mentor he refers to as "Uncle Bob" for teaching this lesson in a prior case involving a below-knee amputee with a deceptively normal creatinine. Serum creatinine is generated by muscle metabolism, so it functions partly as a marker of muscle mass rather than purely of renal filtration. In a patient who is missing a limb — and therefore has substantially less muscle mass than expected — creatinine will underestimate the true degree of renal impairment, making cystatin C a more reliable marker of GFR in this population. This matters not only for the antibiotic in question but for every renally-cleared medication the patient was receiving, including the gabapentin and oxycodone. Reza deduces the culprit antibiotic is cefepime, a fourth-generation cephalosporin with a well-recognized risk of neurotoxicity — including encephalopathy and myoclonus — particularly when it accumulates in the setting of unrecognized renal impairment.

Robbie confirms the diagnosis is exactly correct, revealing that the patient's cystatin C-based estimated GFR corresponded to CKD stage 4, while his creatinine-based estimate corresponded to only CKD stage 2 — a dramatic discrepancy driven by his reduced muscle mass from the amputation. Once cefepime was dose-adjusted for his true renal function, the patient woke up and fully recovered over the following two to three days.

Both hosts emphasize that this reflects no fault on the part of the vascular team or any other service involved — everyone involved, including the PM&R-equivalent workup earlier in the episode and the vascular team's own reasonable initial steps, acted out of genuine care for the patient. Reza reflects that mistakes and worry in medicine are themselves evidence of caring, and that self-kindness after an error or near-miss — learning from it and moving forward rather than dwelling in guilt — is part of sustainable practice.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Data Point | Effect on Differential |
|---|---|
| New oxycodone and gabapentin started just before symptom onset, in a patient with baseline CKD | Raises suspicion for medication-accumulation-driven encephalopathy as the leading toxic-metabolic cause |
| New rivaroxaban plus chronic aspirin | Raises concern for occult intracranial hemorrhage despite absence of focal neurologic findings; prompts head CT |
| Normal point-of-care glucose, normal basic labs, normal head CT | Excludes the most immediately dangerous and reversible causes; permits a more measured, sequential workup |
| Gradual onset over ~12 hours in a previously independent, cognitively intact patient | Supports an acute toxic-metabolic or iatrogenic process over a primary structural or baseline dementia process |
| Symptoms worsen despite stopping oxycodone and gabapentin | Argues against those two medications as the sole explanation; keeps the differential open for an unrecognized ongoing exposure |
| Reveal: pre-existing contralateral above-knee amputation | Reframes the reliability of the creatinine entirely — true renal function is worse than the creatinine suggests |
| Reveal: patient is on cefepime | Identifies the specific renally-cleared, neurotoxic agent accumulating due to underestimated renal impairment |
| Cystatin C-based eGFR (CKD stage 4) vs. creatinine-based eGFR (CKD stage 2) | Confirms and quantifies the degree to which creatinine had been falsely reassuring |
| Resolution of encephalopathy within 2-3 days of cefepime dose adjustment | Confirms cefepime neurotoxicity as the unifying diagnosis |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Cefepime neurotoxicity illness script**: encephalopathy, sometimes with myoclonus or hallucinations, occurring in the setting of renal impairment and cefepime accumulation — often insidious in onset and easily misattributed to other sedating medications or delirium.
- **Anchoring on visible medication changes**: the team's attention naturally gravitated to the two newly started, well-known sedating medications (oxycodone, gabapentin) as the presumed cause, illustrating how the most visible or recently changed variable can overshadow an equally present but less obvious one (the antibiotic).
- **"You cannot see what you do not know" — knowledge-gated pattern recognition**: Robbie's explicit framing of why the original team missed the connection — the muscle-mass/creatinine/cystatin C relationship in an amputee is a specific piece of knowledge that, once known, immediately reframes the whole case; without it, the data was present but invisible.
- **Case-based recall as a diagnostic trigger**: Reza's ability to make the diagnosis instantly upon hearing "amputation" is directly traced to a specific prior teaching case from a mentor ("Uncle Bob"), reinforcing a recurring cross-episode theme in this series.
- **Reassurance from a "normal" lab value**: the case is a direct parallel to the show's broader theme of not fully trusting reassuring data (here, a normal-appearing creatinine) without first evaluating whether that data point is actually a valid proxy for what's being measured in this particular patient.

## Teaching Discussion

The episode's central teaching point is a precise pharmacologic and physiologic one: creatinine is not a pure measure of renal filtration but a function of both filtration and muscle mass, and it will systematically overestimate renal function — appear falsely reassuring — in any patient with reduced muscle mass, amputees being the clearest example. Cystatin C is offered as the more reliable alternative in this population, with direct clinical consequences for dosing any renally-cleared medication, not just the antibiotic ultimately responsible in this case.

A second, more structural teaching point is embedded in how the case was presented: Robbie deliberately withheld two key facts to demonstrate that diagnostic reasoning is only as good as the information — and the knowledge to interpret that information — available to the clinician at the time. The original team's workup was not a failure of effort or diligence (both hosts explicitly avoid blaming the vascular team or earlier trainees), but a gap in a specific, transferable piece of knowledge that the case now closes for the show's listeners.

Finally, the episode closes on a professional-development note distinct from the clinical content: an explicit discussion of self-compassion after diagnostic delays, framing worry and self-criticism as byproducts of caring rather than evidence of incompetence, and crediting the intergenerational transmission of specific clinical pearls (from "Uncle Bob" through Robbie and Reza to the audience) as a core part of how medical practice actually improves over time.
