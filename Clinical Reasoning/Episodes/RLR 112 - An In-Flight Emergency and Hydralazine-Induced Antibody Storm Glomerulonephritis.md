## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Reza opens with a brief personal story: responding, off-duty and without equipment, to an in-flight medical emergency involving a woman with presyncope, ultimately attributed to orthostatic hypotension from gastroenteritis and trazodone. The main case is Rabih's: a 78-year-old man with longstanding stage 3 CKD on losartan, hydrochlorothiazide, and hydralazine, found on routine nephrology labs to have a creatinine spike and a new anemia. A fluid challenge fails to reverse the creatinine, and the workup pivots from prerenal causes toward glomerular disease once hematuria, heavy proteinuria, and a prior UA already showing smoldering glomerular involvement are uncovered. Serologies return an "antibody storm" — positive ANA, anti-dsDNA, PR3, and MPO together — pointing to hydralazine as the trigger for an overlap of drug-induced lupus-like disease and ANCA-associated glomerulonephritis. Despite stopping the drug and starting steroids and rituximab, kidney function does not recover, and the patient progresses to dialysis within two months.

## Pearls

- The differential for sudden presyncope/near-fainting is efficiently split into hypotension versus hypoglycemia; empiric bedside maneuvers (Trendelenburg positioning/leg elevation for suspected hypotension, a sugar-containing snack for suspected hypoglycemia) can be started immediately, before any vital signs are available, especially in a resource-poor environment.
- Trazodone and other sedating/antihypertensive-adjacent medications can contribute to orthostatic hypotension, and a history of preceding gastroenteritis (vomiting/diarrhea) further supports a volume-depletion mechanism for an otherwise unexplained presyncope episode.
- Responding to an emergency without the usual clinical infrastructure (nurses, automated vital sign equipment, point-of-care testing) is a fundamentally different cognitive and logistical experience than managing the same problem in a hospital — a reminder of how much of "medicine as a team sport" is normally invisible until it is suddenly absent.
- When a patient with known CKD develops an unexplained rise in creatinine that is not temporally linked to an obvious hemodynamic trigger (vomiting, diarrhea, new diuretic, hypotension), a prerenal cause is comparatively unlikely, and the workup should move more quickly toward intrinsic renal or postrenal causes.
- The most common causes of CKD in adults are diabetic kidney disease and a variety of tubulointerstitial diseases (including chronic NSAID use); hypertension's causal role in CKD is actively debated, since hypertension is frequently a consequence of underlying kidney disease (e.g., unrecognized reflux nephropathy) rather than its cause — a caution against reflexively blaming "longstanding hypertension" for progressive CKD.
- A "definitive" fluid challenge (e.g., 2 liters) can be used deliberately to take prerenal azotemia fully off the table diagnostically, even when the patient does not appear clinically volume-depleted — the goal is certainty about fluid status, not empiric treatment.
- Deciding whether to hold a renin-angiotensin-system blocker (ACE inhibitor/ARB) in the setting of AKI is a genuine tension: these drugs are long-term renoprotective, and many nephrologists will tolerate a modest creatinine rise to preserve that benefit, but a sufficiently large creatinine delta favors holding the drug until the picture clarifies.
- The urinalysis is, in practice, mostly a test for glomerular disease — proteinuria and hematuria on a UA localize pathology to the glomerulus, while a very low urine specific gravity is a subtler clue toward tubulointerstitial dysfunction (impaired urinary concentrating ability via aquaporin channels).
- When hematuria and AKI occur together, the efficient differential is short: urinary obstruction (most common), bilateral kidney stones (or stones in a single functioning kidney), or glomerular disease — a bladder/renal ultrasound without hydronephrosis and a low pretest probability of painless bilateral stones together push hard toward glomerulonephritis.
- A spot urine protein-to-creatinine ratio is more reliable than the semi-quantitative protein read on a standard UA, which can vary meaningfully with blood pressure, hydration, and time of day; a markedly elevated ratio adds real diagnostic weight to a glomerular hypothesis.
- Comparing a current UA to prior UAs is essential for distinguishing acute from acute-on-chronic glomerular disease — if hematuria and proteinuria were already present months earlier, the process is not new, and the differential for a chronic-with-acute-flare glomerulopathy (classically lupus nephritis including drug-induced lupus, IgA nephropathy, and C3 glomerulopathy) differs meaningfully from that of a purely acute process (e.g., ANCA-associated vasculitis or anti-GBM disease in isolation).
- When multiple autoimmune serologies that normally belong to distinct, "elegant" diseases are positive simultaneously (here, ANA/anti-dsDNA alongside PR3- and MPO-ANCA), this "antibody storm" pattern should raise suspicion for a discrete external or internal trigger — most commonly a drug (hydralazine, procainamide, isoniazid), an infection (including unusual case-report associations like Bartonella endocarditis or syphilis), or, less commonly, an underlying malignancy (notably Hodgkin lymphoma and angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma) — rather than assuming a genuine overlap syndrome.
- Complement levels are a high-yield discriminator among the immune-complex causes of glomerulonephritis: persistently normal complements in the setting of hematuria and proteinuria should raise concern for IgA nephropathy in particular, which (along with C3 glomerulopathy) can remain "blood-work negative" even when strongly suspected, ultimately requiring biopsy for diagnosis.
- Nephritic-spectrum glomerulonephritis is comparatively "blood-work rich" — serologic and complement testing frequently yields a strong diagnostic clue — in contrast to nephrotic syndrome, where most causes are idiopathic glomerulopathies that usually require biopsy regardless of the blood work obtained.
- Outpatient- or clinic-presenting AKI that arises without a clear preceding hemodynamic trigger deserves the same diagnostic rigor as inpatient AKI, arguably more: in the hospital, AKI is often a bystander of the primary illness, but AKI discovered incidentally in clinic is more likely to itself be the primary pathology — and a patient who is "surprised" by their own AKI (i.e., it arose insidiously, without an obvious explanation) should not be a surprise to end up needing dialysis if the underlying process goes unrecognized.

## Problem Representation

**Personal case:** A previously well air traveler develops presyncope shortly before takeoff, ultimately attributed to orthostatic hypotension from a combination of recent gastroenteritis-related volume depletion and trazodone, managed with reflexive bedside assessment and stabilization in a resource-limited in-flight setting.

**Main case:** A 78-year-old man with longstanding stage 3 CKD on an ARB, a thiazide, and hydralazine, found incidentally on routine labs to have a creatinine rise and a new anemia, ultimately diagnosed with hydralazine-triggered "antibody storm" glomerulonephritis (an ANCA-associated and immune-complex overlap superimposed on underlying diabetic nephropathy), progressing to end-stage renal disease despite treatment.

## Case Narrative

**The in-flight case.** Reza described being roused from an exhausted, half-asleep state on a delayed flight by an announcement requesting a medical professional. He found a 50-year-old woman on her knees in the aisle, reporting severe lightheadedness and a feeling that she was about to faint. Asked how he would approach this as an ED physician, Rabih laid out an efficient initial framework: presyncope of this kind is most often hypotension or hypoglycemia, with hypotension the more concerning of the two, and recommended immediate empiric maneuvers — placing the patient in a position that assists venous return (Trendelenburg/leg elevation, airway permitting) and offering a sugar source — even before any vital signs were available.

Working through a focused history at the patient's side, without any monitoring equipment, Reza learned that she had taken trazodone about thirty minutes earlier, had experienced significant gastroenteritis with loose stools the prior night, had a history of hypothyroidism on levothyroxine, and had no history of diabetes or hypoglycemia. He reasoned toward orthostatic hypotension compounded by trazodone and recent volume losses from her gastroenteritis. Practical obstacles (a locked first-aid kit accessible only to a credentialed provider, a poor-quality manual blood pressure cuff and stethoscope, no nurse or additional medical personnel aboard) made physical exam data difficult to obtain, but the patient remained conversant throughout, without chest pain, dyspnea, or loss of consciousness, supporting a hypovolemic/orthostatic mechanism over a more emergent cardiac or neurologic process. Paramedics met the aircraft and transported the patient; she did not require CPR, and Reza reflected afterward on how differently the same clinical reasoning feels without the usual support infrastructure of nursing staff and automated equipment — prompting him to plan carrying a personal stethoscope and automated vital-sign device going forward.

**The nephrology case.** Rabih then presented a case unfolding over weeks to months in his own nephrology-adjacent care: a 78-year-old man with hypertension and stage 3 CKD (baseline creatinine 1.8-2.1 for roughly a decade), followed in nephrology clinic, on losartan, hydrochlorothiazide, and hydralazine. Routine pre-visit labs showed his creatinine had risen to 3.5 and his hemoglobin had fallen from 10 to 7; he reported only mild fatigue, with no decreased urine output or other symptoms, prompting a hospital referral.

Reza reasoned that patients with an already-vulnerable organ (CKD) are more likely to develop new disease within that same organ, and organized his approach around the most common causes of CKD progression — predominantly diabetic kidney disease and tubulointerstitial disease (e.g., from chronic NSAID use) — explicitly noting that hypertension's causal role in CKD is contested, since it is often a consequence of underlying kidney disease rather than its cause. Because this AKI-on-CKD arose without an obvious hemodynamic trigger (no vomiting, diarrhea, or new diuretic), he judged a prerenal mechanism less likely and prioritized moving toward intrinsic renal or postrenal causes, while still planning a deliberate fluid challenge to settle the question definitively. He flagged the new anemia as requiring its own workup (iron deficiency versus EPO deficiency, with multiple myeloma as the most morbid unifying explanation for progressive anemia plus progressive kidney dysfunction), and specifically flagged hydralazine as a medication capable of causing drug-induced autoimmune disease, worth revisiting if the kidney injury took on an autoimmune flavor. He recommended a bladder/renal ultrasound and a urinalysis with urine protein-to-creatinine ratio as next steps, and advised giving 2 liters of fluid — not because he believed the patient was truly volume-depleted, but to remove any doubt about whether fluid responsiveness could explain the creatinine — while holding the losartan, hydrochlorothiazide, and hydralazine, acknowledging the usual tension around discontinuing a renoprotective ARB but judging the creatinine delta too large to continue it for now.

The bladder ultrasound showed no urinary retention. After 2 liters of fluid, the creatinine barely moved (3.5 to 3.3). The UA showed a specific gravity of 1.013, mildly positive protein, 92 red blood cells, 7 white blood cells, and negative nitrites and ketones. Reza reframed the UA's real purpose as being fundamentally a test for glomerular disease (proteinuria and hematuria as the key findings, with specific gravity offering a secondary clue toward tubulointerstitial dysfunction if very low), and organized his reasoning around the narrow differential for hematuria co-occurring with AKI specifically — urinary obstruction, bilateral (or single-kidney) kidney stones, or glomerular disease — judging obstruction essentially excluded by the ultrasound and painless bilateral stones highly improbable, leaving glomerulonephritis as the leading hypothesis. He requested a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (more reliable than the UA's semiquantitative protein estimate, which can vary with blood pressure and time of day) and recommended spinning the urine to look for dysmorphic red cells, acanthocytes, or RBC casts, alongside a search for extrarenal clues of glomerulonephritis (worsening blood pressure, skin capillary bleeding), while explicitly cautioning that the case should be pursued with "seriousness but humility," since an unrelated process (e.g., bladder cancer) causing incidental hematuria alongside separate kidney dysfunction remained possible.

The urine protein-to-creatinine ratio returned markedly elevated at 1.6, reinforcing the glomerular hypothesis. Rabih then supplied a UA from five months earlier that already showed hematuria (36 red cells) and florid proteinuria — evidence that the glomerular process was not brand new. Reza used this to narrow the differential specifically for an acute-on-chronic glomerular disease, reasoning that it excluded the more purely acute presentations of ANCA-associated vasculitis and anti-GBM (Goodpasture's) disease in their classic forms, leaving lupus nephritis (including drug-induced lupus), IgA nephropathy, and C3 glomerulopathy as his short list — while candidly noting after the fact that this initial short list would need to be revised. The renal ultrasound showed echogenic but normal-sized kidneys without hydronephrosis, and spun urine possibly showed dysmorphic red cells.

Reza then outlined the serologic workup: ANCA (given the shortlist gap he would later revise) and anti-GBM testing regardless, plus complement levels as a key discriminator (persistently normal complements would raise particular concern for IgA nephropathy, which — along with C3 glomerulopathy — can remain "blood-work negative" despite strong clinical suspicion), the patient's ethnicity (Pacific Rim ancestry raising IgA nephropathy's prior probability), an infectious screen (HIV, hepatitis B and C, both diagnostically and to screen before immunosuppression), an autoimmune screen (ANA, anti-dsDNA, and antihistone antibody, the latter specifically relevant to drug-induced lupus), and a paraproteinemia screen for monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance (SPEP and serum free light chains). He noted that nephritic-spectrum glomerulonephritis is comparatively rich in diagnostic blood work, in contrast to nephrotic syndrome, where most causes are idiopathic glomerulopathies typically requiring biopsy regardless of serology.

The results reframed the entire case: ANA and anti-dsDNA were both floridly positive, and both PR3- and MPO-ANCA were positive with a positive overall ANCA screen. Reza recognized this pattern of multiple, disease-crossing autoimmune serologies as an "antibody storm" (a term he credited to a colleague's teaching), explaining that autoimmune diseases are typically "elegant" — each with a fairly specific serologic signature — so when serologies belonging to distinct diseases (lupus-type and ANCA-type) are simultaneously positive, the more likely explanation is a discrete trigger rather than a genuine overlap syndrome. Triggers can be exogenous (drugs — hydralazine chief among them, alongside procainamide and isoniazid — or infections, with case-report examples including Bartonella endocarditis and syphilis producing similarly promiscuous serologic positivity, and a cross-reference to a prior case involving silicosis as a pneumoconiosis-related trigger) or endogenous (malignancy, notably Hodgkin lymphoma and angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma). Given the patient's hydralazine exposure, Reza flagged this as the leading exogenous trigger, while cautioning that an underlying malignancy masquerading behind the coincidental hydralazine prescription could not be excluded until the patient's response to stopping the drug and immunosuppressing could be observed — recommending stopping hydralazine, empirically immunosuppressing, and pursuing a kidney biopsy to confirm the diagnosis rather than waiting for full certainty given the disease's morbidity.

A difficult conversation followed: the patient, having researched hydralazine's known toxicity online, became angry and questioned why his primary care physician had prescribed it. Rabih modeled a communication approach centered on identifying the emotional content behind the patient's words rather than answering the literal question — validating the patient's distress and suffering directly, avoiding both defensiveness on behalf of the prescribing physician and any explicit criticism of that physician's judgment, while privately holding the clinical view that hydralazine is rarely an appropriate choice in modern practice. He noted the importance of assuming good intent from other clinicians in the absence of direct knowledge of their reasoning, while acknowledging that this assumption is not always correct.

The kidney biopsy showed 3 of 19 nephrons with crescent formation alongside nodular diabetic mesangial sclerosis — a mixed picture reflecting chronic diabetic kidney disease with a superimposed drug-induced glomerulonephritis that had gone unrecognized long enough to compound the damage. Guidance from a nephrology colleague characterized the picture as an overlap between drug-induced ANCA-associated vasculitis and pauci-immune glomerulonephritis (rather than classic drug-induced lupus, which does not typically cause this degree of renal injury), recommending high-dose steroids and rituximab. The patient received methylprednisolone and rituximab; his creatinine did not improve, though he avoided an acute indication for dialysis at discharge. Within one to two months of discharge, he had progressed to end-stage renal disease requiring dialysis.

Reflecting on the case, Rabih and Reza converged on a shared take-home point: AKI discovered in the outpatient setting, without a preceding hemodynamic trigger, deserves full diagnostic rigor beyond the reflexive assumption of prerenal azotemia, because such AKI is more likely to represent the primary disease process itself (rather than a bystander finding, as is common with inpatient AKI) — and a patient whose AKI arrives as a genuine surprise should not be a surprise if it ultimately ends in dialysis.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Hydralazine-induced antibody storm glomerulonephritis illness script: a patient on chronic hydralazine develops acute-on-chronic glomerulonephritis with simultaneously positive serologies spanning lupus-type (ANA, anti-dsDNA) and ANCA-type (PR3, MPO) autoimmunity, reflecting a drug trigger rather than a genuine overlap syndrome, and requiring biopsy to characterize the degree of superimposed injury on any pre-existing renal disease.

The case models disciplined "seriousness with humility": pursuing an aggressive glomerulonephritis workup while explicitly holding space for an alternative, unrelated explanation (e.g., bladder cancer causing incidental hematuria) until the data forecloses it. It also illustrates a deliberate anti-anchoring habit — narrowing a differential (here, to three chronic glomerular diseases) while remaining willing to revise that list in real time once new data (the antibody storm) arrives, rather than defending the original framework.

## Teaching Discussion

The central nephrology teaching point is the discipline of separating a truly acute glomerulonephritis from an acute-on-chronic process by deliberately comparing current urine studies against prior ones — a step that meaningfully narrows the differential and, in this case, correctly anticipated that a purely acute-only entity (isolated ANCA vasculitis or anti-GBM disease) was less likely, even though the final diagnosis ultimately still involved ANCA serology in an overlap, hydralazine-triggered form.

A second teaching point is the "antibody storm" concept: when serologies belonging to different, normally distinct autoimmune diseases are simultaneously and strongly positive, the correct response is not to diagnose an overlap syndrome by default, but to hunt for a unifying trigger — a drug, an infection, or a malignancy — that can produce promiscuous autoantibody positivity.

A third teaching point is communication-focused: modeling how to respond to a patient's anger at a prior clinician's prescribing decision by identifying and validating the emotional content of the complaint, rather than either defending or condemning a colleague's clinical judgment made without full context — a skill treated as being just as much a part of excellent clinical reasoning as the diagnostic workup itself.
