## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A man in his 50s with presumed but never crystal-confirmed gout presents with five days of severe, sleep-disrupting left knee pain. A methodical approach to monoarticular arthritis leads to arthrocentesis, but the fluid is only mildly inflammatory with no crystals seen — leaving the patient in severe pain with no clear diagnosis. Rather than accepting a falsely reassuring tap, the discussants question whether the process is truly intra-articular at all; a repeat exam localizes the tenderness to prepatellar, and MRI followed by dual-energy CT reveal severe patellar tendinosis studded with urate deposition — gout tendinitis — a periarticular crystalline process masquerading as joint disease.

## Pearls

- The first branch point in any possible joint complaint is trauma, including "hidden" trauma from pivoting, jumping, or deceleration during activity that the patient may not consciously register as an injury; the second is whether the process is truly intra-articular or periarticular (bursa, ligament, tendon).
- Pain severe enough to prevent sleep, or so exquisite that a bedsheet touching the joint is intolerable, is a classic feature of crystalline arthropathy (gout or CPPD) and should raise its probability disproportionately relative to other causes of joint pain.
- Only about 10% of patients with a clinical diagnosis of gout have ever had crystal-proven disease; in a classic clinical context, gout can reasonably be diagnosed presumptively, and dual-energy CT now offers strong positive predictive value as a non-invasive alternative to arthrocentesis when a joint is difficult to access.
- A young patient (well under the typical age of onset) presenting with recurrent crystalline arthritis deserves a secondary-cause workup rather than attribution to age alone — for gout, a renal panel to assess urate clearance; for CPPD, magnesium, calcium/parathyroid hormone, and consideration of ferritin for hemochromatosis (the "H's": hypomagnesemia, hyperparathyroidism, hemochromatosis).
- A synovial fluid nucleated cell count below roughly 2,000 argues against both septic and truly inflammatory intra-articular crystalline arthritis in an immunocompetent patient — but the absence of visualized crystals on a single tap does not rule out crystalline disease, since crystals are notoriously easy to miss microscopically, and a technically inadequate aspiration (needle not truly intra-articular, minimal fluid obtained) should always be considered before crystalline disease is excluded.
- Crystalline disease is not confined to the joint space: urate and calcium pyrophosphate crystals can deposit in tendons, ligaments, bursae, skin, and even heart valves, and a periarticular crystalline process (such as gout tendinitis) can mimic an intra-articular flare on exam, including causing warmth and severe pain, while repeatedly yielding non-diagnostic joint aspirates.
- Gouty tophi are not simply deposits of crystals — they represent a granulomatous foreign-body reaction to urate crystals, and their presence establishes a diagnosis of chronic tophaceous gout; unlike CPPD, which currently has no disease-modifying therapy, gout has effective urate-lowering treatment (including pegloticase for refractory cases), making accurate distinction between the two clinically consequential.

## Problem Representation

A man in his 50s with a history of presumed but never crystal-confirmed gout (prior ankle flares treated empirically with allopurinol and colchicine) presents with five days of progressively severe, sleep-disrupting left knee pain and effusion; an initial arthrocentesis is only mildly inflammatory with no crystals identified, and subsequent re-examination, MRI, and dual-energy CT reveal that the true driver is severe patellar tendinosis with urate crystal deposition — gout tendinitis — a periarticular crystalline process rather than a primary intra-articular one.

## Case Narrative

A man in his 50s presents to the emergency department with severe left knee pain that began five days earlier and has progressively worsened to the point that he cannot sleep and is requesting analgesia.

The first responding discussant frames the presenting problem as likely musculoskeletal and begins with the most important branch point for any joint complaint: was there trauma? He notes that trauma can be obvious or subtle — a sudden pivot or jump during a sporting activity, for instance, involves real acceleration/deceleration forces that a patient may not register as an injury. The second key branch point is whether the process is intra-articular or periarticular, the latter encompassing bursa, ligament, and tendon (remembered via the mnemonic BLT). Signs of systemic inflammation would push the differential toward a broader systemic process. He highlights one detail as especially telling: pain severe enough to disrupt sleep is a hallmark of crystalline disease, which can be so exquisite that even a bedsheet touching the joint provokes a reaction. His initial plan is to examine the joint and periarticular space for inflammation and effusion and to ask specifically about recent trauma or precipitating activity.

A second discussant adds a practical historical question: how did the patient arrive at the ED — walking, with a cane or walker, or assisted? This turns out to be revealing, as the patient arrived by ambulance, underscoring the severity of his presentation. On exam, the knee is warm — notably warmer than the contralateral knee — with a modest effusion and marked tenderness to palpation.

The second discussant cautions that "knee pain" as a chief complaint is too narrow a framing, since the pain could originate from periarticular structures rather than the joint itself; however, because the entire knee — not a focal periarticular structure such as the prepatellar bursa or a ligament insertion — appears swollen, a ruptured Baker's cyst (which can diffusely extravasate fluid and mimic intra-articular pathology) is the main alternative to keep in mind, but is considered less likely given the joint's warmth. This reframes the problem as presumed monoarticular arthritis, with a "trusted trifecta" differential: septic (infectious) arthritis, crystalline arthropathy, or, less commonly, inflammatory osteoarthritis. While arthrocentesis is the definitive next test, he cautions against rushing to it without first surveying the rest of the body: the skin, for tophi (suggesting chronic tophaceous gout) or stigmata of endovascular infection (Osler nodes, Janeway lesions), and, less commonly, a rash suggesting erythema migrans; and a history of any prosthetic material anywhere in the body (a major risk factor for endovascular infection and secondary septic arthritis).

The presenting discussant reports the skin exam is unremarkable and the patient is afebrile. The patient volunteers that he believes this is his gout, recounting roughly three prior ankle flares, none of which were ever tapped for confirmation; he takes allopurinol 150 mg daily and has colchicine at home, having taken one or two doses before arrival without effect.

One discussant reflects on the patient's presumed gout history: crystalline disease (gout and CPPD/pseudogout) is often taught as purely "acute," when in fact both have chronic phases as well. He notes that only about 10% of patients with a clinical gout diagnosis have ever had crystal-proven disease — in a classic clinical context, gout can be diagnosed presumptively, and dual-energy CT (DECT) now offers a strong, non-invasive alternative with good positive predictive value. Gout characteristically favors cooler, more distal joints (temperature favoring urate crystal precipitation), which is why the ankle and first MTP are common initial sites; he finds it notable that this patient's prior ankle flares were never confirmed by tap, since an ankle (unlike the first MTP) is generally an accessible joint to aspirate. With knee involvement now present, he recommends a knee X-ray specifically to look for chondrocalcinosis — a thin linear calcification within the cartilage — which, if present, is a strong predictor of symptomatic CPPD (though it can also be present without ever causing symptoms). He argues it would be premature to settle on gout alone here, especially given the patient is already on allopurinol (which works by keeping serum urate below its roughly 6.8 mg/dL solubility threshold, above which crystal precipitation occurs) — continued flares on adequate urate-lowering therapy should prompt reconsideration, including the possibility of coexisting disease, since an infected joint can itself precipitate a secondary gout flare. He cites a JAMA Rational Clinical Examination series article addressing whether an adult with knee pain has a septic joint, built around three parameters — fever, leukocytosis, and pain with range of motion — noting that the absence of all three substantially lowers the probability of septic arthritis. His conclusion: the patient still needs an arthrocentesis and a peripheral white blood cell count.

The white blood cell count returns at 7.9. Both responding discussants praise the presenting discussant's use of the term "inflammatory osteoarthritis" — he elaborates that many patients labeled with plain osteoarthritis likely have underlying CPPD driving an inflammatory component, a connection he only came to appreciate in the past year. Knee X-rays show no calcification (no chondrocalcinosis).

Asked whether to obtain more labs or proceed directly to arthrocentesis, one discussant offers a broader practical reflection: nearly every acutely swollen knee should be tapped, for three converging reasons — diagnostic clarity (ruling septic arthritis in or out), direct therapeutic benefit from fluid removal, and the fact that the best treatment for crystalline or osteoarthritic inflammatory flares is an intra-articular steroid injection, which requires the joint to be accessed regardless. From a practical ED workflow standpoint, blood work and cultures add substantial delay (potentially an hour or more), whereas a tap can yield an answer — and treatment — within 30 to 60 minutes; in a patient with a low pretest probability for septic arthritis (as here: recurrent monoarticular pattern, which septic arthritis does not typically produce; afebrile; no leukocytosis; no overt endovascular risk factors), blood work is often skippable, though two blood cultures and screening for sexually transmitted infection (including gonorrhea at relevant mucosal sites) would be reasonable if septic or gonococcal arthritis were genuinely suspected. He argues this patient nonetheless needs a secondary laboratory workup for a different reason: at only 50 years old, recurrent gout without a clear precipitant (such as a strong family history) is unusual, and the pattern of early ankle-then-knee involvement raises specific suspicion for CPPD rather than gout — in a relatively young patient with CPPD, secondary causes should be sought via a renal panel (secondary gout), magnesium and calcium/parathyroid studies (CPPD), and, if supported by further history, serum ferritin for hemochromatosis.

Labs return: uric acid 6.2 despite allopurinol (adherence felt to be reliable); CRP 9.5; ESR 60. Arthrocentesis is performed: a bloody tap yields 194 nucleated cells (well below the roughly 2,000-cell threshold for an inflammatory effusion), Gram stain is negative, and rheumatology, reviewing the fluid directly, does not identify crystals. The patient remains in severe pain, nearly in tears, unable to tolerate even the bedsheet touching his knee, and is not given an intra-articular injection, since the team is not convinced enough of any single diagnosis to treat empirically.

One discussant praises the earlier reasoning about secondary causes of CPPD in young patients, offering the mnemonic "H's" — hypomagnesemia, hyperparathyroidism, hemochromatosis — and recalls a similar prior RLR case in which CPPD deposited in a femoral tendon and spilled over into the joint space, mimicking a crystalline flare, while repeated taps kept showing the joint itself was not particularly inflammatory. He now raises two live possibilities: either this remains crystalline disease with a technically inadequate or false-negative tap (questioning how confident the proceduralist was that the needle was truly intra-articular, and how much fluid was actually obtained — no diagnostic test, including arthrocentesis, is perfectly sensitive), or this is not a true intra-articular process at all, given the sub-inflammatory cell count — with osteoarthritis (though the x-ray lacked typical osteophytes or erosions) or, more likely given the clinical severity, a periarticular process (tendon, ligament, or bursa) as the actual driver. His recommendation: clarify directly with the proceduralist how confident they were in needle placement and fluid yield, and pursue advanced imaging — MRI preferred for soft tissue and tendon/ligament detail, CT as an alternative that can also assess for an occult fracture. He emphasizes that failing to see crystals does not exclude crystalline disease, since crystals are easy to miss microscopically, but a genuinely low nucleated cell count in an immunocompetent patient argues against both infection and a true intra-articular inflammatory flare being severe enough to explain this presentation.

The presenting discussant reveals that the original tap, performed by a rheumatologist skilled at identifying crystals, is reconsidered the next morning by a second rheumatologist, who on exam determines the tenderness and swelling are actually prepatellar rather than truly intra-articular, and orders an MRI. The MRI shows diffuse, severe tendinosis of the patellar tendon.

Another discussant notes that this localization reinforces that crystalline disease deposits broadly throughout the body, not just in joints — recalling a prior RLR case of gout crystal deposition on the mitral valve as an extreme example — and that crystalline disease might be better conceptualized as a systemic disease predominantly affecting the synovium, with extension into adjacent structures including tendons, ligaments, and skin. He briefly considers alternative causes of tendinopathy (fluoroquinolone exposure, enthesitis from the spondyloarthropathy family) but, given the patient's history of recurrent monoarticular arthritis, concludes the tendon process is most likely also crystalline (uncertain whether gout or CPPD specifically) and states he would be comfortable starting empiric anti-crystalline treatment (NSAIDs, colchicine, or steroids) on this data alone. He distinguishes the diagnostic question (gout, warranting continued or increased allopurinol given the still-elevated uric acid; versus CPPD, made less likely by the absence of chondrocalcinosis on this x-ray, though additional films of the wrist and pubic symphysis would be reasonable to be thorough) from the immediate treatment question, and flags the patient's age and unusually aggressive presentation as grounds to eventually pursue uric acid clearance studies and a more detailed family history.

The presenting discussant reveals the actual resolution: as soon as MRI was decided upon during rounds, he also ordered a dual-energy CT scan, curious whether the gout might be located somewhere unexpected, since he had not previously known gout could cause tendinitis. The DECT showed the patellar tendon "lit up" with color-coded urate deposition, along with tophi identified within the knee joint as well. Rheumatology started the patient on steroids immediately, and he improved substantially within one to two days; he was discharged on an increased dose of allopurinol (300 mg), with rheumatology follow-up planned. The radiologist's read noted the tendon changes resembled "jumper's knee" — tendinosis (chronic degenerative change) rather than tendinitis (acute inflammation) — suggesting a preexisting, likely overuse-related chronic tendon vulnerability that then became a site for acute urate deposition, echoing the very first point raised in the case about subtle, unrecognized mechanical trauma. The presenting discussant retrospectively wonders whether the patient's prior "ankle gout" episodes were actually ankle tendinitis rather than true intra-articular disease, though this was not specifically re-confirmed. He also reflects that this was the third case of unusual-site gout he had personally diagnosed in about three years (previous examples: spinal gout, wrist gout), and in all three, the site was inaccessible to direct arthrocentesis, with dual-energy CT providing the answer each time.

In discussion, one discussant clarifies two teaching points: that gouty tophi are not simply deposits of crystals but represent a granulomatous foreign-body reaction to urate crystals, and that their presence establishes chronic tophaceous gout; and that chronic gout is preferable to chronic CPPD from a management standpoint, since gout has effective urate-lowering therapy (including pegloticase — an intravenous recombinant uricase enzyme that can drive uric acid to near-undetectable levels for refractory cases, prescribed by rheumatology) whereas CPPD currently has no disease-modifying therapy and is managed with indefinite anti-inflammatory treatment for flares.

Closing the episode, the second responding discussant shares a nearly identical personal case, differing only in that the culprit was CPPD rather than gout — learned firsthand how difficult it is for a non-expert to distinguish periarticular from intra-articular processes at the bedside, particularly in a patient whose pain limits a nuanced exam. He has since adopted routine use of a single dose of intramuscular ketorolac before attempting a difficult or nuanced joint exam or tap, to improve patient comfort without materially affecting outcomes even if septic arthritis is ultimately diagnosed. In his case, the joint required five separate taps (with a steroid injection given around the fourth) before an MRI provided the correct answer.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The case models resistance to false reassurance from a negative test: a low-but-not-zero inflammatory cell count and an absence of visualized crystals on a single arthrocentesis are treated as data points to be interpreted in context (technical adequacy of the tap, pretest probability) rather than as definitive exclusion of crystalline disease — an explicit reminder that even a "gold standard" test like arthrocentesis has real sensitivity limitations.

The presenting discussant's own diagnostic journey — ordering a dual-energy CT out of curiosity after learning, mid-case, that gout could cause tendinitis — is offered as a model of updating one's illness script in real time rather than closing the case once the joint tap failed to confirm the leading hypothesis.

Both discussants explicitly connect this case to a previously encountered, structurally similar case (CPPD depositing in a femoral tendon), illustrating how an illness script built from one crystalline arthropathy (CPPD-in-tendon) directly informed pattern recognition for a different crystalline arthropathy (gout-in-tendon) in a later, unrelated patient.

## Teaching Discussion

Any joint complaint should begin with two branch points: was there trauma (including subtle, activity-related trauma), and is the process intra-articular or periarticular (bursa, ligament, tendon)? Pain severe enough to prevent sleep or make bedsheet contact intolerable is a classic feature of crystalline arthropathy.

A "trusted trifecta" differential for monoarticular arthritis — septic, crystalline, and (less commonly) inflammatory osteoarthritis — should be paired with a quick survey of the skin (for tophi or endovascular stigmata) and a specific question about prosthetic material anywhere in the body before proceeding to arthrocentesis.

Only a minority of clinically diagnosed gout is ever crystal-proven; dual-energy CT is an increasingly useful non-invasive tool, particularly for joints or periarticular structures that are difficult to access by needle, and can reveal unexpected sites of urate deposition such as tendons.

A young patient with recurrent crystalline arthritis warrants a secondary-cause workup rather than attribution to age alone: a renal panel for secondary gout, and magnesium, calcium/parathyroid studies, and consideration of ferritin (hemochromatosis) for secondary CPPD.

A synovial fluid nucleated cell count below roughly 2,000 argues against both septic arthritis and a genuinely inflammatory intra-articular process in an immunocompetent patient, but a negative crystal exam does not exclude crystalline disease — always consider whether the aspiration itself was technically adequate before ruling out the diagnosis.

Crystalline disease is fundamentally a systemic process affecting the synovium broadly, with the capacity to deposit in tendons, ligaments, bursae, skin, and even heart valves — a periarticular crystalline process can mimic intra-articular disease on exam (including warmth and severe pain) while yielding a non-diagnostic joint aspirate, and should be actively considered when arthrocentesis findings don't match the clinical severity.

Distinguishing gout from CPPD has real management consequences: gout has effective urate-lowering therapy (including pegloticase for refractory, tophaceous disease), while CPPD currently has no disease-modifying treatment and is managed indefinitely with anti-inflammatory therapy for flares.
