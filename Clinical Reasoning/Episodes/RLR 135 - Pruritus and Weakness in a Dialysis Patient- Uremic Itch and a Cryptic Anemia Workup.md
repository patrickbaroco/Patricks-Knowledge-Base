## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 92-year-old man on hemodialysis presents with severe pruritus (no rash) and diffuse weakness after weeks of skipping dialysis sessions. Robbie works the itch-without-rash differential to uremic pruritus from underdialysis, which resolves once dialysis resumes — but the weakness persists, unmasking a hemoglobin of 6 against a baseline of 12. The anemia workup becomes a study in ESRD-specific mechanisms (EPO deficiency, acquired von Willebrand deficiency from GI AVMs, beta-blocker-blunted tachycardia) and a masterclass in why "normal" ferritin and TSAT can be deeply misleading in a dialysis patient — a relative drop from the patient's own prior baseline (ferritin 1000/TSAT 33% a year earlier) reframes today's "normal" 466/23% as actually low. A medical student's pattern-recognition on serial post-transfusion hemoglobins pushes the team toward GI consultation despite reassuring iron indices; endoscopy finds only duodenitis, insufficient to explain the degree of anemia. The episode closes without a definitive anemia diagnosis (cryptic GI blood loss vs. early myelodysplastic syndrome), resolved instead through explicit shared decision-making with a 92-year-old patient who is comfortable, asymptomatic, and wants to go home.

## Pearls

- Pruritus without rash splits into rash-that-just-hasn't-appeared-yet (bullous pemphigoid can have a pruritic prodrome preceding rash by weeks in elderly patients), itch-causing-secondary-rash (excoriations from cholestatic or uremic itch), and truly no-rash systemic causes.
- The most common non-systemic causes of itch are mundane: xerosis (dry skin), opioid-induced pruritus, and — easy to skip past — psychiatric causes like delusional parasitosis or methamphetamine-associated pruritus.
- Fatigue and weakness are almost never an intrinsic brain problem; they reflect the composition of the blood — metabolic/electrolyte derangement, hematologic disease, a sedating or myopathic medication (gabapentin flagged specifically), or an infectious process.
- Work up weakness/fatigue in stages: a pillbox review, then basic labs and an infection screen, then advanced testing (blood and fungal cultures, peripheral smear) if the first pass is unrevealing.
- Uremic pruritus is a real and common cause of itch without rash in underdialyzed ESRD patients, and it resolves with adequate dialysis — a BUN of 122 on arrival in a patient who had cut back to roughly weekly sessions was the tell here.
- In ESRD, anemia is rarely explained by EPO deficiency alone; acquired von Willebrand deficiency and GI arteriovenous malformations driven by turbulent dialysis-access flow are an ESRD-specific bleeding mechanism worth naming explicitly.
- Transfusion thresholds (Hgb <7 with symptoms) should be acted on immediately; the mechanism can — and should — be investigated in parallel, not before.
- Beta-blockers (here, carvedilol) blunt the compensatory tachycardia that would otherwise be an early clue to ongoing blood loss, making vital signs less reliable in on-beta-blocker bleeding patients.
- Multiple myeloma is an explicit "do not miss" in the anemia workup of a longstanding dialysis patient — SPEP, UPEP, and serum free light chains belong in the initial panel, not as an afterthought.
- Zinc-containing denture cream causing copper deficiency is an esoteric but real cause of unexplained cytopenias in elderly, denture-wearing patients and is cheap to screen out early.
- The "substance or stem cell" framework for anemia of production: either something is suppressing production (a toxin or medication — alcohol, methotrexate) or something needed for production is missing (EPO, thyroid hormone, testosterone, B12, thiamine, pyridoxine). If all substances are checked and normal, with no bleeding or hemolysis, the next step is heme-onc referral for bone marrow biopsy to evaluate for MDS or aplastic anemia.
- Reticulocyte count plus LDH and haptoglobin quickly separates a hypoproliferative (production) anemia from a hemolytic one — don't skip this step even when the story "feels" like blood loss.
- Ferritin and TSAT are acute-phase reactants and are notoriously unreliable in ESRD; a value that looks population-normal can still represent a major relative drop from that specific patient's own prior baseline, and that comparison — not the population reference range — is what should drive the decision to pursue iron deficiency further.
- Serial post-transfusion hemoglobin trends that fail to "hold" (rising less than expected, or drifting back down between transfusions) are a quiet but important signal of ongoing loss, and are easy for a busy team to under-weight next to a normal-looking ferritin.
- Not every anemia needs to be fully characterized before discharge — in a frail, comfortable, asymptomatic patient near the end of a long disease course, explaining the differential (cryptic GI bleed vs. slowly progressive MDS), confirming neither is emergent, and safety-netting for red flags can be the right clinical endpoint.

## Problem Representation

A 92-year-old man on hemodialysis with a recent pattern of dialysis nonadherence presents with new severe pruritus without rash and diffuse weakness, found to have a markedly elevated BUN consistent with underdialysis and a hemoglobin of 6 g/dL down from a baseline of 12 g/dL one year prior, in the setting of a remote history of GI bleeding from duodenal AVMs.

## Case Narrative

Reza opened with a golf analogy — "let the ball tell you your swing" — as a framing device for a case he said would unfold the way it actually did in real time, without him steering it toward a tidy answer in advance.

The patient was a 92-year-old man on maintenance hemodialysis who, for several weeks, had been skipping sessions and was down to roughly once a week instead of his prescribed schedule. He presented with two complaints: severe, sleep-disrupting pruritus with no visible rash, and diffuse weakness.

Robbie took the pruritus first. He laid out a framework for itch-without-visible-rash: either a rash is present but hasn't fully declared itself yet (bullous pemphigoid, notably, can produce a pruritic prodrome in elderly patients that precedes the bullous eruption by weeks), or the itch is secondary to an occult process like cholestasis that will eventually produce excoriations, or there is no rash at all because the cause is systemic. Before chasing anything exotic, he wanted the mundane causes ruled out: xerosis, opioid-induced pruritus from a medication list, and — worth a deliberate look — psychiatric causes such as delusional parasitosis or methamphetamine-associated itch.

The diffuse weakness changed the framing. Robbie made the point that fatigue and weakness are essentially never a primary brain problem — they are a readout of what's in the blood: electrolytes and metabolic status, hematologic disease, a sedating or myopathic drug (he specifically flagged gabapentin), or infection. His approach was staged: review the pillbox, run basic labs and an infection screen, and only escalate to blood and fungal cultures or a peripheral smear if that first pass didn't explain things.

The itch resolved the moment the team addressed the obvious lead: his BUN was 122 on arrival, and once he was put back on his proper dialysis schedule, the pruritus went away. Uremic pruritus from underdialysis, straightforwardly.

The weakness didn't go away, and that's where the episode's real work began. Labs showed a hemoglobin of 6 g/dL with a mildly elevated MCV around 95–96, a steep fall from a documented hemoglobin of 12 a year earlier. The patient had a remote history of a significant upper GI bleed (three units of packed red cells) with EGD-proven bleeding duodenal AVMs, previously treated endoscopically. He was on carvedilol.

Robbie reasoned through anemia in ESRD as a layered problem. EPO deficiency is always present and always contributes, but rarely explains a drop this dramatic on its own. A more ESRD-specific mechanism is acquired von Willebrand deficiency plus bleeding from GI AVMs, both related to the turbulent flow through a dialysis access — a real and underappreciated cause of chronic blood loss in this population. With hemoglobin under 7 and the patient symptomatic, the threshold for transfusion was already met; his advice was to transfuse now and investigate the mechanism in parallel, not sequentially. He flagged the carvedilol as a confounder for interpretation going forward — a beta-blocker blunts the compensatory tachycardia that would otherwise hint at ongoing bleeding, so vital signs would be a less trustworthy early-warning sign in this patient than in a non-beta-blocked one.

The physical exam (a digital rectal exam performed, somewhat reluctantly, by the intern, with a bit of comic byplay about the attending delegating the task) didn't obtain stool on the first pass. Vitals were unremarkable — heart rate in the 80s, normal blood pressure. Conjunctival pallor was present; palmar crease pallor was not. No murmur, no rash.

Labs sent in parallel with the transfusion included a homocysteine, which came back elevated, with an MMA pending; empiric folic acid and B12 were started while results were awaited. SPEP, UPEP, and serum free light chains were sent as well — Robbie was explicit that multiple myeloma is a "do not miss" in a longstanding dialysis patient with new anemia, not an afterthought test. Iron studies returned a ferritin of 466 and a TSAT of 23%, both initially read by the team as reassuringly within normal range.

Robbie walked through the anemia-of-production framework he uses when bleeding and hemolysis have been reasonably excluded: "substance or stem cell." Either a substance or drug is actively suppressing marrow production (alcohol, methotrexate), or a substance needed for production is deficient (EPO, thyroid hormone, testosterone, B12, thiamine, pyridoxine). If a full substance workup comes back clean and there's no evidence of bleeding or hemolysis, the next step is a heme-onc referral for bone marrow biopsy to look for myelodysplastic syndrome or aplastic anemia. He also flagged an esoteric but cheap-to-exclude cause specific to elderly, denture-wearing patients: zinc-containing denture cream can cause copper deficiency and unexplained cytopenias. To distinguish a hypoproliferative process from hemolysis, he recommended a reticulocyte count alongside LDH and haptoglobin to rule out "sneaky hemolysis" before assuming this was purely a production problem. Those came back with a normal LDH, normal haptoglobin, and a hypoproliferative reticulocyte count — consistent with, though not conclusive for, a production-side process.

The turn in the case came from a medical student on rounds, who had been tracking the actual hemoglobin trajectory across transfusions: roughly 6, up to 8 after transfusion, drifting back down to 7, up again to around 8 with another unit, drifting back to roughly 7.4 — a pattern that wasn't holding the way a purely production-limited anemia should. The student argued for a GI consult despite the "normal-looking" ferritin and TSAT. Reza, invoking the golf framing again, agreed to call GI.

The GI consultant — a former chief resident the hosts described as cut from the same cloth — initially agreed that scoping wasn't clearly indicated, given the tempo and the reassuring iron studies. That's when Reza caught the detail that reframed the whole ferritin conversation: a year earlier, when this patient's hemoglobin was still a normal 12, his ferritin had been 1000 and his TSAT 33%. Today's ferritin of 466 and TSAT of 23% — read in isolation as "normal" — actually represented a substantial relative drop from his own baseline. The teaching point: ferritin is an acute-phase reactant, and its reliability as a marker of iron stores is degraded in ESRD; the number has to be interpreted against the patient's own prior values, not just a population reference range. On that basis, GI proceeded with endoscopy. That night, nursing reported melena and hematochezia — never directly witnessed by the physicians — reinforcing the case for a GI source. Endoscopy, however, found only duodenitis: mild inflammation, no active bleeding, and nothing severe enough to explain the magnitude of the anemia.

Robbie's final synthesis held the tension between two live possibilities: iron deficiency from cryptic GI blood loss (the base-rate favorite, and consistent with his known AVMs) versus myelodysplastic syndrome (the diagnosis carrying the most morbidity if missed). He suggested a peripheral smear review for dysplastic features as a relatively fast way to move the needle on that question. But he pulled back from chasing a definitive answer for its own sake, reframing around the patient himself: 92 years old, already living with ESRD as a life-limiting condition, asymptomatic and comfortable, and wanting to go home. The concluding move was explicit shared decision-making — laying out both possibilities for the patient, confirming that neither required emergent action, and choosing not to over-medicalize an anemia that wasn't currently causing him symptoms.

He was discharged home. The pruritus was resolved, he had no fatigue, he had a caregiver with him around the clock, and he was DNR/DNI with a good understanding of his prognosis. The anemia mechanism was left genuinely open — by design, not by omission.

Reza closed by tracing the proximal-cause chain the case had actually followed: pruritus, caused by misdialysis, caused in turn by his weakness, caused by his anemia, caused by either a cryptic GI bleed or an emerging MDS — with the shared decision-making conversation as, in his words, the cherry on top. He referenced two prior cases as texture: a case of Robbie's own, at his home hospital, of a dialysis patient with anemia that turned out to be myeloma; and an earlier RLR case involving a patient with alcohol use and anemia, where a surprisingly low ferritin was the thread that unraveled a GI bleed — reinforcing, from the opposite direction, just how much weight a single ferritin value can carry when read correctly.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Effect on Leading Hypothesis |
|---|---|
| Pruritus with no visible rash, no new medications, no psychiatric history | Lowers probability of drug-induced or delusional-parasitosis itch; raises probability of a systemic or missed-dermatologic cause |
| BUN 122 on arrival, recent pattern of skipped dialysis sessions | Strongly raises probability of uremic pruritus from underdialysis |
| Pruritus resolves after resuming adequate dialysis | Confirms uremic pruritus as the itch etiology |
| Hgb 6 (down from 12 one year prior), history of duodenal AVMs with prior GI bleed | Raises probability of recurrent AVM-related GI blood loss as the cause of weakness |
| Ferritin 466 / TSAT 23%, read against population norms | Falsely lowers probability of iron deficiency if interpreted without the patient's own baseline |
| Ferritin was 1000 / TSAT 33% one year earlier (same patient, at normal Hgb) | Reframes "normal" current ferritin/TSAT as a significant relative drop — raises probability of iron deficiency/blood loss |
| Homocysteine elevated, MMA pending | Keeps B12/folate deficiency on the differential as a contributing substance-deficiency cause |
| LDH normal, haptoglobin normal, reticulocyte count hypoproliferative | Lowers probability of hemolysis; supports a production-limited process (EPO deficiency, MDS, or substance deficiency) over consumption |
| Serial post-transfusion hemoglobins fail to hold (6→8→7→8→7.4) | Raises probability of ongoing occult blood loss over a pure production problem |
| Melena/hematochezia reported by nursing overnight | Raises probability of an active GI bleeding source |
| Endoscopy shows only mild duodenitis, no active bleeding | Lowers probability that GI blood loss alone fully explains the degree of anemia; reopens MDS as a live possibility |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Anchoring on a single "normal" ferritin/TSAT value nearly closed the GI-bleed hypothesis prematurely; the case is a clean example of reference-range anchoring bias being corrected by deliberately comparing the value to the patient's own prior data rather than to population norms. The medical student's contribution is a good illustration of the value of a fresh, less-anchored observer noticing a trend (the non-sustaining post-transfusion hemoglobin pattern) that the more senior team, having already provisionally filed the ferritin as reassuring, had under-weighted. The episode also models comfort with an unresolved differential: rather than premature closure around either cryptic GI bleed or MDS, the team tolerated ambiguity once both possibilities were confirmed to be non-emergent, and let the patient's goals — not diagnostic completeness — determine the endpoint of the workup.

## Teaching Discussion

The episode's throughline is that "normal" is a relative term in ESRD, and nowhere more so than with ferritin and TSAT — an acute-phase reactant whose population reference range can actively mislead when a patient's own baseline is available and ignored. It also models a clean division between action and investigation in symptomatic anemia: transfuse to meet an established threshold immediately, then investigate mechanism in parallel rather than delaying transfusion for a complete workup. The uremic pruritus thread is a reminder that not every itch needs an exotic explanation — checking dialysis adequacy first is often both the fastest and most correct move. Finally, the closing shared-decision-making conversation is presented as a legitimate clinical endpoint in its own right, not a failure to reach a diagnosis: in a frail, comfortable, asymptomatic 92-year-old, confirming that neither leading possibility is emergent and safety-netting for red flags can be exactly the right amount of workup.
