## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 65-year-old woman walks up a steep hill to the ED for progressive constipation unrelieved by an enema and a bowel regimen, and is found to have new urinary retention (PVR 512) alongside sinus tachycardia. Guest neurologist Aaron Berkowitz localizes bowel-plus-bladder dysfunction to the S2–4 roots (cauda equina/conus medullaris), reasoning through why isolated bowel symptoms are almost never neurologic and why an L1 lesion can still strike the sacral cord given where the spinal cord actually ends. A CT read as showing only diffuse constipation is later over-read, at the ED physician's insistence, to reveal an L1 lytic lesion compressing the cord — the same patient who, three weeks earlier, had been discharged from that same ED after a benign-seeming episode of scapular pain that resolved with ibuprofen, now understood in retrospect as an early presentation of multiple myeloma.

## Pearls

- Constipation carries its own red-flag triad, analogous to polyneuropathy's: young age of onset, acute onset, and systemic symptoms (here, sinus tachycardia) — any one should prompt looking past the reflexive, benign default.
- Sinus tachycardia is "a proximate diagnosis, not a terminal one" — once confirmed on EKG (narrow complex, P before QRS), the real work is asking what's driving it: pain, hypovolemia, infection, PE, thyrotoxicosis, and anemia are the usual suspects to screen first.
- Isolated bowel symptoms are almost never neurologic; a true neurologic cause requires the bladder to be involved too, because the parasympathetic supply to both the distal gut and the bladder converges in the S2–4 roots — ask about the bladder before invoking neurology for bowel complaints alone.
- Because the spinal cord ends at the L1–L2 vertebral level in adults (with the cauda equina's roots traveling further down to their exit foramina), a lesion at the L1 vertebral level is actually striking the most distal, sacral segments of the cord (the conus medullaris) — not a "high" lesion as the vertebral level alone might suggest.
- Bedside confrontational strength testing of the legs is often falsely reassuring; a patient can defeat simple resistance testing yet still have real proximal weakness revealed only by functional, full-body-weight maneuvers (squatting, rising from a chair with arms crossed, standing on one heel or toe).
- The classic CRAB mnemonic for myeloma (hyperCalcemia, Renal failure, Anemia, Bone lesions) lists bone lesions last, but in real-world presentation bone disease often comes first — the other markers of end-organ damage can still be absent, as they were here.

## Problem Representation

A 65-year-old woman with progressive, first-time constipation unrelieved by an enema and outpatient bowel regimen presents with new leg heaviness, sinus tachycardia, and, once specifically checked, a post-void residual of 512 mL, with a normal bedside strength and reflex exam; localization of the combined bowel-and-bladder dysfunction to the sacral (S2–4) cord ultimately traces, on a re-read CT scan, to an L1 lytic lesion compressing the conus medullaris — the first overt sign of multiple myeloma in a patient whose only preceding symptom, three weeks earlier, had been a self-limited episode of point-tender scapular pain.

## Case Narrative

A 65-year-old woman who looks unusually worn down presents to the ED reporting that she still hasn't had a bowel movement. She explains that she had come in three days earlier with two days of constipation, had a KUB showing stool burden, received an enema with initial relief, and was discharged on a bowel regimen (Miralax and a suppository, "above and below") — but despite adherence, she remains unable to have a bowel movement and now also feels generally unwell, with heavier-feeling legs. Notably, she walked the entire way up the steep hill to the hospital unassisted, and this is her first time ever experiencing constipation.

A bedside neurologic exam shows full strength and normal reflexes, but her heart rate is 110. Before more data are shared, the discussant frames constipation as usually benign (opiates, immobility, and reduced motility account for most inpatient cases) but flags three red flags analogous to those used for polyneuropathy: young age of onset, acute onset, and systemic symptoms — this patient has the latter, via her tachycardia. Because her pain had fully resolved with laxative therapy on the prior visit, treatment response is used as a diagnostic tool, favoring the possibility that dehydration or discomfort from constipation itself is driving the tachycardia rather than a separate process — but confirmation still requires working through the standard causes of sinus tachycardia: pain, hypovolemia, infection, pulmonary embolism, thyrotoxicosis, and anemia, after first confirming a stable EKG.

A post-void residual, obtained per routine ED practice, returns at 512 mL — new urinary retention. Guest discussant Aaron Berkowitz, a neurologist, reasons that isolated bowel symptoms are almost never a neurologic complaint in his experience, but the presence of bladder involvement changes the calculus entirely: the parasympathetic supply to the distal gastrointestinal tract and the bladder both arise from the S2–4 nerve roots (unlike the proximal gut, supplied by the vagus), making it hard to imagine a neurologic lesion affecting the bowel without also affecting the bladder — and vice versa. He works through acute-versus-chronic spinal cord physiology: a chronic lesion produces an upper-motor-neuron pattern (spasticity, bladder hyperactivity), while an acute lesion behaves like spinal shock — flaccid, areflexic, closer to a cauda equina picture — complicating interpretation of "normal" reflexes and strength on a single acute exam.

He raises two important caveats about the exam already performed: normal confrontational leg strength does not reliably exclude subtle proximal weakness, since pushing against resistance under-challenges muscles that can otherwise support the patient's full body weight (walking up a steep hill, in this case); a squat, a rise from a chair with arms crossed, or single-leg heel/toe raises are more sensitive functional tests. Reflexes must also be interpreted against age — normal ankle reflexes in a very elderly patient can, by some conventions, be considered relatively hyperreflexic given how commonly ankle jerks are physiologically lost with age. With no weakness, no radicular pain, and no sensory complaints beyond bowel and bladder, Dr. Berkowitz localizes to the S2–4 roots specifically, noting that the distinction between conus medullaris and cauda equina syndrome is anatomically and clinically overlapping enough that he does not find it practically useful to separate them — both point to the same next step, an MRI of the lumbosacral spine. He also raises two non-localizing alternatives for completeness: autonomic dysreflexia (a rehab-medicine-recognized phenomenon in spinal cord injury patients, where a full bladder or bowel triggers tachycardia and diaphoresis) and primary autonomic neuropathy (which can present with isolated bowel/bladder dysfunction plus subtler signs like pupillary or sweating abnormalities) — both felt less likely here given the acute, isolated presentation.

At this point the discussant pivots the story, presenting what sounds like an entirely separate case from two weeks earlier: a patient in her 60s with several days of point-tender pain over the scapula, no trauma, otherwise well, with a normal exam apart from focal tenderness. Asked how he'd approach this in an outpatient setting, the guest raises two possibilities: a preceding dermatomal pain that later develops vesicles (shingles), prompting a skin exam, and unrecognized trauma from activity the patient wouldn't think to mention (a new sport, a fall) — both worth asking about directly. The actual exam was unremarkable, no vesicles were present, and the presenter treated empirically with ibuprofen for a presumed mild inflammatory process; the patient returned days later reporting complete resolution and asked to switch back to Tylenol given concern about NSAID-related peptic ulcer risk. Pressed on whether this outcome would fully close the loop, the guest affirms it would — a benign presentation, treated, resolved, case closed.

The twist is then revealed: this is the same patient. The scapular pain visit, its resolution, a follow-up visit just to discuss switching analgesics, and now this fourth visit for constipation are all chapters of one continuous story — made vivid because the ED physician had built a real relationship with her over repeat visits (bonding over a shared love of Snickers bars) and was specifically alarmed by how unwell she looked compared to prior encounters. That alarm prompts a request for the radiologist to take a second look at the CT abdomen/pelvis already obtained (which had shown diffuse constipation without mechanical obstruction, with the bladder distension not appreciated because it had already been drained by straight catheterization before the scan). The over-read reveals a large lytic lesion at L1 compressing the spinal cord, plus scattered bony lesions elsewhere in the spine without mass effect — missed on the original read.

Dr. Berkowitz integrates this with the earlier neurologic reasoning: an L1 vertebral lesion, given where the spinal cord actually terminates (L1–L2, owing to the spinal column outgrowing the cord during development, with cauda equina roots traveling further down to their exit foramina), is striking the most distal, sacral segment of the cord — the conus medullaris — fully consistent with an isolated bowel-and-bladder syndrome rather than the more extensive deficits a "high" L1 lesion might otherwise suggest. He also offers, for completeness, that a prodromal frozen-shoulder-type presentation can rarely herald Parkinson's disease or its autonomic-predominant variants (multiple system atrophy) — a hypothesis explicitly discarded once the actual explanation (myeloma) emerges, but raised as an example of how retrospective coherence can tempt overfitting rare explanations onto vague, resolved complaints.

With diffuse lytic lesions established, cancer is treated as the leading category (with no clinical signal toward infection or a non-infectious inflammatory process), and the workup follows the base rate of disease: confirming up-to-date mammography, screening for lung cancer given the tachycardia's residual differential (including a low threshold for CTA to evaluate for PE from a hypercoagulable, cancer-associated state), and myeloma. Labs, fluids, and a CT chest/abdomen/pelvis are pursued; the tachycardia and AKI resolve with fluids, the CT chest is normal, and there is no hypercalcemia, anemia, or persistent renal insufficiency. The guest flags explicitly that a normal CT chest does not substitute for a mammogram (confirmed up to date and normal a year prior) and reframes the classic CRAB myeloma mnemonic: in real-world presentation, bone lesions often precede the other three markers rather than following them as the acronym's letter order implies — so myeloma should not be discounted by the absence of hypercalcemia or renal failure. SPEP, immunofixation, serum free light chains, and urine protein electrophoresis are sent.

The SPEP is diagnostic: an IgG lambda monoclonal spike over 4 g/dL and a kappa-to-lambda free light chain ratio over 150 (a ratio over 100 being essentially diagnostic in this context) confirm multiple myeloma — notably without the more advanced end-organ markers (hypercalcemia, renal failure, anemia) that classically accompany a later-stage presentation. She receives high-dose IV dexamethasone in the ED and undergoes debulking surgery three days later; final tissue pathology is still pending at the time of discussion, but the SPEP result has already confirmed the diagnosis.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Treatment is used explicitly as a diagnostic test twice in this case: first, full symptomatic relief with laxative therapy on the initial visit is used to argue that the constipation itself (not a separate process) explains the associated tachycardia; second, the NSAID-responsive resolution of the scapular pain on the "decoy" visit is used, in the moment, to close that case appropriately — only for that same resolution to be reinterpreted in hindsight once the fuller picture emerges.

The episode explicitly models the limits of real-time diagnosis versus retrospective coherence: the guest reflects that a benign-seeming complaint (mild, point-tender shoulder pain resolving with an over-the-counter NSAID) does not warrant an exhaustive malignancy workup at the time it presents, and that punishing oneself in hindsight for not extensively testing every mundane complaint is the wrong lesson — medicine's signal-to-noise ratio is low, and it typically takes more than one data point (a returning patient, a new finding) for a truly rare process to rise above the noise.

The ED physician's continuity with this specific patient — built over multiple visits and a shared personal rapport — is explicitly credited as the reason the CT scan was pushed for a second, more careful read; without that relationship-driven alarm at how unwell she looked, the original (missed) read may have stood, illustrating how clinical gestalt built from repeated contact with a patient can catch what a single encounter's imaging read did not.

A closing pearl from mentor Lawrence Tierney is invoked directly: "if a patient with a [compressive] spinal cord lesion walks into the hospital, if that doctor is smart enough to diagnose it early, they should be able to walk out of the hospital" — framing early recognition of a still-reversible compressive lesion as the entire game, since delayed diagnosis risks permanent myelopathy.

## Teaching Discussion

Constipation shares a red-flag structure with other common, usually-benign complaints (e.g., polyneuropathy): young age of onset, acute onset, and systemic symptoms should each prompt looking beyond the default benign explanation (opiates, immobility, reduced motility).

Sinus tachycardia should always be treated as a proximate finding requiring its own explanation once confirmed on EKG (narrow complex, P wave before each QRS) — the standard causes to screen are pain, hypovolemia, infection, pulmonary embolism, thyrotoxicosis, and anemia.

The parasympathetic innervation of the distal gastrointestinal tract and the bladder both originate from the S2–4 nerve roots (distinct from the vagal supply to the more proximal gut), which is why isolated bowel dysfunction without bladder involvement is rarely neurologic, while combined bowel-and-bladder dysfunction should prompt localization to this sacral region.

Acute spinal cord lesions behave like spinal shock (flaccid, areflexic — mirroring flaccid paralysis in the limbs), while chronic lesions produce an upper-motor-neuron pattern (spasticity, bladder hyperreflexia); this distinction matters for interpreting an exam performed early in a compressive process, where reflexes and strength may appear falsely normal.

Because the spinal cord terminates at the L1–L2 vertebral level in adults (a consequence of the vertebral column outgrowing the cord during development, with the cauda equina's roots traveling further down the canal to their exit foramina), a lesion at the L1 vertebral level actually involves the most distal (sacral, conus medullaris) segment of the cord — not a rostral, "high" lesion, despite L1's position in the vertebral column.

Conus medullaris syndrome and cauda equina syndrome have overlapping, difficult-to-separate clinical pictures in practice (conus: more midline pain, possible preserved/brisk reflexes from residual cord tissue; cauda equina: more radicular pain, lost reflexes from root involvement) — the distinction is often not clinically actionable, since both point toward the same next step, an MRI of the lumbosacral spine.

Confrontational bedside strength testing under-detects subtle weakness, particularly proximally, because simple resistance testing doesn't require the examiner to apply full body-weight-equivalent force; functional maneuvers (squatting, rising from a chair with arms crossed, single-limb heel/toe raises) more reliably reveal weakness a patient can otherwise "pass" on confrontational testing.

For diffuse lytic bone lesions without a clear infectious or inflammatory signal, cancer should be the leading category by default; workup should apply the base rate of disease (confirm mammography status, assess lung cancer risk factors, and evaluate for myeloma), recognizing that a normal CT chest excludes neither breast cancer (which requires dedicated mammography) nor early myeloma (which can precede the classic CRAB tetrad's other components).

Structurally compressive cauda equina or conus medullaris syndrome is a surgical (or radiation oncology) emergency because compression without tissue damage is reversible if promptly decompressed; the "surgical emergency" framing should be qualified to structural/compressive causes specifically, since inflammatory or infectious causes of cauda equina dysfunction (arachnoiditis, sarcoidosis) are managed medically rather than surgically.
