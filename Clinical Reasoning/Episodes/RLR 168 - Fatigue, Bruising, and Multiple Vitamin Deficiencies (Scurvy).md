> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 45-year-old man presents with a few weeks of [[fatigue]], newer nausea with a little blood-streaked emesis, and — what finally brought him in — leg swelling and striking [[ecchymoses|bruising]]. His history holds two quiet clues: heavy nightly alcohol (self-reported three-to-four beers) and **[[activated charcoal]] prescribed off-label for IBS**, taken several times daily. Labs show an isolated **[[indirect hyperbilirubinemia]]** (total bili 5, direct 1), **anemia** (Hgb 5.7, MCV 91) with reticulocytosis, mild **[[leukopenia]]** (WBC 3.7), borderline thrombocytopenia (136), and an **INR of 1.9 with a normal PTT**. A methodical [[hemolysis workup]] is negative (normal LDH/haptoglobin, negative [[direct antiglobulin test|DAT]], normal smear), a CT pan-scan shows no occult bleed, the INR **corrects fully with a single dose of vitamin K**, and the marrow shows erythroid hyperplasia with **increased iron stores but no ring sideroblasts**. The unifying diagnosis is **multiple vitamin deficiencies** — undetectable [[vitamin C deficiency|vitamin C]] ([[scurvy]]) plus low [[thiamine deficiency|B1]] and [[pyridoxine deficiency|B6]] — from alcohol and charcoal binding water-soluble vitamins. The cognitive-autopsy lesson: the team over-framed the anemia as **acute** when it was subacute/chronic, and the path forward was **solving the "why"** (nutrition, hidden alcohol) rather than only cataloguing the "what."

**Pearls**

- **[[Fatigue]] is a neurological sensation, not a vague symptom** — its schema overlaps heavily with [[altered mental status]] ([[MIST]]), and the shared workup answer is "show me what's in the blood": electrolytes, thyroid, infection, iron, anemia.
- **Any bruising or bleeding triggers three reflex questions**: platelet count, INR, PTT. Bruising is bleeding into skin, and the coagulation profile localizes it.
- **[[Indirect hyperbilirubinemia]] has a hemolysis mimic**: as large [[ecchymoses]]/hematomas are resorbed, breakdown of extravasated red cells raises indirect bilirubin — so a "hemolytic" picture can be pure bleeding. (And true hemolysis rarely pushes bilirubin above ~6.)
- **[[Haptoglobin]] is the most sensitive test for hemolysis** — a normal haptoglobin (with normal LDH and a negative DAT) effectively takes hemolysis off the table.
- **Bleeding with normal platelets and underwhelming coagulopathy** opens a specific schema: qualitative platelet defects, qualitative coagulopathy, vessel-wall disease — and the no-miss **[[Factor XIII deficiency]]**, which is invisible on PT/PTT and needs a direct factor level.
- **Iron excess in the *marrow*** (not the liver) points to the [[sideroblastic anemia]] spectrum and its acquired causes — alcohol, drugs, copper deficiency, **B6 deficiency** — a very different pathway from [[hemochromatosis]].
- **Get the tempo right, or nothing else works**: reframing this anemia from acute to subacute dissolved the paradox of "profound anemia with no hemolysis and no big bleed."

**Problem Representation**
A 45-year-old man with heavy alcohol use and chronic activated-charcoal ingestion presents with subacute fatigue, ecchymoses/petechiae, edema, isolated indirect hyperbilirubinemia, non-hemolytic anemia, leukopenia, and a vitamin K–responsive coagulopathy, ultimately diagnosed with multiple water-soluble vitamin deficiencies (scurvy plus thiamine and pyridoxine).

## Case Narrative

This is a guest episode: **Paul Kunit**, a clinician-educator at St. Louis University and a CP Solvers listener "since the very beginning," presents a case from his residency that he has revisited for years. Rabih hosts and discusses the opening alone because Reza is caught in a rapid response — "a rapid was called for a slow heart rate" — and joins after the second aliquot.

Paul opens: a 45-year-old man with a few weeks of progressive [[fatigue]], then a couple of weeks of bad nausea with three episodes of emesis containing a little blood (no bile), and finally the alarm — leg swelling and, more worrying to him, **bad bruising on both legs**. Rabih reframes fatigue not as a throwaway complaint but as a **neurological sensation**, and offers the thought exercise of overlaying the [[fatigue]] schema on the [[altered mental status]] schema ([[MIST]]): the overlap is enormous, and the common thread is "blood, blood, blood" — electrolytes, thyroid, anemia, infection. The workup for fatigue, like AMS, is fundamentally **"show me what's in the blood."** Given swelling plus bruising, he anticipates the fatigue will translate into either a volume-status or a hematologic problem, and wants the exam to clarify the edema and whether the "bruising" is purpuric or ecchymotic.

More history: anxiety, **IBS (diarrhea-predominant)**, no tobacco, but **three-to-four beers nightly**, propranolol PRN, and — unusually — **[[activated charcoal]] prescribed by a PCP for IBS**. Now present, Reza flags two threads. First, IBS is not a diagnosis of exclusion but does demand a coherent illness script (the modern framing: a [[disorder of gut-brain interaction]]), and a diarrhea-predominant label should not close the door on other causes. Second, activated charcoal off-label is either a **red herring or the key** — its only classic use is binding a recent toxic ingestion — and he wonders aloud whether it could somehow drive bleeding. But it is the **bruising** that anchors him: bruising means bleeding into the skin, and *any* bleeding demands the three questions — **platelet count, INR, PTT**.

The exam: normal vitals, **scleral icterus**, no JVD or murmurs, mild epigastric tenderness, pitting edema to above the knees, and **large ecchymoses and petechiae on the anterior legs**. Rabih's line becomes the chapter title — "too many colors that shouldn't be there" — and he leans toward an unhappy **liver** (jaundice, epigastric pain, bleeding, plus a non-FDA-approved toxin), while conceding how hard it is to **disentangle heme from hepatic** when they interact (invoking a Wilson's hemolytic crisis as an example of the merge).

The labs sharpen and complicate: **normal BMP, normal AST/ALT/alkaline phosphatase**, but **total bilirubin 5 with direct only 1** (indirect-predominant); **WBC 3.7, Hgb 5.7, MCV 91, platelets 136**; **INR 1.9 with a normal PTT**. Reza builds the [[indirect hyperbilirubinemia]] framework: elevated bilirubin comes down to hepatobiliary disease versus **hemolysis**, and an indirect predominance puts hemolysis front and center — but he shares a humbling prior case where an identical picture with a negative smear and DAT turned out to be **resorption of extensive ecchymoses** raising the indirect bilirubin. So he runs two hypotheses in parallel: real hemolysis (send smear, DAT, and note hemolysis rarely exceeds a bilirubin of 6) versus bruising as the *source* of the indirect bilirubin. The isolated **PT elevation** (normal PTT) points him to **factor VII**, and he wonders about an acquired factor deficiency, antibiotics, or vitamin K deficiency — while puzzling over the **edema** (which Rabih ties to portal hypertension and the liver).

Rabih pumps the brakes: this is the 5% of patients where even after the labs you have neither a diagnosis nor a clear next step, and you must slow down. He reframes what is *known* — the patient is bleeding (the exam proves it), the edema may simply be blood in the legs rather than transudate, the indirect hyperbilirubinemia-plus-anemia may be a separate hemolytic problem *or* a marker of hematoma resorption and occult bleeding, the **leukopenia** does not map onto bleeding, and the **INR is "tantalizingly annoying"** — abnormal but not 7. His plan: low threshold for **visceral imaging** to exclude occult bleeding, a basic **hemolysis workup** ([[direct antiglobulin test|Coombs]] + smear), and **vitamin K** to see if the INR melts away — while fixating on the bleeding first, as the deadliest thread.

Paul delivers the next data: **LDH and haptoglobin normal, DAT negative, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis unremarkable, reticulocytes 6%**, and the **INR normalized after one dose of vitamin K**; the **smear is unremarkable**. Reza now moves decisively: **[[haptoglobin]] is the most sensitive test for hemolysis**, so a normal haptoglobin (with normal LDH, negative DAT) lets him **take hemolysis off the table**. The reticulocytosis with an MCV of 90 says the marrow is responding to an anemia, and acute anemia has three causes — **bleeding, hemolysis, or leukemia** — but there are no peripheral blasts and no DIC. Yet he is stuck: a hemoglobin of 5.7 with a *negative* pan-scan and only bruises does not reconcile a truly acute bleed, and hemolysis is excluded.

The bleeding-with-normal-platelets-and-underwhelming-coagulopathy schema surfaces the no-miss: **[[Factor XIII deficiency]]** — invisible on PT/PTT, potentially deadly, diagnosable only by a **direct factor level**. But Rabih pivots to the vitamin K responsiveness as a clue toward a **nutritional** story, and — noting the marrow's forthcoming **iron excess** — reasons that iron overload in the *marrow* (not the liver) sits on the [[sideroblastic anemia]] spectrum, whose acquired causes include drugs and **B6 deficiency / vitamin C deficiency**. He articulates the method that cracks the case: stop cataloguing *what* the patient has and **solve the *why*** — the risk factors, the person before the illness, the **nutritional history** and likely **hidden alcohol use**. His proposed move is a harmless, high-return intervention: **IV vitamin C**.

Paul confirms the final data: **ferritin 480 with normal transferrin saturation** (arguing against iron-deficiency and for an acute-on-nutritional process), EGD showing only **gastritis**, and a **normocellular marrow with trilineage hematopoiesis, erythroid hyperplasia, and increased iron stores without ring sideroblasts**. Then the "diagnostic test": **vitamin levels** — **thiamine (B1) low at 26, pyridoxine (B6) low at 4, and vitamin C undetectable**. The diagnosis is **multiple vitamin deficiencies — [[scurvy]] plus B1 and B6 — from the combination of alcohol use and activated charcoal binding water-soluble vitamins**.

In the **cognitive autopsy**, Reza names the central error: the team **framed the anemia as acute** when nutritional deficiency causes a **subacute-to-chronic** anemia — the fatigue was the subacute anchor, while nausea, swelling, and bruising were the acute overlay. Getting the tempo wrong pigeonholed the entire reasoning. Paul adds the mechanism: [[vitamin C deficiency]] causes anemia not only via bleeding into tissues but through **impaired red-cell maturation and iron metabolism**, and the ecchymoses-with-petechiae correctly pointed at platelet or **vessel-wall** disease all along.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The decisive bias is **tempo mis-framing**: anchoring on *acute* anemia (with its bleeding/hemolysis/leukemia triad) generated an unsolvable paradox once hemolysis was excluded and no large bleed was found. The correction — recognizing a **subacute/chronic** nutritional anemia — dissolved the paradox. A second, more subtle trap is the **hemolysis mimic** of indirect hyperbilirubinemia: without Reza's prior experience of hematoma resorption raising indirect bilirubin, the team might have over-invested in a hemolytic workup. The rescue heuristic is **"solve the why"** — shifting from an exhaustive catalog of abnormalities to the patient's risk factors and pre-illness life (nutrition, concealed alcohol), which is what surfaced the unifying nutritional diagnosis. The episode also models **calibrated humility** (both experts openly "stuck") and the value of a **harmless, high-ROI empiric** (IV vitamin C) when the pretest probability of a treatable deficiency is meaningful.

## Teaching Discussion

**Fatigue is a blood-based workup.** Treating [[fatigue]] as a neurological sensation whose schema mirrors [[altered mental status]] ([[MIST]]) reframes a "nonspecific" complaint into a structured search: electrolytes, thyroid, glucose, infection, and — centrally — the [[complete blood count|blood counts]]. The difference from AMS is that fatigue is rarely an intra-brain problem (sleep disorders and sedating drugs excepted); most of the yield is in the blood.

**Bruising is data, and the coagulation trio localizes it.** Any bleeding — epistaxis, hematemesis, hematuria, ecchymoses — should reflexively prompt **platelet count, INR, PTT**. Here that trio produced a specific pattern (normal platelets, isolated PT elevation, normal PTT) that both pointed at factor VII / vitamin K and, crucially, was *underwhelming* relative to the severity of bruising — the mismatch that ultimately demanded a vessel-wall/nutritional explanation.

**The indirect-bilirubin hemolysis mimic.** [[Indirect hyperbilirubinemia]] classically splits into hepatobiliary disease and **hemolysis**, but a third, easily missed mechanism is **resorption of extravasated blood** — large [[ecchymoses]] or hematomas breaking down to raise indirect bilirubin. Two anchors help: hemolysis rarely drives bilirubin above ~6, and **[[haptoglobin]] is the most sensitive screen** — a normal haptoglobin with normal LDH and a negative [[direct antiglobulin test|DAT]] essentially excludes hemolysis. Running the "hemolysis vs bleeding-as-source" hypotheses in parallel is the disciplined move.

**Bleeding with a clean coagulation profile.** When a patient bleeds despite normal platelets and only mild coagulopathy, the differential is qualitative platelet dysfunction, qualitative coagulopathy, and **vessel-wall disease** — and the buried no-miss is **[[Factor XIII deficiency]]**, which does not prolong PT or PTT and requires a **direct factor assay**. Vessel-wall causes are rarely life-threatening but are exactly where [[scurvy]] lives, since vitamin C is required for collagen synthesis and vascular integrity.

**Marrow iron localizes the problem.** Iron excess in the **bone marrow** (as opposed to the parenchymal iron of [[hemochromatosis]], which loads the liver) places a case on the [[sideroblastic anemia]] spectrum. Even without ring sideroblasts, this frame usefully surfaces the **acquired** causes — alcohol, isoniazid and other drugs, copper deficiency, and **pyridoxine (B6) deficiency** — dovetailing with the eventual multi-vitamin diagnosis.

**Tempo is the master variable.** The cognitive-autopsy lesson is that **getting the tempo right precedes getting the diagnosis right**. Nutritional deficiencies produce **subacute-to-chronic** anemia; forcing them into an *acute* frame made the data irreconcilable. The [[vitamin C deficiency|scurvy]] anemia itself is multifactorial — bleeding into tissues, impaired erythroid maturation, and disturbed iron metabolism — which is why it need not obey the "acute anemia = bleed/hemolysis/leukemia" rule.

**Solve the "why," and add charcoal to the list.** The method that cracked the case was refusing to stop at *what* the patient had and instead asking *why* — probing nutrition and likely concealed alcohol use. The novel mechanism is worth filing: **[[activated charcoal]]**, taken multiple times daily, can bind **water-soluble vitamins**, so a patient already marginal from alcohol can be tipped into overt **multiple deficiencies** (undetectable vitamin C, low B1 and B6). Multiple contributors are more common than a single unifying cause, and a harmless empiric (**IV vitamin C**, broad repletion) offers a high return when deficiency is plausible.
