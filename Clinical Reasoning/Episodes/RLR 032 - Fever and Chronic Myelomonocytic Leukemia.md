## Pearls

- **When the history is unreliable, decide explicitly what to bold and what to bracket.** Rabih makes acute [[Fever|fever]] the bold core — corroborated by leukocytosis and thrombocytopenia — and puts the chart's "abdominal pain" in parentheses, because a hearing-impaired patient interviewed without aids or good collateral means the *localizing* complaint may be an artifact of a bad history. [[Framing Effect|How the data are weighted]] is the first move when jumping onto a moving train.
- **Anemia plus thrombocytopenia is not automatically DIC — hold your breath and read the smear for three things.** Rabih's checklist: **schistocytes** (MAHA/TTP), a **heparin** exposure (HIT), and **blasts** (leukemia). The reflex to run with sepsis-DIC is usually right, which is exactly why the checklist exists; here the answer was blasts.
- **Sustained [[Monocytosis|monocytosis]] has a short differential led by [[Chronic Myelomonocytic Leukemia|CMML]]** — everything else is reactive (TB is the classic infectious link) — and the automated differential can misread monocytes as blasts or activated lymphocytes, so verify on the smear. Chronicity is the single strongest discriminator: monocytes persistently >1×10⁹/L for >6 months points hard at a chronic marrow process. [[Base Rate|Base rate]] plus tempo.
- **Read the smear as the marrow talking to the blood, and ask *pulled* vs *pushed*.** Reticulocytosis and large platelets are a peripheral **pull** (destruction/consumption with a responsive marrow); monocytosis and immature myeloid forms are a marrow **push** (an infiltrative process crowding cells out). Seeing both at once means a *simultaneous* marrow and peripheral process — a primary leukemia driving immune-mediated destruction (the [[Evans Syndrome|Evans]]-like arc). [[Test Interpretation]].
- **When a treated hypothesis doesn't respond, force the three-way split: wrong drug, right diagnosis needing more time, or wrong diagnosis.** Rising WBC and persistent fever *after* source control — plus an interventional radiologist who says the gallbladder "didn't look infected" — argue for wrong/incomplete, and [[Acalculous Cholecystitis|acalculous cholecystitis]] is itself usually *triggered* by another critical illness, so an untriggered case demands "what set this off?" [[Diagnostic Momentum|Momentum resisted, not inherited.]]
- **Nodules are deep-space lesions — either a primary deep dermatosis or infiltration from afar — and a full-body skin exam can put the answer on the surface.** [[Skin Nodule|Rabih's schema]]: [[Panniculitis|panniculitis]] or [[Sweet Syndrome|neutrophilic dermatosis]] (primary) versus granulomatous ([[Cutaneous Tuberculosis|TB]]) or metastatic tumor including [[Leukemia Cutis|leukemia cutis]] (infiltrative). After thousands of dollars of imaging, looking under the gown found the nodule; biopsy of the accessible lesion clinched it.

## Problem Representation

An 84-year-old man with diabetes, hypertension, and gout — with no medical care in over a decade and a severely limited history (hearing impairment with refused aids, limited English, unhelpful collateral) — is admitted for fever (to 104°F at home) and right-upper-quadrant pain, later reframed on better interpreter history as three-plus weeks of nausea, anorexia, and fever. He is febrile and tachycardic in atrial fibrillation, with a WBC of 22k (rising to 29k with a striking monocyte predominance, ~14k), hemoglobin 10.8, platelets 58k, a mild AST>ALT transaminitis with a rising direct-predominant hyperbilirubinemia, an elevated BNP (700 from prior 200s), a mild AKI, an elevated LDH, and small pulmonary opacities. Cross-sectional imaging shows an edematous, markedly thickened, stoneless gallbladder read as acalculous cholecystitis; a cholecystostomy tube grows only scant Proteus and the gallbladder "did not look infected," while fever and leukocytosis persist despite source control and broad antibiotics, and MRCP shows normal ducts with a decompressed but still-thickened gallbladder. The peripheral smear shows monocytosis with immature myeloid cells, reticulocytosis, and large platelets, and a full-body skin exam reveals a nodular erythematous chest rash — biopsied to leukemia cutis (MPO/lysozyme/CD56-positive blasts), with bone marrow confirming chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML-2), the unifying diagnosis behind the fever, monocytosis, cytopenias, cutaneous nodules, and pulmonary and (pseudo-cholecystitic) organ involvement.

## Case Narrative

Guest Dr. Will Cope — a clinical-reasoning educator at UCLA — presents a patient he first "met through the chart," having come on service two days into the admission. An 84-year-old man with diabetes, hypertension, and gout was admitted for four days of fever to 104°F and right-upper-quadrant pain with intermittent headache, poor appetite, and gas. The history is severely limited: the patient is hearing-impaired and refuses to place his aids for the interpreter interview, speaks limited English, and the son cannot supply medications or history. On presentation he is febrile, tachycardic to 139 in atrial fibrillation, and normotensive. Labs show hemoglobin 10.8, WBC 22k, platelets 58k, a mild AKI, an AST/ALT both in the 40s with alk phos 150 and total bilirubin 2 (direct 1), and a BNP of 700 (prior in the 200s). A chest film shows small opacities (aspiration vs atelectasis vs scarring); CT and ultrasound show an edematous, markedly thickened, *stoneless* gallbladder read as concerning for [[Acalculous Cholecystitis|acalculous cholecystitis]]. He is started on vancomycin/piperacillin-tazobactam and fluids.

Rabih frames the challenge of jumping onto a "moving train": deciding what to **bold** and what to put in **parentheses**. He bolds acute [[Fever|fever]] — corroborated as infection by the leukocytosis and thrombocytopenia of sepsis physiology — and brackets the abdominal pain, given the unreliable history. Working acute abdominal pain by [[VIPO Framework|VIPO]] (vascular, inflammatory, perforation, obstruction), he notes the *inflammatory/infectious* branch is relatively uncommon and, when present, points to the three G's — GI, GU, and GYN sources. The gallbladder is on the hook, but the elevated BNP and pulmonary opacities are a wrench: the true process may be hiding in the **chest** (the heart) and falsely localizing to the right upper quadrant via [[Congestive Hepatopathy|congestion]] squeezing the gallbladder. Reza turns to the labs, pairing the anemia with a platelet count of 58k: [[Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation|DIC]] is prioritized given infection, but anemia-with-thrombocytopenia also demands consideration of a [[Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia|microangiopathic]] process. The AST>ALT ratio hints at an *extrahepatic* source of abnormal chemistries, and the indirect hyperbilirubinemia could be hemolysis (or Gilbert's) — so he would get a smear, LDH, haptoglobin, and PT/PTT/fibrinogen, even floating a hypothetical TTP-with-mesenteric-thrombosis to explain RUQ pain plus cytopenias.

A cholecystostomy tube goes in within 18 hours, but over the next 48 the patient keeps fevering, the WBC climbs to 29k, and the LFTs worsen (AST 67, alk phos 205, total bilirubin 5, direct 4.1). Reza reframes: with source control achieved, is the antibiotic wrong, does the right diagnosis just need more time (like pyelonephritis), or is the hypothesis wrong entirely? The uptrending WBC and persistent fever favor the third — a resistant organism, an additional source, or a wrong tree — and, critically, acalculous cholecystitis is usually *triggered* by another severe illness, so "what set this off?" remains unanswered. He would image the chest and check a troponin (myocarditis). Rabih adds: scrutinize the EKG for conduction disease (endocarditis), call micro, and — given diabetes — stay open to [[Candidemia|candidemia]] (blood-culture sensitivity only ~70%), while prioritizing source control.

Will delivers the yield. The interventional radiologist says the gallbladder "really didn't look like a gallbladder infection," with only scant Proteus from the tube; the MRCP shows normal ducts and a decompressed-but-still-thickened gallbladder; antibiotics are switched for C. diff concern. And the overlooked datum: the WBC of 29k is only ~9.5k segmented neutrophils and ~14k **monocytes** — a striking [[Monocytosis|monocytosis]] present at admission too. Rabih flags the caveat that automated counters misread monocytes (possibly blasts or activated lymphocytes), but names that *sustained* monocytosis has a short differential led by [[Chronic Myelomonocytic Leukemia|CMML]], and that chronicity is the key. Reza makes the leukocytosis the [[Center of Gravity|center of gravity]]: an 84-year-old marrow, largely replaced by fat, that can still mount this output should give pause — is this a *primary* white-cell disorder or reactive? Following Gurpreet Dhaliwal's teaching, the monocytosis schema is CMML first, then everything inflammatory. He would get a smear, consider [[Myelodysplastic Syndrome|MDS]] (age, MCV) and its transformation potential, and track two arcs: an underlying leukemia rendering the patient immunocompromised with a superimposed untreated infection, versus a non-infectious primary hematologic malignancy.

Heme-onc reads the smear as monocytosis with a left shift and immature myeloid cells, no blasts, large platelets, and reticulocytosis — "likely reactive versus a chronic hematologic process." Reza seizes on two findings: the reticulocytosis and the **large platelets** (distinct from the *giant* platelets of Bernard-Soulier). Large platelets mean the marrow is releasing immature forms because the periphery is destroying them — raising [[Immune Thrombocytopenia|ITP]] (secondary) — and reticulocytosis reflects a marrow responding to acute anemia (blood loss or [[Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia|hemolysis]]). Rabih formalizes it: the smear shows things that "shouldn't be there" — monocytes, reticulocytes, large platelets — the marrow talking to the blood. But there is a tension. Reticulocytosis and large platelets are a peripheral **pull** (something is destroying cells and the marrow is compensating), whereas monocytosis is a marrow **push** (a primary process crowding cells into the periphery). Both at once means a *simultaneous* marrow and peripheral process. The most powerful next step is establishing the **chronicity** of the monocytosis — sustained monocytosis over months would anchor a chronic marrow syndrome with a superimposed peripheral destructive process — and the open question is whether CMML causes immune cytopenias. Reza names the unifying possibility: a primary hematologic malignancy driving [[Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia|AIHA]] and [[Immune Thrombocytopenia|ITP]] — an [[Evans Syndrome|Evans-syndrome]]-like picture, as CLL classically does, though the monocyte analogue is uncertain. A look-back is unrevealing: no records for over a decade.

Will finally *meets* the patient, with family present. A careful interpreter history reframes the chief complaint from abdominal pain to three-plus weeks of nausea and anorexia with fever; the man was born in Iran but moved to the US in childhood with no travel in 50 years. And a full-body skin exam — Will's favorite maneuver — reveals a nodular, erythematous, palpable rash over the anterior chest (with lesions on the arms and back), present about ten days and worsening. Rabih offers a [[Skin Nodule|nodule schema]]: nodules are deep-space processes, meaning either a primary deep skin disease — [[Panniculitis|panniculitis]] (fat) or a [[Sweet Syndrome|neutrophilic dermatosis]] like Sweet syndrome — or infiltration from afar — granulomatous disease (nodular [[Cutaneous Tuberculosis|TB]]) or a metastatic tumor, liquid ([[Leukemia Cutis|leukemia cutis]]) or solid. He notes nodules imply chronicity (unless Sweet, which could be a reactive component here) and that *monocytes love tissue* — a mechanistic bridge between blood monocytosis and skin infiltration.

Reza reads it as one process infiltrating skin, lung, and marrow *plus* peripheral destruction, and would send a direct Coombs to tie it together. Delighted the patient is from his hometown, he lays out two hypotheses. First, granulomatous infection — **tuberculosis**, endemic in Iran, which can infiltrate skin (erythema induratum, erythema nodosum, or direct nodules), bone, and lung, cause monocytosis (a classic old association, like the tree-in-bud sign), and would not respond to the given antibiotics. Second, **malignancy** — leukemic infiltration of the subcutis — which would explain the monocytosis, the elevated LDH, and the lungs. The tiebreaker is a biopsy of the accessible nodule. Rabih agrees the honest answer is that only chronicity data or tissue can break the TB-versus-CMML tie (fever + nodules fits either, with Sweet as a possible acute overlay), and that flow cytometry showing a monoclonal population would help.

The same-hour dermatology biopsy clinches it: **leukemia cutis** — dermal infiltration by large agranular blasts positive for myeloperoxidase, lysozyme, and CD56 (a myeloid/myelomonocytic acute leukemia). The bone marrow confirms **CMML-2** — chronic myelomonocytic leukemia with a peripheral blast preponderance (a repeat smear now showed blasts; <20% blasts defines the higher-grade CMML-2, ≥20% would be AML). He was treated with hydroxyurea then decitabine, with a guarded prognosis (median overall survival ~16 months). In a closing [[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive autopsy]], Reza notes how easy it was to run the severe thrombocytopenia as sepsis-DIC — and how Rabih's discipline saves you: when you see anemia or thrombocytopenia, hold your breath at the smear and run the checklist — **schistocytes, heparin, blasts** — trending the smear over time. Rabih reflects on the [[Diagnostic Momentum|momentum]] of thousands of dollars of accumulated data that Will's push to examine the skin overturned, echoing a recent case where a rash revealed the cause of an SIADH (cf. [[RLR 029 - Dyspnea and Disseminated Varicella-Zoster|RLR 029]]), and commits to looking "under the gown."

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Framing Effect|Data triage — bold versus parentheses]].** Rabih's opening move on a chart-only, hearing-limited history is to weight the data explicitly: fever (with lab corroboration) in bold, abdominal pain in parentheses. Naming which findings you trust — and why — is what keeps a bad history from silently anchoring the localization, and it leaves room to promote the bracketed items later.
- **[[Diagnostic Momentum|Momentum resisted on a moving train]].** The acalculous-cholecystitis label, a placed drain, and days of accumulated data create strong inertia; the corrective is the three-way "wrong drug / more time / wrong diagnosis" split, the proceduralist's bedside impression, and the reminder that acalculous cholecystitis should have a *trigger*. The episode is a clinic in reopening an inherited frame rather than treating harder within it.
- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|Stacked schemas]] carry the case.** VIPO for abdominal pain, the monocytosis differential (CMML then reactive), the pulled-versus-pushed reading of the smear, the anemia/thrombocytopenia "schistocytes-heparin-blasts" checklist, and the nodule schema (panniculitis/neutrophilic dermatosis vs infiltrative TB/leukemia) each convert an overwhelming problem into a few moves — and it is the *nodule* schema, applied to a finding only the skin exam revealed, that localizes the answer.
- **[[Center of Gravity|Choosing the center of gravity]].** Reza deliberately relocates the organizing finding from the gallbladder to the leukocytosis/monocytosis, reasoning that an 84-year-old marrow producing this output is the anomaly that most constrains the differential — the pivot from an infectious to a hematologic frame.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Reading the smear and the counts against physiology]].** Large versus giant platelets (peripheral destruction vs inherited macrothrombocytopenia), reticulocytosis as a marrow response rather than an infiltrative output, the AST/ALT ratio pointing extrahepatic, and the caveat that automated differentials misclassify monocytes/blasts — each datum is interpreted by mechanism, not read off the report.
- **[[Base Rate|Base rate]] and the [[Cognitive Autopsy|autopsy]].** Sustained monocytosis is CMML-until-proven-otherwise by base rate; DIC is the base-rate read of sepsis thrombocytopenia, which is exactly why the closing autopsy formalizes the smear checklist as a guard. Both hosts and the guest model [[Diagnostic Humility|humility]] about thin CMML scripts and commit to reading — and to the physical exam that reset the case.

## Teaching Discussion

- On a poor history, weight the data explicitly — bold the corroborated findings (here fever, backed by leukocytosis and thrombocytopenia) and bracket the uncertain localizers — and revisit the brackets as new data arrive, rather than letting a limited interview anchor where you look.
- Meet anemia-plus-thrombocytopenia with a deliberate pause at the smear for schistocytes (MAHA/TTP), a heparin exposure (HIT), and blasts (leukemia) before defaulting to DIC; the default is usually right, which is why the exceptions need a hard-wired checklist, and trending the smear over time can reveal blasts a first look missed.
- Treat sustained monocytosis as CMML until proven otherwise, confirm the monocytes on the smear (automated counters misclassify monocytes, blasts, and activated lymphocytes), and prioritize establishing chronicity — persistent monocytosis over months reframes an acute febrile illness as a chronic marrow disorder with an acute overlay.
- Read the peripheral smear as the marrow's conversation with the blood: reticulocytosis and large platelets signal a peripheral pull (destruction/consumption with marrow compensation), while monocytosis and immature myeloid forms signal a marrow push (infiltration); the two together imply simultaneous marrow and peripheral disease, and a primary hematologic malignancy can drive AIHA and ITP (an Evans-like picture).
- When a treated diagnosis fails to respond, split the failure three ways — wrong antibiotic, right diagnosis needing more time, or wrong diagnosis — and remember that acalculous cholecystitis is typically triggered by another critical illness, so an untriggered, non-responding case should prompt a search for the real driver (and a call to the proceduralist for their direct impression).
- Approach cutaneous nodules as deep-space lesions: primary deep dermatoses (panniculitis, neutrophilic dermatosis/Sweet) versus infiltration from afar (granulomatous disease including TB, or metastatic tumor including leukemia cutis); a full-body skin exam is high-yield and biopsy of an accessible nodule can be the fastest route to a diagnosis buried under expensive imaging.
- Keep tuberculosis on the differential for a patient from an endemic region with multi-organ involvement and monocytosis (a classic association), and use tissue — skin biopsy and flow cytometry — to break a TB-versus-leukemia tie that clinical data alone cannot.
