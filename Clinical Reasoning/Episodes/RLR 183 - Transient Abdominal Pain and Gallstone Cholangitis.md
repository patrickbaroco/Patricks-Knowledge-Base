## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A paired-case teaching episode. Rabih presents **two 54-year-old men**, each with the same near-identical chief concern — "I had severe abdominal pain yesterday, but it's gone now" — the same benign risk profile (HTN, HLD, mild obesity), and the same completely **normal vitals, exam, and initial workup**. In **Case 1** the pain was **abrupt — "hit by lightning"** — and, after a reassuring EKG and labs and a discharge with **[[Return Precautions|return precautions]]**, he came back 24 hours later with **RUQ tenderness**, a new **WBC of 17**, **AST/ALT in the 300s** with **normal ALP and bilirubin**, and **lactate 2.5**. Reza reads the **[[Transient Abdominal Pain|transient, hyperacute]]** onset as either a **vascular event** or an **[[Intermittent Luminal Obstruction|intermittent obstruction of a hollow lumen]]**, then — with the transaminase-predominant pattern and RUQ pain — runs to the **[[Hepatocellular vs Cholestatic Pattern|hepatobiliary system]]** and the can't-miss **[[Acute Cholangitis|cholangitis]]**, reasoning that **[[ALT Rises First|ALT/AST rise first]]** in biliary obstruction and that **[[Cholecystitis|cholecystitis]] shouldn't elevate transaminases** — so this is **[[Choledocholithiasis|choledocholithiasis]]**. CT shows a dilated, enhancing common bile duct and intrahepatic biliary dilation with **no visible stone** (radiolucent **[[Cholesterol Stones|cholesterol stones]]**); he goes for **[[ERCP|ERCP]]** with stone/sludge removal and a stent, treated for cholangitis (blood cultures negative). **Case 2** is deliberately identical *except* the pain came on **gradually over three hours** — and this patient simply gets better ("stop calling me"). The episode's payload is two lessons: the clinical one — **abrupt "lightning-strike" onset is a discriminator that tends to recur**, and "sudden onset is often sudden *recognition* of something slowly brewing" — and the meta one — the **[[Outcome Bias|outcome does not reflect the quality of the reasoning]]**, so a bad result should trigger neither self-blame nor reflexive practice change, while still leaving room to reflect for genuine lessons.

## Pearls

- **Sudden onset, short duration, and complete resolution place a pain in the hyperacute category — think vascular event or intermittent obstruction of a hollow lumen.** More than the *location*, the **[[Illness Tempo|temporal signature]]** drives the worry: a **[[Intermittent Luminal Obstruction|lumen that obstructs and then relieves]]** (a volvulus that detorses, a strangulation that reduces, a **stone that passes**, or bowel **[[Angioedema|angioedema]]**) explains transient severe pain, as can a transient vascular event — a framing that keeps a "resolved" complaint from being falsely reassuring.
- **Abrupt, "hit-by-lightning" onset is a high-yield discriminator — and it tends to recur.** The two cases were identical on presentation except that Case 1's pain was **thunderclap** and Case 2's built up **gradually over hours**; in medicine, unlike life, being **[[Thunderclap Onset|struck by lightning once]]** predicts it coming back, and — as Reza notes — a sudden onset is often just the **sudden *recognition*** of something slowly brewing.
- **A stone that passes early can leave no biochemical fingerprint, because the labs lag — and when they turn, ALT/AST rise first.** Enzymes and counts can be normal in the first hours (Case 1's initial labs were clean), so normal early labs don't exclude a biliary event; **[[ALT Rises First|transaminases rise before bilirubin]]** in obstruction, so RUQ pain with an **AST/ALT bump, leukocytosis, and a rising lactate** should raise **[[Acute Cholangitis|cholangitis]]** — the can't-miss — while remembering **[[Cholecystitis|cholecystitis]] alone should not elevate transaminases**, making **[[Choledocholithiasis|choledocholithiasis]]** the better fit.
- **Radiolucent cholesterol stones are often invisible on CT — diagnose the duct, not the stone.** The signature is the **ductal picture** (a dilated, enhancing **[[Common Bile Duct|common bile duct]]** tapering toward the pancreas, intrahepatic biliary dilation, a distended gallbladder), not a visualized calculus, because **[[Cholesterol Stones|cholesterol stones]]** are non-radiopaque; the answer is **[[ERCP|ERCP]]** with stone extraction and stenting plus antibiotics.
- **The outcome does not reflect the quality of the clinical reasoning — resist letting a single result drive self-blame or a reflexive change in practice.** A bad outcome after sound reasoning (giving **ceftriaxone** and seeing SJS/TEN; discharging a truly reassuring patient who returns with cholangitis) is **[[Outcome Bias|not a reasoning error]]**; guard equally against **[[Hindsight Bias|hindsight bias]]** and against the arrogance of never reflecting — feel *for* the patient, but keep judgment objective, and reflect only for lessons that genuinely generalize.
- **Honor the worried partner, and beware availability bias between back-to-back cases.** A loved one's alarm ("I've never seen him wince like this") is meaningful collateral distinct from baseline anxiety and can justify going a step further (as calling to close the loop did); at the same time, a dramatic Case 1 should not distort your workup of an identical-looking Case 2 — **[[Availability Heuristic|treat the patient in front of you]]**.

## Problem Representation

Two 54-year-old men with hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and mild obesity each present with a chief concern of severe abdominal pain the prior day that had fully resolved, with normal triage vitals, a normal examination, and — in both — a normal initial evaluation (EKG, troponin, CBC, liver enzymes, lipase, lactate, urinalysis). In the first patient the pain had been abrupt and excruciating ("hit by lightning") and lasting only hours; he was discharged with return precautions and returned 24 hours later with right-upper-quadrant tenderness, a new leukocytosis (WBC 17), an AST/ALT elevation into the 300s with normal alkaline phosphatase and bilirubin, and a lactate of 2.5 — a transaminase-predominant, right-upper-quadrant syndrome consistent with choledocholithiasis and acute cholangitis, confirmed by CT (dilated, enhancing common bile duct and intrahepatic biliary dilation without a visible radiolucent cholesterol stone) and treated with antibiotics and ERCP with stone/sludge removal and stenting. The second patient, whose otherwise identical presentation differed only in that the pain had come on gradually over three hours, remained entirely well without recurrence — the paired contrast isolating abrupt onset as the discriminating feature, and framing the episode's central lesson that outcome does not equal reasoning quality.

## Case Narrative

*This is a paired-case teaching episode: two deliberately similar presentations built to isolate a single discriminating feature and to teach how to hold oneself accountable (or not) to outcomes. The clinical diagnosis lives in Case 1; Case 2 is the benign mirror.*

**Case 1.** A **54-year-old man** — otherwise healthy but for hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and mild obesity (BMI 32) — presents because, the **day before**, he had **severe, excruciating abdominal pain** that **completely resolved within about three hours**; he thought nothing of it until his wife insisted he be seen. By the time he arrives (roughly 16 hours pain-free), his **vitals and exam are normal**. Reza's attention fixes on the **[[Illness Tempo|temporal signature]]** rather than the location: **abrupt onset with short duration and complete resolution** puts him in a **hyperacute** category — a **vascular event**, or an **[[Intermittent Luminal Obstruction|intermittent obstruction of a hollow lumen]]** (a strangulation that reduces, a volvulus that detorses). Candidly, he says that if this were a well-appearing family member now pain-free, he might not send them in — and, facing a normal H&P, he would **do less**: perhaps an **EKG** for safety, no imaging or labs, and clear **[[Return Precautions|return precautions]]** to come back *if the pain recurs* so it can be captured.

The patient, distressed on his wife's behalf, pushes for more, so a full workup is done — and everything is normal (**EKG, troponin, CBC, LFTs, lipase, lactate**, even a clean UA). He is reassured and discharged. **Twenty-four hours later he returns** with **severe acute abdominal pain lasting 30–40 minutes** and is now **tender in the right upper quadrant** with normal vitals. Reza reframes: the pattern is **intermittent, hyperacute obstruction of a lumen**, and though a vascular cause is possible, he favors "**something hollow getting obstructed and then relieving itself**." The repeat labs — normal only 48 hours earlier — now show a **WBC of 17**, **AST/ALT in the 300s**, a **normal ALP and bilirubin**, a normal lipase, and a **lactate of 2.5**. Reza runs to the **[[Hepatocellular vs Cholestatic Pattern|hepatobiliary system]]**: the transaminase-predominant bump with RUQ pain localizes to the liver/biliary tree, and the leukocytosis plus lactate raise a **brewing infection** — the can't-miss being **[[Acute Cholangitis|cholangitis]]**. He reasons the mechanism aloud: **[[Cholecystitis|cholecystitis]] should not elevate AST/ALT**, so a transaminase rise means **[[Choledocholithiasis|a stone in the duct]]**, and **[[ALT Rises First|ALT/AST are the first enzymes to move]]** in biliary obstruction, before the bilirubin — so yesterday's pain may have been a **stone that passed and left no fingerprint** (too early, or non-obstructing), and today a second stone is obstructing and infecting. He would empirically cover (ceftriaxone/metronidazole or piperacillin-tazobactam) and get to imaging.

The **CT** shows a **distended gallbladder**, **intrahepatic biliary dilation**, and a **dilated, enhancing common bile duct** tapering toward the pancreas — but **no visible stone**, because **[[Cholesterol Stones|cholesterol stones are radiolucent]]** on CT. He receives **piperacillin-tazobactam** and goes emergently to **[[ERCP|ERCP]]**, where **stones and sludge are removed and a stent placed**; he is discharged 48 hours later with a diagnosis of **cholangitis** (blood cultures negative) and, ten days on, is doing well.

**Case 2.** Rabih then presents a **second 54-year-old man**, "tweaking a few details," with the **same risk profile** and the **same story** — bad abdominal pain yesterday, now resolved, here today "to look after myself" — and **normal vitals and exam**. Primed by Case 1 (and naming the **[[Availability Heuristic|availability bias]]**), Reza resolves to **treat the patient in front of him** and do exactly what he did before: a workup and EKG, all normal, and discharge. This time, at 48 hours and again at 5 days, the patient is **completely well** — "stop calling me." Same story, same normal results: one path led to cholangitis that, untreated, could have killed; the other to a man annoyed by follow-up calls.

**The paired lesson.** Reza offers the principle that "took ten years to learn": **the outcome does not reflect the quality of the reasoning, and an outcome should not, by itself, change behavior.** His analogy — giving **ceftriaxone** and seeing **SJS/TEN** does not make the antibiotic choice wrong, nor should it be avoided forever — captures the **[[Outcome Bias|outcome/resulting bias]]**: feel *for* the patient, but do not collapse into **self-blame**, and guard equally against the arrogance of never reflecting. Case 1's cholangitis "shouldn't change anything," yet Rabih's instinct to **call and close the loop** was still right, "because we never know." Rabih, reflecting from "13 years in a high-velocity ED," adds the second victim's survival lesson — if you feel bad every time an unpredictable event happens, you cannot function; acceptance is what makes **learning** possible. Then he reveals the one feature he had **deliberately under-emphasized**: Case 1's pain was **abrupt ("hit by lightning")**, Case 2's was a **gradual, vague ache over three hours** — everything else identical. His takeaway: **abrupt onset is worrisome and tends to recur**, and Reza layers on the corollary that **"sudden onset is often just sudden *recognition*"** of something slowly brewing (a stone arriving before the labs can catch up). Even so, Reza holds the line: an abrupt onset with a normal workup still doesn't justify more testing — perhaps a few hours' observation or tighter return precautions — because the discipline is to be **less emotional, less self-blaming, more objective, and more kind**.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Illness Tempo|Temporal signature over location]].** The organizing move is that *how the pain began and ended* (abrupt, brief, fully resolved) matters more than *where* it is — placing it in a hyperacute bucket of vascular events and **[[Intermittent Luminal Obstruction|intermittent luminal obstruction]]** (volvulus/detorsion, strangulation/reduction, a passing stone, bowel angioedema).
- **Abrupt onset as a recurring discriminator.** "**[[Thunderclap Onset|Hit by lightning]]**" onset is the single feature separating the dangerous case from the benign one, and it **tends to recur** — with the corollary that sudden onset is often sudden *recognition* of a slowly brewing process (the stone that arrives before the labs).
- **[[Test Interpretation|Test interpretation]] and lab lag.** Normal early labs do not exclude a biliary event (a passed stone leaves no fingerprint); when enzymes move, **[[ALT Rises First|ALT/AST rise before bilirubin]]**, and a transaminase-predominant RUQ syndrome with leukocytosis and lactate points to **[[Choledocholithiasis|choledocholithiasis]] / [[Acute Cholangitis|cholangitis]]**, not isolated **[[Cholecystitis|cholecystitis]]**. Radiolucent **[[Cholesterol Stones|cholesterol stones]]** mean you diagnose the **duct** on CT, not the stone.
- **[[Availability Heuristic|Availability bias]] between paired cases.** Case 1 primes fear for Case 2; the discipline is to **treat the patient in front of you** and let an identical, reassuring workup lead to the same reasonable disposition.
- **[[Outcome Bias|Outcome/resulting bias]] — the central lesson.** Two identical decisions produced opposite outcomes; the **quality of the reasoning was the same**. A bad outcome after sound reasoning (ceftriaxone→SJS/TEN) is not a reasoning error, and should trigger neither **self-blame** nor reflexive practice change — while **[[Hindsight Bias|hindsight]]** must not make the "obvious" answer look like it was knowable up front.
- **[[Base Rate|Base rate]] and honoring collateral.** Most transient, resolved abdominal pain with a normal workup is benign; a **worried partner** ("I've never seen him wince like this") is meaningful signal that can justify going a step further, distinct from baseline anxiety.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Emotional regulation and humility]].** The meta-skill — being less self-blaming, more objective, and kind, reflecting only for lessons that generalize — is framed as essential to lasting well in medicine (the "second victim" survival lesson), especially at the academic-year turnover.

## Teaching Discussion

- Weigh the temporal signature of pain above its location: abrupt onset, short duration, and complete resolution define a hyperacute category best explained by a vascular event or an intermittent obstruction of a hollow lumen, so a "resolved" complaint should not be reflexively reassuring.
- Treat abrupt, lightning-strike onset as a discriminating, worrisome feature that tends to recur, and remember that sudden onset is frequently the sudden recognition of a slowly brewing process rather than a truly instantaneous one.
- Do not let normal early labs exclude a biliary event, because a stone that passes may leave no biochemical trace and enzymes lag the insult; when they rise, AST/ALT move before bilirubin, so right-upper-quadrant pain with transaminitis, leukocytosis, and lactate should raise cholangitis, keeping in mind that cholecystitis alone should not elevate transaminases.
- Diagnose the duct rather than the stone on CT: cholesterol stones are radiolucent and often invisible, so a dilated, enhancing common bile duct with intrahepatic biliary dilation is the operative finding, and the treatment is antibiotics plus ERCP with stone extraction and stenting.
- Separate the quality of a decision from its outcome: sound reasoning can yield a bad result (a discharged patient who returns with cholangitis; ceftriaxone followed by SJS/TEN) without being an error, so resist both self-blame and reflexive practice change, and guard against hindsight bias that makes the answer look knowable in advance.
- Reflect on cases for lessons that genuinely generalize while avoiding two failure modes — collapsing into self-blame and arrogantly never reflecting — and cultivate emotional regulation as a core clinical skill, particularly for trainees and new attendings at the academic-year transition.
- Give real weight to a worried partner or family member, whose alarm differs from baseline anxiety and can justify closing the loop (a follow-up call, tighter return precautions, brief observation), while resisting availability bias so a dramatic prior case does not distort the workup of the patient in front of you.
