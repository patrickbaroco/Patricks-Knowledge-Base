## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 26-year-old woman, otherwise athletic, presents with a week of episodic [[Dizziness|lightheadedness]], bilateral blurry vision without diplopia, a strange "pocket of moving fluid" in her abdomen, and positional imbalance. Exam pivots the case: her abdomen is normal, but she has a **left-sided [[Cerebellar Ataxia|cerebellar syndrome]]** — dysmetria and dysdiadochokinesia on the left, hypermetric saccades, extinguishing nystagmus, wide-based ataxic gait — with preserved reflexes and a negative Romberg. Yet the MRI is clean and the LP is normal, and history reveals this is her *third* stereotyped, stress-triggered episode (one after a prior surgery), each lasting days to weeks and resolving. An exhaustive exclusionary workup follows — negative infectious, autoimmune-encephalitis, and paraneoplastic panels; equivocal copper studies that ultimately argue *against* [[Wilson Disease|Wilson disease]]; normal ammonia; negative heavy metals; unrevealing genetics; negative malignancy imaging including PET — while steroids and IVIG give at most marginal benefit. The hosts move deliberately through a [[Dizziness|dizziness]] schema, [[Ataxia|ataxia]] localization, a worst-case [[Worst-Case Reasoning|vascular/compressive/inflammatory]] frame for a central lesion, and a [[Myoclonus|myoclonus]] schema, resisting the urge to call it psychiatric — and only after multiple neurologists complete the workup is the final, humbling label reached: **[[Functional Neurological Disorder|functional neurological disorder]] (conversion disorder)**, a [[Destination Diagnosis|destination diagnosis]] of exclusion.

## Pearls

- **Trust the triage.** Of blurry vision, imbalance, and an abdominal sensation, the patient led with [[Dizziness|dizziness]] — and in a young person without intoxication or chronic neurologic disease, the symptom the body chooses to foreground deserves to be studied first. [[Trust the Triage|Trusting the patient's own triage]] focuses the workup rather than diffusing it.
- **Dizziness resolves into three organ systems.** [[Schema-Based Reasoning|Push away the angst]] and ask which systems are on the hook: **cardiovascular** (orthostasis), **neurologic** (vertigo, plus transient intracranial hypo- or hypertension), and the **blood** that connects them (hypoglycemia, anemia, or a diffuse toxic-metabolic process — the last usually announced by other lab derangements like [[Hyponatremia|hyponatremia]] or [[Hypercalcemia|hypercalcemia]]).
- **Localize ataxia before you differentiate it.** [[Ataxia]] splits into **cerebellar** vs **sensory**: preserved reflexes and a *negative* [[Romberg Sign|Romberg]], with nystagmus and hypermetric saccades, place this lesion in the cerebellum, not the dorsal columns or peripheral nerve — and because cerebellar signs are **ipsilateral**, left-sided dysmetria localizes to the *left* cerebellar hemisphere. That single localization makes MRI the mandatory next test.
- **For any central neurologic event, anchor in the worst case and sort by mechanism.** [[Worst-Case Reasoning|Assume the worst]] — is this **vascular** (stroke, dissection), **compressive** (mass), or **inflammatory** (infectious, post-infectious, autoimmune)? The MRI adjudicates all three at once. And when a new neurologic syndrome appears in someone carrying a *clinical* label like migraine, put a **question mark** on that label rather than [[Anchoring Bias|anchoring]] to it.
- **Copper studies are read together, not one at a time.** In true [[Wilson Disease|Wilson disease]] the [[Test Interpretation|pattern]] is **low** ceruloplasmin (the carrier protein is downregulated when copper is in excess) with a **markedly high** 24-hour urine copper. This patient's urine copper was only *mildly* elevated and her ceruloplasmin was *high* — the opposite direction — which, with absent Kayser-Fleischer rings, argues against Wilson rather than for it.
- **Some diagnoses can only be made at the end of the road.** A [[Destination Diagnosis|destination diagnosis]] like [[Functional Neurological Disorder|functional neurological disorder]] is reached only after everything else is excluded; the [[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive-autopsy]] discipline is to resist labeling a suffering patient "in their head" *before* a thorough workup — and, once expert neurologists have done that work, to accept the diagnosis while respecting the stigma it still carries.

## Problem Representation

A 26-year-old athletic woman with a nominal history of migraine without aura presents with one week of episodic lightheadedness, bilateral blurry vision without diplopia, positional imbalance requiring her to hold onto objects, and an odd sensation of moving abdominal fluid. Examination reveals a lateralized left cerebellar syndrome — left-sided dysmetria and dysdiadochokinesia, hypermetric saccades, extinguishing horizontal nystagmus, and a wide-based ataxic gait — with intact strength and sensation, preserved reflexes, and a negative Romberg, localizing to the left cerebellar hemisphere. Neuroimaging (CT and contrast MRI) and lumbar puncture are unrevealing, transaminases are mildly elevated (ALT 122, AST 292) with normal alkaline phosphatase, and the history discloses two prior stereotyped episodes — one following cholecystectomy — that were stress-triggered, lasted days to weeks, and remitted. After a negative infectious, autoimmune, paraneoplastic, metabolic (Wilson, urea-cycle, heavy-metal), and genetic evaluation, and only marginal response to steroids and IVIG, the recurrent, stress-triggered, exclusionary syndrome is ultimately diagnosed as functional neurological disorder (conversion disorder).

## Case Narrative

**Aliquot 1.** A 26-year-old woman with migraines without aura presents to the ED with a week of episodic [[Dizziness|lightheadedness]], a few days of an abnormal abdominal sensation like a moving "pocket of fluid," and, the day before arrival, imbalance with bilateral blurry vision — worse with positional change, forcing her to hold onto objects — but *no* diplopia. Rabih begins with a principle: of all these complaints, the patient chose to lead with the dizziness, and absent intoxication or chronic neurologic disease, we should [[Trust the Triage|trust the body's triage]] and study it first. He lays out the [[Schema-Based Reasoning|dizziness schema]] — three organ systems: **cardiovascular** (orthostasis), **neurologic** (vertigo, and transient intracranial pressure shifts), and the **blood** linking them (hypoglycemia, anemia, or toxic-metabolic derangement, usually with other lab clues). Reza flags the patient's youth as its own red flag — a young person has such reserve that an ED visit implies something is genuinely wrong — and reasons through the blurry vision: bilateral, not improved by covering one eye, so not an extraocular-muscle or single-retina problem; most blurry vision is refractive, but bilateral non-refractive blurring raises retrochiasmatic processes, lens edema from hyperglycemia, or hyperviscosity. He ties the visual symptom to positional dizziness and floats episodic [[Orthostatic Hypotension|orthostatic hypotension]] and the abdominal "succussion splash" of possible [[Ascites|ascites]] as threads to run down.

**Aliquot 2.** The abdomen is normal — no ascites. But the neurologic exam is the pivot: pupils reactive, fields intact, **extinguishing horizontal nystagmus** on lateral gaze, **hypermetric but intact saccades**, normal strength/tone/sensation, **preserved reflexes**, a **negative Romberg**, and **left-sided [[Ataxia|dysmetria and dysdiadochokinesia]]** (finger-nose, heel-shin) with a wide-based ataxic gait needing support. Reza works the [[Anatomical Approach|localization]]: ataxia is **cerebellar** vs **sensory**; the preserved reflexes and negative Romberg push away sensory, while nystagmus and hypermetric saccades pull toward central [[Cerebellar Ataxia|cerebellar]] disease. Cerebellar signs are **ipsilateral**, so left-sided findings localize to the *left cerebellar hemisphere*. That localization makes MRI the mandatory next step, and he begins to run the DDx by [[Illness Tempo|tempo]] — hyperacute, acute, subacute, chronic. Rabih reconciles the one oddity — a fixed cerebellar lesion should give *continuous*, not episodic, symptoms — by invoking the rare entity of **central paroxysmal positional vertigo**, keeping a central process viable, and frames the two big moves: assume the [[Worst-Case Reasoning|worst case]] (a **vascular** event — stroke, cervical artery [[Cervical Artery Dissection|dissection]] in a young patient), then the other central mechanisms (**compressive** mass, **inflammatory** infectious/autoimmune), all adjudicated by MRI. Critically, he questions the migraine label: a *clinical* diagnosis should carry a **question mark** once a new neurologic syndrome appears, lest it mask a vasculopathy or a slow benign mass.

**Aliquot 3.** CBC normal; CMP notable for **ALT 122, AST 292** with normal alkaline phosphatase. CT head normal. **Contrast MRI: no acute abnormality** (only a punctate T2 focus read as a dilated perivascular space). HIV, syphilis, hepatitis serologies, B12, vitamin E, and Lyme all negative; TSH mildly high at 5.95 with normal free T4 and normal anti-TPO; ANA, SSA/SSB, and celiac serologies negative. History deepens: **two prior similar episodes**. Years earlier, ataxia episodes lasting 30 minutes to 2 hours were attributed to a "positive" Lyme test and treated with 28 days of IV ceftriaxone with apparent improvement. A second episode followed a **cholecystectomy** — she woke from anesthesia with myoclonic movements and profound incoordination, could not walk for days, used a walker for weeks, improved on topiramate plus a short clonazepam course, and returned to baseline by ~6 weeks. Reza and Rabih converge on the frame: an **episodic, triggered, recurrent, MRI-negative left cerebellar syndrome** with mild transaminitis. Rabih pushes toward the meninges/parenchyma (LP) and, if that is negative, toward the metabolic profile as the only positive signal. Reza is genuinely surprised by the negative MRI — MS would show demyelination, paraneoplastic disease would not remit and recur so cleanly — and volunteers the neuro-hepatic overlap of **[[Wilson Disease|Wilson disease]]** (plus a Coombs-negative hemolytic anemia), while chasing the transaminitis with a [[Test Interpretation|source workup]] (CK for muscle, retic/smear for hemolysis, RUQ ultrasound). He also floats the humbling possibility that this is a **common disease presenting atypically** — a bizarre migraine variant that responds to migraine therapy — the kind of hypothesis to test against a Google search and a neurologist, never to settle on prematurely.

**Aliquot 4.** CK normal, reticulocyte count normal, RUQ ultrasound normal; family history negative for migraine, positive for an autoimmune condition (Sjögren or lupus) in a relative. CMV IgM negative, **EBV IgM positive**, VZV IgM negative. **Serum copper mildly high (181; ULN 140), ceruloplasmin high (41; ULN 40), 24-hour urine copper mildly high (40; ULN 30). LP completely normal**; meningitis/encephalitis panel and autoimmune-encephalitis panel (including NMDAR) negative. Reza reads the EBV IgM as a red herring — nothing about the case fits mononucleosis, and IgM is a weaker test than VCA serologies — and reads the acellular CSF as evidence against meningeal inflammation (though not against parenchymal encephalitis, which often shows elevated protein). Rabih delivers the [[Test Interpretation|copper interpretation]]: true Wilson disease shows a **markedly** elevated urine copper with a **low** ceruloplasmin (the carrier protein is downregulated in copper excess); here the urine copper is only mildly high and the ceruloplasmin is *not* suppressed — the pattern points *away* from Wilson, best resolved by liver copper quantification or genetics. He then reframes the whole case toward **[[Illness Script Flexibility|flexible]] metabolic/toxic reasoning**: an intermittent, profound, stereotyped syndrome with negative structural imaging invites intermittent toxin exposure or an inborn error that flares with **protein catabolism** — surgery, fasting, or steroids. His two known examples are a **[[Urea Cycle Disorder|urea-cycle disorder]]** (check serum ammonia) and **[[Maple Syrup Urine Disease|maple syrup urine disease / branched-chain amino acid disorder]]**, and he raises the strategic point that a 26-year-old with a possible late-onset pediatric metabolic disease may need a **pediatric neurologist**.

**Aliquot 5.** Ammonia normal; genetic testing inconclusive. She is discharged to acute rehab for three weeks. Outpatient course: a repeat autoimmune-encephalopathy panel is negative, ophthalmology finds **no Kayser-Fleischer rings**, and the team entertains **[[Opsoclonus-Myoclonus Syndrome|opsoclonus-myoclonus syndrome]]** — prompting CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, mammogram, and **PET-CT, all negative for malignancy**. Empiric **pulse steroids** yield partial improvement, a prolonged prednisone taper follows, and later **IVIG** gives no benefit. Reza reads the marginal steroid response as *time*, not treatment — a complete response would favor autoimmune disease, worsening would favor occult infection, but "a little better" argues the steroids did little. Rabih, struck that the episodes are lasting *longer* each time, pushes a thorough toxin search: **heavy metals**, and — cued by the abdominal symptom — **[[Bismuth Toxicity|bismuth toxicity]]** from possible Pepto-Bismol use. Reza increasingly suspects a **hereditary/genetic** basis, noting that lingering undiagnosed cases most often resolve to a genetic-metabolic cause, and would consider referral to the NIH Undiagnosed Diseases Program or a comparable center rather than piling on tests that could cause harm.

**Aliquot 6 and resolution.** Heavy metals (arsenic, mercury, cadmium) negative; opsoclonus improves with gabapentin; genetic testing and family pedigrees remain inconclusive. She returns to work and driving, functional but unable to hike or ski, with a persistent left-sided ataxic, jerky syndrome. With jerking now confirmed as **[[Myoclonus|myoclonus]]**, Reza pulls up the myoclonus schema he built with Aaron Berkowitz: secondary causes by **[[MIST Mnemonic|MIST]]** — **M**etabolic, **I**nfectious, **S**tructural, **T**oxin/meds — versus **primary neurologic** disorder. Here there is no toxin, the MRI excludes structural disease, infection would not wax and wane, and metabolic screens are clean — leaving, at ~75% confidence, a primary neurologic disorder. Within that: **[[Hashimoto Encephalopathy|Hashimoto encephalopathy]]** (unlikely — anti-TPO low, CSF normal), **anti-Ri opsoclonus-myoclonus** (paraneoplastic, breast/lung — imaging negative so far), **[[Myoclonus-Dystonia|myoclonus-dystonia]]** (DYT11), and **[[Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease|CJD]]** (excluded by age and tempo). After consultation across multiple neurologists, the final diagnosis is **[[Functional Neurological Disorder|functional neurological disorder]] (conversion disorder)** — a movement-disorder subtype, diagnosed by exclusion after negative genetic, metabolic, toxic, and imaging workup, and supported by symptoms that increase with attention and decrease with distraction, stress-triggered episodes (viral illness, surgery), and a history of anxiety. As a healthcare worker, the patient finds the label distressing given its stigma; she improves with physical and behavioral therapy. Reza's [[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive autopsy]] is his pride in *not* reaching for "functional" prematurely — insisting on a thorough workup before attributing symptoms to the mind — while Rabih names the deeper lesson: FND is a **[[Destination Diagnosis|destination diagnosis]]**, a working label reached only at the end of the journey, carrying more than its share of medical stigma.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring]] on the migraine label — and refusing it.** The patient arrives pre-labeled with migraine, and the disciplined counter-move is to place a *question mark* on any clinical diagnosis once a new neurologic syndrome emerges; anchoring here would have foreclosed the cerebellar localization and the exclusionary workup that followed.
- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|Schemas]] as the scaffolding at every layer.** Three sequential schemas carry the case: a **dizziness** schema (cardiac / neuro / blood), an **ataxia** localization (cerebellar vs sensory), and a **myoclonus** schema (MIST vs primary neurologic). Each converts an overwhelming symptom into a small, checkable list.
- **[[Anatomical Approach|Localization]] before differentiation.** The exam does the heavy lifting: reflexes, Romberg, nystagmus, and saccades separate cerebellar from sensory ataxia and lateralize the lesion, so imaging is aimed rather than fished.
- **[[Worst-Case Reasoning|Worst-case reasoning]] for a central event.** Before base rate, the hosts sort any central neurologic syndrome into **vascular / compressive / inflammatory** and assume the most dangerous (stroke, dissection) until the MRI clears it — the same morbidity-first instinct seen in RLR 036.
- **[[Illness Script Flexibility|Script flexibility]] when the common scripts fail.** With structural and inflammatory disease excluded, the reasoning bends toward **intermittent toxic-metabolic and hereditary** disease that flares with protein catabolism (urea-cycle disorders, maple syrup urine disease) — and toward Wilson disease, whose script is then *rejected* on the [[Test Interpretation|direction of the copper studies]] rather than their mere abnormality.
- **[[Premature Closure|Resisting premature closure]] on "it's psychiatric."** The central cognitive achievement is *not* labeling a distressed young woman as functional before the workup — [[Diagnostic Humility|humility]] that the diagnosis is real and disabling, and that "functional" earns its place only as a [[Destination Diagnosis|destination diagnosis]] after exclusion, not as an early escape hatch.

## Teaching Discussion

- When a patient volunteers many symptoms, weight the one they lead with: absent intoxication or chronic neurologic disease, the body's own triage is a signal, and studying the leading complaint focuses the workup instead of diffusing it across every organ.
- Run dizziness through a three-system schema — cardiovascular (orthostasis), neurologic (vertigo and transient intracranial pressure change), and blood (hypoglycemia, anemia, toxic-metabolic) — and remember that diffuse toxic-metabolic dizziness usually travels with other lab derangements.
- Separate cerebellar from sensory ataxia at the bedside: preserved reflexes and a negative Romberg with nystagmus and hypermetric saccades indicate cerebellar disease, and because cerebellar signs are ipsilateral, lateralized findings localize the hemisphere and direct the MRI.
- Treat a *clinical* diagnosis like migraine as provisional once a new neurologic syndrome appears — put a question mark on it — and, for any central event, assume the worst case and sort by vascular, compressive, and inflammatory mechanisms, all of which the MRI can address.
- Interpret copper studies as a pattern: Wilson disease is low ceruloplasmin with markedly high urine copper (and often Kayser-Fleischer rings); a mildly high urine copper with a normal-to-high ceruloplasmin points away from Wilson, and liver copper quantification or genetics is the confirmatory step.
- Let stereotyped, stress-triggered, remitting neurologic episodes with normal structural imaging steer toward intermittent toxic exposures and inborn errors that flare with protein catabolism (urea-cycle disorders, branched-chain amino acid disease); a normal ammonia lowers urea-cycle disorders, and a late-onset pediatric metabolic disease may warrant a pediatric neurologist.
- Read empiric-therapy responses as data: a complete steroid response favors autoimmune disease and worsening favors occult infection, but marginal improvement often reflects the natural course rather than the drug.
- Approach myoclonus with the MIST schema (metabolic, infectious, structural, toxin/meds) versus a primary neurologic disorder, and recognize functional neurological disorder as a real, disabling destination diagnosis of exclusion — earned only after a thorough workup, supported by attention-dependent and distraction-responsive signs, and deserving of care that acknowledges its stigma.
