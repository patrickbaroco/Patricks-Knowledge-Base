## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Two cases. In the first, a cachectic 60-year-old man with two weeks of abdominal pain and weight loss is found to be deeply jaundiced with a direct hyperbilirubinemia wildly disproportionate to his transaminases, ultimately traced to a solitary 13.6 cm hepatic mass. Reza builds a careful pretest-probability case for GI-primary metastasis over hepatocellular carcinoma (no cirrhosis, negative hepatitis serologies, normal AFP) — and is proven wrong: the mass is HCC, and a GI fellow's note reframes the entire exercise as a lesson in base rate overriding seemingly disconfirming individual features. In the second, Reza presents a patient with newly diagnosed pancreatic head adenocarcinoma whose labs include a jarring sodium of 120 — which Rabih correctly identifies, from the pattern of the discrepancy alone, as pseudohyponatremia from lipoprotein X interference, then catches a second lab artifact (a falsely elevated potassium from bilirubin interfering with the same plasma-based assay) that a colleague had to point out.

## Pearls

- Malaise and abdominal pain are each individually nonspecific complaints (abdominal pain can arise from within, above, or below the diaphragm; malaise can accompany nearly any illness), but combining them can still narrow a differential — here, abdominal pain was used to focus a nonspecific weight-loss workup toward the GI tract, even though abdominal pain alone would not have justified that focus.
- A useful mental branch point when approaching abdominal pain is whether the case will turn out to be "image positive" or "image negative" — recognizing that this can rarely be predicted with confidence before contrast-enhanced imaging is actually obtained, and that a scan without contrast is not a reliable way to call a case "image negative."
- Jaundice is a comparatively high-yield finding for cognitive investment compared to nonspecific complaints like malaise: the eventual final diagnosis in a jaundiced patient will almost always explain the jaundice itself, making it worth converting immediately into "hyperbilirubinemia — direct or indirect?" rather than treated as just one more symptom in a list.
- The severity/extent of visible jaundice roughly tracks bilirubin height: hemolysis alone rarely pushes bilirubin above 6 mg/dL without a second insult (like a superimposed biliary obstruction), so jaundice extensive enough to involve the trunk and sclerae strongly suggests a much higher, likely double-digit bilirubin and points toward a direct (rather than purely indirect/hemolytic) process.
- Cachexia is a poor discriminator of diagnosis but a strong discriminator of tempo — true muscle and fat wasting takes months to develop, so a patient presenting with a "two-week" history who is already cachectic actually has a subacute-to-chronic underlying process, regardless of how the chief complaint is initially framed.
- Jaundice accompanied by abdominal pain points away from the classically "silent" causes of painless jaundice and toward "loud," obstructive hepatobiliary causes — a distinction worth explicitly naming when building the differential.
- When transaminases are only mildly elevated (roughly 3-4x the upper limit of normal) but bilirubin is markedly elevated (roughly 15-20x the upper limit of normal), that disproportion argues against a primarily hepatocellular/intrahepatic process and toward an extrahepatic biliary process — though imaging is still required to actually localize the lesion.
- CT with contrast is generally preferred over ultrasound for evaluating the biliary system and liver parenchyma (ultrasound remains superior for the gallbladder specifically), and a triple-phase CT (arterial, venous, delayed) can sometimes characterize a liver mass well enough to diagnose hepatocellular carcinoma or pancreatic adenocarcinoma on imaging alone, without biopsy.
- A PET-CT adds no diagnostic value when a good-quality, contrast-enhanced cross-sectional CT has already failed to identify a malignancy — it should not be reflexively ordered as a "next step up" after a negative contrast CT.
- A truly solitary hepatic mass is far more consistent with a primary liver tumor than with metastatic disease once it becomes very large — a small solitary lesion could plausibly be an early, isolated metastasis, but a mass large enough to have grown to 13+ cm would typically have already seeded additional lesions elsewhere if it were metastatic, making a single large lesion a point in favor of a primary tumor.
- Sheer diagnostic base rate can and should outweigh several individually disconfirming features: hepatocellular carcinoma remained a reasonable — and ultimately correct — diagnosis in a patient without cirrhosis, with negative hepatitis serologies, and with a normal AFP, because HCC's overall prevalence as the most common cause of a solitary intrahepatic mass was high enough to survive those individually negative findings, especially once cholangiocarcinoma's own low base rate (only ~10% of cholangiocarcinoma is intrahepatic; the other 90% arises in the extrahepatic biliary tree) was factored in as the main competing hypothesis.
- When a single lab value seems inexplicably out of step with the rest of a coherent clinical picture (here, a sodium of 120 in a patient whose other findings all fit a straightforward pancreatic cancer story), consider whether it represents a true, independently-mediated abnormality (i.e., not simply hypovolemic or SIADH-mediated hyponatremia layered on top of the primary diagnosis) versus a laboratory artifact, rather than immediately forcing it into the existing framework.
- Pseudohyponatremia from an accumulated interfering substance (hemolysis being the most familiar cause; hypertriglyceridemia and, via the molecule lipoprotein X, severe hyperbilirubinemia being less familiar ones) affects indirect-electrode plasma/serum-based chemistry analyzers but does not affect whole-blood, direct-electrode point-of-care or blood gas analyzers — a real discrepancy between a plasma sodium and a point-of-care/VBG sodium is itself strong evidence for an assay artifact, and a corrected serum osmolality that comes back isotonic/normal confirms it.
- The same indirect-electrode plasma assay interference that produces pseudohyponatremia from severe hyperbilirubinemia can simultaneously produce a falsely elevated potassium on the same panel — a discrepancy between an alarming BMP potassium and a normal point-of-care/whole-blood potassium should prompt confirmatory whole-blood testing (and an EKG check) before treating what may be a fictitious hyperkalemia.

## Problem Representation

Case 1: A cachectic man with subacute weight loss, direct hyperbilirubinemia disproportionate to his transaminases, and a solitary large hepatic mass, ultimately diagnosed with hepatocellular carcinoma despite an unfavorable pretest profile. Case 2: A man with newly diagnosed pancreatic head adenocarcinoma whose markedly abnormal sodium and potassium on a standard chemistry panel are both revealed to be laboratory artifacts of severe hyperbilirubinemia interfering with indirect-electrode assay measurement.

## Case Narrative

### Case 1: The Solitary Liver Mass

The case opened with a 60-year-old man presenting with two weeks of abdominal pain, weight loss, and malaise. Malaise was set aside as diagnostically unhelpful, and even abdominal pain and weight loss were flagged individually as nonspecific — abdominal pain can originate from structures within, above, or below the diaphragm, and weight loss can reflect an inflammatory process, poor access to or intake of food, malabsorption, a chronic underlying condition, or a hypermetabolic state like thyrotoxicosis. Even so, combining the two symptoms directed attention toward the GI tract, with the tempo (roughly two weeks) framed as subacute pending further data, and the overall diagnostic uncertainty at this stage explicitly acknowledged rather than papered over.

Before further data was revealed, the discussion previewed how an experienced clinician approaches this kind of presentation: essentially betting on whether the case will turn out to be "image positive" or "image negative," while recognizing this can't be confidently predicted without contrast-enhanced imaging, and building a mental checklist for the exam (auscultation, palpation, inspection for zoster) alongside planning for labs and imaging as the tools most likely to actually clinch the diagnosis.

On exam, the patient had mild tachycardia with otherwise normal vitals, was visibly cachectic, and was deeply jaundiced — icteric sclerae, with jaundice extending down to at least the umbilicus. His abdomen was scaphoid, with a palpably enlarged liver, no ascites, no edema, and a clear cardiopulmonary exam. Jaundice was singled out as an unusually high-yield finding compared to the earlier nonspecific complaints — the eventual diagnosis would almost certainly explain the jaundice — and was immediately converted mentally into "hyperbilirubinemia, direct or indirect." The extent and severity of jaundice observed was used to estimate bilirubin height even before labs returned: hemolysis alone rarely produces bilirubin above roughly 6 mg/dL without a second insult, so jaundice this extensive suggested a much higher, likely double-digit bilirubin and a predominantly direct process. The patient's cachexia was reinterpreted specifically as a tempo clue rather than a diagnostic one — true wasting takes months, meaningfully lengthening the presumed timeline of the underlying disease beyond the two-week chief complaint — and the combination of abdominal pain with jaundice was framed as pointing toward "loud" (symptomatic, obstructive) rather than "silent" (painless) causes of jaundice, prompting specific questions about stool color (pale stools from bilirubin failing to reach the GI lumen) and urine color, while acknowledging that fractionating the bilirubin and obtaining imaging would ultimately do more diagnostic work than the history alone.

Labs confirmed the hypothesis: total bilirubin 26, direct component 23; AST, ALT, and alkaline phosphatase each roughly 3-4 times the upper limit of normal; INR 1.3; WBC 15 with neutrophilic predominance; hemoglobin 12; platelets and renal function normal. The striking disproportion between markedly elevated bilirubin (roughly 15-20x normal) and only mildly elevated transaminases (roughly 3-4x normal) was used to argue against a primarily intrahepatic/hepatocellular process and toward an extrahepatic biliary process, though imaging would still be required to localize the lesion definitively. Acute liver failure was explicitly considered and excluded (no encephalopathy, INR not critically elevated, transaminases not high enough), while cholangitis was kept on the table given the tachycardia, leukocytosis, and abdominal pain — prompting blood cultures and empiric antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam or ceftriaxone plus metronidazole) while awaiting further workup, with an explicit caution that a cachectic patient's inability to generate a robust febrile response could mask how sick they truly were. The hemoglobin of 12 was interpreted as more consistent with anemia of chronic inflammation than hemolysis.

CT of the abdomen and pelvis with contrast revealed a 13.6 x 7.2 cm mass in the left hepatic lobe, with no other abdominal disease and a subsequent CT chest showing no thoracic disease. The previously palpable liver was retrospectively attributed to this mass — palpable hepatomegaly was framed as reflecting one of three general categories: infiltration, inflammation, or congestion. A triple-phase CT protocol (arterial, venous, delayed) was discussed as a way to potentially characterize the mass enough to diagnose hepatocellular carcinoma or pancreatic adenocarcinoma on imaging criteria alone. The differential for the mass itself was built out methodically: neoplastic causes (metastatic disease, most commonly originating from the GI tract given its direct portal venous drainage into the liver — prompting questions about the patient's last colonoscopy and a low ferritin as a clue toward occult GI blood loss — versus a primary hepatic tumor, more likely given the solitary nature of the lesion), with hepatocellular carcinoma considered even in the absence of cirrhosis (particularly in the context of Hepatitis B, prompting hepatitis serologies and questions about country of origin) and cholangiocarcinoma considered given the mass's proximity to the biliary system. Non-neoplastic causes were also enumerated: a large hepatic abscess (capable of obstructing a major bile duct by mass effect alone), a simple cyst (unlikely to explain the weight loss and pain unless truly massive), and specifically an echinococcal (hydatid) cyst, flagged for its relevance to travel history and the risk of anaphylaxis if biopsied without first considering it. A PET-CT was explicitly discounted as a next step, since it adds no value once a good contrast CT has already been obtained.

Hepatitis serologies returned negative, re-review of the imaging showed no evidence of cirrhosis, and an MRI with and without contrast was concerning for malignancy but could not further specify the tumor type, prompting a biopsy recommendation. Antibiotics were discontinued at GI's request, given the radiologist's confidence that this represented malignancy with patent major bile ducts, and the patient's tachycardia and leukocytosis improved with hydration alone — supporting that infection had not been the primary driver of his presentation.

Before the biopsy result was available, an explicit pretest-probability exercise was conducted: given a normal AFP, the absence of cirrhosis, and negative hepatitis serologies, hepatocellular carcinoma was ranked comparatively low, while cholangiocarcinoma and a GI-primary metastasis were both considered, with base rate (GI cancer being a much more common source of liver disease than primary cholangiocarcinoma) favoring a GI primary as the leading hypothesis. The biopsy result reversed this expectation entirely: the mass was hepatocellular carcinoma. A GI fellow's note reframed the reasoning after the fact with two key teaching points: first, that a solitary lesion this large (over 13 cm) is far more consistent with a primary tumor than with metastatic disease, since a metastatic process large enough to reach that size would typically have already produced additional lesions elsewhere — a small solitary lesion is plausible as an early, isolated metastasis, but a large solitary one is not; second, that despite the absence of cirrhosis, a negative AFP, and no classic imaging features of cirrhosis, HCC's sheer prevalence as the most common primary liver malignancy kept it a reasonable diagnosis throughout — and became even more so once weighed against cholangiocarcinoma's own low base rate specifically for the intrahepatic form (only around 10% of all cholangiocarcinoma cases arise in the intrahepatic biliary tree, the vast majority instead arising in the extrahepatic ducts).

### Case 2: The Sodium (and Potassium) That Didn't Belong

Reza then presented a second, shorter case as a single large data "aliquot," deliberately withholding interpretation to let Rabih reason through the full picture at once: a man in his sixties with a recent diagnosis of pancreatic adenocarcinoma at the head of the pancreas, sent to the hospital by his primary care doctor after abnormal outpatient labs — alkaline phosphatase 1000, bilirubin 30 (majority direct), AST and ALT in the hundreds, a sodium of 120, a glucose of 400, and a normal creatinine. Reza specifically noted that the sodium, initially measured on a standard BMP, returned near-normal (134) on a subsequent VBG, and then returned low again (120) on a repeat BMP hours later — deliberately walking Rabih through the same sequence of data as it had actually unfolded clinically.

Rabih immediately accepted the bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, and transaminase pattern as fully explained by the known pancreatic head cancer (via both biliary obstruction and/or hepatic involvement) and the glucose of 400 as consistent with pancreatogenic diabetes, which complicates roughly 30% of pancreatic cancer cases. The sodium of 120, however, was flagged as suspicious specifically because it was picked up incidentally on routine labs rather than through a symptom-driven workup — an "out of the blue" finding in an otherwise coherent clinical story. After mentally correcting for the glucose-driven dilutional effect (estimating a true, glucose-corrected sodium closer to 124-125), the value remained lower than expected and still did not sit comfortably within the rest of the picture.

Rabih organized his reasoning using a three-category framework for hyponatremia: ADH-independent, appropriately-ADH-mediated (true volume depletion), and inappropriately-ADH-mediated (SIADH). His key heuristic was that when a hyponatremia finding feels genuinely surprising given the rest of the clinical context, that surprise itself is a clue that the sodium is likely not truly ADH-mediated at all — prompting consideration of a lab "fake-out" rather than forcing the value into a hypovolemic or SIADH explanation. He generalized this to a broader principle: suspect a fake-out whenever a patient has an accumulated pigmented or interfering substance in the blood, with hemolysis as the most familiar cause, and — specifically relevant here — hypertriglyceridemia and severe hyperbilirubinemia (via a molecule called lipoprotein X, which interferes with certain sodium assay methods) as less familiar but well-described causes. The critical discriminating feature he named was that this type of interference affects indirect-electrode, plasma-based chemistry analyzers but does not affect whole-blood, direct-electrode point-of-care or blood gas analyzers — meaning the actual discrepancy already observed (a low BMP sodium versus a normal VBG sodium) was itself strong evidence for pseudohyponatremia from lipoprotein X, further supported by a subsequently obtained glucose-corrected serum osmolality that returned isotonic, exactly as predicted.

Reza then revealed a second twist: the same panel had also shown a potassium of 6 on the BMP, while the VBG potassium had been normal — a discrepancy he admitted he had initially overlooked entirely, having fixated on the sodium anomaly. A colleague pointed out that bilirubin itself can independently interfere with indirect-electrode potassium measurement on standard plasma-based chemistry panels, while whole-blood, direct-electrode methods are unaffected. After confirming a reassuringly normal EKG, dedicated whole-blood sodium and potassium were sent and both returned entirely normal — establishing that both the sodium and the potassium abnormalities on the standard BMP had been pure laboratory artifacts of the patient's severe hyperbilirubinemia, with no true underlying electrolyte derangement at all.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding (Case 1) | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| Jaundice extending to the trunk, deeply icteric | Suggests a likely double-digit bilirubin and a predominantly direct process |
| Cachexia despite a "two-week" chief complaint | Reframes the true tempo as subacute-to-chronic, not acute |
| Abdominal pain + jaundice together | Points toward "loud"/obstructive causes over "silent"/painless jaundice |
| Bilirubin ~20x ULN, transaminases only ~3-4x ULN | Favors an extrahepatic/obstructive process over a primarily hepatocellular one |
| Solitary 13.6 cm hepatic mass | Favors a primary hepatic tumor over metastatic disease (metastases this large would typically not remain solitary) |
| No cirrhosis, negative hepatitis serologies, normal AFP | Individually lower HCC probability, but insufficient to overcome HCC's high overall base rate |
| Cholangiocarcinoma's low intrahepatic base rate (~10% of all cases) | Further lowers cholangiocarcinoma's relative probability versus HCC in this location |

| Finding (Case 2) | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| Sodium 120 found incidentally, out of step with the rest of the clinical picture | Raises suspicion for a lab artifact rather than true hyponatremia |
| BMP sodium 120 vs VBG sodium 134 (discrepant by assay method) | Strongly suggests an indirect-electrode assay interference (pseudohyponatremia) |
| Glucose-corrected serum osmolality isotonic | Confirms pseudohyponatremia rather than true hypotonic hyponatremia |
| BMP potassium 6 vs VBG potassium normal | Strongly suggests the same assay interference affecting potassium |
| Whole-blood (direct-electrode) sodium and potassium both normal | Confirms both abnormalities were laboratory artifacts of severe hyperbilirubinemia |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Hepatocellular carcinoma illness script (atypical presentation): a solitary, large hepatic mass causing obstructive jaundice, occurring even without cirrhosis or classic risk factors (Hepatitis B being the most notable non-cirrhotic association), with a negative AFP not excluding the diagnosis — a reminder that HCC's role as the single most common primary liver cancer means it retains meaningful pretest probability even when several individually associated features are absent.

Pseudohyponatremia/pseudohyperkalemia from severe hyperbilirubinemia illness script: markedly abnormal sodium and/or potassium on a standard plasma-based chemistry panel in a patient with severe hyperbilirubinemia, discordant with a normal point-of-care or blood-gas value measured by direct electrode, and resolving entirely on dedicated whole-blood testing.

Case 1 is a direct, explicit lesson in base-rate reasoning overriding a seemingly well-supported alternative hypothesis — Rabih's own pretest-probability ranking, built from individually reasonable disconfirming features, turned out to be wrong precisely because it under-weighted HCC's sheer prevalence advantage. Case 2 demonstrates the discipline of trusting an internal sense of "this doesn't fit" as license to question a lab value's validity rather than reflexively incorporating it into the existing diagnostic frame, paired with the humility to acknowledge (as Reza does) that a second, related artifact was nearly missed entirely.

## Teaching Discussion

Case 1's central teaching point is the disproportionate-elevation heuristic for interpreting liver chemistries: a bilirubin far out of proportion to transaminase elevation should shift suspicion toward an extrahepatic/obstructive process, while still requiring imaging to localize the actual lesion — paired with the broader diagnostic habit of converting a symptom like jaundice immediately into its underlying physiologic question (direct versus indirect hyperbilirubinemia) rather than treating it as just one item on a symptom list. The case's most memorable lesson, though, is the explicit base-rate correction delivered by the GI fellow's note: individually disconfirming features (no cirrhosis, negative AFP, negative hepatitis serologies) do not automatically overcome a diagnosis's overall prevalence advantage, and a rigorous pretest-probability exercise should weigh a competing hypothesis's own base rate (cholangiocarcinoma's low intrahepatic frequency) rather than treating it as the default beneficiary of HCC's disconfirming features.

Case 2's central teaching point is a discriminating diagnostic habit: when a single lab value surprises you in an otherwise coherent clinical picture, actively seek a second, differently-obtained measurement of the same analyte (a point-of-care or blood-gas value) before accepting or acting on the original result, since electrode methodology differences can reveal an assay artifact that would otherwise be invisible. The case also models catching one's own blind spot in real time — Reza's candid admission that he had been so focused on the sodium that he initially overlooked an equally artifactual, and potentially more immediately dangerous, potassium abnormality.
