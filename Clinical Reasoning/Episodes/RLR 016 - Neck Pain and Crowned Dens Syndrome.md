## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A middle-aged man presents with one week of non-traumatic, escalating, severe [[Neck Pain|neck pain]]. He is afebrile with normal vitals, holds his neck rigid, has diffuse muscular tenderness without bony point tenderness, and — critically — a completely normal [[Neurologic Exam|neurologic exam]] (no Horner's, normal pupils, intact distal strength, normal gait and mental status); the only other finding is mild osteoarthritic change in the knees. Reza works it with a musculoskeletal [[Anatomical Approach|anatomical approach]] — an imagined arrow through the neck passing skin/nerve → subcutaneous tissue → fascia → muscle → bone → vessel — using the normal exam to make [[Myelopathy|myelopathy]] and [[Radiculopathy|radiculopathy]] unlikely and steering toward an articular/vertebral rather than muscular process. Labs show a normal CBC with a high ESR and CRP; renal and hepatic panels are normal. An [[MRI|MRI]] is read as a pathologic fracture of the [[Odontoid Process|odontoid process]], prompting a C-collar and emergent neurosurgery consultation — but the neurosurgeon, reading the film without ever meeting the patient, says it is *not* a fracture. A [[CT|CT]] confirms no fracture, only aggressive inflammation around the dens; knee films show bilateral [[Chondrocalcinosis|chondrocalcinosis]]. The patient is nearly back to normal within an hour of a single dose of IV [[Ketorolac|ketorolac]]. The unifying diagnosis is **[[Crowned Dens Syndrome|crowned dens syndrome]]** — a rare cervical manifestation of [[CPPD|calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease]] (pseudogout) — with the knee chondrocalcinosis as the extra-articular tell. Reza closes with a [[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive autopsy]], reframing the near-miss "fracture" as a [[Framing Effect|framing]] trap and the swift NSAID response as its own diagnostic test, while Rabih highlights that the pivotal move in real time was a non-radiologist expert overriding the imaging read.

## Pearls

- **Acute, escalating, severe [[Neck Pain|neck pain]] with *no prior history of neck pain* is the phenotype that should perk the antenna.** Most neck pain is chronic and non-specific — a flare of a known problem — so the single most valuable triage question is "have you ever had neck pain before?" A confident *never, and now it is devastating* reframes the complaint from benign to worth a full workup.
- **A normal [[Neurologic Exam|neurologic exam]] localizes by exclusion.** Intact distal strength, normal sensation, normal gait, and absent [[Horner Syndrome|Horner's]] make [[Myelopathy|spinal-cord]] and [[Radiculopathy|nerve-root]] pathology unlikely and push the reasoning toward the [[Anatomical Approach|musculoskeletal column itself]] — muscle, bone, or joint — even though it is ultimately a nerve that carries the pain signal.
- **Tempo and severity override base rate.** Osteoarthritis is common and the patient had OA knees, but degenerative joint disease of the spine does not usually announce itself as acute, escalating, severe pain over a week — so a common disease with the wrong *tempo* should not be forced to explain the presentation.
- **Extra-articular crystal clues unify a confusing joint.** Bilateral knee [[Chondrocalcinosis|chondrocalcinosis]] is the fingerprint of [[CPPD|CPPD]], and finding it turns an isolated, alarming cervical lesion into a systemic crystalline arthropathy — **[[Crowned Dens Syndrome|crowned dens syndrome]]**, pseudogout of the periodontoid tissues.
- **[[CT|CT]], not [[MRI|MRI]], is the arbiter of bone and calcium.** MRI over-called a pathologic fracture; CT showed no fracture, only periodontoid inflammation and calcification. When the question is "is this bone broken, infected, or crystal-laden?", the crystals and cortex are seen best on CT.
- **A brisk response to therapy is diagnostic data.** Near-complete relief within an hour of one dose of IV [[Ketorolac|ketorolac]] is faster than a fracture, infection, or malignancy could resolve, and — with the imaging and knee films — retrospectively confirms an inflammatory crystalline process ([[Treatment as a Diagnostic Test|treatment as a diagnostic test]]).

## Problem Representation

A middle-aged man with only hypertension in his history presents with one week of non-traumatic, escalating, severe neck pain; he is afebrile with normal vitals, holds the neck rigidly with diffuse paraspinal muscular tenderness but no bony point tenderness, can flex his chin toward his chest yet resists rotation, and has an entirely normal neurologic exam and only mild osteoarthritic knee changes otherwise. Inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP) are elevated with an otherwise normal CBC and normal renal and hepatic panels; MRI is misread as an odontoid pathologic fracture, but CT shows no fracture — only aggressive periodontoid inflammation — and knee films reveal bilateral chondrocalcinosis, establishing crowned dens syndrome from CPPD, which resolves almost immediately with a single dose of an NSAID.

## Case Narrative

After an opening riff (Rabih has shaved his head; a debate about the "regular amount of shampoo"), Rabih presents a case he calls a real journey. A middle-aged man reports one week of non-traumatic, escalating, severe [[Neck Pain|neck pain]]. Reza notes approvingly that, for once, he has been given age, sex, duration, and tempo up front, and that the *worsening* course over a week already argues against a simple muscle strain toward something more sinister.

Reza sets his method: for any musculoskeletal complaint he takes an [[Anatomical Approach|anatomical approach]], imagining an arrow driven through the neck that passes in sequence through skin and cutaneous nerves, subcutaneous tissue, fascia, muscle, bone, and finally vessels. Each layer is a candidate site of pathology. He offers two vignettes from his own practice — his own days-long neck stiffness after unaccustomed exercise (a benign strain) and a recent patient with cervical [[Radiculopathy|radiculopathy]] who was doing jumping jacks a day after 40 mg of prednisone — before setting them aside as not matching this escalating, non-traumatic course. His next step is vitals and a neck exam.

Rabih supplies the exam. The patient looks uncomfortable but is afebrile with normal vitals. He holds the neck rigid, can flex his chin toward his chest but hates turning side to side, and has considerable tenderness to palpation of the overlying neck muscles bilaterally with *no* prominent bony tenderness. A deliberately careful [[Neurologic Exam|neurologic exam]] is completely normal: no [[Horner Syndrome|Horner's]], normal pupils, intact distal strength, normal gait, and excellent mental status. Because there was no trauma yet so much pain, Rabih performs a broader-than-usual exam — abdomen, cardiac, skin all unremarkable — finding only mild osteoarthritic changes in both knees. The only pertinent history is hypertension. He sends the patient to a bed for IV access, gives one dose of IV [[Ketorolac|ketorolac]] for the severe pain, and orders basic labs.

Reza reasons from the normal exam. Assessing motor strength in a patient in this much pain is hard, but it was done and was normal with no sensory deficit — which matters because the question is *where* the pathology sits. He does not think this is [[Myelopathy|myelopathy]] (cord) or [[Radiculopathy|radiculopathy]] (root): there is no radiation, and bilateral radicular disease would be odd, though he cannot fully exclude it since it is ultimately the nerve that transmits the pain. That leaves either muscle or a vertebral/articular process, and the worsening week-long course makes a simple muscle strain unlikely. He then zooms out to the knees: different [[Crystalline Arthropathy|crystalline]] and rheumatologic diseases favor different joints, and the knee is a classic site for both osteoarthritis and [[CPPD|pseudogout]] ([[Chondrocalcinosis|chondrocalcinosis]]). He notes that [[Gout|gout]], pseudogout, and [[Rheumatoid Arthritis|rheumatoid arthritis]] can all have cervical manifestations, whereas an acute, severe, escalating cervical picture from osteoarthritis alone would be unusual. Favoring the joints, he asks for cervical X-rays, a CK, and basic labs (CBC, CMP) to look for a systemic inflammatory signature.

Rabih returns the data: CBC normal, ESR high, CRP high, renal and liver panels normal. Then the twist — an [[MRI|MRI]] read as a *pathologic fracture of the [[Odontoid Process|odontoid process]]*. The patient is emergently placed in a C-collar and neurosurgery is consulted; the neurosurgeon looks at the film and says, "I don't know if that's fractured." Reza pauses on the teaching point that an ED evaluation of a joint problem is fundamentally asking three questions — fracture, infection, or crystals — and that imaging is only useful insofar as it explains the patient in front of you. He would want better bony imaging, and, because bone is best analyzed on [[CT|CT]], he would get a CT of the neck along with knee X-rays to try to link the two sites. He also raises, presciently, that pseudogout can cause the "crown dens" pathology of the cervical spine.

Rabih delivers the resolution. [[CT|CT]] shows *no* pathologic fracture — only acute inflammation surrounding the dens; the MRI had over-called it. Knee films show bilateral [[Chondrocalcinosis|chondrocalcinosis]] (obtained after the CT). Meanwhile, with the ketorolac in his system for about an hour, the patient was almost back to normal. The unifying diagnosis is **[[Crowned Dens Syndrome|crowned dens syndrome]]** — a rare manifestation of [[CPPD|pseudogout]]. He was admitted because the stakes were too high to assume the most benign cause (osteoarthritis and malignancy were briefly entertained) and to observe his response to NSAIDs alone, which was dramatic; a chart check weeks later confirmed he remained well.

Reza performs a real-time [[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive autopsy]]. The patient came with acute inflammation and elevated ESR/CRP; the crown-dens picture and knee chondrocalcinosis of pseudogout unify the two sites, and the rapid response to an NSAID — sometimes our response to therapy is our diagnostic test — seals it. In real time, Rabih explains, the pivotal move was social and cognitive as much as radiologic: the neurosurgeon called the diagnosis off the MRI, before the patient returned, without the knee films — a reminder that the "expert radiologist" is sometimes the surgeon who reads these films constantly. He was initially dumbfounded because an atraumatic pathologic fracture in an otherwise-well man made little sense: malignancy does not usually announce itself as a bare bony lesion snapping a neck without antecedent weight loss and malaise, so the whole picture never fit cancer. The episode ends on the teamwork theme — internists, surgeons, and radiologists each occasionally making the call the others miss — and a plug for Reza's newly released book.

|---|---|---|
| One week of escalating, non-traumatic severe neck pain | Sinister/inflammatory process over simple strain | Raised |
| *No prior history* of neck pain | Non-specific chronic neck pain | Strongly lowered |
| Afebrile, normal vitals | Overt sepsis / systemic infection | Lowered |

| ESR and CRP high, CBC normal | Inflammatory (crystal/infection) over mechanical | Raised |

| Neurosurgeon: "not a fracture" | Pathologic fracture | Lowered |

| Bilateral knee chondrocalcinosis | CPPD / pseudogout | Confirmed unifier |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Anatomical Approach|Anatomical reasoning]] as the engine.** Reza's "arrow through the neck" (skin/nerve → subcutaneous → fascia → muscle → bone → vessel) is a structured way to enumerate candidate sites; layered against a normal [[Neurologic Exam|neurologic exam]], it lets him localize by exclusion — away from cord and root, toward the articular column — before any imaging.
- **[[Framing Effect|Framing]] and [[Anchoring Bias|anchoring]] on the MRI read.** The MRI report of a "pathologic fracture" is a high-gravity label that nearly fixed the team on malignancy/infection and triggered a C-collar and emergent consult. The correction did not come from re-examining the patient but from an expert who refused the frame — a clean example of how a confident imaging read can anchor a whole team until someone actively reopens it.
- **[[Base Rate|Base rate]] disciplined by tempo.** Reza repeatedly re-centers the common causes (osteoarthritis is common; the patient even has OA knees) yet declines to let the base-rate favorite explain an acute, escalating, severe course it does not typically produce — base rate informs the prior but does not override discordant tempo.
- **[[Illness Script Command|Illness-script command]] across crystalline disease.** He carries scripts for [[Gout|gout]], [[CPPD|pseudogout]], and [[Rheumatoid Arthritis|RA]] with their differing joint predilections and cervical manifestations, which is what lets the knee [[Chondrocalcinosis|chondrocalcinosis]] snap the cervical lesion into the [[Crowned Dens Syndrome|crowned dens]] script rather than remaining an isolated finding.
- **[[Treatment as a Diagnostic Test|Therapeutic response as data]].** The near-immediate relief from a single NSAID dose is read as confirmatory — too fast for fracture, infection, or tumor — an explicit instance of treatment doubling as the next diagnostic step.
- **[[Availability Heuristic|Availability]] surfaced and set aside.** Reza volunteers two vivid recent cases (his own exertional neck strain; a radiculopathy patient who responded to prednisone) but consciously flags them as not fitting this tempo, modeling how to notice an available anchor without being captured by it.
- **[[Cognitive Autopsy|Cognitive autopsy]] and distributed expertise.** The closing reflection reframes the case in hindsight and credits the neurosurgeon's off-the-MRI diagnosis — a reminder that diagnostic expertise is distributed across specialties and that whoever reads a given film most often may outperform the nominal expert.

## Teaching Discussion

- In a patient with neck pain, the highest-yield triage question is whether they have ever had neck pain before; a first-ever, escalating, severe, atraumatic episode is the presentation that warrants a full evaluation rather than symptomatic discharge.
- A normal neurologic exam is powerful evidence in the cervical spine: intact strength, sensation, gait, and pupillary findings make cord and root disease unlikely and redirect attention to the vertebrae, joints, and soft tissue, even though the pain is nerve-mediated.
- When a common diagnosis (osteoarthritis) is present but its usual tempo does not match an acute escalating course, treat the mismatch as a signal to keep searching rather than as license to close on the familiar answer.
- For a confusing cervical lesion, look for extra-articular clues: bilateral knee chondrocalcinosis identifies CPPD and unifies the picture as crowned dens syndrome — pseudogout of the periodontoid tissues.
- Match the modality to the question: CT is superior to MRI for bone and calcium, and here it distinguished an over-called pathologic fracture from periodontoid inflammation and calcification; the three questions an acute joint evaluation should answer are fracture, infection, and crystals.
- Read imaging as a hypothesis that must explain the patient, and be willing to let a non-radiologist expert who reads a particular study constantly override the initial report — teamwork across internists, surgeons, and radiologists is part of the diagnostic process.
- A rapid, near-complete response to an NSAID is legitimate diagnostic evidence for an inflammatory crystalline arthropathy, faster than fracture, infection, or malignancy could resolve.
