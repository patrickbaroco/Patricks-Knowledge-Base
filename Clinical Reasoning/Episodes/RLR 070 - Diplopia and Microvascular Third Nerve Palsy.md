## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> An 84-year-old man with hypertension and hyperlipidemia (no diabetes) wakes at 3 a.m. with sudden **[[Diplopia|double vision]]**. Aaron Berkowitz builds the localization from first principles: **monocular** diplopia (persists with one eye covered) is an [[Ophthalmology|ophthalmologic/lens]] problem; **binocular** diplopia is **ocular misalignment** and, in neurology, localizes *below* the gaze centers — to a **[[Third Nerve Palsy|cranial nerve 3/4/6]]**, the **[[Internuclear Ophthalmoplegia|MLF]]**, the **extraocular muscles** (thyroid eye disease, orbital mass), or the **[[Myasthenia Gravis|neuromuscular junction]]** (myasthenia, the great mimic). From a *still frame* he reads a subtly **ptotic, mildly abducted left eye**, and — with the [[Ptosis|ptosis]] confirmed — reasons toward a **third nerve palsy**: [[Ptosis|ptosis]] localizes to CN3, [[Horner Syndrome|Horner]], myasthenia, or levator dehiscence, but ptosis *plus* an eye-movement deficit points to **CN3**. The video shows the left eye can't adduct with intact abduction and (per Aaron) intact vertical gaze and **small, non-blown pupils** — an **isolated, [[Pupil-Sparing Rule|pupil-sparing]] third** localizing to the **subarachnoid space**, most likely **[[Microvascular Cranial Nerve Palsy|microvascular]]** (periorbital pain fits). The not-to-miss is a **[[Posterior Communicating Artery Aneurysm|PComm aneurysm]]** (pupillomotor fibers run on the nerve's surface), so despite pupil-sparing he'd still image; **[[Giant Cell Arteritis|GCA]]** gets an ESR/CRP in this age. ## Pearls

- **First fork in [[Diplopia|double vision]]: monocular vs binocular.** If covering *either* eye abolishes it, it's **binocular** (ocular **misalignment**); if it persists with one eye covered (and vanishes covering the other), it's **monocular** — a lens/refractive/[[Ophthalmology|ophthalmologic]] problem, not neurologic. ([[RLR 070 - Diplopia and Microvascular Third Nerve Palsy|RLR 070]])
- **Binocular diplopia localizes *below* the gaze centers.** Frontal-eye-field and PPRF lesions cause **conjugate gaze deviation, not diplopia**; disconjugate gaze requires a lesion of **CN 3/4/6 (nucleus or nerve), the [[Internuclear Ophthalmoplegia|MLF]], the extraocular muscles** (thyroid eye disease, orbital mass), or the **[[Myasthenia Gravis|neuromuscular junction]]**. That short list is the entire playing field. ([[RLR 070 - Diplopia and Microvascular Third Nerve Palsy|RLR 070]])
- **[[Myasthenia Gravis|Myasthenia]] mimics any gaze palsy — suspect it when exams disagree.** Ocular myasthenia can look like a sixth, an [[Internuclear Ophthalmoplegia|INO]], or a partial third, and it **fluctuates** — so if two examiners an hour apart localize *differently*, think fluctuating myasthenia. The **[[Ice Pack Test|ice-pack test]]** (ptosis improves with cooling) is a sensitive, specific bedside confirmer. ([[RLR 070 - Diplopia and Microvascular Third Nerve Palsy|RLR 070]])
- **[[Ptosis|Ptosis]] narrows to four causes — and ptosis + an eye-movement deficit means [[Third Nerve Palsy|CN3]].** Ptosis is CN3 (levator palpebrae), [[Horner Syndrome|Horner syndrome]] (oculosympathetic), myasthenia, or aponeurotic **levator dehiscence** (an ophthalmologic fake-out). Because CN3 innervates the levator *and* multiple extraocular muscles, ptosis with a gaze deficit localizes to the third nerve. ([[RLR 070 - Diplopia and Microvascular Third Nerve Palsy|RLR 070]])
- **Localize an isolated third by the [[Pupil-Sparing Rule|pupil]] and the company it keeps.** A third with a **contralateral hemiparesis/movement disorder** is midbrain (Weber/Benedikt/Claude); with **CN 4/6 and V1/V2** it's cavernous sinus/skull base; **truly isolated** puts it in the **subarachnoid space**. **Pupil-sparing** favors a **[[Microvascular Cranial Nerve Palsy|microvascular]]** infarct (central fibers), while **pupil involvement** flags a compressive **[[Posterior Communicating Artery Aneurysm|aneurysm]]** (surface pupillomotor fibers). ([[RLR 070 - Diplopia and Microvascular Third Nerve Palsy|RLR 070]])
- **[[Microvascular Cranial Nerve Palsy|Microvascular]] third/fourth/sixth palsies aren't only diabetic — and the pupil rule isn't perfect.** Hypertension and hyperlipidemia alone suffice, periorbital **pain** is common ("it's painful to infarct the nerve"), and pupil-sparing does **not** fully exclude aneurysm — so most still get a **CTA**, plus **[[Giant Cell Arteritis|GCA]]** labs (ESR/CRP) in older patients with any new eye or head symptom. Recovery over months; treat diplopia with an **alternating eye patch** or prism. ([[RLR 070 - Diplopia and Microvascular Third Nerve Palsy|RLR 070]])
- **Trust but verify the eye clinic.** Ophthalmology carries more tools (formal fields, [[Maddox Rod|Maddox rod]], prisms) and can see subtle misalignments/field cuts the generalist misses — but they localize the *eye*; the neurologist/generalist must **do the full exam and catch the systemic or CNS cause**. ([[RLR 070 - Diplopia and Microvascular Third Nerve Palsy|RLR 070]])

## Problem Representation

An 84-year-old man with hypertension and hyperlipidemia but no diabetes (recent normal A1c) wakes at 3 a.m. with acute, persistent binocular diplopia and a vague periorbital discomfort, having gone to bed normal. Examination shows a partially ptotic left eye that cannot adduct past the midline, with intact abduction, intact (or near-intact) vertical gaze, and small, symmetric, reactive pupils, and an otherwise pristine neurologic exam; ophthalmology, who saw him first, called it a complete pupil-sparing third nerve palsy with impaired up-gaze. The isolated, pupil-sparing third-nerve pattern localizes to the subarachnoid segment and is most consistent with a microvascular (ischemic) oculomotor palsy; with a normal ESR and a normal CTA (no aneurysm), he is managed with an alternating eye patch and expected recovery over months.

## Case Narrative

Rabih presents (with Aaron Berkowitz solving and Reza heckling): an older man with **double vision**. Aaron starts at the top of the tree. **Diplopia is monocular or binocular**: binocular reflects **ocular misalignment** (each eye's image lands in a different retinal spot) and resolves when *either* eye is covered; **monocular** persists with one eye covered and is a **lens/[[Ophthalmology|ophthalmologic]]** problem. For neurology, the target is **binocular** diplopia, which must localize **below the gaze centers** — frontal-eye-field and PPRF lesions produce **conjugate gaze deviation, not diplopia**. Walking the horizontal-gaze circuit (FEF → contralateral PPRF → CN6 nucleus → via the **[[Internuclear Ophthalmoplegia|MLF]]** → contralateral CN3 nucleus → medial rectus, yoking the eyes), he defines the **playing field**: a lesion of **CN 3/4/6**, the **MLF**, the **extraocular muscles** (thyroid eye disease, orbital mass), or the **[[Myasthenia Gravis|neuromuscular junction]]**. He adds the myasthenia pearl — it **mimics any gaze palsy and fluctuates**, so exams that disagree an hour apart should raise it — and Marty Samuels' aphorism that **diplopia is always acute to the patient** (you compensate until you can't, then it's suddenly double).

From a **still frame**, Aaron reads a subtly **ptotic left eye sitting mildly abducted**, with intact seventh nerves (good nasolabial folds) making an adjacent-nucleus sixth-plus-seven localization less likely. He teaches the practical skill Rabih asked for — **which eye is abnormal** — as the old-fashioned **"H"**: follow the finger to each extreme, hold it, be patient, repeat, hold up the lids, and if stuck, do the rest of the exam and return; **cover/uncover** testing and **Herring's law** (a covered weak eye relaxes and drifts toward its weak muscle) help, and ophthalmology's **[[Maddox Rod|Maddox rod]]/prisms** resolve the subtle cases. On **[[Ptosis|ptosis]]**, he runs the four-cause differential — **CN3** (levator palpebrae), **[[Horner Syndrome|Horner syndrome]]**, **myasthenia**, and **levator dehiscence** (an ophthalmologic fake-out, distinguished by lifting the redundant skin; the **[[Ice Pack Test|ice-pack test]]** confirms myasthenia) — and notes that a Horner from the one place it could coexist with a gaze deficit (the cavernous sinus) essentially never presents in isolation because CN3 sits right there.

Putting ptosis together with the eye being **down-and-out** and a **not-blown pupil**, Aaron builds a **[[Third Nerve Palsy|third nerve palsy]]** and localizes it: an isolated third (no contralateral hemiparesis/movement disorder → not midbrain **Weber/Benedikt/Claude**; no CN4/6 or V1/V2 → not cavernous sinus/skull base) sits in the **subarachnoid space**, and there, a single-nerve lesion is most likely **[[Microvascular Cranial Nerve Palsy|microvascular]]**. The **[[Pupil-Sparing Rule|pupil-sparing]]** pattern fits: microvascular infarcts strike the nerve's **central** fibers and spare the **surface** pupillomotor fibers (which a compressive **[[Posterior Communicating Artery Aneurysm|aneurysm]]** would hit first). Reza marvels that Aaron reached the answer *before the video played*.

The **video** confirms it: on attempted left gaze the left eye abducts and the right adducts (fine); on right gaze the right eye abducts fully but the **left eye barely reaches the midline** (adduction deficit), with **intact vertical gaze** and **small, reactive, non-blown pupils** on lid elevation. Six is working, the pupil is spared — so the deficit is **medial rectus / partial CN3 / MLF**, and with the ptosis, a **partial pupil-sparing third**. A wrinkle: **ophthalmology** (who saw him first) called it a **complete** third with **impaired up-gaze**, whereas Aaron's exam showed vertical gaze intact — a reminder that a lesion can be **partial or evolving** ("ischemic but not yet infarcted"), and that different examiners/tools catch different things.

History fits: **84, HTN and HLD, no diabetes**, sudden onset overnight, an otherwise **pristine** neuro exam (confirmed by neurology residents), and a **vague periorbital ache**. Aaron notes microvascular ocular-motor palsies are **not exclusively diabetic** (HTN/HLD suffice), that periorbital pain is common, and that the **pupil rule is imperfect** — so he'd **reluctantly get a CTA** to exclude aneurysm (citing a recent VA case where a clean-looking pupil-sparing third harbored a basilar-tip aneurysm) and check **[[Giant Cell Arteritis|GCA]]** labs, while candidly admitting he's sent countless ESRs for new eye/headache in older patients and **never diagnosed GCA from the neurology side** (those patients get caught and treated upstream). Workup: **ESR normal, CTA normal** (no causative or incidental lesion); management is an **alternating eye patch** with expected recovery over months and neuro/ophtho follow-up.

The closing meta-lesson (Rabih's ED vantage): **trust but verify**. The ED sits between ophthalmology and neurology; ophthalmology localizes the *eye* with superior tools (formal fields, Maddox rod, prisms) and can detect subtle field cuts/misalignments the generalist misses — but it is **not their job** to work up the systemic/CNS cause, so the generalist must **do the full neuro exam and zoom out**. Aaron's parallel from residency (patients sent across the street from the eye ER with a printed hemianopia the neurologist could barely elicit) underscores both the **sensitivity of the ophthalmologic exam** and the need to reconcile "I can't see out of my left eye" (which may be a left *field* cut, not a left *eye* problem) with a real neurologic localization.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Anatomical Approach|Localization as the entire method]].** Every step is anatomy: monocular vs binocular, above vs below the gaze centers, the horizontal-gaze circuit, and then third-nerve segmental localization (midbrain vs cavernous sinus vs subarachnoid) — reasoning to the answer from a still frame.
- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|The misalignment schema and the ptosis schema]].** Two compact schemas (CN3/4/6–MLF–muscle–NMJ; and CN3–Horner–myasthenia–dehiscence) convert a bewildering exam into a few discriminable options, with myasthenia flagged as the universal mimic.
- **[[Return to the Bedside|The exam is the test]].** The "H," patience and repetition, lid elevation, cover/uncover with Herring's law, and the ice-pack test are the decisive maneuvers — and the discordance with ophthalmology is resolved by recognizing a partial/evolving lesion.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Reading the pupil and the rules]].** Pupil-sparing (central vs surface fibers) supports microvascular over aneurysmal, but the **imperfect rule** mandates imaging anyway — an explicit lesson in not over-trusting a heuristic.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Trust but verify, and honest rules]].** Ophthalmology sees what the generalist can't, yet localizes only the eye; the pupil rule and the "diabetic" label are both imperfect; and Aaron's candor about never diagnosing GCA neurologically models calibrated, humble practice.
- **[[Illness Tempo|Tempo]] (Samuels).** "Diplopia is always acute to the patient" — compensation masks slow misalignment until it breaks, so onset timing must be interpreted, not taken at face value.

## Teaching Discussion

- Separate monocular from binocular diplopia first: monocular (persists with one eye covered) is an ophthalmologic/lens problem, while binocular (resolves covering either eye) is ocular misalignment and the neurologic target.
- Localize binocular diplopia below the gaze centers to CN 3/4/6, the MLF, the extraocular muscles, or the neuromuscular junction, remembering that frontal-eye-field/PPRF lesions cause conjugate gaze deviation, not diplopia.
- Suspect ocular myasthenia whenever a gaze palsy fluctuates or two examiners localize differently, and confirm with the ice-pack test.
- Work ptosis through CN3, Horner syndrome, myasthenia, and levator dehiscence, and recognize that ptosis with an eye-movement deficit localizes to the third nerve.
- Localize an isolated third nerve palsy by its companions (midbrain crossed syndromes; cavernous-sinus CN4/6 and V1/V2; otherwise subarachnoid) and by the pupil: sparing favors microvascular, involvement flags aneurysm.
- Know that microvascular ocular-motor palsies occur with hypertension/hyperlipidemia (not only diabetes), often carry periorbital pain, and that pupil-sparing does not fully exclude aneurysm — so image (CTA) and check GCA labs in older patients.
- Manage microvascular palsies with an alternating eye patch or prism, expecting recovery over months.
- Trust but verify referrals from the eye clinic: reconcile "eye" complaints (which may be field cuts) with a full neurologic exam and pursue the systemic cause the ophthalmologist won't.
