## Pearls

- **When [[Hyponatremia]] and a symptom complex (nausea/vomiting/abdominal pain) coexist, default to the more common causal arrow**: the symptoms usually drive the hyponatremia (via non-osmotic vasopressin release), not the reverse — though a self-reinforcing vicious cycle can develop once hyponatremia is present. The depth of the sodium level helps calibrate which direction dominates; sodium below ~126 starts to plausibly cause symptoms itself (nausea, and neurologic findings from confusion to seizure to coma).
- **A falling BUN is an underused nutritional and volume-status clue.** Low [[BUN as a Clinical Clue|BUN]] reflects reduced urea-cycle input (amino acid/protein turnover) and should prompt questions about nutritional status and risk of beer potomania, in addition to signaling hypovolemia.
- **Hyponatremia correction is a procedural hazard, not just a lab abnormality to fix.** Volume resuscitation in a hypovolemic, non-osmotic-vasopressin-driven patient can flip off vasopressin secretion once volume is restored, producing an abrupt water diuresis and overly rapid sodium correction — risking [[Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome]] even at starting sodiums above 120, though this is far more common below 115.
- **An anion gap metabolic acidosis is usually the dominant, explanatory abnormality even when a competing alkalosis (e.g., from vomiting) is also present**; use the [[KLU Heuristic|KLU heuristic]] (ketones, lactate, uremia) as the base-rate-driven differential before considering exogenous anions (toxic alcohols, salicylates, other ingestions).
- **Nausea/vomiting-predominant abdominal presentations, especially with improvement on supportive care alone and no prior abdominal surgery, should raise obstruction on the [[VIPO Framework]]** (vascular, inflammatory, perforation, obstruction) — because obstruction classically causes disproportionate nausea/vomiting relative to pain, and severe pain in bowel obstruction is often a late sign of infarction.
- **A single normal lipase does not rule out an intra-abdominal process requiring imaging**, and the framing of a case (leading with "nausea and vomiting" versus "abdominal pain") can itself bias the diagnostic momentum toward a less anatomically directed workup.

## Problem Representation

A man in his 50s with well-controlled HIV on antiretrovirals, a history of alcohol use disorder (reportedly not currently drinking), and chronic pancreatitis with a prior ERCP stent presents with three days of nausea, vomiting, and epigastric pain radiating to the back, found to have severe hyponatremia (123) with a wide anion gap metabolic acidosis, a disproportionately low BUN, urinary ketones, and an initially normal lipase — a picture that evolved, after volume resuscitation and correction of ketosis, into a persistent abdominal pain syndrome ultimately explained by [[NSAID-Induced GI Complications|NSAID-induced]] gastric ulcers causing partial gastric outlet obstruction.

## Case Narrative

Bob Centor presents the case as a resident-facing consult: a man in his 50s with well-controlled HIV, a past history of alcohol use disorder (currently denying use), and documented chronic pancreatitis (without exocrine or endocrine insufficiency) status post ERCP stent placement years prior. He develops three days of nausea and vomiting with abdominal pain, prompting an ED call for admission for "nausea, vomiting, and hyponatremia."

Initial vitals are unremarkable (afebrile, BP 135/87, HR 80 on a beta blocker, RR 16, pain 7/10). Labs show a leukocytosis (WBC 7.8), sodium 123, chloride 77, bicarbonate 19 (anion gap ~27), BUN 5, creatinine 0.7 (baseline), and glucose 149 (not materially affecting the corrected sodium). Potassium is unresultable due to hemolysis.

The team defers seeing the patient in favor of working the data first, tracking hyponatremia, low BUN, and the anion gap in parallel. Serum osmolality is ordered given the sodium is low enough to plausibly be symptomatic. Urinalysis later shows a specific gravity of 1.017 (urine osmolality ~510) and significant ketones; serum osmolality is low; lactate and alcohol level are negative. The patient receives normal saline for volume repletion and chloride replacement to address the mixed metabolic alkalosis (from vomiting) and ketosis-driven anion gap acidosis. By the next day, the anion gap has closed, bicarbonate has risen appropriately, and sodium has corrected at a safe rate — but abdominal pain persists, now radiating to the back, and the patient still cannot tolerate oral intake.

Lipase, initially normal (13), is repeated and remains normal, despite an elevated alkaline phosphatase (246) and known pancreatic stent history. Bowel sounds are initially absent on exam, then present the following day. Given the atypical absence of an early CT scan in a patient with persistent abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting, the group flags this as unusual, and Bob frames the case as a self-directed cognitive autopsy on why imaging was delayed.

CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast eventually shows no pancreatitis but reveals wall thickening in the gastric antrum. GI endoscopy reveals three large gastric ulcers. On further history (not available on admission), the patient had been started on meloxicam for arthritis roughly a week prior, prescribed by rheumatology — a detail not initially disclosed. The final diagnosis is NSAID-induced gastric ulcer disease causing partial gastric outlet obstruction, which explains the vomiting-predominant presentation, the initial hyponatremia and ketosis (from poor intake), and the persistent postprandial abdominal pain.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

|---|---|---|
| Nausea/vomiting + abdominal pain + sodium 123 | Symptom complex → hyponatremia (non-osmotic vasopressin release) vs. hyponatremia → symptoms | Favors symptoms-driving-hyponatremia given the degree of associated GI complaints; reconsidered if sodium were markedly lower |
| Low BUN (5) | Hypovolemic hyponatremia; also raises beer-potomania risk given uncertain nutritional status | Both raised in parallel — reasoning holds multiple contributors open rather than anchoring on one |
| Anion gap ~27 with normal lactate, negative alcohol level, urinary ketones present | Ketosis (from poor intake/vomiting) as cause of anion gap acidosis | Confirmed; uremia excluded by normal BUN/creatinine; toxic ingestion downgraded but not fully excluded until acetone/osmolar gap addressed |
| Normal serum osmolal gap | Toxic alcohol ingestion | Down-weighted |
| Resolution of anion gap and hyponatremia with fluids/chloride repletion, but persistent abdominal pain | Metabolic derangements were a downstream consequence, not the primary disease | Confirms arrow direction: GI syndrome → electrolyte/acid-base abnormalities, not the reverse |
| Alkaline phosphatase 246, prior ERCP stent, pain radiating to back | Recurrent pancreatitis or biliary obstruction | Raised, but never confirmed |
| Lipase normal x2 | Pancreatitis | Down-weighted, though not to zero given chronic pancreatitis history and possibility of a non-elevated lipase in recurrent disease |
| Absent bowel sounds (day 1) then present (day 2) | Complete bowel obstruction | Down-weighted — inconsistent with a clinically well-appearing, non-distended patient |
| Vomiting-predominant symptoms improving with supportive care, no prior abdominal surgery | Gastric outlet or bowel obstruction (VIPO framework) | Raised as leading hypothesis pre-CT |
| CT: antral wall thickening, no pancreatitis | Gastric process (ulcer, mass, inflammation) rather than pancreatic/biliary | Strongly favored |
| Endoscopy: three large gastric ulcers + meloxicam history obtained | NSAID-induced gastric ulcer disease with partial gastric outlet obstruction | Confirmed as unifying diagnosis |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring]] on chronic pancreatitis**: the patient's known pancreatitis history, ERCP stent, and elevated alkaline phosphatase created diagnostic momentum toward a biliary/pancreatic explanation, delaying consideration of a primary gastric process even after two normal lipase values.
- **[[Framing Effect|Framing]] in symptom presentation**: the case was introduced to the resident as "nausea, vomiting, and hyponatremia," not "abdominal pain," and the group explicitly notes that this framing may have shaped the initial workup — de-emphasizing the anatomic abdominal complaint in favor of the metabolic derangement.
- **Base-rate reasoning under uncertainty**: absent a CT scan, the group invokes the principle that very few abdominal presentations (they cite roughly eight: pancreatitis, DKA, hernia, zoster, urinary retention, inferior MI, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, and PD-related peritonitis) can be diagnosed with confidence on history, exam, and labs alone — everything else requires imaging.
- **VIPO framework applied to symptom proportionality**: nausea/vomiting out of proportion to pain, in a patient without prior abdominal surgery, is used to favor obstruction over vascular, inflammatory, or perforative etiologies, since obstruction pain becomes severe only late (with bowel infarction).
- **Deliberate cognitive autopsy**: Bob Centor frames the entire case not as a "guess the diagnosis" exercise but as a structured retrospective review of a real delay in ordering the CT scan, modeling non-punitive self-audit of clinical reasoning.
- **Acknowledged tradeoff of sensitivity vs. overtesting**: Rabih explicitly reframes the "miss" as a reasonable tradeoff — over-scanning every nausea/vomiting/hyponatremia presentation would be low-yield, and this category of missed-then-caught diagnosis may be an acceptable cost of not overtesting.

## Teaching Discussion

- **Which way does the arrow point between hyponatremia and a GI symptom complex?** Sodium depth is the first anchor: above ~126, symptoms are unlikely to be explained by the sodium itself, so the presumption should run from the GI syndrome to the hyponatremia (via non-osmotic vasopressin release from volume depletion). Below that threshold, hyponatremia becomes plausible as a co-contributor, and a self-reinforcing loop (nausea worsens intake and volume status, which worsens sodium, which worsens nausea) becomes the more accurate model. Serum and urine osmolality, urine sodium, and volume exam findings are the data points that actually adjudicate the direction rather than just asserting it.
- **A disproportionately low BUN is more than a "normal renal function" data point.** In this case it functioned as a proxy for poor nutritional/protein intake, which is what put beer potomania on the table alongside straightforward hypovolemic hyponatremia — a low-solute diet limits free-water excretion independent of volume status. The same low-BUN, low-intake picture supported ketosis as the anion gap driver, since inadequate caloric intake plus vomiting is a classic ketogenic setup. The teaching point: a low BUN should prompt a nutritional/intake question, not just a renal-function checkbox.
- **Correcting hyponatremia in this kind of patient is riskier than the sodium number alone suggests.** Once volume is restored with fluids, the non-osmotic vasopressin drive that was holding water in shuts off, producing a water diuresis that can correct sodium faster than intended — risking osmotic demyelination even above the more commonly cited sodium-115 danger zone, just less often. The lesson is to anticipate this shift before it happens (frequent recheck, planned rate of correction) rather than treating fluids as low-risk simply because the starting sodium isn't critically low.
- **KLU (ketones, lactate, uremia) is the right first pass for an anion gap acidosis, but a negative KLU workup doesn't end the search.** It should prompt consideration of exogenous anions — toxic alcohols (via osmolar gap), salicylates, and other ingestions — before the gap is called "unexplained." Here, KLU (specifically ketosis) was sufficient, so the workup didn't need to extend further, but the discussion treats that as good luck as much as good reasoning.
- **Two sequential normal lipase values meaningfully lower — but don't eliminate — the probability of an acute pancreatic process,** especially in someone with known chronic pancreatitis, where baseline pancreatic reserve can blunt lipase elevation even during a real flare. The case ultimately shows the pain wasn't pancreatic at all, but the group is explicit that a normal lipase alone shouldn't have been treated as sufficient to close that door — it needed imaging to actually settle the question.
- **Symptom proportionality is a genuine discriminator on the VIPO framework.** Nausea/vomiting clearly outweighing pain, in a patient without prior abdominal surgery, points toward obstruction — because obstruction pain is often mild until late (infarction), whereas vascular, inflammatory, and perforative processes tend to declare themselves with pain earlier and more insistently. This patient's pain-light, vomiting-heavy course was itself diagnostic signal, not just a vague GI complaint to work around.
- **Case framing measurably shapes downstream reasoning, and this case is presented as an example of that risk.** Leading with "nausea, vomiting, and hyponatremia" (as the ED did) rather than "abdominal pain" nudged the workup toward electrolyte correction and away from anatomic imaging. The practical guard is to explicitly re-list the patient's active problems in neutral terms at each handoff, rather than inheriting the framing of whoever presented the case last — a chief complaint is often already a diagnostic hypothesis in disguise.
- **A deliberate "cognitive autopsy" — reviewing a diagnostic delay that didn't cause harm — has value precisely because it doesn't wait for a bad outcome to trigger review.** Bob Centor's framing treats the delayed CT as a learning opportunity regardless of outcome, which is a lower-stakes and more frequent way to calibrate judgment than only debriefing true misses. Rabih's counterpoint is also worth holding onto: the goal isn't zero delay, since scanning every nausea/vomiting/hyponatremia presentation immediately would be low-yield: the goal is noticing *why* the delay happened this time, so the same reasoning can be applied faster next time a similar pattern shows up.
