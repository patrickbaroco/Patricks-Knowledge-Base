## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Reza brings two real, currently-active cases from his own team. In the first, a healthy 76-year-old man's month-long gait decline was labeled BPPV at an outside hospital, until Reza's team recognizes the alarming pace of his functional loss and pursues an MRI that reveals scattered posterior circulation embolic strokes. In the second, an elevated lipase in a man with advanced dementia and recurrent anxiety-driven ED visits leads — after a thoughtfully deferred, then reconsidered, CT scan — to an unexpected finding of rectal wall thickening and a large obstructing ureteral stone that turns out to be the real explanation for his recurring "chest pain." The episode closes with a moving, unscripted update: the T-cell lymphoma/dysphagia patient from RLR 156 has passed away.

## Pearls

- Dizziness has a useful first branch point: is it a primary/intrinsic brain problem (like vertigo) or a perfusional/extrinsic problem (blood flow to the brain)? When history and exam don't clearly commit to one side, actively provoking the symptom (having the patient walk) is often more diagnostically useful than further passive observation.
- A helpful teleological framing for vague symptoms: dizziness exists to tell the body not to walk, the same way nausea exists to tell the body not to eat — patients who report "dizziness" but can walk without difficulty are often describing something else entirely (fatigue, malaise), not a true vestibular or perfusional process.
- A patient whose baseline function was completely normal just weeks (or even a month) prior, now presenting with a significant new functional deficit, deserves a markedly lower diagnostic threshold than the same deficit developing gradually over months to years — the abruptness of the delta, not just the severity of the current deficit, is what should drive urgency.
- The general principle behind ordering low-yield-but-high-stakes tests (a CTA, an EKG, a troponin with low pretest probability) generalizes to any workup: when the downside of a missed diagnosis is severe (e.g., stroke, life-threatening), the diagnostic threshold for pursuing confirmatory imaging should be set much lower than it would be for a purely benign, non-time-sensitive process.
- CT is a comparatively poor test for the posterior fossa specifically, even though it performs reasonably well for supratentorial subacute strokes of similar duration — a negative CT head should not be treated as meaningfully reassuring against a posterior circulation process, and MRI should be pursued when posterior circulation stroke remains a real consideration.
- A "central paroxysmal positional vertigo" pattern exists as a genuine, if uncommon, sinister mimic of benign paroxysmal positional vertigo — a reminder not to reflexively attribute positional, episodic vertigo symptoms to a benign peripheral cause without at least considering the central alternative, particularly when the clinical trajectory doesn't fit the benign pattern.
- A subacute stroke can present less like a slowly progressive process and more like an abrupt event that then plateaus ("water spilled on the ground" rather than "water slowly boiling") — a distinction worth actively asking about in the history, since it changes how the imaging findings should be interpreted.
- A scattered pattern of embolic infarcts confined to one side of the posterior circulation, without involvement elsewhere in the brain, points toward a single culprit vessel (e.g., the ipsilateral vertebral artery) via artery-to-artery embolism — mechanically similar to a coronary thrombus causing distal embolization.
- Unlike carotid disease, vertebral artery disease has few available mechanical/interventional treatment options for most patients; management typically mirrors medical management of a coronary thrombotic event — a defined course of dual antiplatelet therapy followed by risk factor modification.
- Avoiding diagnostic-momentum bias applies to how a team talks about a prior institution's reasonable-but-incorrect working diagnosis: praising your own team's process (fresh eyes, willingness to re-walk the patient, escalating imaging appropriately) is more constructive and more accurate than criticizing a prior team that reached a defensible conclusion with the information available to them at the time.
- Macrolipase (a benign, congenitally large lipase-immunoglobulin complex causing a persistently elevated but clinically insignificant lipase level), renal failure, and diabetic ketoacidosis are recognized causes of an elevated lipase that do not represent true pancreatitis — a false-positive elevation should be considered whenever the clinical picture (epigastric or back pain, supportive CT findings) doesn't otherwise fit.
- In a patient whose ability to report symptoms is limited (advanced dementia, aphasia, or similar), "no reported symptoms" cannot be treated with the same reassurance it would carry in a patient who can reliably self-report — the workup threshold should account for the possibility that real symptoms are simply being camouflaged by the patient's underlying condition.
- An abnormal imaging finding described by radiology in diagnostic language (e.g., "proctitis") is a description of an appearance, not a confirmed diagnosis — rectal or bowel wall thickening on CT has a differential that includes both a mundane, common process (stercoral colitis/proctitis from a large stool burden exerting pressure) and a much more morbid one (malignancy), and both should be explicitly considered before anchoring on the label given by the read.
- Ureteral stone size is a strong predictor of spontaneous passage likelihood, with passage probability dropping sharply as stone size approaches roughly 10 millimeters — a useful, quantifiable way to estimate whether a given stone is likely to need intervention versus watchful management.
- Reframing a consult call as an invitation ("may I invite you to help care for this patient?") rather than a directive or demand changes the tone of the interaction and tends to produce more collaborative, engaged responses from consultants — a small communication shift with an outsized effect on how requests for help are received.
- When a patient has limited ability to advocate for themselves after discharge (cognitive impairment, lack of a reliable support system, difficulty navigating follow-up logistics), it can be appropriate to go beyond the standard discharge process — informally curbsiding a specialist, personally coordinating follow-up — even though this level of individualized effort isn't scalable to every patient and every situation.

## Problem Representation

**Case 1:** A 76-year-old man with hypertension and hyperlipidemia, previously fully independent, developed gait instability over about a month that accelerated in the final two weeks; initially diagnosed at an outside hospital as BPPV based on positional dizziness and discharged, he re-presented with worsening orthostatic-triggered unsteadiness and a non-focal exam, was found on MRI/MRA to have a scattered pattern of embolic infarcts confined to one side of the posterior circulation (attributed to ipsilateral vertebral artery artery-to-artery embolism), and was managed medically with dual antiplatelet therapy before discharge to a skilled nursing facility for rehabilitation.

**Case 2:** An elderly man with advanced dementia at a community living facility, sent to the emergency department for a recurring pattern of chest pain and anxiety that resolved with anxiolytics and had a negative cardiac workup, was found to have an incidentally elevated and rising lipase; after initial watchful observation was reconsidered given his limited ability to self-report symptoms, a CT scan revealed unexplained rectal wall thickening (read as "proctitis") and a large, previously undiagnosed obstructing ureteral stone with hydronephrosis that the team ultimately concluded was the real, previously unrecognized explanation for his recurrent presenting complaint, managed with collaborative GI and urology input while balancing further invasive workup against his overall vulnerability and goals of care.

## Case Narrative

**Case 1.** Reza presents a currently active case from his own hospital team: a 76-year-old man with hypertension and hyperlipidemia, otherwise healthy and previously fully active and independent, who developed gait instability starting about a month prior to presentation.

Before hearing further detail, Robbie frames dizziness around a first branch point — is this a primary brain (intrinsic, vertiginous) problem, or a perfusional (extrinsic, blood-flow-related) one — noting that bedside tools can usually help distinguish the two.

Reza fills in the story: about four to five days before presenting to his hospital, the patient went to a neighboring facility reporting four days of positional dizziness triggered by head movement, was diagnosed with BPPV, and discharged with outpatient follow-up arranged. He then presented to Reza's hospital describing a persistent sense of instability and unsteadiness, most pronounced specifically when moving from lying to standing. The initial exam showed no focal neurologic deficit (though the patient wasn't yet ambulated), with normal vitals apart from mild hypertension (systolic 160, compared to a baseline typically in the 130s-140s).

Robbie notes the story doesn't yet commit clearly to either branch of his framework — gait ataxia on exam would favor a brain problem, while a strongly positional-on-standing pattern would favor a perfusional one — and offers a guiding principle: the purpose of dizziness is to warn the body not to walk, just as nausea warns against eating. Since the story hasn't yet revealed why the body is issuing that warning, he recommends doing the uncomfortable thing and actually walking the patient to provoke and localize the underlying problem, noting that patients who report dizziness but walk without difficulty are often describing something else (fatigue, malaise) rather than a true vestibular or perfusional syndrome.

Reza adds more detail: the patient describes his legs as feeling "like noodles." Bedside exam shows normal strength throughout, no focal deficits, an unsteady but not frankly ataxic gait (no dysmetria or dysdiadochokinesia elicited), and orthostatic vitals are being obtained. A CT head is already available and is normal. Asked whether he'd pursue MRI/MRA at this point, Robbie reasons that diagnostic clarity is needed given the patient's genuine reduction in safe ambulation, which risks progressing if left unaddressed; while acknowledging real uncertainty between a cardiovascular and a brain cause, the fact that the patient can walk far enough to feel wobbly well before developing chest pain or shortness of breath leans him toward an intrinsic brain process. Given the patient's relative stability, he'd pursue a non-emergent MRI, escalating urgency if no alternative extra-neurological explanation emerges over the following weeks.

Reza then adds the detail that changes everything: per the family, the patient was completely independent and functioning normally with zero ambulation issues two weeks ago, and normal a full month ago — the functional decline has therefore been genuinely rapid, not a slow chronic process. Robbie immediately revises his urgency: this degree of dysfunction over such a short timeframe is alarming, and while an EKG and telemetry are reasonable (since structural heart disease doesn't typically progress this quickly, though a vascular process might), the highest-yield next step is an MRI without delay. Reza connects this explicitly to a broader principle the show has invoked before: tests like a CTA, an EKG, or a troponin are pursued even at low pretest probability specifically because the target diagnosis is life-threatening, which lowers the diagnostic threshold accordingly — here, the alarming rate of functional decline plays the same role in lowering the threshold for MRI. The resident performs a HINTS exam (a skill Reza notes requires months of deliberate practice to perform reliably).

MRI/MRA of the head and neck the next day reveals multiple embolic ischemic strokes, all confined to one side of the posterior circulation, involving multiple brain structures in a scattered pattern that correlates fully with the patient's neurologic syndrome.

Robbie reflects that subacute strokes have "snuck up" on many clinicians before, and is curious in retrospect whether the patient's decline was actually more abrupt in onset than initially described, followed by a plateau — likening this pattern to water spilled on the ground and staying there, rather than water on a slowly boiling stove. He reasons that the fully unilateral, scattered-but-confined-to-one-territory pattern points to a single culprit vessel — tracing back to the ipsilateral vertebral artery via artery-to-artery embolism, mechanically analogous to a coronary artery thrombus causing distal embolization. He notes that, unlike carotid disease, vertebral artery disease offers few mechanical treatment options for most patients, so management mirrors the standard approach to a coronary thrombus: a defined course of dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin plus clopidogrel) followed by risk factor modification.

The actual workup and management matched Robbie's prediction closely: a bubble echocardiogram was negative (with the usual caveat that roughly 20% of people have an incidentally detected PFO of uncertain significance), a Zio patch was placed for extended cardiac monitoring, and the stroke team recommended aspirin, clopidogrel, and a statin — including 90 days of dual antiplatelet therapy, a duration Reza notes is a newer recommendation relative to what he learned in residency, which Robbie confirms he's aware of from his attending years without being certain of the exact source. The patient developed mild swallowing difficulty requiring speech-language pathology evaluation and was ultimately discharged to a skilled nursing facility for rehabilitation, having been judged too functional for acute inpatient rehab.

Reza reflects on how he handled the case with his team: rather than criticizing the outside hospital for missing the diagnosis (which had reached a reasonable, defensible conclusion — BPPV — given the information available and understandable diagnostic momentum), he instead praised his own team for the group effort that avoided simply deferring to the prior read, emphasizing that the same mistake could have happened to anyone. Robbie closes the case by naming two teaching threads: CT's comparatively poor sensitivity for the posterior fossa specifically (even though it likely would have picked up a similar duration of subacute stroke elsewhere in the brain), and the existence of "central paroxysmal positional vertigo" as a genuine, if uncommon, sinister mimic of benign positional vertigo — not necessarily the mechanism in this specific case, but a reminder not to reflexively attribute paroxysmal positional symptoms to a benign cause.

**Case 2.** Reza presents a second, also currently active case: an elderly man with advanced dementia living at a community living center, sent to the emergency department for chest pain and anxiety — a recurring pattern from prior hospitalizations. The overnight resident performed a thorough workup: troponin negative, EKG normal, chest pain fully resolved with anxiolytics. A lipase was also sent (without a clearly specific indication) and returned elevated — 400 initially, 350 on a repeat the same day — alongside a history of some belly pain reported the day before. Relevant background: a prior PET-CT, obtained for an unrelated reason, had incidentally shown a small right pleural effusion with mild FDG avidity (too small to biopsy, stable on subsequent imaging) and FDG avidity in the region of the distal sigmoid colon. By morning, the patient is asymptomatic, and the team debates whether to pursue a CT scan for the elevated lipase.

Robbie's initial instinct is "no symptoms, no problem" as a tentative default, but he immediately qualifies it: the real question is whether he's truly asymptomatic or whether symptoms are being camouflaged by his dementia. He also reframes the goal beyond ruling out an acute emergency, toward keeping the patient comfortable and symptom-free going forward. His leading concern for a dynamic, episodic lipase elevation is intermittent biliary obstruction from choledocholithiasis, and he'd want to know if the patient has a known history of gallstones; absent an urgent indication, he leans toward watchful observation, reasoning that time is a reasonably safe diagnostic tool in a patient who is already vulnerable on multiple fronts.

Asked for a broader differential of an elevated lipase outside true pancreatic duct obstruction, Robbie offers macrolipase (a benign, congenitally large lipase-antibody complex causing a persistently but falsely elevated level), renal failure, and diabetic ketoacidosis as the false-positive causes he's personally encountered, while acknowledging the full list is likely longer. Reza adds the clinical caution that a decompensating diabetic patient's elevated lipase shouldn't be labeled pancreatitis without supporting findings like epigastric or back pain and confirmatory CT changes.

The overnight team, led by a resident Reza specifically credits by name (Michelle) for her thoughtful restraint, initially defers the CT scan, reasoning there's no urgency to rush toward imaging given the resolved presentation. On rounding the next morning, Reza's own team reconsiders: given the patient's dementia and limited ability to reliably report symptoms, his "chest pain" could plausibly have been referred abdominal pain, and he may genuinely have pancreatitis after all. They obtain a CT scan, explicitly weighing the downside (radiation, cost, and the risk of finding incidental findings without an obvious next step) against the value of clarity.

The CT reveals three findings: a redemonstration of the known small right pleural effusion; new rectal wall thickening, explicitly described by radiology as "proctitis"; and a large stone in the right ureter with associated hydronephrosis (the patient has a known history of nephrolithiasis, including a prior left ureteral stone that passed spontaneously).

Robbie pushes back on accepting "proctitis" as a settled diagnosis from the CT description alone, noting the only confirmed finding is rectal wall thickening — a finding whose differential spans a much more mundane, common process (stercoral proctitis/colitis, from pressure exerted by a large stool burden, especially plausible in an older, likely immobile patient) to a considerably more morbid one (malignancy, referencing a prior RLR case where apparent "proctitis" on imaging turned out to be rectal cancer). For the ureteral stone, he reasons that the presence of hydronephrosis confirms it's actively obstructing and symptomatic, and that stone size predicts the likelihood of spontaneous passage — probability of spontaneous passage drops sharply as size approaches roughly 10 millimeters (essentially zero at that size, rising by roughly 10 percentage points per millimeter of reduction). He separates the acute question (facilitating stone passage) from the background question (further evaluating the rectal wall thickening, complicated by needing a goals-of-care/decision-maker conversation given the patient's dementia).

Reza notes there was no stool actually present in the rectum on the CT — Robbie considers this evidence against, though not conclusive against, a stercoral process, since stool could have been present earlier and since passed, including via an intervening bowel regimen. The team discusses tenesmus as the single most specific symptom of true proctitis, while acknowledging this patient's dementia limits his ability to reliably report it; decides, given diagnostic surprises happen, to screen broadly for sexually transmitted causes of proctitis (gonorrhea, chlamydia, HSV, HIV) despite low pretest suspicion; and flags real concern that the concurrent FDG-avid pleural effusion and FDG-avid colonic finding could represent a disseminated malignancy (solid tumor favored over a hematologic process, though both considered).

Rather than placing a formal, directive GI consult, Reza's team informally runs the case by the GI fellow (a former chief resident) in a collegial, non-demanding way. The fellow points out the patient had a colonoscopy three years prior with no cancer found, that typical wait times for a repeat scope with biopsy run around six months, and recommends sending stool studies first, holding the patient's apixaban (prescribed for atrial flutter, which Reza notes carries a somewhat lower thromboembolic risk than atrial fibrillation given its more electrically organized/less desynchronized nature), and reserving colonoscopy for if stool studies are unrevealing — judging the six-month timeline acceptable given the low-grade, stable nature of the pleural/colonic FDG avidity. The fellow also offers an insight the team hadn't considered: the ureteral stone itself is probably the actual explanation for the patient's recurrent presenting complaint, reframing what had been anchored purely as a "rule out ACS" presentation. Urology is similarly engaged collegially, without a directive request, to weigh in on stone management.

Reflecting on the exchange, Robbie shares a phrase from one of his ER mentors for framing consult calls: "Hi there, may I invite you to help me care for this patient?" — reframing the request as a privilege extended to the consultant rather than a demand placed on them, and noting how effective this framing has proven, whether or not the exact words are used. He connects this to being married to a rheumatology fellow, which has given him an unusually close view of what the consultant experience is actually like. He closes the case reflecting on how often biliary tree obstruction has humbled him more than renal obstruction in older patients, and on the value of having clinched a concrete explanation for this patient's presentation, since both biliary colic and renal colic are likely to recur — and now the team (and any future team) will have a real diagnosis to consider the next time he presents with "anxiety."

The episode closes with an unscripted, personal update: Reza shares that the patient from RLR 156 — the man with T-cell lymphoma and progressive, ultimately undiagnosed oropharyngeal dysphagia and dysphonia — has passed away. He reflects warmly on the patient's positive attitude and gratitude throughout his care despite his inability to speak, and expresses gratitude for having had the opportunity to care for him.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

**Case 1**

| Data Point | Effect on Differential |
|---|---|
| Positional dizziness with head movement, diagnosed as BPPV at outside hospital | Initially anchors toward a benign peripheral vestibular cause |
| Persistent unsteadiness, worse specifically with supine-to-standing transition | Raises consideration of a perfusional/orthostatic contributor alongside a possible intrinsic cause |
| Normal strength, non-focal exam, unsteady but non-ataxic gait; normal CT head | Does not clearly localize; CT's limited posterior fossa sensitivity means it cannot meaningfully lower suspicion for a posterior circulation process |
| Family history: fully independent and normal 2 weeks and 1 month prior | Sharply raises urgency and lowers the diagnostic threshold for MRI, given the abrupt, severe functional decline over a short timeframe |
| MRI/MRA: scattered embolic infarcts confined to one side of the posterior circulation | Confirms an ischemic stroke etiology; localizes to a single culprit vessel (ipsilateral vertebral artery) via artery-to-artery embolism |
| Negative bubble echo, no other stroke territories involved | Supports a large-vessel/artery-to-artery mechanism over a cardioembolic or diffuse process |

**Case 2**

| Data Point | Effect on Differential |
|---|---|
| Recurrent chest pain/anxiety presentations, negative troponin/EKG, resolution with anxiolytics | Supports a recurring anxiety-driven pattern, though this framing is later challenged |
| Elevated and rising lipase (400 → 350) without clear pancreatic symptoms in a patient with dementia | Raises concern that reported symptoms are unreliable; keeps pancreatitis and its mimics (macrolipase, renal failure, DKA) on the differential |
| CT: rectal wall thickening ("proctitis") | Raises a differential from mundane (stercoral proctitis) to morbid (malignancy); label alone insufficiently specific |
| CT: large obstructing right ureteral stone with hydronephrosis, known history of nephrolithiasis | Raises probability that the stone itself — not anxiety or ACS — is the true and previously unrecognized source of his recurrent presenting complaint |
| Incidental FDG-avid pleural effusion and FDG-avid distal sigmoid colon finding | Keeps an occult malignant process on the differential, tempering the urgency of the stone/proctitis workup relative to it |
| No stool present in the rectum on CT | Modestly lowers, but does not eliminate, probability of a purely stercoral cause of the rectal wall thickening |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Intrinsic-versus-extrinsic dizziness framework, resolved by provocation**: using deliberate symptom provocation (walking the patient) rather than passive observation to break a diagnostic stalemate.
- **Delta-over-time as an urgency driver**: explicitly using the steepness of functional decline (normal a month ago, severely impaired now), not just the current severity, to recalibrate diagnostic threshold.
- **Low-pretest-probability-but-high-stakes testing principle**: generalizing the logic behind reflexively ordering a CTA/EKG/troponin (severity of the missed diagnosis lowers the required pretest probability) to a new clinical scenario (MRI for stroke).
- **Diagnostic-momentum avoidance, modeled as team culture**: explicitly declining to criticize a prior team's reasonable-but-wrong conclusion, instead crediting the current team's process.
- **Camouflaged-symptom reasoning in cognitively impaired patients**: treating "no reported symptoms" as unreliable rather than reassuring when a patient's ability to self-report is compromised.
- **Radiology-label skepticism**: treating "proctitis" as a description of rectal wall thickening rather than an established diagnosis, and generating a full differential (common to morbid) rather than anchoring on the report's wording.
- **Collegial/invitational consult framing**: an explicit communication technique (asking rather than directing) credited with producing more engaged, higher-quality consultant input — directly credited with surfacing the case's actual unifying explanation (the ureteral stone).
- **Reframing the "problem" mid-case**: the GI fellow's incidental observation that the stone likely explains the presenting complaint retroactively reframes the entire case away from its initial "rule out ACS" anchor.

## Teaching Discussion

Both cases in this episode share a throughline: a plausible, already-rendered diagnosis (BPPV; anxiety-driven chest pain) is not treated as final simply because it's reasonable and already documented elsewhere in the chart. In Case 1, what breaks the anchor is a specific, actively elicited piece of history (the rapid functional decline) that recalibrates urgency; in Case 2, it's a structural systems decision (reconsidering a deferred CT given a compromised historian) plus a fresh pair of eyes from a curbside consult that surfaces an explanation nobody had been looking for.

Case 1 also models a broader diagnostic-threshold principle explicitly borrowed from other high-stakes, low-pretest-probability testing scenarios (CTA for PE, troponin for low-suspicion chest pain): the threshold to test should track the severity of what you're trying not to miss, not just the raw probability of finding it. Case 2 extends this into communication and systems practice — the "may I invite you" consult framing and the deliberate choice to informally curbside rather than formally consult are presented as practical tools that materially changed the outcome of the case, not just interpersonal niceties.

The episode's closing personal note — the death of the RLR 156 patient — serves as a quiet reminder that not every case resolves toward diagnostic triumph, and that the value of careful, humble, patient-centered reasoning holds regardless of ultimate outcome.
