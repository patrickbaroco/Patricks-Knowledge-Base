## Summary

> A Neurology VMR built around an **81-year-old woman** with **4–6 weeks of constant right-sided headache** and **painful, intermittent blurry vision** — worse on the right, with pain on eye movement (especially rightward gaze), tearing, and episodic horizontal **diplopia** — superimposed on fatigue, bilateral shoulder/hand joint pain, and a heavy cardiovascular/autoimmune history (psoriatic/rheumatoid arthritis on an IL-17 inhibitor, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, COPD, diabetes). Discussants Jeffrey (presenting), Valerie, and Sebastian build the diplopia pathway (muscle → neuromuscular junction → CN III/IV/VI → cavernous sinus → brainstem/MLF), flag **glaucoma** and **giant cell arteritis (GCA)** as the two can't-miss diagnoses for headache-plus-eye-pain in an elderly patient, and — despite an unremarkable initial neurologic exam, bland CSF, and only mildly elevated inflammatory markers — pursue vessel imaging that reveals **bilateral supraclinoid internal carotid artery stenosis with wall enhancement**, plus incidental systemic atherosclerotic/aneurysmal findings. A temporal artery biopsy confirms **giant cell arteritis**, but despite steroids and tocilizumab the patient suffers progressive, multifocal cerebral infarction and new pachymeningeal enhancement, ultimately dying of treatment-refractory strokes — the rare, underrecognized, and often treatment-resistant complication of **GCA-associated CNS vasculitis**.

## Pearls

- **Headache-plus-eye-pain in a patient over 50 has two can't-miss diagnoses: glaucoma and giant cell arteritis.** Both are emergencies that can cause irreversible vision loss, and both should be actively screened for (intraocular pressure, temporal artery tenderness) even when the story sounds primarily neurologic.
- **Papilledema is not a synonym for optic disc edema** — papilledema specifically means disc edema *from elevated intracranial pressure*. Optic neuritis can cause disc edema (if anterior) or no disc edema at all (if posterior/retrobulbar), so a normal fundoscopic exam does not rule out optic nerve inflammation.
- **A bland, unremarkable CSF does not exclude a CNS vasculitic process.** Vasculitis confined to the vessel wall — rather than producing meningeal/parenchymal inflammation — can spare the CSF entirely; a normal LP should shift suspicion toward a vascular or vessel-wall process rather than closing the case.
- **Giant cell arteritis is treated empirically on clinical suspicion, before biopsy confirmation** — the risk of irreversible vision loss from waiting outweighs the risk of reduced biopsy yield, and arteritic changes can still often be seen on biopsy even after several days of steroids.
- **GCA most classically affects the intracranial circulation at the supraclinoid segment of the internal carotid artery, often bilaterally** — a rare but recognizable pattern distinct from typical atherosclerotic stenosis.
- **A negative Congo red stain on temporal artery biopsy helps exclude amyloid-beta-related angiitis**, a mimicker of GCA/CNS vasculitis particularly relevant in elderly patients with progressive intracranial vessel disease.
- **GCA-associated CNS vasculitis is a rare, likely underrecognized complication** (estimated in roughly 10–15% of cases with modern high-resolution vessel-wall imaging), producing dural arterial inflammation and pachymeningitis — and these patients are often **refractory to standard GCA therapy** (tocilizumab), with case-series evidence favoring cyclophosphamide instead.
- **Clinical worsening after a "confirmed" diagnosis should trigger active reconsideration, not just treatment escalation** — the group explicitly revisits infectious, malignant, and drug-induced causes of pachymeningeal enhancement when the patient deteriorated despite GCA-directed therapy, rather than assuming simple treatment failure.

## Problem Representation

An 81-year-old woman with psoriatic/rheumatoid arthritis (on an IL-17 inhibitor), atrial fibrillation, heart failure with a bioprosthetic mitral valve, COPD, and type 2 diabetes presents with 4–6 weeks of constant right-sided headache with intermittent retro-orbital and temporal pain, intermittent blurry vision worse on the right, pain with eye movement (especially rightward gaze), tearing, and episodic horizontal diplopia, superimposed on fatigue, bilateral shoulder and hand joint pain, and new nocturia. Exam shows low-grade fever, tachycardia, mild hypotension, inflammatory bilateral shoulder/hand arthritis, and an entirely normal neurologic and cranial nerve exam apart from right-eye cotton wool spots without papilledema and normal intraocular pressures, with no temporal artery tenderness. Inflammatory markers are mildly elevated (ESR 68, CRP 1.5); an extensive infectious and autoimmune serologic workup is negative; a shoulder aspirate shows sterile inflammatory arthritis; and an initial lumbar puncture is essentially bland. MRI orbit/brain and MRA head reveal moderate-to-severe stenosis of the bilateral cavernous and supraclinoid internal carotid arteries with vessel wall thickening and enhancement, alongside incidental systemic atherosclerotic and aneurysmal aortic findings on CT angiogram without evidence of active large-vessel vasculitis. A temporal artery biopsy confirms transmural inflammation consistent with giant cell arteritis (Congo red negative). Despite steroids and subsequent tocilizumab, the patient develops progressive multifocal cerebral infarction (including the globus pallidus, then a large MCA territory infarct) and new diffuse pachymeningeal enhancement, and ultimately dies of treatment-refractory strokes attributable to GCA-associated CNS vasculitis.

## Case Narrative

Jeffrey gives the chief complaint: headache with painful blurry vision. Sebastian asks Valerie for her initial approach. She starts with the headache: distinguishing primary from secondary headache, using the presence of painful blurry vision as a red flag pushing toward secondary causes, and layering an anatomical approach (intracranial structures — brain, meninges, vessels; extracranial structures — temporal artery in GCA; and referred pain from the neck, ear, or eye) on top of the primary/secondary framework. She flags the painful blurry vision as making a benign primary headache (migraine, tension, trigeminal neuralgia/cluster spectrum) less likely, though acknowledges some overlap, and lists emergent ophthalmologic causes to rule out — glaucoma, orbital cellulitis — alongside optic neuropathy (classically optic neuritis, inflammatory or ischemic). Sebastian builds on this, noting the two threads — headache, and pain specifically with eye movement — together point toward optic neuritis with its anterior/posterior and inflammatory/infectious/infiltrative/metabolic/genetic differential, and that the overlap of headache-plus-eye-pain in this age group makes **glaucoma** and **giant cell arteritis** his top two can't-miss diagnoses.

Jeffrey gives the fuller HPI: an 81-year-old woman with 4–6 weeks of new constant right-sided headache — intermittent pain behind the right eye, near the forehead and right temple — associated with intermittent blurry vision (worse right, possibly also affecting the left eye), pain with eye movement especially on rightward gaze, tearing when the headache is severe, and episodic horizontal diplopia; she also notices some left-eye blurriness and pain with movement. Pertinent positives: fatigue, bilateral shoulder and hand joint pain, and new nocturia (waking hourly to urinate, which she wonders is contributing to her fatigue). Pertinent negatives: no eye discharge, floaters, flashes, curtain-like vision loss, paresthesias, weakness, photophobia, phonophobia, nausea, vomiting, trauma, or fever, and no antecedent trigger. She was started on the NSAID etodolac two weeks earlier by neurology with minimal improvement.

Valerie synthesizes: an elderly patient with a subacute-to-chronic, intermittently progressive headache, invoking the **SNOOP**-style red-flag framework — systemic signs (fatigue, joint pain, sleep disturbance) and focal neurologic findings (intermittent blurry vision, tearing/autonomic signs, diplopia) both present. She sets the headache schema aside to focus on the diplopia schema specifically: first confirming binocular (versus monocular/ophthalmologic) diplopia, then working through the extraocular muscles (thyroid eye disease, infiltrative/deposition disease causing muscle enlargement), the neuromuscular junction (myasthenia, especially with fatigability or diurnal pattern), the nerves (III, IV, VI — with rightward-gaze-predominant pain suggesting a horizontal diplopia schema centered on CN VI and the horizontal gaze centers), the cavernous sinus (where multiple nerves converge), and the brainstem (pons and midbrain, MLF/PPRF for conjugate gaze). She notes the intermittent, several-week time course raises an inflammatory process. Sebastian agrees that time course and localization are the two key organizing features here: 4–6 weeks raises inflammatory, infectious, metabolic/nutritional, or malignant tempo; localization implicates the optic nerve (CN II, given the visual symptoms), the extraocular muscles/CN VI (given pain on rightward gaze), and possibly a skull-base or meningeal process if findings turn out to be bilateral or multifocal. He's struck that the patient was discharged on an NSAID despite multiple headache red flags — age over 50, constant pain, and focal features — and states that anyone over 50 with a new headache warrants neuroimaging. Asked what three exam findings he'd most want, Sebastian prioritizes eye movements, a full cranial nerve exam (to assess for multifocal cranial neuropathy suggesting an infiltrative process), and a fundoscopic exam specifically for optic disc edema — clarifying that "papilledema" specifically denotes disc edema from elevated intracranial pressure, and that optic neuritis can present with or without disc edema depending on whether the inflammation is anterior or posterior.

Jeffrey continues the history: past medical history of psoriasis versus psoriatic/rheumatoid arthritis on secukinumab (an IL-17 inhibitor), heart failure from coronary disease with mitral stenosis status post bioprosthetic valve replacement (on warfarin), atrial fibrillation, COPD, hyperlipidemia, and type 2 diabetes, with recently increased diuretics for lower-extremity edema; family history of breast and colon cancer in multiple relatives; and a significant smoking history, quit 16 years prior, with no alcohol or drug use. Valerie highlights the psoriasis/rheumatoid arthritis history as most concerning — a potential route to CNS involvement via the meninges or vessels — and Sebastian agrees, adding that the immunosuppression (secukinumab) raises concern for opportunistic infection (toxoplasmosis, syphilis, viral etiologies) affecting the optic nerve alongside the primary inflammatory/rheumatologic possibility, while considering atherosclerotic/thromboembolic disease (smoking history, atrial fibrillation) as comparatively lower on the list given the systemic-symptom-heavy presentation.

Exam (a composite of rheumatology and outpatient neurology assessments, later repeated on admission): low-grade fever (100.8°F), mild hypotension (100/60), tachycardia (110), normal respiratory rate; inflammatory arthritis of bilateral hands and shoulders, no lymphadenopathy, no neck stiffness, no rash. Neurologic exam is essentially normal: normal mental status, memory, language, and fund of knowledge; full visual fields, no ptosis, no nystagmus, full and smooth extraocular movements without abnormal saccades, pupils equal and reactive; no facial weakness or sensory deficit; midline tongue without atrophy; 5/5 strength throughout with normal bulk/tone and no drift, tremor, or fasciculations; intact sensation and coordination (finger-nose, finger taps, arm rolling); narrow-based gait with intact narrow-based tandem walking. Ophthalmology's exam adds right-eye cotton wool spots without papilledema and normal intraocular pressures bilaterally; there is no temporal artery tenderness.

Valerie reviews this against her optic neuropathy checklist (central visual field defects, relative afferent pupillary defect, disc edema versus disc pallor/atrophy suggesting chronicity) — none clearly present — while flagging the fever and headache combination as raising meningitis, particularly given the subacute-to-chronic tempo consistent with infectious (TB, fungal) or inflammatory/rheumatologic/malignant causes. She also circles back to the nocturia, which Jeffrey clarifies is likely explained by recently increased diuretics for heart-failure-related edema rather than a primary neurologic process. Asked how to further evaluate for meningismus, Valerie lists nuchal rigidity and Kernig/Brudzinski signs (acknowledging their limited ~50% sensitivity) and fundoscopy for signs of elevated pressure; Sebastian adds photophobia, gentle assessment of neck resistance, the jolt accentuation test (shaking the head 2–3 times per second — sensitive for meningeal irritation), and screening for a non-blanching rash concerning for meningococcemia if bacterial meningitis is suspected.

Working through the workup with the **UBERS** mnemonic (Urine, Blood, ECG, Radiology, Special testing), the group sends: blood cultures; CBC and metabolic panel; ESR and CRP (with Sebastian noting the value of comparing to this rheumatoid arthritis patient's prior baseline levels); RPR/syphilis serology, Bartonella serology, and a QuantiFERON-TB gold; autoimmune serologies (ANA-type panel, rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP) if not already done; and, prompted by a chat suggestion, thyroid function tests given the ocular symptoms. For CSF, Valerie requests cell count, protein, glucose, cultures, VDRL, cytology and flow cytometry (given the immunosuppression and malignancy concern), and fungal antigen testing; Sebastian adds AFB/fungal stains and cultures, a meningitis/encephalitis PCR panel, toxoplasmosis CSF antigen, and a Lyme antibody panel. For imaging, both favor an MRI brain with and without contrast; Sebastian additionally recommends orbital imaging with fat suppression and contrast (to assess for optic neuritis or orbital myositis) and vessel imaging, given vasculitis is on the differential and vessel-wall enhancement protocols can help characterize it.

Results return: leukocytosis (14), hypokalemia (3.0) and a mild AKI (creatinine 1.3) otherwise-normal chemistries; ESR 68 and CRP 1.5 (both elevated); negative blood cultures, HIV, QuantiFERON-TB, hepatitis B/C, Bartonella serology, and syphilis antibody testing; negative rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP, and ANCA; normal IgG4 and thyroid studies. A shoulder aspirate for the inflammatory arthritis shows 29,000 white cells with no crystals and a negative bacterial culture. The initial LP is essentially bland: 19 RBCs, 2 WBCs, normal protein and glucose, negative viral meningitis/encephalitis PCR panel, negative cryptococcal antigen, negative CSF VDRL, and negative AFB/fungal cultures. Valerie notes the bland CSF is reassuring against bacterial meningitis but doesn't exclude processes that spare the CSF — vasculopathy or vasculitis confined to the vessel wall, whether primary (PACNS) or secondary to her rheumatologic disease, infection, or malignancy.

Imaging (obtained as an outpatient and leading to her admission) reveals: MRI orbit showing a stable, previously known right ICA aneurysm, stenosis of the supraclinoid ICA (left greater than right), and enhancement of the bilateral supraclinoid ICA segments; a follow-up MRA head/angiogram showing moderate-to-severe stenosis of the bilateral cavernous and supraclinoid internal carotid arteries with vessel wall thickening and enhancement; and a CTA chest/abdomen/pelvis (obtained given concern for systemic vasculitis) showing no active vasculitis but incidental calcified and non-calcified atherosclerotic plaque in the aortic arch and branches, a juxtarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm, and multifocal mild arterial stenoses of other aortic branches. Valerie reads this as focal, bilateral supraclinoid ICA vasculopathy with enhancement — consistent with an inflammatory vasculitic process, distinguishing it from her incidental atherosclerotic/aneurysmal burden — and suggests sending additional CSF autoimmune studies (e.g., Sjögren's-related antibodies) to look further for a secondary vasculitis cause. Sebastian agrees the imaging strongly supports a vasculitic process, broadens the secondary-vasculitis differential (infectious — especially VZV — and autoimmune — rheumatoid, Sjögren's), and, lacking an extracranial biopsy target, suggests a temporal artery ultrasound (for a halo sign) as a path to tissue diagnosis, while keeping lymphoma-associated vasculopathy on the list given the patient's age. Asked what he would do empirically while awaiting results, Sebastian states he was taught to treat presumed GCA empirically — given the risk of irreversible vision loss — even at some cost to biopsy yield, and that with the fever, he would also have considered empiric CNS antimicrobial coverage pending CSF results.

Jeffrey reveals the next chapter: rheumatology's strong clinical impression was giant cell arteritis, and high-dose steroids were started. Two days later, the patient develops new altered mental status — initially attributed to hyperglycemia (glucose to 400, in the setting of high-dose steroids) or steroid psychosis, prompting a steroid dose reduction (to 50 mg IV Solu-Medrol) and initiation of an insulin drip — but neurology's exam reveals new perseveration and aphasia. A repeat MRI shows an acute right globus pallidus infarct, worsening bilateral ICA stenosis, and a new left A1 segment stenosis, concerning for worsening vasculitis; a repeat (non-contrast, due to declining renal function) MRA shows new mild luminal narrowing of the bilateral distal P2 segments and persistence of the previously seen ICA/A1 stenoses. A temporal artery biopsy, performed after four days of steroids, shows transmural inflammation with a non-specific infiltrate consistent with giant cell arteritis, with a negative Congo red stain. The patient is diagnosed with GCA, started on tocilizumab, and discharged on prednisone and tocilizumab — but one week later suffers acute neurologic decline, found by her daughter unable to speak or rise from a chair. A new MRI shows multiple new cerebral infarctions across multiple vascular territories, including a large MCA-territory infarct with cerebral edema (no hemorrhagic conversion), stable previously seen stenoses, and new diffuse, smooth pachymeningeal enhancement.

Faced with this deterioration despite GCA-directed treatment, Valerie reconsiders the differential rather than assuming simple treatment failure: infections that could be missed or masked by immunosuppression (TB, fungal), infiltrative causes of diffuse pachymeningeal enhancement (sarcoidosis, though its behavior of worsening under steroids gives her pause), and malignancy (again suggesting flow cytometry). Sebastian, noting that typical CNS vasculitis outcomes — while often disappointing — don't usually deteriorate this precipitously, wonders about a missed secondary or superimposed process: drug-induced vasculitis (raising a question about the IL-17 inhibitor or tocilizumab itself), an undertreated infection (fungal, parasitic), or a paraneoplastic process (which might produce only temporary improvement without treating the true driver) — and would want the CSF cytology/flow cytometry results, would ask rheumatology about alternative immunosuppression, and would re-consult infectious disease.

Jeffrey reveals the outcome: repeat workup (a second temporal artery biopsy, LP, and MRA) reproduced essentially the same findings — this was still giant cell arteritis. The explanation is that GCA can, in a small subset of patients, extend intracranially and cause **CNS vasculitis** — a phenomenon a recent paper in the *Journal of Rheumatology* estimates may occur in roughly 10–15% of GCA cases and often goes undetected without high-resolution vessel-wall MRI, which can show dural artery vasculitis producing pachymeningitis. These patients are frequently refractory to standard GCA therapy (tocilizumab), unlike this case's outcome; Jeffrey notes that, in retrospect, escalating to cyclophosphamide — a stronger CNS-vasculitis treatment with some supporting case-series data even specifically for GCA-associated CNS vasculitis — might have been considered. Despite treatment, the patient ultimately died from her multifocal strokes. Jeffrey highlights that, in hindsight, she had the classic age and painful-headache-plus-blurry-vision presentation for GCA (albeit with some atypical features), that bilateral supraclinoid ICA involvement is the most characteristic — if rare — pattern of intracranial GCA, and that her bilateral shoulder/hand inflammatory arthritis was consistent with polymyalgia rheumatica, a classic GCA association — but that her CNS vasculitis ultimately proved refractory to timely immunosuppression.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Unifying Diagnosis|Testing whether one diagnosis explains everything, and revising when it stops fitting.]]** Rather than assuming GCA-directed treatment failure when the patient worsened, the group explicitly reopens the differential (infection, malignancy, drug-induced vasculitis) before ultimately recognizing that the SAME diagnosis — GCA — does explain the deterioration, just via a rare intracranial-extension mechanism they hadn't yet considered.
- **[[Base Rate|Age-appropriate epidemiologic weighting.]]** The "can't-miss" framing of glaucoma and GCA for headache-plus-eye-pain is explicitly tied to the patient's age (>50/81 years old), illustrating how base rates by age group shape which emergent diagnoses get prioritized.
- **[[Illness Script|Testing the optic neuropathy and meningitis scripts explicitly against the exam.]]** Valerie runs the patient's findings against a structured optic neuropathy checklist (visual fields, RAPD, disc edema/atrophy) and a meningismus checklist (nuchal rigidity, Kernig/Brudzinski, jolt accentuation), using mismatches to responsibly downgrade rather than force a fit.
- **[[Test Interpretation|A bland CSF is not treated as ruling out CNS disease.]]** The group explicitly reasons that vessel-wall-confined vasculitis can spare the CSF, using the normal LP to redirect suspicion toward a vascular process rather than closing the case.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Treating empirically despite diagnostic uncertainty, and staying open after "confirmation."]]** The team treats presumptively for GCA before biopsy confirmation (accepting the biopsy-yield tradeoff), and later resists anchoring on that same diagnosis when the patient doesn't improve as expected — both instances of tolerating uncertainty rather than forcing premature closure in either direction.

## Teaching Discussion

- For headache with eye pain in a patient over 50, actively screen for the two can't-miss diagnoses: glaucoma (intraocular pressure) and giant cell arteritis (temporal artery tenderness, jaw claudication, PMR symptoms, ESR/CRP).
- Distinguish papilledema (disc edema specifically from elevated intracranial pressure) from optic neuritis-related disc edema (anterior) or normal-appearing disc with posterior/retrobulbar optic neuritis.
- Use a structured diplopia localization pathway (muscle → NMJ → CN III/IV/VI → cavernous sinus → brainstem/MLF/PPRF) and correlate with which direction of gaze worsens the pain or diplopia.
- Remember a bland CSF does not exclude CNS vasculitis, since vessel-wall-confined inflammation can spare the subarachnoid space.
- Treat suspected GCA empirically with steroids before biopsy confirmation given the risk of irreversible vision loss; a temporal artery biopsy can still show diagnostic changes days into treatment.
- Recognize bilateral supraclinoid ICA stenosis with wall enhancement as a characteristic (if rare) pattern of intracranial GCA, and polymyalgia rheumatica-type bilateral shoulder/hip girdle pain as a classic associated finding.
- Use a negative Congo red stain on temporal artery biopsy to help exclude amyloid-beta-related angiitis as a GCA/CNS-vasculitis mimicker.
- Know that GCA-associated CNS vasculitis is likely underrecognized (~10–15% by recent estimates), often visible only on high-resolution vessel-wall MRI as dural arterial inflammation/pachymeningitis, and is frequently refractory to standard GCA therapy (tocilizumab) — consider cyclophosphamide when standard therapy fails.
- When a patient worsens despite treatment for a seemingly confirmed diagnosis, actively reconsider infectious, malignant, and drug-induced alternatives before assuming simple treatment failure — but also stay open to the possibility that the original diagnosis is correct and simply has a rarer, more severe phenotype than initially recognized.
