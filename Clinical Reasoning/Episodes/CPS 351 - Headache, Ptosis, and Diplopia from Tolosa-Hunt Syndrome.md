> [!summary] Summary
> A 65-year-old woman with a week of new, unilateral right-sided headache (initially treated as sinusitis) develops sudden right ptosis and diplopia. Exam localizes to the right [[Cavernous Sinus Syndrome|cavernous sinus]] — a pupil-sparing partial CN III palsy plus partial CN VI palsy plus V1 sensory loss (the "O-TOM-CAT" territory) — but the first contrast MRI/MRA, LP, and an exhaustive autoimmune/infectious panel are all normal. An empiric oral steroid trial brings gradual improvement, but three months later the syndrome recurs, faster and worse; this time contrast MRI catches subtle enhancement along the lateral wall of the cavernous sinus, and IV steroids resolve the deficits within ten hours. Final diagnosis: **[[Tolosa-Hunt Syndrome]]**, an idiopathic granulomatous inflammation of the cavernous sinus — a diagnosis of exclusion that can be imaging-negative on a first pass and is classically, sometimes dramatically, corticosteroid-responsive.

## Pearls

- **O-TOM-CAT**: the cavernous sinus's lateral wall carries (superior→inferior) CN **O**culomotor (III), **T**rochlear (IV), **O**phthalmic V1, **M**axillary V2; the internal **C**arotid **A**rtery and CN **T**abducens (VI) float free within the sinus body rather than in the wall — which is why an isolated CN VI palsy (unlike III/IV/V1/V2) can occasionally occur without any other cavernous sinus signs.
- A **pupil-sparing** CN III palsy classically argues *against* an external compressive lesion (aneurysm) and *for* a microvascular/ischemic ("diabetic") third nerve, because the pupilloconstrictor fibers run peripherally in the nerve and are the first casualties of external compression but are relatively spared by an infarct of the nerve's interior — but this rule is imperfect: a cavernous sinus lesion can selectively hit the nerve's *superior* division (ptosis + elevation paresis) while sparing the *inferior* division that carries the pupillomotor fibers at that point, mimicking a "spared" pupil despite a genuine structural lesion.
- **Imaging-negative does not mean localization-negative.** A compelling exam-based localization (here: CN III + CN VI + V1 on one side = cavernous sinus) should still drive management even when MRI/MRA and LP are clean — subtle cavernous sinus enhancement can be missed by a first read and only appreciated later, by a subspecialist neuroradiologist or on repeat imaging during a flare.
- **[[Tolosa-Hunt Syndrome]]** is a diagnosis of exclusion (rule out aneurysm, carotid-cavernous fistula, cavernous sinus thrombosis, mucormycosis, neoplasm, GCA) that is classically and often dramatically steroid-responsive — IV steroids can succeed where an oral trial only partially works, and a rapid, striking response to steroids is itself a diagnostic clue.
- **GCA can rarely cause diplopia** (via ischemia of the ocular motor nerves or a posterior-circulation-predilected vasculitic stroke) in addition to its classic visual-loss presentation (arteritic ischemic optic neuropathy) — but per the discussants' shared clinical experience, actually *finding* a case of GCA on routine screening is rare, and the absence of jaw claudication and constitutional symptoms argues against it.
- Before starting high-dose steroids for a presumed inflammatory process, rule out (or empirically cover) infections that can disseminate catastrophically under immunosuppression — TB (PPD/IGRA) and *Strongyloides* (empiric ivermectin) — a practice consistent whether the patient is being treated in the US or in Myanmar.

## Problem Representation

A 65-year-old woman with hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and a 40-year smoking history presents with a new, sudden-onset right-sided headache following a week of untreated sinus-type symptoms, followed by sudden right ptosis and painful diplopia. Exam reveals a right pupil-sparing partial CN III palsy, a partial CN VI palsy, and V1-distribution sensory loss, with an otherwise normal neurologic exam — localizing to the right cavernous sinus. Extensive vascular, infectious, autoimmune, and CSF workup is unrevealing; symptoms partially respond to empiric oral steroids, then recur three months later with faster tempo, at which point repeat contrast MRI reveals subtle cavernous sinus wall enhancement and IV steroids produce dramatic resolution.

## Case Narrative

The case was presented by Dr. A. Thant, an aspiring neurologist from Myanmar, and discussed by Dr. Valeria Roldan, a recent medical graduate from Lima, Peru, also an aspiring neurologist, together with Aaron Berkowitz.

The chief complaint: a 65-year-old woman with headache and double vision. Valeria's opening framework tackled the two chief complaints separately before merging them. For **headache**, she emphasized starting broad — remembering that not all headache causes are intracranial (the ear, skull, and extracranial arteries can all refer pain) — before applying a primary-versus-secondary framework and screening for red flags: systemic symptoms/fever, a new neurologic localizing finding (the diplopia itself counts), older age, pregnancy, seizures, and papilledema. For **diplopia**, she laid out a full [[Diplopia]] localization pathway: the extraocular muscles themselves (e.g., thyroid eye disease), the neuromuscular junction (myasthenia), the ocular motor nerves (CN III, IV, VI) as they pass through the [[Cavernous Sinus Syndrome|cavernous sinus]], and finally the brainstem — noting that a brainstem lesion severe enough to cause isolated diplopia would usually also produce other findings given how tightly packed the relevant structures are there, and that "time course is always queen in neurology."

Berkowitz highlighted this as a teaching moment about **System 1 versus System 2 reasoning**, invoking Daniel Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow*: Valeria had given the deliberate, "thinking slow" anatomic approach first, but he asked what came to mind fast, given the combination of an older woman with headache *and* diplopia. Both agreed: [[Giant Cell Arteritis]] pattern-matches immediately in this demographic. Berkowitz used this to make two points at once — thinking fast is valuable and often correct, but it must not be allowed to anchor the workup and crowd out the System 2 anatomic reasoning that follows. He also flagged, candidly, that despite sending ESR/CRP on many older patients with headache over years of practice, he personally had almost never caught a case of GCA that way — echoing a nearly identical aside from [[CPS 379 - Sudden Diplopia and One-and-a-Half Syndrome from a Dorsal Pontine Stroke|CPS 379]] earlier in this same diplopia batch. He cited a JAMA rational clinical examination review concluding that, absent jaw claudication and constitutional symptoms, GCA is unlikely — though diplopia, when present, is one of the higher-yield predictors. Dr. A, asked about her experience in Myanmar, reported seeing GCA only twice in a busy practice with very few neurologists — reinforcing that this is a genuinely low-incidence diagnosis even where it is actively sought.

He also walked through the anatomy of **secondary headache**, organizing it into three buckets: intracranial (brain, meninges, vasculature, ventricles), head-and-neck (refractive error, dental disease, sinus disease, cervical disc disease), and systemic (viral illness, post-vaccination, etc.) — and simplified the SNOOP-style red-flag mnemonics into two questions he actually uses at the bedside: what are the headache's *characteristics* (pace of onset — thunderclap versus indolent; worse on waking or awakening the patient from sleep, suggesting elevated intracranial pressure) and its *context* (immunocompromise, cancer history, older age, pregnancy)?

Dr. A's history was presented as a systematic, negative-laden review of systems that Berkowitz praised as itself diagnostic — every "no" told the listener what she had considered and excluded. The patient, a right-handed 65-year-old, developed a sudden, unusual, throbbing right temporal/orbital headache without fever, congestion, lacrimation, rhinorrhea, or proptosis, not associated with aura, nausea, vomiting, photophobia, or phonophobia, and unlike anything she had experienced before. A week prior she had been treated empirically for "sinusitis" with antibiotics for rhinorrhea; the headache persisted, and after about a week she developed sudden right-sided ptosis and diplopia. She denied anosmia, facial numbness, facial asymmetry, hearing loss, dysphagia, dysarthria, and any limb weakness or sensory change; gait was normal.

Valeria distilled the red flags precisely: sudden-onset ptosis with diplopia points to CN III, which immediately raises the question of pupil involvement; the history-taking around congestion/lacrimation/rhinorrhea was aimed at excluding a trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia (e.g., cluster headache) or Horner syndrome/carotid dissection; the absence of other cranial neuropathies and long-tract signs argued against a brainstem process; and the absence of migrainous features and the fact that this was entirely new for the patient both pointed away from a primary headache disorder and toward a secondary cause. Given the sudden ("thunderclap-adjacent") onset, both discussants initially prioritized vascular etiologies — a PCOM aneurysm compressing CN III with subarachnoid hemorrhage (possibly a sentinel bleed), or, given the preceding sinus symptoms and the patient's diabetes and smoking history, an intracranial extension of sinusitis (with cavernous sinus thrombosis or a fulminant infection such as mucormycosis flagged as a specific concern, though her relatively well appearance after a week argued against it).

**Past medical history**: hypertension, type 2 diabetes (HbA1c 7%); no jaw claudication reported. **Medications**: amlodipine, metformin, multivitamins. **Social history**: current smoker, 40 pack-years' worth of exposure (4–5 cigarettes/day for 40 years), no alcohol or drug use; lives with her niece's family.

**Exam**: T 97.4°F, HR 80, BP 140/90, RR 12, SpO2 100% on room air. Cardiac exam notable for a mid-systolic murmur at the aortic area, no gallops or rubs; lungs clear; abdomen benign; no edema. Neurologically: alert and oriented ×3, 3/3-word recall at five minutes, normal language. CN I normal. CN II: pupils equal, round, and reactive to light, no relative afferent pupillary defect. **CN III/IV/VI**: right ptosis, and the right eye was restricted in *all* directions of gaze but retained a small amount of abduction — i.e., CN VI was involved but only partially, not just CN III. The left eye had a full range of motion. **CN V**: reduced pinprick sensation in the right V1 distribution only, sparing V2 and V3. **CN VII**: no facial weakness (orbicularis oculi/oris 5/5). **CN VIII**: hearing intact. **CN IX/X**: voice and palate elevation normal. **CN XI**: intact. **CN XII**: tongue midline, no atrophy. Motor: normal tone and full power in all limbs, normal and symmetric reflexes, flexor plantar responses. Sensory (elsewhere): intact pain, vibration, and proprioception. Cerebellar: normal finger-to-nose and heel-to-shin, no dysmetria, normal gait.

Valeria immediately asked about the pupil before hearing the rest of the exam — a pupil-sparing third nerve palsy narrows the differential away from a classic compressive PCOM aneurysm (which would be expected to hit the peripherally-located pupilloconstrictor fibers early) and toward either a microvascular/ischemic third nerve (favored by the patient's diabetes and smoking) or a lesion positioned to spare those particular fibers despite being structural. But learning that the right eye was restricted in *every* direction — not just the CN III-innervated directions — and that V1 sensation was also reduced, she and Berkowitz converged on the [[Cavernous Sinus Syndrome|cavernous sinus]] as the localization: CN III + CN VI + V1, all ipsilateral, using the O-TOM-CAT framework (wall: CN III, CN IV, V1, V2; floating within the sinus body: the internal carotid artery and CN VI, which is why it can occasionally be spared or isolated). Berkowitz noted the cavernous sinus is also one of the only places in the body where an artery runs inside a vein.

He revisited the pupil-sparing question at length: while pupil-sparing classically favors a non-compressive (microvascular) etiology, a structural lesion confined to the cavernous sinus can also spare the pupil if it selectively affects the nerve's *superior* division (causing ptosis and elevation paresis) while leaving the *inferior* division — which is thought to carry the pupillomotor fibers at that point in the nerve's course — untouched. He referenced his own teaching notes on this nuance, acknowledging that the exact rotation of fascicles within the third nerve as it travels through the cavernous sinus is not something he fully understands "down to the wire," and that a carotid-cavernous aneurysm could in principle produce exactly this pattern.

With the differential narrowed to a cavernous sinus process, the leading candidates were: carotid-cavernous aneurysm, carotid-cavernous fistula (against which was the absence of conjunctival injection), cavernous sinus thrombosis (particularly worrying given the preceding sinus symptoms and diabetes), an intracranial extension of infection (mucormycosis), and — less discussed at this point but revisited later — [[Tolosa-Hunt Syndrome]], an idiopathic inflammatory condition of the cavernous sinus that Valeria recalled but could not initially name.

Valeria requested contrast-enhanced MRI/MRA of the brain targeting the cavernous sinus, explaining that contrast highlights both vascular structures (timed for arterial or venous phases) and any breakdown of the blood-brain barrier from inflammation, infection, or neoplasm. She also flagged the importance of the cardiac and carotid exam — echoing the bedside-exam teaching of the late Dr. Marty Samuels — given the murmur noted on exam and the theoretical possibility of an embolic source; Berkowitz agreed this deserved attention but noted that an embolic shower selectively hitting three nerves within a single cavernous sinus, rather than scattered parenchymal territory, would be an anatomic long shot.

**Labs**: Hgb 11, WBC 11,000, platelets 250, ESR 21, CRP 13.25 (mg/L) — an ESR under half the patient's age (a common rough reassurance threshold) and a CRP that is mildly elevated but not "GCA-high." TSH, LFTs, and basic metabolic panel normal; HbA1c 7%. **Contrast MRI/MRA brain**: normal. **LP** (after excluding contraindications): normal opening pressure, cell count, glucose, protein, and cytology. Autoimmune panel (anti-dsDNA, p-ANCA, c-ANCA, ANA, rheumatoid factor, anti-acetylcholine receptor antibody) and infectious serologies (Lyme, HIV, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, syphilis) all normal.

The unrevealing contrast MRI and MRA surprised both discussants — an imaging-negative unilateral cavernous sinus syndrome. Berkowitz reframed this as neurology's core value proposition: the exam gave a precise localization that imaging then failed to explain, but that doesn't erase the localization — it means either the lesion is genuinely subtle (as later proved true, when a colleague's similar case was read as showing faint cavernous sinus wall enhancement only by an experienced neuroradiologist) or the cavernous sinus, being a dural fold rather than part of the subarachnoid space, simply doesn't always light up even when inflamed, and an LP would not be expected to reflect a process confined there. With vascular, neoplastic, and infectious causes reasonably excluded, the working diagnosis became [[Tolosa-Hunt Syndrome]] by exclusion — an idiopathic granulomatous inflammation of the cavernous sinus/superior orbital fissure. Before starting steroids, the team confirmed the patient had been screened for conditions that could worsen catastrophically with immunosuppression (tuberculosis, strongyloidiasis) — in Dr. A's practice in Myanmar, as in Berkowitz's own, empiric ivermectin is given alongside steroids in this situation rather than waiting on stool studies, mirroring the same practice used before treating neurocysticercosis with steroids and antiparasitics.

An empiric **oral steroid trial** was given; symptoms improved gradually over time, though not dramatically. Three months later, the patient returned with the same right-sided periorbital/temporal headache, this time more severe, and with sudden ptosis and diplopia developing within a single day (faster than the first episode) along with increased fatigue. The full infectious/autoimmune workup was repeated and was again negative, but this time **contrast MRI revealed focal asymmetric enhancement along the lateral wall of the right cavernous sinus, posterior to the orbital apex** — the radiographic signature of Tolosa-Hunt syndrome. She was treated with **IV methylprednisolone**, and her symptoms resolved within ten hours.

Berkowitz closed with an anecdote from a colleague, Dr. Doug Pett, whose patient had a scan compellingly consistent with Tolosa-Hunt; an initial oral steroid trial produced only partial, then worsening symptoms, but switching to IV steroids on subspecialist advice led to immediate resolution — reinforcing that IV corticosteroids may succeed where oral dosing falls short in this condition, and that the speed and completeness of the steroid response is itself part of the diagnostic picture. The episode closed with a tribute to Dr. Marty Samuels, Berkowitz's mentor, whose extensive bedside-exam teaching videos (including hours-long sessions on the general exam, mental status, and cranial nerves) continue to be studied by trainees worldwide, including — as this episode's own discussion illustrated — a medical student in Lima, Peru.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

**[[Tolosa-Hunt Syndrome]]** — idiopathic granulomatous inflammation of the cavernous sinus/superior orbital fissure presenting as painful ophthalmoplegia; a diagnosis of exclusion after ruling out vascular (aneurysm, carotid-cavernous fistula, cavernous sinus thrombosis), neoplastic, and infectious (including mucormycosis in diabetic/immunocompromised patients) causes; MRI may show cavernous sinus wall enhancement, but this can be absent or too subtle to detect on an initial read; classically and sometimes dramatically responsive to corticosteroids, with IV dosing occasionally succeeding where oral dosing only partially works; can recur.

**[[Cavernous Sinus Syndrome]]** (localizing framework, not a diagnosis in itself) — ipsilateral combination of CN III, IV, VI, and V1 (± V2) deficits, with variable pupil involvement depending on which fascicles/divisions of CN III are affected; the O-TOM-CAT anatomy explains why CN VI can be selectively spared or isolated (it floats free in the sinus body rather than sitting in the wall).

**[[Anchoring Bias]] (new exemplar)** — Berkowitz's explicit invocation of Kahneman's System 1 ("thinking fast": older woman + headache + diplopia → GCA) versus System 2 ("thinking slow": deliberate anatomic localization) framework. The fast pattern-match to GCA was reasonable and worth holding onto, but ultimately proved to be a red herring; the lesson is not to distrust fast pattern recognition, but to keep running the slow, systematic analysis in parallel rather than letting the fast hypothesis foreclose it.

**[[Base Rate]] (new exemplar)** — a second consecutive diplopia-batch episode (after [[CPS 379 - Sudden Diplopia and One-and-a-Half Syndrome from a Dorsal Pontine Stroke|CPS 379]]) in which Berkowitz volunteers that his personal yield on GCA screening is very low despite years of sending ESR/CRP on older patients with headache — reinforcing, with a cited JAMA rational-clinical-exam review, that the absence of jaw claudication and constitutional symptoms meaningfully lowers post-test probability even in a demographic where GCA is a reflexive "can't-miss."

**[[Diagnostic Humility]]** — trusting a compelling exam-based localization even when first-pass imaging and CSF studies are entirely normal; the eventual diagnosis was only radiographically confirmed on a *second* contrast MRI, during a recurrence, and even then the enhancement was described as subtle enough that a less experienced reader might miss it.

## Teaching Discussion

- **O-TOM-CAT** as a memory aid for cavernous sinus contents: the lateral wall carries CN **O**culomotor (III), **T**rochlear (IV), **O**phthalmic (V1), and **M**axillary (V2) divisions of the trigeminal nerve; the internal **C**arotid **A**rtery and CN abducens (VI) — no**T** in the wall — float within the body of the sinus itself. This explains why an isolated CN VI palsy can occasionally occur without other cavernous sinus signs, while III/IV/V1/V2 involvement tends to travel together.
- The cavernous sinus is one of the few places in the body where an artery (the internal carotid) runs *inside* a venous structure.
- **Multiple cranial neuropathy localization schema**, reinforced again in this episode (see also [[CPS 428 - Diplopia, Fever, and Neuro-Behcet's Disease|CPS 428]] and [[CPS 419 - Headache, Painful Blurry Vision, and Giant Cell Arteritis with CNS Vasculitis|CPS 419]]): once several cranial nerves are involved together, ask whether they localize to the brainstem, the subarachnoid space, the skull base (including the cavernous sinus), the orbit, or whether this is a neuromuscular "fakeout" (myasthenia gravis, Guillain-Barré/Miller Fisher variant) that mimics multiple cranial neuropathies without a discrete structural lesion.
- **Pupil-sparing versus pupil-involving CN III palsy** — the classic teaching (compressive lesions hit the peripherally-located pupillomotor fibers first; ischemic lesions spare the nerve's periphery and thus the pupil) is a useful heuristic but not absolute: a cavernous sinus lesion confined to the nerve's superior division can spare the pupil despite being genuinely structural, because the pupillomotor fibers are believed to travel in the inferior division at that point in the nerve's course.
- **Headache framework**: three buckets of secondary headache causes (intracranial — brain/meninges/vasculature/ventricles; head-and-neck — refractive error, dental disease, sinus disease, cervical disc disease; systemic — viral illness, post-vaccination, etc.); red flags simplified to headache *characteristics* (pace of onset, waking the patient from sleep) and *context* (immunocompromise, malignancy history, age, pregnancy).
- **Trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias** (cluster and its relatives) were considered and downgraded given the absence of ipsilateral autonomic features (lacrimation, rhinorrhea) — but the discussion noted these rare primary headache disorders can themselves rarely be secondary to pituitary or cavernous sinus pathology, since V1 passes directly through that region, which is part of why imaging is still obtained even when a TAC is suspected.
- **Pre-steroid infectious risk mitigation**: before high-dose corticosteroids for a presumed inflammatory process, screen for or empirically treat tuberculosis (PPD/IGRA) and *Strongyloides* (empiric ivermectin) to avoid precipitating disseminated infection — a practice the discussion confirmed is consistent between US and Myanmar clinical settings.
- **IV versus oral steroids in Tolosa-Hunt**: an anecdote from a colleague's case in which an oral steroid trial produced only partial, then worsening symptoms, while IV methylprednisolone (on subspecialist advice) produced prompt resolution — suggesting IV dosing may be preferable when the diagnosis is strongly suspected and rapid, convincing resolution is itself diagnostically useful.
- A tribute to the late Dr. Marty Samuels, whose extensive bedside neurologic examination teaching (including the practice of examining the heart and carotids as part of every neurologic exam) continues to be studied by trainees internationally.
