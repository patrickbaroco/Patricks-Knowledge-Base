## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 72-year-old man with paroxysmal left flank pain and a remote asymptomatic kidney stone turns out to have unintentional weight loss, a markedly elevated white count with bands and monocytosis, and an obstructing ureteral stone causing AKI on a solitary-nephron-equivalent kidney. The discussant's [[Five Ps of Intrinsic Renal Disease]] schema and a chronic, subacute [[Tumor Lysis Syndrome]] physiology point to an underlying myeloproliferative neoplasm — ultimately labeled BCR-ABL-negative, JAK2-positive CML-pattern disease — with a late twist of spurious hyperkalemia from in vitro cell lysis.

## Pearls

- When a patient says "flank" or "kidney" pain, first distinguish a deep, movement-independent process (likely renal or retroperitoneal) from a superficial, mechanical, movement-worsened process (likely musculoskeletal back pain) — most reported "flank pain" is actually the latter.
- Flank pain is not just kidney and back: the lower lobe/pleura, the adrenal gland, the pelvic organs (including ovarian torsion), the aorta, and the spleen can all present as flank pain, and a subacute systemic tail (weight loss, fatigue) to an otherwise classic colicky story should prompt consideration of these alternatives.
- A remote, asymptomatic kidney stone does not predict future symptoms — kidney stone disease behaves like gallstone disease, where most stones sit quietly and only a subset ever cause obstruction and pain.
- On day one of unexplained intrinsic renal disease, run through the [[Five Ps of Intrinsic Renal Disease]] — pigment, paraprotein, platelets (MAHA/TMA), pharmacy, and pointy things (crystalline disease) — before defaulting to the glomerular/tubular/vascular schema.
- Classic tumor lysis syndrome's biochemical tetrad (hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, hyperuricemia) reflects an *acute*, high-volume cell-lysis event; the same underlying process — cellular contents including uric acid overwhelming the kidney — can occur chronically and subacutely (as in a myeloproliferative neoplasm), producing an incomplete biochemical picture that should not be used to exclude the diagnosis.
- Listening carefully in the first five minutes of a patient encounter — and ordering a complete history, exam, and basic labs before anchoring on the most convenient diagnosis (a "classic" kidney stone) — is what surfaces the systemic disease hiding behind a routine-sounding complaint; the same lesson applies to a new compression fracture that deserves a calcium/phosphate/PTH workup before being written off as simple osteoporosis.

## Problem Representation

A 72-year-old man with a remote, asymptomatic kidney stone and several months of unintentional weight loss and fatigue presents with paroxysmal left flank pain and dysuria; marked leukocytosis with bands and monocytosis, an acute kidney injury, splenomegaly, hyperuricemia, and an obstructing left ureteral stone in a functionally solitary kidney together localize the process to a chronic myeloproliferative neoplasm with subacute urate nephropathy and stone disease, precipitated into an obstructing acute presentation.

## Case Narrative

A 72-year-old man presents to the emergency department with severe, paroxysmal left flank pain lasting minutes at a time, recurring repeatedly, severe enough that he wants to stay completely still; he also reports dysuria without suprapubic pain, chest pain, or shortness of breath. Further history reveals unintentional weight loss of about 30 pounds over the past year (160 to 130 pounds), increasing fatigue, and a kidney stone diagnosed about a year earlier that was never symptomatic.

The discussant opens three threads: how to approach flank pain in general, how the systemic symptoms complicate that approach, and what a history of an asymptomatic kidney stone means. Practically, "flank" pain is first triaged into deep (kidney) versus superficial (paraspinal muscle/back) by asking whether it is mechanical — worse with movement, leaning, or position — in which case a back/musculoskeletal source is favored, or independent of movement, in which case a deep (renal) source is favored. Beyond back and kidney, the differential for flank pain also includes the lower lobe/pleura, the adrenal gland, the pelvic organs (a VMR case of ovarian torsion presenting as abrupt flank pain is cited), the aorta, and the spleen. The absence of any reported movement-related component favors a kidney source, and the paroxysmal quality favors a stone specifically — but the subacute systemic tail (weight loss) keeps the broader differential open in the background.

Because roughly 20–30% of patients imaged for a symptomatic stone have an unrelated, asymptomatic contralateral stone, a stone only causes symptoms when it obstructs — kidney stone disease is analogized to gallstone disease, where most stones sit quietly. The initial operating hypothesis is simplified: could chronic weight loss have led to hypercalcemia, which produced first non-occlusive and now an occlusive stone? If that hypothesis and a plain stone hypothesis both fail to bear out, the discussant plans to return to the full flank-pain differential.

On exam: flank pain with CVA tenderness, thin appearance, mild tachycardia, afebrile, normal blood pressure. Management approach: IV/IM ketorolac for pain; because the presentation is atypical (a yet-to-be-explained systemic wasting syndrome, plus dysuria raising concern for superimposed infection), the plan is full labs plus a contrast CT rather than the usual UA-and-non-contrast-CT pathway reserved for a classic stone story (chosen because up to 30% of patients with a classic kidney stone lack hematuria). A radiologist's rule of thumb is cited: giving IV contrast reduces CT sensitivity for significant obstructing stones from about 98% (non-contrast) to about 95% (with contrast) — a small enough tradeoff that contrast is used directly rather than obtaining sequential non-contrast-then-contrast phases and doubling radiation exposure.

Labs return striking: CBC shows hemoglobin 8.9 (MCV 99), white count 102,000 with 74% neutrophils and 14% bands, platelets 526,000; a CBC from a year earlier already showed a low-normal white count of 7 with a monocytosis of 2,000, hemoglobin 10.2 (MCV 100), and platelets 280,000. Chemistry shows creatinine 4.47 (BUN 38) against a baseline of 1 a year earlier, bicarbonate 24, potassium 4.1, anion gap 10, phosphorus 3.2, calcium 9.4 (normal). UA shows many white and red cells, positive leukocyte esterase, negative nitrites, and large occult blood. CT shows splenomegaly (13 cm), a 6 mm obstructing left ureteral stone with hydronephrosis and hydroureter, a 3–4 mm asymptomatic contralateral stone, and multiple bladder stones. Uric acid returns at 14.7.

The extreme leukocytosis with bands, combined with a subacute rather than fulminant presentation and a background of chronic monocytosis, argues against an acute bacterial process and toward a signature of chronicity — splenomegaly plus a high burden of kidney stones. The stones are hypothesized to be uric acid stones, reflecting high cell turnover from an underlying hematologic process; this is explicitly framed as a slow-motion version of tumor lysis syndrome — the same pathophysiology as acute chemotherapy-induced tumor lysis (cellular contents, here predominantly uric acid, overwhelming the kidney) but stretched over months rather than days, allowing time for urate crystals to enlarge and precipitate as macroscopic stones in the ureter and bladder rather than causing acute tubular obstruction.

Because the contralateral kidney is not obstructed, the patient should retain at least 50% of his nephrons, arguing against a purely post-renal (obstructive) explanation for the AKI and toward an intrinsic renal process. Rather than the standard glomerular/tubular/vascular schema, the discussant applies a practical "five Ps" framework for intrinsic renal disease on day one: pigment (hemolysis, rhabdomyolysis — not supported here), paraprotein (a hematologic fingerprint — worth pursuing), platelets (MAHA/TMA — excluded by the high, not low, platelet count), pharmacy (drug-induced interstitial nephritis, including immunotherapy — not applicable), and pointy things (crystalline disease — the same calcium oxalate or urate crystals causing macroscopic stones can also deposit microscopically as nephrocalcinosis or urate nephropathy in the tubules).

Synthesizing the signature — splenomegaly, urate elevation clogging the kidney macroscopically and microscopically, and a hematologic-appearing CBC — the working diagnosis becomes a myeloid neoplasm, mentally modeled along two axes: acute versus chronic, and myeloid versus lymphoid. Predominantly myeloid cells (neutrophils, bands, monocytes) together with a pre-existing chronic monocytosis favor a chronic myeloid leukemia-type syndrome or a myeloproliferative neoplasm that has now accelerated, with the bone marrow as the center of gravity for the disease.

Tumor lysis physiology is generalized: acute cell lysis releases toxic intracellular contents specific to the process — uric acid in tumor lysis, CK in rhabdomyolysis, hemoglobin in hemolysis — and all of these processes have subacute or chronic analogues (patients with polycythemia vera have higher rates of gout and low-grade kidney dysfunction from chronic urate elevation) alongside their classic acute forms. Because a white count this high implies substantial ongoing cell turnover and death, and because the AKI itself would independently reduce uric acid clearance, the relationship between the AKI and the hyperuricemia is judged bidirectional; the presence of urate-type kidney stones is used as a proxy for elevated urinary uric acid concentration, strengthening the case that uric acid is being actively excreted (and precipitating) in the kidney, not merely accumulating in the blood because of reduced clearance. Absence of the other classic tumor lysis findings (normal potassium, calcium, and near-normal phosphate) is explicitly reframed not as evidence against tumor lysis, but as evidence that this is a slower, incomplete version of the same physiology.

On the obstruction question: hydronephrosis on CT tells the urologist only that a given stone is symptomatic (obstructing), not how urgently it needs intervention; stone size, not the presence of hydronephrosis itself, predicts spontaneous passage — stones under 10 mm are likely to pass with medical expulsive therapy and observation. In an otherwise healthy patient this stone (6 mm) would typically not even warrant a urology referral. Here, however, the reasoning changes because the contralateral kidney is compromised by the underlying disease process (not truly "normal" reserve) — analogous to a patient with a prior lobectomy developing pneumonia in the remaining lung — so every functioning nephron matters, and urology is consulted urgently.

Urology places a left ureteral stent; with fluids, creatinine improves from 4 to the mid-2s and the white count falls from 100,000 to 80,000; empiric ceftriaxone is discontinued after negative urine cultures. Peripheral smear shows marked leukocytosis, left shift, monocytosis, and thrombocytosis without blasts or schistocytes. BCR-ABL fusion, JAK2, MPL, and CALR testing are sent; rasburicase is given and uric acid becomes undetectable the next day. Hematology-oncology favors a chronic process, most likely CML, noting that the elevated cell turnover (and resulting uric acid) requires emergent treatment while the underlying chronic disease itself does not, and the patient is discharged pending further outpatient workup.

He returns the next day with fatigue: creatinine 2.5, but potassium now 6.2 despite an undetectable uric acid, phosphate rising slightly (5–6 from a baseline of 3), and JAK2 V617F now positive at 47% (reference under 1%). A plasma potassium sent to investigate the surprising hyperkalemia is normal, confirming spurious (in vitro) hyperkalemia from ongoing cell lysis in the collection tube rather than true hyperkalemia. Bone marrow biopsy shows trilineage maturing hematopoiesis with increased megakaryocytes, no excess blasts, no fibrosis, and no dysplasia; the clinical team ultimately frames the diagnosis as BCR-ABL-negative, JAK2-positive CML-pattern disease, starts hydroxyurea while awaiting confirmatory results, and plans a JAK2 inhibitor once JAK2 positivity is confirmed — complicated by subsequent severe infections requiring ICU-level care, attributed to functionally impaired white blood cells.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The discussant explicitly narrates his own real-time error and self-correction around the tumor lysis triad/tetrad: initially reasoning that a normal potassium, calcium, and phosphate argued against tumor lysis syndrome, then recognizing that a syndrome classically taught as an acute biochemical tetrad can present incompletely when the same physiology unfolds subacutely — a direct parallel to the [[Susac Syndrome]] and Wernicke's-encephalopathy triad lessons about not requiring every classic feature before accepting a diagnosis.

A remote, asymptomatic kidney stone is explicitly used to recalibrate the pretest probability that the current pain represents a new, unrelated event rather than an extension of known disease — an application of [[Base Rate]] reasoning to a single organ's stone burden, analogized directly to gallstone disease.

The [[Five Ps of Intrinsic Renal Disease]] schema (pigment, paraprotein, platelets, pharmacy, pointy things) is offered explicitly as a more practical alternative to the traditional glomerular/tubular/vascular framework for approaching intrinsic AKI on "day one," before more specific data are available — an example of substituting a fast, pattern-matching heuristic for a slower, more exhaustive schema when time and information are limited.

## Teaching Discussion

Roughly 20–30% of patients imaged for a symptomatic kidney stone have an unrelated, asymptomatic contralateral stone; because a stone only produces symptoms when it obstructs, a history of a prior asymptomatic stone should not be assumed to predict future symptomatic events, nor should it be dismissed as irrelevant to a new presentation.

Contrast CT reduces sensitivity for clinically significant obstructing stones only marginally (from roughly 98% non-contrast to 95% with contrast), so in atypical presentations where contrast adds diagnostic value elsewhere (here, for possible infection or a systemic process), a single contrast-enhanced study is preferred over sequential non-contrast-then-contrast imaging, which doubles radiation exposure for minimal added stone sensitivity.

Stone size, not the presence of hydronephrosis, is the key predictor of spontaneous passage: hydronephrosis only confirms that a stone is obstructing (and therefore symptomatic); stones under 10 mm are managed with medical expulsive therapy and observation in most patients, while urgency of urologic intervention should escalate sharply whenever a stone causes AKI, particularly in a patient with reduced nephron reserve from an unrelated process.

Tumor lysis physiology generalizes beyond its classic acute chemotherapy context: any process that releases large quantities of a cell's toxic intracellular contents (uric acid in tumor lysis, CK in rhabdomyolysis, hemoglobin in hemolysis) can injure the kidney, and each of these processes has a chronic or subacute analogue (e.g., chronic urate nephropathy and gout in polycythemia vera) in addition to its classic acute presentation.

A sudden, unexplained hyperkalemia result should prompt two questions: is it real (checked via a plasma or point-of-care potassium, which avoid the tourniquet-phenomenon and in vitro cell lysis artifacts of a standard serum draw, particularly relevant in a patient with a very high white count), and, if real, could it reflect adrenal insufficiency.

The four core myeloproliferative neoplasms can be organized against the CBC itself as a memory device: chronic myeloid leukemia against the white count, polycythemia vera against the hemoglobin, essential thrombocythemia against the platelet count, and myelofibrosis (often a secondary progression of one of the other three) against the hematocrit; the four corresponding confirmatory mutations are BCR-ABL (100% specific for CML) and JAK2, MPL, and calreticulin (CALR) for polycythemia vera, essential thrombocythemia, and myelofibrosis, with bone marrow biopsy required for confirmation in all but CML and polycythemia vera.
