## Pearls

- Rigors ("chills on steroids" — visible shaking, distinct from ordinary chills) sharply raise the positive predictive value for transient bacteremia; in a hospitalized patient, a repeat fever alone doesn't necessarily warrant recollecting blood cultures, but a repeat fever accompanied by new rigors does.
- When working up acute fever, translate "fever" to "inflammation" and ask two questions: where is it happening, and what is driving it. Most acute-onset (day-scale) fevers land within one of a small set of organ systems (the "infection 1.0" schema — pulmonary, GI, genitourinary, skin/soft tissue), which can be screened quickly through targeted history and exam.
- A well-appearing patient with fever is not automatically reassuring — the value of "looking fine" depends heavily on immune status, since an immunocompromised host may lack the machinery to mount visible signs of infection even when significantly infected.
- "Draw and hold": when there's genuine uncertainty about whether to pursue a bacterial workup, blood cultures can be drawn and held rather than sent to the lab immediately — deferring the decision without requiring a second venipuncture if the picture worsens. Whether to send (and eventually treat) should be tied specifically to whether you're already inclined to give empiric antibiotics.
- Nearly everyone experiences brief, self-cleared "ambient bacteremia" from ordinary mucosal activity (brushing teeth, bowel movements); true pathologic bacteremia usually requires either a compromised immune system, a foreign body (catheter, prosthetic valve or joint), IV drug use, or a skin/mucosal breach that lets transient organisms stick rather than being cleared.
- Literature cited on air suggests roughly 50-70% of patients manifesting true rigors have underlying bacteremia — a strong argument for prompt blood cultures and consideration of empiric antibiotics — though the classic teaching that rigors specifically predict gram-negative (endotoxin-mediated) bacteremia is only a weak, probabilistic association, not a reliable rule.
- "Evolution > CT": in a host with an intact immune system capable of generating localizing symptoms, the absence of pulmonary or abdominal complaints (plus normal liver chemistries) is a more reliable early guide than reflexive cross-sectional imaging — chest CT and pan-CT scanning are better reserved for a true fever-of-unknown-origin evaluation (weeks of unexplained fever) than an acute 48-hour presentation.
- When bacteremia is found without an obvious portal of entry, the differential for the hidden source can be organized by the identified organism: Staph aureus raises endocarditis, septic thrombophlebitis, osteomyelitis, or epidural abscess; a Klebsiella-type organism without urinary symptoms raises an occult GI source, including malignancy breaching the mucosal barrier.
- Not every exogenous, environmentally-acquired organism is "atypical" in the intracellular/rickettsial/spirochete sense — non-commensal organisms like Pseudomonas and Vibrio species can cause straightforward bacteremia purely through environmental exposure (soil, seafood) rather than through breach of a normal host-flora reservoir, and classic host risk factors (e.g., cirrhosis for Vibrio vulnificus) aren't always present; sometimes a large exposure inoculum alone is sufficient.

## Problem Representation

A man in his 70s with well-controlled hypertension and hyperlipidemia presents to the VA emergency department with two days of fever and chills, found on exam to have observed shaking rigors, sinus tachycardia, and an otherwise reassuring physical exam without localizing symptoms — ultimately found, after an unrevealing localizing workup, to have Vibrio vulnificus bacteremia traced to seafood consumption four days prior in New Orleans, and treated successfully with intravenous antibiotics without ever identifying a structural portal of entry.

## Case Narrative

Robbie presents a case he didn't witness firsthand but reconstructs for Reza to reason through blind: a man in his 70s checks into the VA emergency department with two days of fever and chills. Reza opens with a framing nugget before diving in — what's usually called "fever" really means an objectively elevated temperature, which is worth translating specifically to inflammation rather than assuming automatically; occasionally a temperature rise is metabolic (hyperthermia) rather than cytokine-driven, though that distinction isn't the focus here. He then draws a sharper distinction within "chills": ordinary chills versus rigors, which he describes as chills on steroids — visible, involuntary shaking. The distinction matters practically, because rigors substantially raise the positive predictive value for transient bacteremia; in a hospitalized patient who spikes a second fever days later, a rigor (not just the fever itself) is what should prompt reflex blood recultures. With chills set aside for the moment, Reza reduces the case to two questions: where is the inflammation happening, and what is driving it. Given the two-day, acute time course, bacterial and viral causes both rise to the top of the differential.

Robbie fills in the history: the patient is uncertain whether he's actually had prior shaking episodes, has no respiratory, GI, urinary, or skin symptoms, and arrives with a wife able to corroborate. Triage vitals show a temperature of 102°F, heart rate 110, normal blood pressure, and a patient who appears comfortable and is breathing well; a thorough head-to-toe exam is entirely normal. Reza likens this to a radiographic finding without a matching clinical picture — an objective abnormality (the fever) without a clear consistent syndrome — and introduces his organ-system screen for acute fever, which he calls "infection 1.0": walk through the lungs, GI tract, genitourinary tract, and skin/soft tissue, since roughly 98% of acute-onset fevers will localize to one of these systems once directly asked about. Because none of them are positive here, Reza turns to host vulnerability as the deciding factor in how reassuring a well-appearing patient actually is — immune status (transplant history, immunosuppressive therapy, HIV, diabetes, cirrhosis) determines whether "looking fine" is meaningful, since a host with an intact immune system needs functioning immune machinery just to manifest the symptoms of infection in the first place. He frames the general principle that most people with an isolated fever and no other symptoms never come to a hospital at all — but this patient did, which by itself warrants a baseline workup, even though it doesn't yet justify aggressive intervention. His proposed initial battery: a careful cardiac exam (to exclude a murmur suggesting subacute endocarditis), a mucosal exam if the patient turns out to be immunocompromised (looking for HSV ulcers), a CBC, liver chemistries, a basic metabolic panel, and blood cultures — collected, but not necessarily sent or treated yet.

Robbie turns this into an explicit teaching he credits to two mentors, Mike and John: "draw and hold." When there's genuine diagnostic uncertainty, blood cultures can be drawn immediately and held at the bedside or lab rather than sent for processing right away — if the clinical picture later worsens (a rising white count, a positive flu swab ruled out), the held bottles can simply be sent without a second venipuncture. The rule he proposes for when to actually send cultures and commit to treatment: order blood cultures only when you're already inclined to give empiric antibiotics; if you're not yet inclined to treat but remain uncertain, draw and hold instead of skipping cultures altogether. Reza endorses this as considerate, patient-centered practice, and confirms that for this patient specifically, he'd draw and hold rather than treat empirically at this point.

Robbie runs a quick simulated branch to stress-test the reasoning: if an instant influenza swab came back positive, Reza would treat as a straightforward viral illness (empiric oseltamivir, no antibiotics, no blood cultures) and discharge. Extending the hypothetical to how long he'd be willing to wait for that same result — instantly is fine, but the comfort threshold shrinks sharply for a higher-risk host (a 70-year-old with a mechanical valve, a prosthetic joint, or an active transplant on immunosuppression), where the differential broadens and waiting becomes riskier. The exercise underscores that syndrome probability is the same regardless of host, but host vulnerability and syndrome severity determine how aggressively to act on it.

Back in the real timeline, roughly ten to fifteen minutes into the visit, a nurse flags that the patient isn't looking well, and the physician returns to find him having observed shaking rigors. Asked how this changes his plan, Reza would now draw and send blood cultures immediately. He walks through the physiology in real time: rigors represent the body's most intense shivering response, generating heat to chase a pathologically elevated hypothalamic temperature set point — something was previously hidden in the bloodstream and is now fully declaring itself. He also flags a quick safety check that shaking chills deserve: confirming the movement is genuinely a rigor and not a seizure or myoclonus, and that mental status remains clear. On whether to start empiric antibiotics immediately, Reza reasons out loud that stable vitals alone wouldn't force his hand, but a febrile, tachycardic, visibly rigoring patient makes early empiric coverage reasonable — targeting broad gram-positive and gram-negative organisms, with MRSA, Pseudomonas, or ESBL coverage added depending on any prior hospital exposure.

Robbie supplies supporting data: studies cited put the probability of bacteremia at roughly 50-70% in patients manifesting true rigors — a strong argument for empiric IV antibiotics. He also poses the counterargument as a two-sided debate: the patient has already tolerated 48 hours of untreated illness without decompensating, so how much incremental risk does one more hour (waiting on the flu swab) really carry? In the actual case, the patient was started on empiric vancomycin and ceftriaxone right around the time the rigors were witnessed. Results return over the following hour: a negative respiratory viral panel, normal renal function and liver chemistries, a normal lactate, a negative urinalysis, a negative chest x-ray, an EKG showing sinus tachycardia, and a white blood cell count of 17,000 with neutrophil predominance.

Reza contextualizes the leukocytosis carefully rather than treating it as diagnostic on its own: a neutrophil-predominant white count reflects a generic physiologic stress response (cortisol and catecholamine release, demargination of neutrophils from the endothelium), not a confirmed bacterial etiology by itself — he notes that most people who get a viral fever at home never have a CBC drawn at all, so the true background rate of neutrophil-predominant leukocytosis in ordinary viral illness is likely underappreciated. Between the patient's age (over 70, itself a marker of some degree of immunosenescence), the witnessed rigors, and the leukocytosis, the team has effectively already committed to inpatient admission and antibiotics; a rapid PCR-based panel run directly on the blood sample should return an organism identification within hours, which will reshape the differential once available.

Robbie then probes the limits of the workup — specifically, how far Reza would push cross-sectional imaging of the chest and abdomen. Reza, citing a mentor he calls Uncle Bob, reiterates that pneumonia is fundamentally a clinical diagnosis: with no cough and no dyspnea, the pretest probability for pneumonia is low, and while a plain chest x-ray can miss x-ray-negative pneumonia, he wouldn't yet pursue a CT chest at only two days of symptoms — that test is better reserved for a true fever-of-unknown-origin evaluation, once fever has persisted for weeks and warranted cross-sectional imaging independent of symptoms. The same logic applies to the abdomen: with no abdominal symptoms and normal liver chemistries (which would flag a hepatobiliary process like a hepatic abscess), he wouldn't pursue a pan-CT scan on presentation, despite the natural hospital reflex to "assume the worst" once a patient is admitted. Robbie distills this into a portable principle: "evolution > CT" — millions of years of the body evolving the ability to localize a problem for the host, in someone whose immune system remains capable of generating local signs, is a better initial guide than reflexive imaging.

No scans are obtained. At twenty-four hours, both blood culture bottles turn positive for gram-negative rods with an unusual curved morphology. Robbie poses the case's central puzzle: how can someone be bacteremic with no identifiable portal of entry — no pneumonia, no UTI, no skin break, nothing? Reza first notes that while rigors are classically taught to specifically predict gram-negative, endotoxin-mediated bacteremia, that "rule" doesn't hold up cleanly in practice — gram-positive bacteremia can also produce rigors, and gram-negative bacteremia can occur without them, so it's a probabilistic association rather than a reliable discriminator. Returning to the "ambient bacteremia" concept from earlier — that everyone transiently seeds their bloodstream through ordinary activities like brushing teeth or having a bowel movement — Reza reframes the real question as whether this patient has some additional vulnerability letting a transient bacteremia stick somewhere instead of being cleared. He works through candidate anatomic niches contingent on the eventual organism: if it turns out to be Staph aureus, he'd immediately think about endocarditis, septic thrombophlebitis, osteomyelitis, or an epidural abscess, and specifically re-ask about previously unmentioned back pain; if it's a Klebsiella-type organism without urinary symptoms, he'd wonder about an occult gastrointestinal source and ask about colonoscopy history, since a GI malignancy can breach the mucosal barrier and seed the bloodstream. With no IV, no Foley catheter, no visible skin breakdown, and no localizing symptoms anywhere, Reza voices real concern that some hidden internal vulnerability — either an occult immune compromise or an occult mucosal malignancy, most likely gastrointestinal — will ultimately explain the bacteremia.

Robbie affirms that Reza is intuitively reasoning about structural vulnerability rather than reaching for a vague, diffuse immune explanation, and then supplies the piece of history that reframes everything: about four days before presenting, the patient had returned from a trip to New Orleans where he ate a large amount of seafood. Reza works the organism out loud in real time: a curved gram-negative rod plus a seafood exposure points toward the Vibrio family, and he lands on Vibrio vulnificus specifically (with a brief aside distinguishing it from Vibrio cholerae). Robbie confirms the diagnosis and adds an important caveat: classic Vibrio vulnificus bacteremia is typically described in patients with cirrhosis or heavy alcohol use, related to iron-overload susceptibility — this patient had neither. The likelier explanation here is simply an unusually large inoculum from the seafood exposure itself, rather than an occult host vulnerability, which means Reza's structural-vulnerability search — while the correct instinct in general — doesn't always turn something up, because sometimes the true explanation really is exposure alone. Robbie generalizes the broader lesson: he'd long implicitly equated "exogenous" organisms with "atypical" ones (intracellular organisms like Rickettsia or spirochetes), but cases like this — and organisms like Pseudomonas, which isn't part of normal human flora either — show there's an entire category of non-commensal, environmentally-acquired organisms capable of causing straightforward bacteremia purely through exposure, without needing to invoke either host structural compromise or an "atypical" intracellular mechanism.

The patient's antibiotics are narrowed to ceftriaxone and later transitioned to an oral quinolone; he has an uncomplicated course and is discharged within two to three days. Closing the episode, Reza turns the question back on Robbie: how would he counsel a friend or family member who called with two days of isolated fever and no other symptoms? Robbie says he'd likely just recommend a home flu or COVID swab — and points out that although bacteremia is taught as an emergency, this particular patient tolerated it for a full 48 hours without any objective marker of instability (no elevated lactate, no renal dysfunction, no change in mental status), so how urgent it truly is in an otherwise young, healthy person remains genuinely uncertain; he notes, half-jokingly, that humanity coexisted with Vibrio species for millennia before modern antibiotics existed. He distills a practical heuristic for whether reported rigors at home should prompt a trip in for blood cultures: weigh how much physiologic room for error the person has against how inconvenient presenting for care would be for them. A frail patient with little reserve should have a low threshold to come in and be cultured regardless of inconvenience; an otherwise healthy person with a full life and substantial room for error can reasonably be allowed to let the illness evolve further before seeking care.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The episode gently corrects a folk teaching that's often absorbed as a hard rule: that rigors specifically predict gram-negative, endotoxin-mediated bacteremia. Reza's real-time correction — gram-positive bacteremia can produce rigors too, and gram-negative bacteremia can occur without them — reframes it as a probabilistic association rather than a discriminating sign, a useful caution against over-trusting pattern-matching heuristics absorbed early in training. The case also plays with anchoring in the opposite direction from usual: once bacteremia is confirmed without an obvious portal of entry, the natural instinct is to reach for an exotic or structurally complex explanation (an occult malignancy, a hidden valvular lesion), and Reza's reasoning down that path is validated as sound methodology — but the actual answer turns out to be simpler (an environmental exposure with a large inoculum), a reminder that a thorough differential doesn't guarantee an exotic answer. Finally, Robbie's own admission that he had implicitly equated "exogenous" with "atypical" organisms is a direct, self-aware illness-script correction, and one he explicitly connects back to the Pasteurella multocida case from 158.

## Teaching Discussion

This episode is built as a deliberately slow, stepwise progression from an abstract, low-information febrile patient to a fully characterized bacteremia case, and its greatest value is in the practice-changing details along the way rather than in the eventual diagnosis alone. The "draw and hold" blood-culture discipline is a concrete, immediately actionable habit that reduces unnecessary venipuncture without sacrificing diagnostic readiness, and tying the decision to send cultures (and eventually treat) specifically to whether empiric antibiotics are already being considered gives trainees a clean decision rule rather than a vague gestalt. "Evolution > CT" is a portable heuristic in the same spirit as the show's other imaging-restraint teachings — useful specifically because it's bounded (an intact immune system, an acute rather than prolonged fever) rather than a blanket argument against imaging.

The back half of the case — reasoning about cryptogenic bacteremia by organism identity, and then discovering that the real explanation was simpler than the structural-vulnerability hypotheses generated — reinforces a theme the show has returned to repeatedly: a rigorous differential is valuable even when the ultimately correct answer turns out not to need it. The closing reflection, where Robbie questions whether the emergent treatment this patient received was strictly necessary given his stability throughout, ties directly into the show's ongoing "meta-skills of practice" and decision-validity-independent-of-outcome threads, and is a good discussion prompt for how much true equipoise exists around treating incidentally discovered bacteremia in stable, low-risk hosts.
