> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A man in his 70s with schizophrenia and newly diagnosed [[heart failure with reduced ejection fraction|HFrEF]] (EF <20%), hospitalized a month and profoundly deconditioned, is paged for as a chronically "soft" blood pressure — systolics in the 80s–90s for weeks — that everyone has come to accept. Reza (presenting) reframes the encounter around the [[cardiovascular reserve|eat-walk-talk]] test and the single alarming finding: **[[jugular venous distension|jugular venous distension]]** signaling [[arterial hypotension with venous hypertension]] rather than benign low pressure. Over successive shifts the picture escalates — fatigue with exertion, no urine output, pallor, and a "flickering" neck. An EKG (obtained by the nurse) is initially read as [[Mobitz I|second-degree type I block]], then reinterpreted by cardiology as **[[atrial flutter]] with variable AV block** (flutter waves at ~300/min). Labs finally sent by an insistent nurse reveal **hemoglobin 6** (from 10), ferritin <50 ([[iron deficiency anemia|iron deficiency]], likely [[gastrointestinal bleeding|GI bleed]]), creatinine 2.2, and **troponin 2000**. The unifying cascade: [[anemia]] → [[atrial flutter]] → loss of the [[atrial kick]] in a stiff, low-EF heart → [[normotensive cardiogenic shock|decompensation and hypotension]]. The episode's spine is humility ([[knowing what you don't know]], asking for help on the EKG) and the nurse as the true hero.

**Pearls**

- **A blood pressure is only a number until the patient and the rest of the vitals give it meaning.** 80/50 may be benign physiology or a catastrophe; the patient's symptoms, heart rate, and the *venous* pressure decide which.
- **Solving hypotension means measuring two pressures, not one.** Benign hypotension (sepsis, hypovolemia, anaphylaxis) is soft arterially *and* venously; [[cardiogenic shock|cardiogenic]] and [[obstructive shock|obstructive]] hypotension pair **low arterial with high venous** pressure — so [[jugular venous distension|JVD]] transforms "benign low BP" into an alarm.
- **"Eat, walk, talk" is the discharge-from-the-bedside test.** A patient comfortable at rest is in primary prevention, but the enormous [[cardiovascular reserve]] hides smoldering disease — the *walk* unmasks whether the process is static or evolving.
- **Listed ≠ taken.** Reconcile what the patient actually swallows: this man self-stopped his losartan and empagliflozin but never skipped his diuretic — reshaping the whole GDMT calculus.
- **[[Atrial flutter]] with variable block is *physiologic* AV block.** At an atrial rate of ~300, dropped beats are the AV node *protecting* the ventricle, not disease — you cannot call pathologic block at flutter rates.
- **The [[atrial kick]] is life-or-death in a low-EF heart.** With EF 20% and a plummeting stroke volume, losing atrial-ventricular synchrony (flutter) drops cardiac output enough to tip a fragile patient into shock — because BP = [[cardiac output]] × [[systemic vascular resistance|SVR]].
- **[[Troponin]] is a superb *negative* predictive test for a stressed heart**, far beyond ACS — a normal trope buys reassurance; a positive one, in ESRD or demand, still means "there's work to do," so don't be afraid to interpret it.

**Problem Representation**
An elderly man with HFrEF (EF <20%) and chronic borderline hypotension develops progressive fatigue, oliguria, pallor, and jugular venous distension, found to have new atrial flutter with variable AV block precipitated by severe iron-deficiency anemia (likely GI bleed), causing loss of atrial kick and cardiogenic decompensation.

## Case Narrative

Reza presents the case exactly as it unfolded across several shifts, insisting from the outset that **the hero is not him — it's the nurse**. At 2 a.m. on a night shift he is paged that a patient has a blood pressure of 80/50. Asked for his instinct, Rabih refuses to react to the number in isolation: every encounter begins with a chief concern, and 80/50 could be normal physiology or a catastrophe depending on the patient's response and the surrounding vitals — heart rate, temperature, respiratory rate. Right now it is "just a number and the context."

The context arrives. The patient is in his 70s with schizophrenia and a probate conservator, and a [[heart failure with reduced ejection fraction|HFrEF]] with EF <20% diagnosed months earlier; he has been hospitalized about a month, deconditioned and debilitated. Reza, who "learned early to always take hypotension seriously and go see the patient," first checks the trend: systolics have oscillated in the high-80s to mid-90s for a month, and a colleague had already built a thoughtful differential and reasonably concluded this was the new baseline, with the patient declining labs. At the bedside the man is awake, alert, fully conversational, using medical terminology (a prior medical-field profession). He feels "fine," denies lightheadedness, but reports a month of exertional dyspnea. Reza notes **flat-to-descended neck veins on first look**, moderate lower-extremity pitting edema with stasis dermatitis, and — crucially — recognizes that even a **pediatric cuff is oversized** for his nearly muscle-free arms; a reading on the other arm gives a systolic of 96, HR in the 70s, SpO₂ 100%. He lets the man sleep.

Rabih builds the framework. A patient comfortable at rest is in **primary prevention** — using the blood pressure to prevent future harm, not to fix an active problem — and the [[cardiovascular reserve|cardiovascular system has vast reserve]] (the same system that sleeps quietly can run a marathon). His rule for leaving the bedside: only walk away definitively if the patient can **eat, walk, and talk**. This man can eat and talk; whether he can *walk* is unknown, and that answer would profoundly change the differential — a few tachypneic, dizzy steps versus a full lap of the floor separates smoldering from static disease. Statistics favor "fine for another month," but the **red flag is the JVP**: all forms of *benign* hypotension carry soft arterial *and* venous pressures (sepsis, hypovolemia, anaphylaxis), whereas [[cardiogenic shock|cardiogenic]] and [[obstructive shock|obstructive]] causes pair low arterial with **high venous** pressure. He adds an honest caveat — [[jugular venous distension|JVD]] has **poor inter-observer reliability** — but because Reza has deliberately cultivated this exam skill, Rabih trusts the finding *more* than he would trust his own.

On the medications, Rabih notes these very GDMT agents (low-dose metoprolol, furosemide, losartan, empagliflozin — not the target doses, and no Entresto) are often *why* advanced-HF patients run soft pressures, and lays out the dilemma: if the softness is a **vascular** problem (over-diuresis, excess afterload reduction), dose-reduce them; but if the JVP is real and this is **venous hypertension**, the patient may need *more* diuretic and *more* GDMT. You cannot decide the next move while stuck in that limbo. Reza then reveals the reconciliation twist: the man **self-discontinued the losartan and empagliflozin**, takes the metoprolol only occasionally, but never misses his diuretic because of leg swelling — "exactly what I would have recommended."

The case escalates on a later cross-cover weekend. A page: **BP 76**. Reza recognizes the name, reflexively thinks "nothing to do," and reflects on habituation — how a systolic of 80 that would never be tolerated as *new* (no floor bed, no monitor) becomes invisible when constant, "like a necklace you stop feeling." But the **nurse insists** he come. At the bedside the man is again asymptomatic, yet the nurse supplies the decisive observations: he looked **extremely fatigued** struggling to the restroom, has made **no urine** this shift, and later — a **flickering in the neck**. Reza initially dismisses the flicker as JVD teaching, but agrees to look.

Rabih updates the frame. The patient's problem is **exertional intolerance** with objective low arterial pressure and persistent venous hypertension; because of its lethality he writes **[[normotensive cardiogenic shock]] in pencil** and tunnels toward it — the same discipline as writing "[[microangiopathic hemolytic anemia|MAHA]]" for new thrombocytopenia-plus-anemia — knowing 95% of [[cardiogenic shock]] has a low EF, so this patient is exactly the right host. The **flickering** he attributes to abnormal tricuspid motion: either [[tricuspid regurgitation]] or **AV dyssynchrony** (atria contracting against a closed tricuspid valve — [[cannon A waves]]), prompting a TR-murmur listen and an EKG to see whether atria and ventricles are talking. He would order essentially everything — CBC, renal and liver panels, type-and-screen for the pallor, BNP, **troponin**, EKG, chest x-ray, and here a **lactate** (which he reserves for exactly this degree of symptoms to catch normotensive cardiogenic shock). Reza notes the reassuringly **warm, well-perfused** skin, though its sensitivity against shock is uncertain.

The nurse obtains an EKG herself. Reza reads it as **second-degree heart block, type I** — progressive PR prolongation then a dropped beat — and Rabih reasons through the heart-block spectrum, flags that an EKG is a *snapshot* and block levels oscillate, and lists the acute bradycardia checklist (**meds, potassium, troponin**, plus vagal/urinary retention). On troponin he delivers the episode's most quotable teaching: a [[troponin]] is an **excellent negative predictive test** for a stressed heart well beyond ACS — a negative one lets you "sleep so much better," a positive one (even in ESRD or demand) simply means "you've got work to do" — and clinicians shouldn't fear interpreting it any more than a D-dimer.

Then the pivot to **humility**. Reza confesses the EKG "just didn't make sense," and rather than force it, he **texted the VA attending cardiologist and a fellow**. The fellow said "ectopic atrial rhythm with dropped beats"; Rabih, handed the tracing, walks through it aloud and lands on "not type I — either type II or complete heart block." **All wrong.** The attending's read: **[[atrial flutter]] with variable AV block** — flutter (F) waves marching at ~300/min — with a subsequent EKG showing a ventricular rate of 150. This reframes the neck flicker and, more importantly, the physiology.

The labs — extracted only because the nurse cajoled a thrice-visiting phlebotomy team and the patient into cooperating — complete the story: **hemoglobin 6** (from 10 two months prior), **ferritin <50** ([[iron deficiency anemia|iron deficiency]], most likely a [[gastrointestinal bleeding|GI bleed]]), **lactate normal**, **creatinine 2.2** (from 1.7), **troponin 2000**. The synthesis: a low-EF heart, stressed by [[anemia]], went into [[atrial flutter]]; the loss of the **[[atrial kick]]** — on which an EF-20% heart is dependent — collapsed stroke volume, and since **BP = [[cardiac output]] × SVR**, the patient became profoundly hypotensive. The chain is **anemia → arrhythmia → decompensation**, and cardiology's guidance is to seek reversible causes, **anticoagulate**, and **reduce afterload** (an atrial-kick-dependent EF-20% heart cannot tolerate the loss of synchrony). The patient — frail, with a failing heart — is ultimately transitioned toward **goals-of-care/hospice** rather than aggressive further workup.

Rabih closes with the clarifying insight he was chewing on the whole time: at an atrial rate of **300**, dropped beats are not pathology — they are the AV node **physiologically protecting** the ventricle from a lethal 300-bpm response. "Atrial flutter with variable block" *is* a form of AV block, but a **smart, physiologic** one; you cannot diagnose *pathologic* AV block when the atria are firing that fast.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Two biases are named and countered. **Habituation to the abnormal** ("the necklace you stop feeling") let a systolic of 80 that would never be accepted as *new* become invisible as a constant — the nurse's refusal to normalize it is the antidote. **Anchoring / premature closure** appears twice: the prior note's reasonable "this is baseline," and Reza's own misread of the EKG as benign Mobitz I. The rescue is **metacognitive**: [[normotensive cardiogenic shock]] written *in pencil* (deliberate tunnel vision on a lethal diagnosis, held revisable), and — most of all — **[[knowing what you don't know]]**: rather than force an EKG interpretation, Reza escalated to cardiology, and the correct read (atrial flutter, not AV block) changed both the physiology and the management. The case is a paean to **distributed cognition** — nurse, hospitalist, fellow, and attending each supplying a piece no one clinician held alone.

## Teaching Discussion

**A blood pressure needs two numbers and a patient.** The organizing lesson for hypotension is that arterial pressure alone is ambiguous; you must estimate the **venous** pressure too. Benign, "warm-and-dry-enough" hypotension — sepsis, hypovolemia, anaphylaxis — is soft in *both* compartments, whereas **[[cardiogenic shock|cardiogenic]] and [[obstructive shock|obstructive]]** states pair low arterial pressure with **elevated venous** pressure. That is why a single JVD finding converted a month of accepted low pressures into an alarm. The honest coda — JVP has **poor inter-observer reliability** — is itself a teaching point: findings are only as good as the examiner, and a clinician who has deliberately trained a skill earns disproportionate trust in it (POCUS of the IJ can adjudicate when the exam is uncertain).

**Reserve, and the eat-walk-talk test.** The cardiovascular system's enormous reserve is what lets a resting patient look well while a smoldering process advances; the maneuver that unmasks it is **ambulation**. "Eat, walk, talk" is a practical bedside gate for how confidently you can step back: failure of any one — especially exertional collapse — should expand rather than contract the differential.

**Reconcile what is actually taken.** GDMT on the med list is not GDMT in the patient. Here the reconciliation was decisive in two directions: it explained why the failing heart would not improve (agents skipped) and it removed "over-afterload-reduction" as the main driver of the soft pressure, sharpening the focus on a mechanical/rhythm cause.

**Normotensive cardiogenic shock, in pencil.** A patient can be in [[cardiogenic shock]] without frank hypotension by textbook thresholds; the tells are hypoperfusion (oliguria, fatigue, rising creatinine and lactate) with venous congestion. Because it is lethal and 95% of cases have a low EF, it belongs as an **operating hypothesis written in pencil** — pursued with tunnel vision until confirmed or excluded, then erased if wrong. The **[[troponin]]** and **lactate** earn their place here: the troponin as a broad *negative predictive* test for a stressed heart (and a prognostic flag when positive), the lactate reserved for exactly this severity to catch occult hypoperfusion.

**The physiology of the arrhythmia.** The keystone insight is that **[[atrial flutter]] with variable AV block is physiologic, protective block** — at an atrial rate near 300, the AV node's dropped beats prevent a deadly 1:1 ventricular response, so "pathologic AV block" is the wrong frame at flutter rates. The clinical danger is not the block but the **loss of the [[atrial kick]]**: atrial contraction normally contributes a modest fraction of ventricular filling in health, but in a stiff, poorly compliant, **EF-20%** ventricle that atrial boost becomes disproportionately load-bearing, so its loss can collapse stroke volume. Since **BP = [[cardiac output]] × SVR**, an already reduced output with no compensatory tachycardia produces profound hypotension. Management follows the cardiology triad: hunt **reversible causes** (here, anemia), consider **[[anticoagulation]]** for the flutter, and **reduce afterload** so the failing ventricle can eject.

**The cascade and its root.** The satisfying unification is a chain — **[[iron deficiency anemia|iron-deficiency anemia]] (likely [[gastrointestinal bleeding|GI bleed]]) → [[atrial flutter]] → loss of atrial kick → cardiogenic decompensation** — where the arrhythmia and the hypotension are downstream of a hematologic root cause. Chasing the anemia (Hgb 6 from 10, ferritin <50) is what closes the loop, and it reframes the goals of care for a frail patient whose heart cannot be fixed.

**Humility as method.** Finally, the episode models **[[knowing what you don't know]]**: recognizing an EKG "that didn't make sense," resisting the urge to force an answer, and escalating to colleagues — a nurse, a fellow, an attending — each of whom supplied part of the truth. The best clinicians are not those who know everything but those who know *when to pause and ask*.
