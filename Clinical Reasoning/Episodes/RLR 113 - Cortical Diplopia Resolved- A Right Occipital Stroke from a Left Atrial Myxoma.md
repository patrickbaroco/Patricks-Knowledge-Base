## Pearls

- The discriminator between ocular and cortical (brain) causes of monocular diplopia is image clarity, not just whether the doubling persists with each eye individually covered: an eye-based cause (e.g., a refractive error or macular edema distorting the retinal surface) can only ever produce one high-quality image plus one blurry, distorted second image, because the eye itself is only capable of producing one crisp image at a time — a cortical cause produces two images of equal, crystal-clear quality.
- Asking a patient with diplopia whether the two images are equally clear or whether one is blurred is a fast, high-yield bedside question that meaningfully narrows localization before any imaging is obtained.
- A focused bedside eye evaluation for diplopia should include inspection for cataracts, visual acuity testing, peripheral visual field testing (a frequently overlooked but highly informative test — its omission in this case was later recognized as a missed opportunity), and, when available, a formal ophthalmologic and fundoscopic exam before proceeding to MRI.
- A visual field cut (e.g., a homonymous hemianopsia) localizes disease to the retrochiasmal visual pathway on the side opposite the visual field defect — a left-sided field cut implicates a right-sided (occipital or optic radiation) lesion.
- Modern stroke treatment paradigms are moving away from strict, fixed time-since-onset cutoffs and toward assessing whether brain tissue remains viable (via perfusion imaging) regardless of how much time has elapsed, meaningfully expanding the window in which intervention may still help select patients.
- A useful framework for stroke etiology is "pump, pipe, plasma": cardiac sources (atrial fibrillation, valvular vegetations or masses, PFO with paradoxical embolism), vascular/pipe sources (carotid or intracranial atherosclerosis visualized on CTA/MRA), and plasma/hypercoagulable sources — with cardiac and vascular causes generally having a higher base rate and being evaluated first, leaving hypercoagulability as a later consideration once the more common categories are excluded.
- Ischemic stroke should not be reflexively assumed to represent a bland (sterile) clot — infective sources (e.g., endocarditis) must be considered as part of the same framework, since missing an infected embolic source has major treatment implications.
- A single EKG, or even a brief inpatient telemetry run, is not sufficient to exclude paroxysmal atrial fibrillation as a stroke etiology; extended cardiac monitoring is often warranted, and the presence or absence of atrial fibrillation has direct implications for whether a patient receives antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy.
- Episodic, brief, fully self-resolving symptoms (here, shortness of breath lasting minutes and then disappearing entirely, with normal exam, labs, EKG, and imaging between episodes) are physiologically hard to explain with common chronic diagnoses (bronchospasm and heart failure do not typically resolve within minutes), and should prompt specific questioning about triggers and positional dependence (an episodic-with-trigger versus spontaneous framework) rather than being dismissed as functional or anxiety-related.
- It is a recognized cognitive trap to mentally discount a patient-reported symptom simply because it does not fit the current working diagnostic framework; symptoms that seem to "not make sense" in the moment can turn out to be the most important clue in the case.
- When considering non-thrombotic causes of vascular occlusion or embolization, the differential includes air (classically from procedural mishaps, such as central line removal without Trendelenburg positioning), fat (from long bone fractures), amniotic fluid (peripartum), and tumor — a useful checklist once a purely thrombotic or infective embolic source has been reasonably excluded.
- The differential for a cardiac valve mass includes infective endocarditis, non-infective (marantic, associated with underlying malignancy, versus Libman-Sacks, associated with lupus — "Libman-Sacks" pairs with "lupus," "marantic" pairs with "malignancy") endocarditis, and other non-infective causes such as tuberculosis; friable non-infective vegetations are notably prone to embolize but often do not damage the valve enough to produce an audible regurgitant murmur, meaning a normal cardiac auscultation does not reliably exclude a friable, embolizing mass.
- Among cardiac masses not situated directly on a valve, a primary cardiac tumor (most commonly the benign myxoma, which favors the left atrium) is more likely than a metastatic malignant lesion; benign masses are generally more common than malignant ones in the heart, though primary cardiac sarcomas exist; benign structural mimics (e.g., a Chiari network or septal hypertrophy) should also be kept in mind.
- A left atrial myxoma can produce two seemingly unrelated presentations from a single lesion: embolic phenomena (including embolic stroke, from fragments breaking off a friable tumor surface) and intermittent, position- or motion-dependent obstruction of the mitral valve orifice (producing episodic dyspnea as the tumor transiently prolapses into or through the valve) — surgical excision is the definitive treatment.

## Problem Representation

A 54-year-old woman with no significant past medical history, presenting with bilateral monocular diplopia subsequently localized to a right occipital ischemic stroke, and with a co-occurring, initially dismissed complaint of brief episodic dyspnea — both ultimately explained by a single large left atrial myxoma causing embolic stroke and intermittent mitral valve obstruction.

## Case Narrative

This episode opens by resolving the diplopia case left as a cliffhanger in RLR 111. Rabih explained that he had deliberately withheld the full case because its ultimate cause opened into an entirely separate diagnostic story, warranting its own episode.

Rabih first asked Reza to restate why the initial cover-test finding — diplopia persisting in each eye individually — had been so significant. Reza reiterated the binocular-versus-monocular framework: binocular diplopia (from ocular misalignment, e.g., a cranial nerve palsy, a neuromuscular junction disorder like myasthenia gravis, or a mass or aneurysm compressing an extraocular muscle's innervation) requires both eyes open, since covering either eye removes the competing misaligned image and restores single vision; because this patient's diplopia persisted with each eye covered individually, the process had to be either bilateral disease within each eye separately or a process beyond the eyes altogether — most likely cortical, assuming a psychogenic cause was appropriately held as a low-probability, last-resort consideration rather than a starting point.

Asked what he would do next, Reza outlined a bedside-to-imaging progression: inspect for cataracts, check visual acuity, test peripheral visual fields (which he specifically flagged as an often-overlooked but highly informative test), obtain a formal ophthalmologic and fundoscopic exam, and, if these were unrevealing or if extraocular clues pointed further afield, proceed to MRI with contrast.

Rabih confirmed this was exactly right, and admitted that in real time he had not tested peripheral visual fields himself — an omission that, in retrospect, would have anticipated the eventual finding. He sent the patient to the VA's ophthalmology service (with a notably fast referral turnaround), and the ophthalmologist returned with a specific new finding and a teaching point: a left-sided visual field cut, localizing to the right occipital lobe, and a diagnosis of cortical diplopia. The ophthalmologist explained the discriminator that had eluded Rabih: in ocular misalignment, the two images a patient sees come from two different eyes; in monocular ocular disease, the two images are both produced by the same eye, and because that eye's optical system can only generate one high-quality image (any second image being a refracted, blurred distortion from the same defective lens or cornea, or from macular edema), an eye-based cause always produces one clear image and one blurry image — never two equally sharp images. This patient reported both images as equally crystal clear, which effectively excluded bilateral ocular disease (e.g., bilateral cataracts) and confirmed a cortical process.

An MRI showed a right occipital ischemic stroke, roughly a week and a half old — recent, but past the window for emergent stroke intervention, though Reza noted (citing a UCSF neurology grand rounds lecture) that modern stroke treatment paradigms increasingly focus on whether brain tissue remains viable via advanced imaging rather than on rigid time-since-onset cutoffs, meaningfully extending the population that might still benefit from intervention.

Reza organized the stroke-etiology workup using his "pump, pipe, plasma" framework: pump (a cardiac source, such as atrial fibrillation or a valvular mass/vegetation, evaluated with echocardiography, including a bubble study for a patent foramen ovale and paradoxical embolism), pipe (vascular sources such as carotid or intracranial atherosclerosis, evaluated with CTA or MRA), and plasma (an underlying hypercoagulable state, typically considered last given the comparatively higher base rate of cardiac and vascular causes, especially with advancing age). He also emphasized that an ischemic stroke should never be assumed to be a bland (sterile) clot without consideration of an infected source such as endocarditis, and that a single EKG (or even a short inpatient telemetry run) is insufficient to exclude paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, which carries direct treatment implications (antiplatelet versus anticoagulant therapy).

Rabih then described the patient in full: 54 years old, otherwise healthy, no significant medical history or medications, non-smoking, non-hypertensive, non-hyperlipidemic. On review of systems, she reported brief, severe episodes of shortness of breath occurring several times a day, lasting only minutes before fully resolving, with a completely normal exam, labs, EKG, and chest X-ray obtained during this window. Reza found this genuinely puzzling: he struggled to identify a pathophysiologic process capable of producing symptoms this transient and fully self-resolving (bronchospasm and heart failure, for instance, do not typically resolve within minutes), briefly considered a panic attack but noted that even panic attacks take time to build and subside, and wanted to ask about positional or activity-related triggers — a line of questioning he and Rabih explicitly identified as reflecting their recurring practice of separating triggered-episodic from spontaneous-episodic symptoms — while adding that he would want chest imaging to extend down through the lungs given the chance to acquire contrast anyway. Rabih candidly admitted he had not asked those specific trigger-related questions in real time.

A CTA of the head and neck returned pristine, and multiple ED EKGs were normal. The patient was admitted for an expedited inpatient stroke workup and telemetry monitoring; over roughly 24 hours she had three further shortness-of-breath episodes, each lasting minutes, with no arrhythmia captured on telemetry during any of them — effectively ruling out atrial fibrillation as the explanation for the dyspnea specifically.

Reza found himself stuck trying to unify the stroke and the episodic dyspnea, noting that with no arrhythmia, no lung pathology, and a subjective, non-hypoxic dyspnea, he did not know where to take the reasoning next. Rabih prompted him to revisit the pump/pipe/plasma framework from a different angle: if the embolic source were not a clot at all, what else can occlude or obstruct a vessel? Reza worked through a list of non-thrombotic embolic sources — infection (though inconsistent with the complete absence of inflammatory findings), air (citing a case of fatal air embolism from central line removal without Trendelenburg positioning), fat (associated with fractures, not present here), and amniotic fluid (not applicable) — before landing on tumor as a genuine possibility, while acknowledging he did not know what specific test would confirm it.

A transthoracic echocardiogram provided the answer directly: a large mass was visualized. Reza reasoned through the cardiac mass differential live: infective endocarditis was already essentially excluded by the clinical picture; non-infective endocarditis remained possible, classically divided into marantic (associated with underlying malignancy) and Libman-Sacks (associated with lupus) forms, with tuberculosis and other processes as less common causes; but he also flagged that non-infective vegetations, while notably friable and prone to embolize, often do not damage the valve enough to produce an audible murmur, so a clean auscultation exam does not exclude this category. He then reasoned that, among masses not situated on a valve itself, a primary cardiac tumor is statistically more likely than a metastatic lesion, with myxoma — which favors the left atrium — being the classic benign example, and benign masses generally outnumbering malignant ones in the heart (while acknowledging primary cardiac sarcomas exist). Given the patient's entirely negative CTA and lack of any other malignancy signal, he judged a primary benign cardiac neoplasm most likely.

Rabih confirmed the diagnosis: a large left atrial myxoma, likely intermittently prolapsing into or obstructing the mitral valve orifice — explaining both the embolic right occipital stroke (from fragments of the friable tumor) and the episodic dyspnea (from dynamic, intermittent obstruction of mitral inflow) as two manifestations of a single unifying diagnosis in an otherwise strikingly healthy patient. The patient was transferred to UCSF for elective, semi-urgent cardiothoracic surgical excision; her outcome after transfer was not known to Rabih, as she left his system's care.

Rabih reflected candidly that he had initially dismissed the episodic shortness of breath as not making clinical sense and had privately attributed it to anxiety or a perceptual phenomenon related to the stroke — describing this as a recurring personal and general cognitive trap of discounting a patient-reported symptom simply because it does not immediately fit the working framework, even while explicitly valuing patient-reported symptoms as reliable data in principle. Reza, in turn, reflected that Rabih's structured approach — teaching the fundamentals of diplopia localization, following through to an MRI few clinicians would obtain for diplopia alone, and then applying the pump/pipe/plasma framework rigorously even when it initially yielded no answer — was what ultimately made space for the correct diagnosis to emerge from a symptom that could easily have been dismissed.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| Diplopia persists in each eye individually when the other eye is covered | Excludes ocular misalignment; localizes to bilateral ocular disease or a cortical process |
| Both images reported as equally clear (not one clear, one blurry) | Excludes bilateral ocular disease (e.g., bilateral cataracts); confirms a cortical (rather than ocular) cause of monocular diplopia |
| Left-sided visual field cut on ophthalmologic exam | Localizes to the right occipital lobe / retrochiasmal visual pathway |
| MRI: subacute right occipital ischemic stroke | Confirms cortical diplopia is due to a right occipital stroke |
| Pristine CTA head/neck, normal EKGs, no arrhythmia on telemetry despite captured symptomatic episodes | Lowers probability of large-vessel atherosclerotic ("pipe") and arrhythmic ("pump"-electrical) stroke mechanisms |
| Young, healthy patient with no vascular risk factors | Lowers baseline probability of atherosclerotic stroke; raises relative probability of a structural cardiac or hypercoagulable source |
| Brief, fully self-resolving episodic dyspnea with normal interval exam/labs/imaging | Argues against bronchospasm or heart failure; favors an intermittent, mechanical, or dynamic process |
| Transthoracic echo: large left atrial mass | Identifies a structural cardiac source capable of both embolization and dynamic valve obstruction |
| Mass not situated on the valve itself, favors left atrium | Favors a primary benign cardiac tumor (myxoma) over infective or metastatic causes |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Left atrial myxoma illness script: an otherwise healthy patient develops an embolic stroke with no identifiable atherosclerotic or arrhythmic source, accompanied by episodic, position- or motion-dependent dyspnea from intermittent mitral valve obstruction — a single left atrial mass explaining both an embolic and an obstructive presentation simultaneously.

The diplopia portion of the case is an explicit, teachable example of localizing sensory pathology by systematically testing image quality, not merely image persistence, illustrating how a seemingly exhausted diagnostic maneuver (the eye-covering test) can still hide a further layer of discriminating information. The dyspnea portion is an explicit, self-critical example of discounting a patient-reported symptom because it does not fit the prevailing hypothesis — a bias both hosts explicitly named and reflected on, rather than one identified only in retrospect by an outside observer.

## Teaching Discussion

The central neurological teaching point is the refinement of monocular diplopia localization beyond the basic cover test: image clarity (one clear plus one blurry image versus two equally clear images) is the discriminator between an ocular and a cortical cause, adding a second, higher-yield layer of bedside reasoning to a framework introduced in the prior episode.

The central cardiovascular teaching point is the "pump, pipe, plasma, and non-thrombotic embolic sources" expansion of the stroke-etiology framework, deployed here specifically once the standard categories (arrhythmia, atherosclerosis, classic hypercoagulability) had been reasonably excluded — illustrating how to keep reasoning productively once an initial framework runs out of easy answers, rather than concluding the case is unsolvable.

The unifying teaching point is the deliberate juxtaposition of a rare and diagnostically satisfying single lesion (a left atrial myxoma) explaining two seemingly unrelated presentations (stroke and episodic dyspnea) in an otherwise maximally healthy patient — modeling the diagnostic instinct that, when the "pipes" and "plasma" are clean and the patient is otherwise robust, an occult structural cardiac process deserves serious consideration, and that an easily dismissed accompanying symptom can be the thread that ties the whole case together.
