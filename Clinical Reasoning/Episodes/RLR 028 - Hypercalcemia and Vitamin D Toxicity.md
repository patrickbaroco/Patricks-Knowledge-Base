## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Recorded before a live audience in Geneva, Reza brings a case he read on for hours after rounding with a medicine team: a highly functional man living in India, visiting Geneva, who became confused about a month after a foot fracture left him sedentary. The pivotal panel is a calcium of 16, a creatinine of 3, and a bicarbonate of 40. Rabih makes the [[Hypercalcemia|hypercalcemia]] the center of gravity for the [[Encephalopathy|encephalopathy]] and runs the causal schema — endocrine, malignancy, ingestion, plus the "two I's" of infection and immobilization. The *high* bicarbonate rewrites the expected hypercalcemia → [[Nephrogenic Diabetes Insipidus|nephrogenic DI]] → AKI → non-gap acidosis cascade: an alkalosis instead points to a calcium-carbonate load, and the man has been taking Tums. [[Parathyroid Hormone|PTH]] is suppressed, a VBG confirms a true metabolic alkalosis (pH 7.56), and the calcium falls with hydration — [[Milk-Alkali Syndrome|milk-alkali syndrome]] looks solved. But out of the ICU the calcium rebounds to 12 and rising, breaking the closure. A [[Calcitriol|1,25-vitamin D]] returns off-assay-high alongside an elevated [[25-Hydroxyvitamin D|25-hydroxyvitamin D]], endoscopy shows multiple gastric ulcers, imaging is clean, and the wife brings in the bottle: cholecalciferol 60,000 IU daily for two months, begun *before* the fracture. The unifying answer is [[Vitamin D Toxicity|vitamin D toxicity]] — driving the hypercalcemia, the thinned bone that fractured, the gastrin-stimulated ulcers, and the nephrogenic DI — with milk-alkali layered on as a [[Physiologic Reserve|second hit]]. The reasoning lessons: hypercalcemia-plus-alkalosis is not synonymous with milk-alkali, you can put an [[Hickam's Dictum|AND rather than an OR]], and a 1,25-vitamin D cannot be read without its 25-hydroxy partner.

## Pearls

- **Hypercalcemia with a metabolic alkalosis classically reads as [[Milk-Alkali Syndrome|milk-alkali]], but the pairing is not pathognomonic — and one process can hide beneath another.** Rabih's line is the thesis of the case: you do not have to put an *OR* between diagnoses, you can put an *AND* ([[Hickam's Dictum|Hickam's dictum]]). Rapid correction with hydration supports milk-alkali; a *rebound* after the ICU does not exonerate an ingestion (some have long half-lives) but does retire milk-alkali as the sole cause and reopens the schema.
- **The direction of the bicarbonate rewrites the hypercalcemia cascade.** [[Hypercalcemia|Hypercalcemia]] → [[Nephrogenic Diabetes Insipidus|nephrogenic DI]] → volume contraction → AKI usually yields a non-gap *acidosis*; when the bicarbonate is instead *high*, an exogenous alkali/calcium load (calcium carbonate) has entered a kidney that can no longer excrete it — the self-amplifying loop of milk-alkali.
- **A 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D level is uninterpretable without the 25-hydroxy level.** A high [[Calcitriol|1,25-vitamin D]] is either 25-hydroxy excess spilling over (ingestion) or pathological [[1-Alpha-Hydroxylation|1-α-hydroxylation]] of normal-to-*low* 25 by a [[Granulomatous Disease|granuloma]] or [[PTHrP|PTHrP]]. The discriminator is the [[25-Hydroxyvitamin D|25-hydroxy]] value: *high* points to ingestion, *low* to conversion. Sending only the 1,25, seeing it elevated, and calling it granulomatous is a common [[Test Interpretation|interpretive trap]].
- **[[Vitamin D Toxicity|Vitamin D toxicity]] is severe, persistent, and slow to clear.** Cholecalciferol is fat-soluble and leaches out of adipose stores for up to ~18 months, so the hypercalcemia is refractory. Treatment is withdrawal of the vitamin plus [[Prednisone|prednisone]] — and, as dark humor, it is the one hypercalcemia where you will *not* have to co-prescribe vitamin D with the steroid.
- **[[Zoledronic Acid|Bisphosphonates]] are contraindicated in milk-alkali.** These patients correct fast with hydration and holding the alkali, and once the calcium falls PTH un-suppresses within minutes; a bisphosphonate that has already driven calcium into bone via the osteoblast will then produce frank hypocalcemia. Corollary pearl: **hypocalcemia after treating a hypercalcemia is itself a diagnostic clue.**
- **A "[[Physiologic Reserve|second hit]]" turns a smoldering process catastrophic.** Population data show many people taking 60,000 IU of vitamin D never become significantly hypercalcemic; layering calcium carbonate onto a host with no calcium reserve is what sends the number to 16 — the same logic as delirium on dementia or AKI on CKD.

## Problem Representation

A previously highly functional older man residing in India and visiting Geneva presents with subacute encephalopathy — confusion and nonsensical speech without focal deficit — developing roughly a month after a foot fracture left him sedentary. He is hypertensive with a normal heart rate and temperature; labs show a calcium of 16 with a normal albumin, a creatinine of 3 without known chronic kidney disease, and a bicarbonate of 40, against a normal CBC, sodium, urinalysis, and chest radiograph. A VBG confirms a primary metabolic alkalosis (pH 7.56 with a normal-to-high PCO2), PTH is suppressed, and a history of regular Tums use for indigestion emerges; the calcium falls with hydration, calcitonin, and zoledronic acid but rebounds to 12 after he leaves the ICU. Ultimately the 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D is immeasurably high with an elevated 25-hydroxyvitamin D, upper endoscopy reveals esophagitis and multiple gastric ulcers, cross-sectional imaging shows no adenopathy or organomegaly, and the family produces a bottle of cholecalciferol 60,000 IU taken daily for two months — yielding vitamin D toxicity as the primary diagnosis with superimposed milk-alkali syndrome as a second hit.

## Case Narrative

Reza is in Geneva, having rounded with a medicine team whose case sent him reading for three or four hours; a graduating resident has just texted that they clinched the diagnosis. Before a live audience, he gives Rabih the gestalt of the case rather than the full data set. A highly educated, highly functional man living in India was visiting Geneva when, about two months earlier, he fell and fractured a bone in his foot, leaving him sedentary and inactive for the ensuing month. His family then noticed he had become bizarre — interacting inappropriately, confused, making nonsensical comments — and brought him to a Geneva hospital. His exam showed hypertension, a normal heart rate and temperature, and no focal neurologic deficit; he simply appeared confused. The CBC was normal. The chemistry panel was the highlight: a bicarbonate of 40, a creatinine of 3 with no baseline and no history of chronic kidney disease, and a **[[Hypercalcemia|calcium of 16]]** with a normal albumin. Sodium, urinalysis, and chest radiograph were all normal.

Rabih begins by separating two neurologic threads: an [[Encephalopathy|encephalopathy]] that was preceded by a fall. He notes that most neurologic diseases stay contained within the nervous system and do not throw off such a prominent systemic laboratory fingerprint, so he is inclined to treat the metabolic derangement as explanatory for the altered mental status — while keeping open the question of *why* the man fell in the first place. The core problem, he decides, is hypercalcemia causing encephalopathy, with everything else as peripheral clues. He lays out his approach to hypercalcemia. The laboratory reflex is PTH versus non-PTH, and within non-PTH, another hormone ([[PTHrP|PTHrP]] or vitamin D) versus metastatic disease. But the better move is to think in *disease categories*: endocrine (most often [[Parathyroid Hormone|primary hyperparathyroidism]], or tertiary hyperparathyroidism given the renal dysfunction), followed closely and more morbidly by malignancy, and third the ingestions — the notorious one being [[Milk-Alkali Syndrome|milk-alkali syndrome]] from calcium carbonate, alongside hydrochlorothiazide and vitamin A.

He then predicts the labs from mechanism. A patient with hypercalcemia and nothing else should develop a [[Nephrogenic Diabetes Insipidus|nephrogenic diabetes insipidus]]; that nephrogenic DI drives an acute kidney injury; and that AKI generates a non-gap metabolic acidosis and a *drop* in the bicarbonate. Here, though, the bicarbonate is 40 — the wrong direction. That combination, hypercalcemia with AKI and a *high* bicarbonate, prioritizes calcium carbonate ingestion, i.e. milk-alkali. His plan: hydrate, watch the creatinine and bicarbonate, and if the hypercalcemia melts away, it was the ingestion; if it persists, move on — perhaps to a coexisting alkalosis from another cause.

Reza confirms the thread. Asked about antacids, the patient reports a month of indigestion treated with regular Tums. A VBG shows a pH of 7.56 with a normal-to-elevated PCO2 — a true metabolic alkalosis, not a compensation. The PTH comes back low. With hydration, calcitonin, and zoledronic acid, the calcium improves. Rabih reads this cleanly: the alkalosis is real, the suppressed PTH excludes primary hyperparathyroidism, and the rapid fall makes malignancy less likely. The only loose thread is *why* the man was taking so many Tums — but he sees no reason yet to pursue an alternate hypothesis, cautioning only that the story is not finished until the improvement is *sustained*, since a bisphosphonate will transiently improve almost anything.

Reza takes a teaching detour into milk-alkali's history. Decades ago Dr. Sippy treated indigestion with "Sippy powder" — magnesium or calcium carbonate taken with milk or cream — and many patients developed significant hypercalcemia. Once recognized, the practice faded with the arrival of H2 blockers and PPIs, and milk-alkali fell below 1% of hypercalcemia causes. But with postmenopausal women taking over-the-counter calcium carbonate for bone protection, it has resurged to become the *third* most common cause, responsible for roughly 10–12%. Mechanistically, the trigger for the hypercalcemia itself is not fully understood, but once present it produces nephrogenic DI two ways: by reducing aquaporins and by blocking the sodium-potassium-2-chloride transporter in the ascending loop of Henle (the loop-diuretic site). The resulting volume contraction drives bicarbonate retention; add ongoing bicarbonate intake and you get a vicious cycle of worsening alkalosis and kidney injury. Stop the alkali, give fluid, and it improves.

Then the case breaks open. Once out of the ICU, the calcium normalized to 10 — and on the next check it was 12 and rising. Rabih holds the line: a rebound does not rule out an ingestion (many have long half-lives), but it reduces the probability of milk-alkali as the *sole* contributor and suggests it merely exacerbated something already brewing. He would re-measure the PTH (an occult primary hyperparathyroidism could have been transiently suppressed) and re-anchor in the likely categories now — malignancy and other ingestions — while giving credence to the "two I's" beyond the common three: **[[Granulomatous Disease|infection]]** (granulomatous) and **[[Hypercalcemia of Immobilization|immobilization]]**, the latter notoriously severe and relapsing-remitting in a man sedentary for months and immobilized on a flight from India. He would complete the hormone analysis — PTHrP (limited in renal dysfunction), plus both 25- and 1,25-vitamin D — and, given hypercalcemia with AKI, send an SPEP and serum free light chains for [[Multiple Myeloma|myeloma]]. He recalls, candidly, accidentally overdosing a patient with vitamin D as an intern, and how long and how much prednisone recovery took.

Here Reza adds a management pearl that surprised him in his reading: zoledronic acid is actually *contraindicated* in milk-alkali. These patients respond so well to hydration and holding the bicarbonate that their calcium falls quickly; PTH is secreted and inhibited within minutes, so as the calcium normalizes the PTH recovers, and a [[Zoledronic Acid|bisphosphonate]] on board will have the osteoblast pull calcium into bone, tipping the patient into hypocalcemia. Rabih extends it into a fresh schema: *hypocalcemia after treating a hypercalcemia* is itself a clue worth processing.

The confirmatory data arrive. The [[Calcitriol|1,25-vitamin D]] is above the assay's ceiling; the [[25-Hydroxyvitamin D|25-hydroxyvitamin D]] is also severely elevated, though less so. Upper endoscopy for the reflux symptoms shows esophagitis and multiple gastric ulcers. The now-lucid patient reports only the "normal low dose" of vitamin D his doctor recommended after the fracture. Rabih works the vitamin D logic: 1,25 is the active mediator of vitamin D-induced hypercalcemia, and a high 1,25 comes either from too much 25 spilling into it or from a hijacked pathway converting normal/low 25 to excess 1,25 (granulomatous disease). The patient's vitamin D use accounts for the elevated 25, but the disproportionately higher 1,25 is an open loop — raising whether he could have a *third* diagnosis (a granulomatous conversion behind the ingestion), a gap in the textbook conversion model, or an accidental prescription of activated 1,25 (calcitriol), the form renal physicians give patients who cannot 1-α-hydroxylate. He would get the actual pills and any prior calcium levels, since a granulomatous process should leave at least mild background hypercalcemia or hypercalciuria.

Pressed to comment on two points, Rabih first drives home the [[Test Interpretation|interpretive]] rule with a two-cups image: a full "1,25" cup can be full because 25 was over-poured and spilled over, or because 1,25 itself was poured directly — so you need *both* values to tell 25-excess from 1,25-hijacking. Second, on the gastric ulcers, he lays out three ways hypercalcemia and multiple ulcers might connect: a [[Gastrinoma|gastrinoma]] (via [[MEN1|MEN1]] with primary hyperparathyroidism — excluded here by the suppressed PTH), a metastatic or local malignancy such as gastric adenocarcinoma, or a granulomatous site such as [[Zollinger-Ellison Syndrome|tuberculosis]] (the patient lives in India) or sarcoidosis — making the endoscopic biopsy valuable. The sexy connection is a gastrinoma; the practical one, medications; the probable one in India, TB.

Reza reframes the vitamin D forms: the parent vitamin D (D2/D3, unhydroxylated), the 25-hydroxy form (hydroxylated in the liver), and the 1,25 form (1-α-hydroxylated in the kidney). Very high 1,25 means either exogenous 1,25, a large 25 load, or PTHrP/granulomatous conversion — and the tell is that in the conversion states the 25 runs *low* (it is being consumed), whereas a 25 ingestion elevates *both*. Here the 25 was elevated but felt mismatched, and he could not call it out of proportion with confidence given too few values. Cross-sectional imaging then showed no lymphadenopathy, no lung opacities, and no hepatosplenomegaly — arguing against granuloma and malignancy. Finally the wife brought in the medication: **cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) 60,000 IU taken daily for two months**, started *before* the fracture. Rabih notes withdrawal is crucial and the treatment of choice is prednisone, with a good long-term prognosis relative to the worst-case metastatic malignancy first suggested by a calcium of 16.

Reza closes with the synthesis: this was not primarily milk-alkali but a story of **[[Vitamin D Toxicity|vitamin D toxicity]]**. Its principal manifestation is hypercalcemia; even low-grade hypercalcemia can thin bone, and he wonders whether the toxicity weakened the bone that fractured. Hypercalcemia produced the neuropsychiatric symptoms, and — via speculated gastrin stimulation — the gastritis and ulcers. Population studies show many people given this dose never develop significant hypercalcemia, but risk climbs sharply with a *second hit*: adding calcium carbonate to a mildly elevated calcium in a host with no reserve sends it through the roof, like AKI on CKD or delirium on dementia. Management is genuinely hard — IV fluids for all; calcitonin (rapid but tachyphylactic at ~48 hours); bisphosphonate (2–4 days, via the osteoblast); [[Denosumab|denosumab]] (anti-RANKL); and steroids, which the authors recommend but which carry their own toll. They gave a second dose of zoledronic acid and planned intermittent prednisone or further bisphosphonate on follow-up, with the vitamin D3 expected to release from adipose for up to 18 months and the abiding worry of [[Nephrocalcinosis|nephrocalcinosis]] and CKD. Everything, Reza says, comes together under vitamin D toxicity: hypercalcemia thinning bone, causing gastritis and ulcers, causing nephrogenic DI — and then the calcium carbonate second hit.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Hickam's Dictum|Hickam's dictum]] as the explicit thesis — "put an AND, not an OR."** The seductive stopping point was "hypercalcemia + alkalosis = milk-alkali; there is only one cause." The rebound of the calcium is what forbids that closure and forces a second, then effectively a third, diagnosis (vitamin D toxicity, milk-alkali, and the immobilization/bone contribution). The case is a clinic in resisting a satisfying unifying label when the data keep an [[Premature Closure|open loop]].
- **[[Premature Closure|Premature closure]] broken by following the trend.** Milk-alkali explained the initial picture *and* corrected with treatment — a doubly reinforcing anchor. Only the disciplined act of re-checking the calcium (and reading its *trajectory* rather than a single normal value) reopened the workup. Durability of response, not initial response, is the test of a hypercalcemia diagnosis.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Interpreting the vitamin D axis by pairing the metabolites]].** The set-piece lesson: a high 1,25 is ambiguous alone (the "two cups"), and the reflex of sending only 1,25 and calling an elevation "granulomatous" manufactures false certainty. The 25-hydroxy value is the discriminator — high with ingestion, low with pathological conversion — so both must be read together, and even then the magnitude of the mismatch may be too small to call.
- **[[Physiologic Reserve|Reserve and the second hit]] as a probabilistic model of severity.** Vitamin D 60,000 IU is often tolerated; the calcium of 16 is a product of *two* insults on a host with no buffer. The dementia→delirium and CKD→AKI analogies frame severity as a function of reserve, not of any single exposure — a transferable way to think about why some patients decompensate on inputs others absorb.
- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|Category-first schema over the lab-first reflex]].** Rabih deliberately reasons in disease categories (endocrine, malignancy, ingestion, plus the two I's) before the PTH/non-PTH laboratory fork, then *predicts* the electrolyte pattern from mechanism (hypercalcemia → nephrogenic DI → AKI → acid-base change). The predicted-versus-observed bicarbonate mismatch is precisely what localizes the ingestion.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Humility and self-disclosure]].** Reza volunteers his intern-year vitamin D overdose and Rabih his own; both admit the tentative, evolving nature of the workup. The shared-mistake candor is presented as part of what makes the reasoning space safe enough to keep questioning a "solved" case.

## Teaching Discussion

- Treat hypercalcemia in disease categories — endocrine, malignancy, and ingestion, with the "two I's" (granulomatous infection and immobilization) held in reserve — and predict the electrolyte consequences from mechanism, because the *direction* of the resulting acid-base disturbance (alkalosis versus the expected non-gap acidosis) is what separates a calcium-carbonate ingestion from the rest.
- Milk-alkali (calcium-alkali) syndrome has swung from Dr. Sippy's powder-and-cream era down below 1% and back up to the third most common cause of hypercalcemia (~10–12%), driven by over-the-counter calcium carbonate for bone health; suspect it with the triad of hypercalcemia, metabolic alkalosis, and AKI, and confirm by response to holding the alkali and hydrating.
- A suppressed PTH confirms the process is outside the parathyroid axis, but does not tell you *which* non-PTH cause; work the vitamin D axis by sending 25- and 1,25-hydroxyvitamin D *together*, since 1,25 alone cannot distinguish ingestion (25 high) from granulomatous or PTHrP-driven conversion (25 low).
- The three forms of vitamin D map to their sites: parent D2/D3 (unhydroxylated), 25-hydroxy (liver), and 1,25-dihydroxy (renal 1-α-hydroxylation, the active hormone); an accidental prescription of activated 1,25 (calcitriol) is a distinct cause of a high 1,25 worth excluding, especially in patients seen by nephrology.
- Bisphosphonates are contraindicated in milk-alkali because the calcium corrects rapidly with hydration and PTH recovers within minutes, so an osteoblast already driven by the drug will produce hypocalcemia — and, inverting the schema, an unexpected hypocalcemia after treating a hypercalcemia is itself a diagnostic clue.
- Hypercalcemia with multiple gastric ulcers can connect through a gastrinoma (with MEN1 and primary hyperparathyroidism — excluded by a suppressed PTH), a malignancy, or a granulomatous site (tuberculosis or sarcoidosis), but can also be a direct effect of hypercalcemia-stimulated gastrin; biopsy the ulcers when the connection is not already explained.
- Vitamin D3 is stored in and slowly released from adipose tissue for as long as ~18 months, so vitamin D toxicity is severe and refractory; manage with withdrawal of the vitamin, IV fluids, calcitonin (rapid, tachyphylactic), bisphosphonate or denosumab, and prednisone (the authors' recommendation, weighed against steroid harms), while watching for nephrocalcinosis and progression to CKD.
