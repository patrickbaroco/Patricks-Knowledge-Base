## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A man in his 60s with decompensated schizophrenia presents to the emergency department wanting only his Foley catheter serviced, and incidental imaging unravels bilateral pulmonary emboli, a right lower lobe cavitary lesion, a thickened/possibly mass-like bladder wall, diffuse infradiaphragmatic lymphadenopathy, and multifocal cerebral infarcts. Robbie frames the vascular findings as "ultra-hypercoagulable" — arterial and venous clot together — and argues the extravascular findings (bladder, nodes, lung) make cancer the leading explanation over the other members of that differential. A recent hospitalization with MRSA bacteremia and a stroke complicates the anticoagulation decision by raising the specter of septic emboli and mycotic aneurysm. The episode spends real time on the practical and ethical texture of the case — a patient without capacity, a sister acting as DPOA, and the limits of what can actually be done to a person who won't cooperate — before a PET-CT and axillary node biopsy return small lymphocytic lymphoma, an indolent diagnosis that both hosts agree is real but doesn't proportionally explain the severity of the clotting, leaving the case deliberately unresolved.

## Pearls

- Arterial and venous thrombosis occurring together in the same patient is a distinct clinical category — Robbie's "ultra-hypercoagulable" state — that should prompt a different, narrower differential than either process alone, unless a right-to-left shunt (PFO) is present, in which case what looks like separate arterial and venous clots may actually be the same paradoxical embolus.
- The ultra-hypercoagulable differential includes heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, antiphospholipid syndrome, thrombotic microangiopathy, paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, Behçet's disease, VEXAS syndrome, hypereosinophilia, and cancer — but only cancer commonly produces prominent extravascular findings (masses, lymphadenopathy, cavitary lesions), which makes cancer the default hypothesis whenever a hypercoagulable picture is accompanied by tissue-level findings outside the vessels.
- Disseminated lymphadenopathy in general should be framed as either infection or cancer in most cases — autoimmune disease presenting primarily as disseminated lymphadenopathy is unusual, with sarcoidosis as a notable exception.
- A history of MRSA bacteremia and stroke should raise the possibility of septic emboli and endocarditis-related thrombotic phenomena, including the rare but real possibility that filling defects on imaging represent infected material (pus) rather than bland clot.
- Mycotic aneurysms tend to occur in unusually distal vascular locations compared to typical atherosclerotic aneurysms, which are more proximal — location is a genuine clue for distinguishing an infectious from a degenerative aneurysm.
- MRI cannot reliably distinguish septic from bland thrombotic occlusion on its own, but the presence of an associated aneurysm raises the probability that an embolus has invasive, inflammatory properties consistent with a septic source.
- Anticoagulation decisions in the setting of possible septic emboli exist on a spectrum rather than a binary: absolute contraindication favors an IVC filter, a clear absence of contraindication favors full anticoagulation, and a relative or uncertain contraindication often favors starting heparin (reversible) without a bolus — a practice with more psychological reassurance than strong evidence behind it.
- Practical, real-world constraints (a patient who won't reliably allow blood draws for PTT monitoring) can legitimately override a theoretically preferred anticoagulant choice — using a low-molecular-weight heparin that doesn't require monitoring can be the safer real-life option even when unfractionated heparin might otherwise be favored.
- Escalating beyond anticoagulation for venous thrombosis (catheter-directed thrombolysis, surgical intervention) should be reserved for evidence that the tissue behind the clot is actually suffering — acute ischemia or chronic venous ulceration — not simply for the presence of a large or symptomatic clot, mirroring the logic used for right heart strain in pulmonary embolism.
- Schizophrenia is a life-limiting diagnosis with meaningfully reduced life expectancy — a fact that is broadly under-recognized as a mortality (not just morbidity) issue, and one that should inform how aggressively a workup is pursued in a patient whose psychiatric illness itself limits both decision-making capacity and tolerance for invasive testing.
- When a patient lacks decision-making capacity, the practical path runs through psychiatric evaluation of whether capacity can be restored, then a surrogate decision-maker (DPOA) — but a surrogate's wishes can still be logistically impossible to execute if the patient physically resists testing or treatment, which can create genuine moral distress without a clean resolution.
- An indolent, low-grade finding (like small lymphocytic lymphoma) that plausibly explains one part of a presentation (diffuse lymphadenopathy) should still be scrutinized against the full clinical picture — if it doesn't proportionally explain the severity of another major finding (here, an extensive, multifocal thrombotic burden), the reasoning chain isn't actually closed, even if it's tempting to stop there.

## Problem Representation

A man in his 60s with decompensated schizophrenia and a recent hospitalization for MRSA bacteremia and stroke presents for routine Foley catheter care and is incidentally found to have bilateral pulmonary emboli, multifocal cerebral infarcts, a right lower lobe cavitary lesion, bladder wall thickening, and diffuse infradiaphragmatic lymphadenopathy, ultimately biopsy-proven as small lymphocytic lymphoma — a diagnosis that plausibly explains the lymphadenopathy but not the full severity of the thrombotic burden.

## Case Narrative

Reza opened by describing a case he said would braid together diagnosis, management, and communication in a way he considered unusually important. He took Robbie straight to the same starting point he himself had encountered: a man in his 60s who arrived at the emergency department with a Foley catheter in place, there — per the documented chief complaint — purely for routine catheter care. As is standard for any ED visit, he underwent some workup, and somewhere along the way that workup escalated into CT imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis.

The findings were extensive. The chest CT showed multiple bilateral pulmonary emboli and a small, roughly one-centimeter cavitary lesion in the right lower lobe. The abdomen and pelvis CT showed a markedly thickened bladder wall with a radiologist's concern for a possible mass, along with diffuse lymphadenopathy concentrated below the diaphragm. A head CT, obtained in the same workup, showed areas of recent ischemic stroke, and a follow-up MRI confirmed multiple acute infarcts scattered across both cerebral hemispheres.

Robbie, hearing all of this delivered as a single dense block of findings from a patient whose only stated complaint was catheter care, reacted with genuine disbelief at the gap between the presenting complaint and the imaging findings — joking that whoever took the original history should reconsider their career, before acknowledging that the gap was simply a function of how the case had been compressed for teaching purposes.

He organized his thinking into two categories: an intravascular problem and an extravascular one. Intravascularly, the patient had both venous and arterial thromboses — pulmonary emboli and cerebral infarcts together. Robbie was explicit that this combination isn't just an ordinary hypercoagulable state; it deserves its own label, which he calls "ultra-hypercoagulable," with one important caveat — if the patient has a patent foramen ovale, the arterial and venous "clots" may not be two separate processes at all, but the same paradoxical embolus crossing from the venous to the arterial circulation through the shunt.

Assuming no PFO, he laid out the ultra-hypercoagulable differential: heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, antiphospholipid syndrome, thrombotic microangiopathy, paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, Behçet's disease, VEXAS syndrome, hypereosinophilia, and cancer. The key discriminator, he argued, was the extravascular findings — the bladder wall thickening, the diffuse lymphadenopathy, the cavitary lung lesion. None of the other entities on that list typically produce tissue-level findings outside the blood vessels; cancer does. So both the base rate among ultra-hypercoagulable conditions and the specific problem representation (clotting plus extravascular disease) pointed toward malignancy as the working hypothesis — which, he noted, likely explained why a radiologist would flag something as subtle as focal bladder wall asymmetry: once bilateral PEs are on the scan, everything else gets read through a cancer-suspicious lens. For disseminated lymphadenopathy specifically, he reiterated that autoimmune disease is rarely the primary driver (sarcoidosis being a notable exception), leaving infection and cancer as the leading categories — with the right lower lobe cavitary lesion as a plausible infectious source, though its small size made it an unconvincing sole explanation for such widespread disease. His initial recommended workup: HIV and RPR as simple screens for infectious causes of disseminated lymphadenopathy, plus a broader risk-factor history for cancer.

Reza then filled in the human context the imaging alone couldn't capture. The patient had decompensated schizophrenia — actively hallucinating, paranoid, and psychiatrically unwell — and was intermittently refusing vital signs, labs, and medications. On exam, he was lying comfortably but resistant to engagement; auscultation and lymph node palpation were both effectively blocked by his refusal to cooperate. Nothing on the limited exam that was possible stood out. Because he lacked apparent capacity to consent to workup and treatment decisions, the team reached his sister, his designated power of attorney, who added the missing history: about three weeks earlier, he'd been hospitalized at another facility under successive involuntary psychiatric holds, where he was found to have MRSA bacteremia (treated with IV antibiotics) and suffered a stroke, for which he was started on aspirin.

Reza posed two questions to Robbie: how does the MRSA history change the anticoagulation calculus, and how should the team navigate the surrounding social and ethical complexity?

On anticoagulation, Robbie reasoned that the MRSA bacteremia and stroke raised a real possibility of septic emboli — that some of the filling defects seen on imaging, including in the pulmonary arteries, could represent infected material rather than bland thrombus, potentially from right-sided endocarditis. He noted this would be an unusually extensive presentation for septic emboli to reach the proximal pulmonary arteries, but not implausible. The critical distinction for anticoagulation safety, he said, was whether there was an associated mycotic aneurysm — since that, more than septic occlusion alone, drives hemorrhagic conversion risk. He recommended obtaining vessel imaging to look specifically for this, framing anticoagulation decisions along a spectrum: an absolute contraindication favors an IVC filter, no contraindication favors full anticoagulation, and a relative or uncertain contraindication often leads clinicians to unfractionated heparin without a bolus — reversible, and psychologically reassuring even without strong evidence behind the no-bolus practice specifically. Given the acuity and severity of this patient's clot burden, he leaned toward starting heparin cautiously rather than withholding anticoagulation. He also offered a useful discriminator for mycotic versus degenerative aneurysms: mycotic aneurysms tend to occur in unusually distal locations compared to the more proximal aneurysms typical of atherosclerotic disease — a teaching point he credited to a prior RLR episode on Bartonella-related vascular disease.

On the ethical dimension, Robbie was direct about the difficulty. He framed schizophrenia itself as a life-limiting diagnosis, with life expectancy meaningfully reduced compared to the general population — a fact he felt is recognized as a source of morbidity but under-recognized as a driver of mortality. The practical pathway, he said, starts with asking psychiatry whether the patient's capacity could ever be restored; if not, decision-making shifts to the DPOA. But even a clear DPOA directive can be logistically impossible to execute if the patient physically resists — pursuing a biopsy or invasive workup against a resistant patient's will can require sedation and force that create genuine moral distress, independent of what's theoretically indicated. He wondered aloud, half rhetorically, how this particular patient — someone who only wanted his catheter serviced — had ended up tolerating an hour-long MRI at all, suggesting that understanding what got him to cooperate (family persuasion, mood, rapport with a particular team member) might be as clinically useful as anything on the scan.

Reza confirmed psychiatry had been consulted and started the patient on Abilify, with some improvement, even as he remained on an involuntary hold. Reza then described his own working differential in real time: was this cancer causing an ultra-hypercoagulable state via marantic (non-bacterial thrombotic) endocarditis or a straightforward hypercoagulable process, a DVT with paradoxical embolism, or was this actually MRSA endocarditis directly causing the multifocal infarcts? He weighed against endocarditis: a normal white count, normal temperature, and only a single set of blood cultures collected so far (with results still pending at under 24 hours), plus difficulty explaining the extensive leg clot burden through endocarditis alone. He examined the patient's extremities specifically for injection sites (given his sister's disclosure of recent drug use) and found none. Neurology recommended unfractionated heparin, but Reza judged this impractical given the patient's inconsistent tolerance for blood draws needed to monitor PTT, and instead started enoxaparin — reasoning it was both a safer real-world option given the monitoring problem and defensible given his low confidence in an infective endocarditis explanation. He discussed this reasoning explicitly with the patient's sister, including the possibility of an adverse intracranial outcome, and she supported the decision.

A medical student's question — whether a fully clotted leg requires thrombolysis or thrombectomy rather than plain anticoagulation — let Robbie generalize the pulmonary embolism framework (intervene only when the right ventricle is suffering) to venous thrombosis generally: intervene beyond anticoagulation only when the tissue behind the clot is actually suffering, either acutely (arterial-pattern ischemia, warranting catheter-directed thrombolysis by interventional radiology) or chronically (venous ulceration, warranting vascular surgery intervention to restore outflow). Discomfort alone doesn't meet that bar; dysfunction does.

Turning to the extravascular findings, Robbie reasoned that bladder metastases and a bladder primary causing an isolated lung nodule would both be exceptionally rare, and recommended cystoscopy with sampling to evaluate the bladder directly — noting, with a wry aside about high smoking prevalence in schizophrenia (cited from a review article), that this history alone raises baseline cancer risk. Urology, once consulted, attributed the bladder wall thickening to the chronic Foley catheter itself rather than a mass, and sent urine cytology instead of proceeding to cystoscopy. For the cavitary lung lesion, pulmonology felt it was too small and nonspecific to pursue aggressively, favoring the possibility of an aspiration-related process (plausible given his substance use and psychiatric instability) and interval imaging over biopsy. For the disseminated lymphadenopathy, a hematology-oncology fellow (a resident's wife, consulted informally while on maternity leave) recommended PET-CT, which showed diffuse FDG avidity across multiple lymph nodes, including a large, accessible axillary node. Rather than excisional biopsy, she recommended multiple core biopsies of that node.

The biopsy returned small lymphocytic lymphoma (SLL) — the lymph node equivalent of chronic lymphocytic leukemia, among the most indolent lymphomas that exist. Robbie's response captured the tension at the heart of the case: SLL is an excellent explanation for diffuse lymphadenopathy, but it does not proportionally explain the degree of "ultra-hypercoagulability" this patient displayed. He framed two competing ways to resolve the mismatch. Either his original instinct that this represented a true ultra-hypercoagulable state was wrong — the patient may simply have ordinary venous thromboembolic disease (common, and frequently without any identifiable cause), with the arterial/cerebral component attributable either to a PFO or to the prior MRSA episode rather than to a separate arterial hypercoagulable process. Or the instinct was right, and the extent of multifocal cerebral disease is genuinely disproportionate to how mild the patient's MRSA course actually was (no valvular complications, no prolonged IV antibiotic course) — in which case SLL, while real, is unlikely to be the full answer, and the team should worry either about hidden transformation to a more aggressive lymphoma (like DLBCL) not captured by the sampled node, or an entirely separate, occult second malignancy, especially given how common incidental SLL/CLL is in older adults as an unrelated finding.

Robbie was candid that he didn't know the right next step — whether to push for immediate rebiopsy or PET-CT, or to let the disease declare itself over time — but was clear that the reasoning chain, as it stood, was not fully closed. Reza reported that the patient was ultimately transferred to the inpatient psychiatric ward with biopsy results pending resolution of other questions, appropriately anticoagulated, with psychiatric stabilization taking priority for now. Both hosts agreed that his hypercoagulability could plausibly be multifactorial — immobilization and age contributing on top of whatever underlying process is eventually found — and that the honest answer, for now, is that the story isn't finished. They closed by naming this explicitly as a case they intend to revisit, likely with a repeat PET-CT and rebiopsy once his psychiatric status is stable enough to support it.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Effect on Leading Hypothesis |
|---|---|
| Bilateral PEs plus multifocal cerebral infarcts in the same patient | Raises probability of an "ultra-hypercoagulable" process (arterial + venous) over a single-territory hypercoagulable state |
| Bladder wall thickening, diffuse infradiaphragmatic lymphadenopathy, RLL cavitary lesion (extravascular findings) | Raises probability of cancer specifically, since most other ultra-hypercoagulable entities lack extravascular manifestations |
| Recent MRSA bacteremia and stroke at an outside hospital | Raises probability of septic emboli/endocarditis as a contributor to the cerebral and pulmonary findings; complicates the anticoagulation decision |
| Normal white count and temperature, only one blood culture set pending, no injection sites found on exam | Lowers probability of active infective endocarditis as the primary driver of the current presentation |
| PET-CT shows diffuse FDG-avid lymphadenopathy with an accessible axillary node | Raises probability of a lymphoproliferative process as the unifying extravascular diagnosis |
| Core biopsy returns small lymphocytic lymphoma (an indolent lymphoma) | Confirms a real diagnosis for the lymphadenopathy but lowers confidence that it alone explains the severity of the thrombotic burden |
| Mild prior MRSA course (no valvular complications, no prolonged antibiotics) despite extensive multifocal cerebral disease | Raises probability that the cerebral findings reflect a true hypercoagulable process rather than being fully explained by the prior infection |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The episode is structured around resisting premature closure: finding SLL is genuinely satisfying because it offers a clean, biopsy-proven answer, and the temptation to stop there — "we found cancer, case closed" — is explicitly named and resisted by both hosts. Robbie's two-branch reasoning (either the ultra-hypercoagulable framing was wrong, or SLL is an incomplete answer) models comfort with holding a diagnosis as provisional even after a confirmatory biopsy returns, rather than treating "a cancer was found" as automatically synonymous with "the cancer that explains everything was found." The case also surfaces an underappreciated bias in how psychiatric illness can shape diagnostic thoroughness — both hosts explicitly reflect on the tension between wanting a complete workup and respecting the real, practical, and ethical limits imposed by a patient's illness and autonomy.

## Teaching Discussion

The core teaching contribution is the "ultra-hypercoagulable" framework itself: recognizing that combined arterial-and-venous thrombosis is a distinct clinical entity (barring a PFO) with its own narrower differential, and that the presence of extravascular findings is what should tip the leading hypothesis toward cancer specifically. The episode is equally valuable as a model of clinical humility under uncertainty at the finish line — accepting a real, biopsy-confirmed diagnosis while still naming that it doesn't fully account for the clinical picture, rather than forcing narrative closure. Finally, the extended discussion of capacity, DPOA decision-making, and the practical limits of what can be done to a resistant, decompensated patient is a rare and valuable treatment of the ethical and logistical texture that surrounds — and sometimes constrains — the "pure" diagnostic reasoning exercise.
