> [!summary] Scan Summary
> An older man walks into triage — not by ambulance — with 90 minutes of [[abrupt onset|abrupt-onset]] severe chest pain radiating to the top of his neck, restless and begging for a hot pack on his neck. [[Abrupt onset]] chest pain reflexively prioritizes [[vascular catastrophe]]: [[aortic dissection]], [[acute coronary syndrome|ACS]], and [[pulmonary embolism]]. Yet every test returns normal — vitals, exam, [[electrocardiogram|EKG]], point-of-care and high-sensitivity [[troponin]], electrolytes, renal function, D-dimer, [[chest radiograph]], and a [[CT angiography|CTA]] read by radiology as showing no dissection and no PE. The disconnect between an apoplectic patient and a stone-cold-normal workup is the whole case. The answer was on the imaging all along: **[[pneumoperitoneum|free air]] under the diaphragm**, initially missed by everyone, from a **[[perforated peptic ulcer|perforated duodenal ulcer]]**. He goes emergently to laparotomy for a [[Graham patch]] repair and recovers. The teaching spine is [[look at your own imaging]], the [[catastrophe schema]] (vascular / perforation / electrical), and the humility that normal early labs and a radiology read can both mislead when the patient looks far sicker than the data.

**Pearls**

- **[[Abrupt onset]] on the chest prioritizes [[vascular catastrophe]]** — dissection, ACS, PE — but a classic presentation of a rare disease is still rare; a "less than 2%" pretest probability of dissection is still worth a [[CT angiography|CTA]] because the cost of missing it dwarfs the cost of the scan.
- **Normal vital signs are not reassurance.** "Normal" is judged against a population reference, not the patient's own baseline (a habitual BP of 180 presenting at 100 is a catastrophe reported as "normal"), and reserves can fail minutes later.
- **Physical exam and imaging findings are asymmetric evidence**: their *presence* (mediastinal widening, pulse deficit) raises post-test probability sharply, but their *absence* does not lower it. Only ~15% of aortic dissections have a blood-pressure differential — and most asymmetry is from [[subclavian stenosis]], not dissection.
- **[[Look at your own imaging]].** Training the eye to spot "dark where it shouldn't be" — [[pneumoperitoneum|free air]] by the diaphragm — is a *different, more learnable* skill than reading subtle EKGs or subsegmental PEs; do it on every scan, and it pays off the one-in-a-thousand time it matters.
- **Early normal labs do not exclude catastrophe.** At 90 minutes the body hasn't mounted a lab response — the same reason [[gallstone pancreatitis]] can present with a normal [[lipase]] ("too fast"). Twenty-four hours later this man's labs would not be pretty.
- When the patient looks far sicker than the data, **re-analyze the data you already have** (and call radiology for a second look) rather than only ordering more.

**Problem Representation**
An older man with abrupt-onset, excruciating chest pain radiating to the neck and a completely normal cardiovascular and vascular workup, ultimately diagnosed with a perforated duodenal ulcer identified as free intraperitoneal air missed on the initial CT angiogram.

## Case Narrative

The patient should have arrived by ambulance but walks into triage instead, in visible distress. An hour and a half ago he had abrupt-onset severe chest pain, "right here in my chest and going to the top of my neck," and what he wants most, beyond an explanation, is a neck massage or a hot pack. Rabih presents the case and asks Reza — the discussant, under the weather this episode — to react to the opening alone.

Reza's frame is immediate and singular: **[[abrupt onset]] on the chest prioritizes [[vascular catastrophe]]**. He recalls a rapid response just days earlier for tearing chest pain radiating to the back, and lays out the category — a [[aortic dissection|dissection]] activating pain receptors that refer to the neck, or [[acute coronary syndrome|ACS]] — and declares he would run toward diagnostics (bilateral blood pressures, [[troponin]], [[electrocardiogram|EKG]], and a [[CT angiography|CTA]]) and not stop until he had an answer.

The data then arrive one point at a time, exactly as they did in real life. **Vitals and exam are normal.** Reza refuses the reassurance: "normal" is measured against a population standard, not the patient's own baseline, and the absence of exam findings — unlike their presence — carries little weight. A pressure of 60/40 would sharpen the picture, but a normal one in a patient describing rip-roaring radiating chest pain changes nothing about the plan. **The EKG shows normal sinus rhythm without ST/T changes.** This is genuinely reassuring against STEMI and lowers the likelihood of ACS overall (no transmural or subendocardial ischemia), though NSTEMI and unstable angina keep the door ajar; the absence of sinus tachycardia also softens the case for [[pulmonary embolism]] with RV strain. The residual worry remains [[aortic dissection]].

**Point-of-care troponin and electrolytes are normal.** Reza invokes the four pillars of diagnosing ACS — story, troponin, EKG, echo — noting the story was compatible but not classic while the trope and EKG argue against myocardial cell death; and he pointedly says a high creatinine would *not* have deterred him from the CTA, because the harm of missing dissection swamps the theoretical nephrotoxicity. **The portable chest x-ray is normal.** Here he cautions on test characteristics: mediastinal widening, if present, raises the probability of dissection substantially, but its absence does not lower it — and portable AP films are unreliable (his rapid-response patient had a strikingly "widened" mediastinum with a *negative* CTA). His mind stays fixed on abrupt, immediately life-threatening vascular disease.

Given normal vitals, exam, EKG, troponin, electrolytes, and x-ray at 15 minutes, Rabih poses the decision: wait for the send-out labs or escalate? Reza escalates without hesitation — a **stat CTA timed for dissection**, not just for PE — reasoning that he would rather order a hundred and catch one or two than be "judicious" and miss a catastrophe. Asked his pretest probability, he estimates **under 2%** for dissection in an all-comer with this presentation, and explains why the normal bilateral pressures don't help: pulse/BP asymmetry is only ~15% sensitive for dissection, and asymmetry itself is far more often due to [[subclavian stenosis]]. Rabih crystallizes it: a classic presentation of a rare disease is still a rare disease.

**The CTA returns: no PE, no dissection, "nothing in the chest."** More data pile up normal — CBC, renal panel, D-dimer, and high-sensitivity troponin. And yet the man looks terrible: restless, unable to sit still, hot pack pressed to his neck. Rabih, stuck and anxious, hands the problem back to Reza, who now splits the syndrome into two Venn diagrams — chest and neck — wonders about an intra-abdominal process referring to the chest (but the normal liver chemistries and white count argue against cholecystitis), and reaches for zoster or a neck x-ray, admitting he is nearly out of moves. Crucially, Rabih had **deliberately withheld that the abdomen was soft and non-tender**, so as not to plant a false clue.

The pivot is procedural rather than deductive: **[[look at your own imaging]]**. Rabih replays the CTA and walks Reza down to the diaphragm, asking what life-threatening entity lives in this region. Reza answers "perforation" — and there it is: **[[pneumoperitoneum|free air]]**, black pockets where none belong, once seen impossible to unsee, with additional intramural/submucosal air in the stomach. A dedicated CT abdomen/pelvis confirms a **likely [[perforated peptic ulcer|perforated duodenal ulcer]]** (no malignant appearance). He goes emergently to the operating room for an exploratory laparotomy with a **[[Graham patch]]** repair, and is discharged five days later, doing well.

Both discussants are emphatic that **they each missed it** — as did the initial read — and that the diagnosis was ultimately made by several people working together in a "circumferential" way. The unexplained thread is the **neck pain**, which was not incidental but one of the most dominant features; they speculate about a [[phrenic nerve]] / diaphragmatic route of referred pain (the way [[pericarditis]] refers to the trapezius and hepatic irritation to the shoulder), leaving it as an open, plausible mechanism rather than a settled fact.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Data point | Prior / framing | Effect on hypotheses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Abrupt-onset chest pain radiating to neck | Abrupt on chest → vascular catastrophe | Prioritizes dissection, ACS, PE; drives aggressive testing |
| Normal vitals + exam | "Normal" is vs population, not patient baseline | No reassurance; absence of findings ≠ lower probability |
| Normal EKG (no ST/T changes) | Story compatible | Excludes STEMI, lowers ACS; less RV strain → PE less likely; **dissection persists** |
| Normal troponin (POC + hs) + electrolytes | 4 pillars of ACS (story/trope/EKG/echo) | No myocyte death; ACS further down; dissection unaffected |
| Normal portable CXR (no wide mediastinum) | Widening raises, absence doesn't lower | Weakly reassuring; AP films unreliable; dissection still open |
| Normal bilateral arm pressures | Pulse deficit ~15% sensitive; asymmetry usually subclavian | Does **not** lower dissection probability meaningfully |
| CTA: no PE, no dissection (as read) | Ruled out the top vascular three | Removes vascular catastrophe; leaves unexplained sick patient |
| Normal CBC, renal, D-dimer, hs-troponin | Early (90 min) → no lab response yet | Normal labs do **not** exclude catastrophe ("too fast") |
| Soft, non-tender abdomen (withheld) | Benign belly exam | Falsely reassuring; perforation can present with soft abdomen |
| **Free air by the diaphragm on re-review** | Catastrophe schema includes perforation | Clinches **perforated duodenal ulcer** → emergent laparotomy |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The dominant error trap is **anchoring on the chest/vascular frame** and treating a cascade of normal tests as cumulative reassurance rather than as a growing mismatch between a very sick patient and benign data. The rescue is not more forward testing but **re-examining data already in hand** — a deliberate counter to the momentum of ordering the next study. Two asymmetry principles recur: exam and imaging findings confirm when present but do not exclude when absent, and early labs lag the pathology. The withheld soft-abdomen detail models how **not** to be anchored by a reassuring exam, since a perforated ulcer can present with a soft belly early. The case is framed throughout with explicit **[[diagnostic humility]]**: both experts, and the radiologist, missed it, and the win came from a system of people re-looking — the antidote to the fantasy that any one clinician catches everything.

## Teaching Discussion

**The [[catastrophe schema]] for chest pain.** Reza's organizing structure for immediately life-threatening chest pain is a triad — **[[vascular catastrophe]]** (dissection, ACS, PE), **perforation**, and **electrical discharge** (malignant arrhythmia). The case's lesson is that the third-string categories cannot be dropped just because the presentation "sounds vascular": perforation belongs on the list for chest and even neck pain, and here it was the answer. Widening the schema at the outset would have kept free air a live consideration when the vascular workup went cold.

**Abrupt onset is a high-value semantic qualifier.** A truly instantaneous, maximal-at-onset pain is the linguistic signature that should trigger the vascular/catastrophe pathway. Reza's pretest-probability discipline matters here: even at a <2% prior for dissection, the expected cost of a miss justifies the CTA. This is the correct handling of a **classic presentation of a rare disease** — the presentation raises the relative likelihood, but the disease's rarity keeps the absolute probability low, and management is driven by consequence, not just probability.

**Normal is a trap word.** Two flavors of "normal" mislead. Normal vital signs are referenced to a population, not the individual, so a large delta from a patient's own hypertensive baseline hides inside a "normal" number; and reserves can decompensate abruptly. Normal early labs reflect **timing**, not absence of disease — the same physiology that yields a normal [[lipase]] in hyperacute [[gallstone pancreatitis]] ("too fast") explains a normal CBC and renal panel 90 minutes into a perforation. Project the labs forward: 24 hours later they will not be normal.

**Asymmetric evidence.** A recurring test-characteristics lesson: for both physical exam and imaging, **presence clinches, absence doesn't clear.** Mediastinal widening on CXR, a pulse deficit, spider angiomata, jaundice — each markedly raises post-test probability when present, but none lowers it when absent. Concretely, the blood-pressure differential is only ~15% sensitive for [[aortic dissection]], and asymmetry is more commonly explained by [[subclavian stenosis]] in older adults — so normal bilateral pressures should never be used to "rule out" dissection.

**[[Look at your own imaging]] — and how to learn it.** The central procedural teaching is that clinicians on the front line should personally review their patients' scans, not to out-read the radiologist on subtle findings but to build a **do-not-miss reflex** for a small number of life-threatening patterns — chief among them **[[pneumoperitoneum|free air]]** as "dark where it shouldn't be," best hunted at the diaphragm on every study. Reza frames why this is learnable: spotting free air is easier to train than reading tricky ST-segments or subsegmental PEs. Two complementary learning strategies emerge: (1) look at a hundred normal films a day so the "converts-to-normal" repetition trains the eye, and (2) whenever a radiologist calls a life-threatening finding, go back and imprint that image (dissection, brain bleed, free air) so the next one — the one that gets missed — is caught. And when the patient looks far sicker than the data, add the human safeguard: call radiology and ask for a second pass.

**Referred pain and the unexplained neck.** The neck pain was the feature that most derailed the reasoning and remains incompletely explained. The discussants reason by analogy to known referral patterns — [[pericarditis]] to the trapezius, hepatic/diaphragmatic irritation to the shoulder via the [[phrenic nerve]] (C3–C5) — and hypothesize a diaphragm-to-cervical route for a perforation's pain. Held appropriately as conjecture, it is a reminder that atypical referral does not exclude a visceral catastrophe, and that a dominant, hard-to-place symptom deserves curiosity rather than dismissal.
