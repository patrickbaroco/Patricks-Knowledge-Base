## Pearls

- **Fever plus acute altered mental status is an inflammation-first syndrome — and in an elderly patient with AMS and no localizer, you are in the CNS ([[Meningoencephalitis|meningoencephalitis]]) until proven otherwise.** Build the clinical syndrome, then lay the *sequence of events* over it; competing localizers (back pain, dysuria, a positive UA) legitimately open extracranial sources, but the do-not-miss default stays until excluded.
- **Read the *character* of the encephalopathy, not just its presence.** A hyperactive, agitated, crying-out patient is atypical for a systemic/toxic-metabolic [[Encephalopathy|encephalopathy]], which usually *dulls and slows* the sensorium — and that single qualitative feature can outweigh a pile of extracranial explanations and keep the brain and spinal cord on the table.
- **Interpret a negative test by its sensitivity for the feared disease.** Non-contrast CT of the spine is ~80% sensitive for [[Vertebral Osteomyelitis|vertebral osteomyelitis]] but only ~15% for [[Spinal Epidural Abscess|epidural abscess]], so a "negative" spine CT excludes almost nothing — the study has to match the target, and here a normal CT gave false reassurance. [[Test Interpretation|The test's power depends on the disease.]]
- **When most of a syndrome improves but a piece persists, force three explanations: incomplete diagnosis, multiple diagnoses, or a complication of the first.** The AKI, uremia, and [[Pyelonephritis|pyelonephritis]] all resolved while the mental status did not move — proof the encephalopathy was never the GU disease, and the cue to shift the [[Center of Gravity|center of gravity]] onto the residual (the pain and the confusion).
- **Spinal cord compression is a time-critical emergency, reasoned like ACS or stroke — the earlier the decompression, the better the recovery ("walk in with paresis, walk out").** In an un-examinable encephalopathic patient, reflexes and withdrawal-to-pain substitute for a participatory motor exam, and the threshold for a contrasted MRI should be low. [[Analogical Reasoning|Time-to-treatment reasoning]] borrowed from vascular emergencies.
- **When you are stuck with no anchor, go back to the bedside and re-take the history.** Re-interviewing the husband reframed the case around *progressive* low-back pain and new leg weakness (she couldn't stand the day of admission) — the myelopathy hiding under the encephalopathy — and the anatomy ([[Batson Venous Plexus|Batson's valveless venous plexus]] linking renal/pelvic drainage to the epidural space) explains how pyelonephritis seeded an [[Spinal Epidural Abscess|epidural abscess]], one of the most commonly missed, most-litigated diagnoses in medicine.

## Problem Representation

A 65-year-old woman with severe rheumatoid arthritis on tofacitinib, type 2 diabetes, and nephrolithiasis presents with acute agitated altered mental status and fever on a background of a cascading week — an urgent-care influenza diagnosis treated with oseltamivir and prednisone, then an ED visit for dysuria and myalgias with a "positive" UA and a CK over 1000 treated as a UTI with cephalexin — now with worsening low back pain and encephalopathy progressing over 24–48 hours. She is febrile and tachycardic with a hyperactive, non-localizing encephalopathy, ill-localized low-back tenderness, severe AKI (creatinine 4.5 from normal, BUN 115, bicarbonate 15), a neutrophilic leukocytosis with a mild anemia and thrombocytopenia, and a florid pyuria (>200 WBC, WBC clumps, bacteria, 81 RBC, 2+ protein, positive nitrite), with heavy recent NSAID use; non-contrast imaging (done because of the AKI) shows an edematous left kidney with perinephric stranding and an unremarkable spine and head. The renal function, uremia, and Klebsiella pyelonephritis improve rapidly with hydration and NSAID cessation, but the encephalopathy and pain persist unchanged; an MRI-negative brain workup and repeated failed LPs leave the picture unexplained until a return to the bedside reframes the case around progressive back pain and lower-extremity weakness, and a contrasted pan-spinal MRI reveals an extensive lumbar-to-thoracic epidural abscess compressing the conus — a Klebsiella spinal epidural abscess (seeded from pyelonephritis via Batson's plexus) with cord compression as the unifying diagnosis, confirmed at neurosurgical washout with resolution of the encephalopathy within 24 hours.

## Case Narrative

Guest Dr. Will Cope presents a case from his first week as an attending: a 65-year-old woman with severe rheumatoid arthritis on tofacitinib (a JAK inhibitor), type 2 diabetes, and nephrolithiasis, brought for altered mental status. The history, mostly from the husband, is a week-long cascade: five days prior, fevers, body aches, and congestion diagnosed as influenza at urgent care and treated with oseltamivir and a 5-day prednisone course; three days prior, an ED visit for dysuria and myalgias with a UA "positive for infection" and a CK over 1000, treated as a UTI with cephalexin; then a return for worsening back pain (attributed to her RA) plus new altered mental status over 24–48 hours. Normally sharp and working as a manager, she is now agitated, screaming intermittently, in evident pain but unable to localize or answer questions.

Reza frames the up-front challenge as separating [[Signal versus Noise|signal from noise]] — often possible only in retrospect — and works the HPI by first naming the clinical syndrome and then overlaying the sequence of events. The syndrome is acute, *hyperactive* altered mental status with fever, which triggers an inflammation schema rooted first in infection; the AMS helps localize the site, making [[Meningoencephalitis|meningoencephalitis]] the do-not-miss default in an elderly febrile patient, while the back pain and dysuria open the spine and urinary tract as alternatives. Laying on the sequence: the flu diagnosis may have been wrong, or right and now driving immune dysregulation; the UTI may be real but is an odd cause of this syndrome unless *ascending* ([[Pyelonephritis|pyelonephritis]]); and the CK elevation, while common with viral illness, narrows toward specific bacteria (e.g., [[Legionella|Legionella]]) if not viral. Rabih adds the immune lens: RA itself is not very immunosuppressive, but the JAK inhibitor may be, and — crucially — *infections* immunosuppress too, with influenza classically predisposing to post-influenza [[Staphylococcus aureus|staph]], strep, and *fungal* superinfection.

The exam: febrile-range physiology with tachycardia, a hyperactive encephalopathy (alert and oriented ×0, crying out, tracking intermittently), ill-localized low-back tenderness, and a non-participatory neuro exam with symmetric reflexes. Rabih makes the pivotal read on the *character* of the confusion: her crying out and hyperactivity are unusual for the dulling, suppressive effect of a systemic [[Encephalopathy|encephalopathy]], so despite the many extracranial "hits" (kidney, back, muscle) that would lower anyone's threshold to be confused, this subtle qualitative feature outweighs them and keeps him localizing *within* the brain — parenchyma, vasculature, ventricles, or meninges.

The labs bring severe AKI (creatinine 4.5, BUN 115, bicarbonate 15) and a florid pyuria (>200 WBC/hpf, WBC clumps, bacteria, 81 RBC, 2+ protein, 1+ nitrite, 3+ leukocyte esterase), with a WBC of 17k, hemoglobin 9.8 (from 12), and platelets 130 (from 240). Reza unleashes the AKI [[Acute Kidney Injury|schema]] — prerenal, intrinsic, postrenal — clearing postrenal with a normal ultrasound, and dwelling on the intrinsic causes: [[Acute Tubular Necrosis|ATN]] (base rate, from prerenal or inflammation), myoglobinuric injury (trend the CK "like a troponin"), [[Interstitial Nephritis|interstitial nephritis]] (the pyuria; think Legionella), and [[Rapidly Progressive Glomerulonephritis|glomerulonephritis]] (the proteinuria and hematuria — get sediment for casts and dysmorphic cells). He reads the UA against the host (diabetic proteinuria vs inflammatory), notes nitrite implies gram-negatives, and cautions that negative ketones don't exclude ketosis (no beta-hydroxybutyrate measured). Rabih takes the CBC: a neutrophilic leukocytosis fits sepsis, but anemia-with-thrombocytopenia invokes the [[Anemia and Thrombocytopenia|five-cause schema]] — [[Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia|MAHA]] (the no-miss, checked on smear), sepsis (the most common, mild here), reduced production (cf. [[RLR 032 - Fever and Chronic Myelomonocytic Leukemia|Will's prior CMML case]]), separate mechanisms (e.g., anemia of chronic disease plus HIT), or one causing the other — and he expands the infectious spotlight to disseminated molds given her diabetes ([[Mucormycosis|mucor]]) and post-influenza state ([[Aspergillosis|aspergillus]]).

The ED could not obtain an LP; a pan-CT (non-contrast, because of the AKI) shows an edematous left kidney with perinephric stranding but no hydronephrosis, and she is started on broad antibiotics (vancomycin, cefepime, ampicillin, metronidazole), acyclovir, and oseltamivir with aggressive fluids — a regimen Reza decodes as empiric coverage for meningoencephalitis (acyclovir for HSV, ampicillin for [[Listeria|Listeria]] over 50, vanc/cefepime for bacterial causes) layered on pyelonephritis coverage. A new history detail lands: she had been taking far more naproxen than usual for the back pain. Rabih uses it two ways — it partly explains the AKI severity, and it *highlights the back pain* as a prominent feature — then delivers the number that reframes the imaging: the sensitivity of spine CT is ~80% for vertebral osteomyelitis but only ~15% for [[Spinal Epidural Abscess|epidural abscess]], so the negative non-contrast CT is nearly useless for the thing that matters. He credits the CT for the *lungs* but not for head, abdomen, or spine, where MRI and contrast are far better.

The course tightens the vise. Renal was consulted and HD consented, but the creatinine improved dramatically with hydration and stopping NSAIDs (to 1.95 by day 3); the urine grew >100,000 CFU of [[Klebsiella|Klebsiella]]. Yet over 48–72 hours — precisely when treated pyelonephritis should improve — the mental status stayed *exactly* the same, with the patient screaming at any movement of any limb, forehead, or back, unable to localize. Reza makes the inference explicit: whatever the GU inflammation, it is *not* the cause of the encephalopathy, so the search returns to the CNS with a now-broadly-treated patient (is this an atypical or non-bacterial process?). Rabih offers the "improved-in-some, unchanged-in-others" reasoning: consider an incomplete diagnosis, [[Hickam's Dictum|multiple diagnoses]], or a *complication* of the first (PRES from the renal/BP swings; influenza-related brain complications), and he re-scrutinizes the CBC trends. Reza adds cefepime and acyclovir neurotoxicity (both need renal dysfunction) and malignant [[Catatonia|catatonia]] (a symptom, high-CK) — but flags that the encephalopathy *predated* the culprit drugs.

The extensive CNS workup is unrevealing: negative blood cultures, negative serum meningoencephalitis panel, a non-status EEG, repeat failed LPs (even under anesthesia by IR), and repeat contrasted CT and MRI of the head that are normal. Rabih builds the [[MRI-Negative Brain Disease|MRI-negative brain disease]] schema — antibody-mediated encephalitis and small-vessel vasculitis (esoteric), migraine/psych, and TIA (the most common) as *intrinsic* MRI-negative causes — but stresses they don't even know the problem is intrinsic to the brain, elevating sneaky *extrinsic* causes: hyperammonemia, nutritional deficiency, and toxins/medications, with cefepime/acyclovir as the standouts (undercut by timing). His pretest probability for intrinsic brain disease drops, and the LP remains important because untreated meningoencephalitis is a humbling MRI-negative cause — though it should not stay perfectly *static*. Reza confesses he is "totally lost."

Will names the lesson he took from the case: when you don't know what is going on, go back to the bedside. Re-interviewing the husband, he learns nothing strictly new but reframes everything around the back: two weeks of worsening low-back pain, debilitating in the 48 hours before admission with intermittent screaming, then *progressive difficulty ambulating*, and on the day of admission legs too weak to stand. Reza connects it to the first aliquot's spinal-infection hypothesis (discitis/osteomyelitis/epidural abscess) and Rabih's CT-insensitivity teaching: this is now [[Spinal Cord Compression|spinal cord compression]] until proven otherwise — a time-critical emergency reasoned by [[Analogical Reasoning|analogy]] to door-to-balloon in ACS and door-to-needle in stroke ("walk in with paresis, walk out"). Rabih notes he reserves the reflex hammer precisely for the altered patient who can't participate, and would check withdrawal to pain while the MRI is arranged. With the AKI now resolved (making contrast safe — the earlier non-contrast studies had been driven by [[Nephrogenic Systemic Fibrosis|gadolinium/NSF]] and contrast-nephropathy fears), a contrasted pan-spinal MRI shows an extensive epidural abscess from the lumbar into the thoracic spine, anteriorly displacing the cord with moderate-to-severe compression of the conus and proximal cauda equina. A medical student supplies the mechanism from anatomy class: [[Batson Venous Plexus|Batson's valveless venous plexus]] connects renal/pelvic venous drainage to the spinal epidural space, and severe pyelonephritis is a reported route to epidural abscess. Neurosurgery washes out gross pus that grows Klebsiella, and her mental status returns to baseline within 24 hours.

In reflection, Rabih notes the epidural abscess is among the most commonly missed and most-litigated diagnoses, made torturous here by an encephalopathy that both obscured the cord signs and was, unexpectedly, *caused* by the abscess (proven by its resolution) — a humbling reminder of how limited the neurologic exam becomes in a confused patient, and of the power of returning to the bedside. Reza's [[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive autopsy]] centers on the imaging gap: contrast was withheld for the AKI (NSF and contrast-nephropathy concerns), and the confounded neuro exam hid the paresis that would have forced earlier contrasted imaging; both hosts are candid that, given the agitation-limited exam, they likely could not have reached it faster than the team did.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Signal versus Noise|Signal versus noise]] and syndrome-then-sequence.** Reza's opening discipline is to name the clinical syndrome (acute hyperactive AMS + fever) and only then overlay the temporal sequence, converting a flood of data into a structured inflammation schema — while conceding the signal/noise split is often clear only in retrospect.
- **Reading the [[Encephalopathy|encephalopathy]]'s character as the highest-yield sign.** Rabih lets one qualitative feature — hyperactivity where suppression is expected — outweigh the many extracranial explanations, a model of weighting a discriminating exam finding over a larger volume of consistent-but-nonspecific data.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Interpreting a negative test by disease-specific sensitivity]].** The set-piece is the spine CT: ~80% sensitive for vertebral osteo, ~15% for epidural abscess, so a normal study is near-worthless for the feared diagnosis. The corollary is matching the modality to the target (MRI/contrast for cord and abscess) and reading the pan-scan credit selectively (good for lungs, weak elsewhere).
- **[[Diagnostic Momentum|Momentum]] and the partial-response reframe.** Pyelonephritis was a good, well-supported hypothesis — and the trap was letting it explain the *encephalopathy*. The corrective is the "improved in some, unchanged in others → incomplete / multiple / complication" rule, which pulls the [[Center of Gravity|center of gravity]] onto the residual features and reopens the CNS/spine.
- **[[Anatomical Approach|Anatomical reasoning]], brain to spine.** Localization runs from brain compartments (parenchyma/vasculature/ventricle/meninges) to the spinal cord and epidural space, and the final mechanistic bridge is pure anatomy — [[Batson Venous Plexus|Batson's plexus]] linking the renal bed to the epidural space — the kind of structural link that unifies a GU infection with a cord syndrome.
- **[[Return to the Bedside|Return to the bedside]] and [[Analogical Reasoning|time-critical analogy]].** Will's meta-lesson — when anchorless, re-take the history — reframes the case around progressive weakness, and Reza reasons cord compression as an emergency by analogy to ACS/stroke time-to-treatment. The closing [[Cognitive Autopsy|autopsy]] and shared [[Diagnostic Humility|humility]] (the contrast-withheld imaging gap; the exam confounded by agitation; "I could not have gotten there faster") model non-defensive review of a near-miss on one of medicine's most-missed diagnoses.

## Teaching Discussion

- Approach fever with acute altered mental status by first naming the syndrome and defaulting to meningoencephalitis in an elderly patient without a localizer, then overlaying the sequence of events; keep the do-not-miss CNS diagnosis alive even as extracranial localizers (back pain, dysuria, a positive UA) open competing sources.
- Weigh the *character* of an encephalopathy: a hyperactive, agitated presentation is atypical for a dulling systemic/toxic-metabolic process and should keep intrinsic brain and spinal-cord disease on the differential even when multiple extracranial insults could plausibly lower the threshold for confusion.
- Interpret negative imaging by its sensitivity for the specific feared disease — non-contrast spine CT is ~80% sensitive for vertebral osteomyelitis but only ~15% for epidural abscess — and match the modality to the target (contrasted MRI for cord compression and abscess), rather than treating any negative scan as reassurance.
- Use the partial-response pattern as a diagnostic instrument: when the AKI, uremia, and infection resolve but the mental status does not, the encephalopathy was never explained by those problems, so consider an incomplete diagnosis, a second diagnosis, or a complication, and move the center of gravity onto the persistent feature.
- Treat spinal cord compression as a time-critical emergency analogous to ACS and stroke, obtain urgent contrasted MRI, and in an un-examinable encephalopathic patient substitute reflexes and withdrawal-to-pain for a participatory motor exam, recognizing that even expert neurologic exams are limited by profound encephalopathy.
- Go back to the bedside and re-take the history when you have no anchor; re-framing around progressive back pain and new leg weakness — not new data, but reweighted data — is what surfaced the myelopathy, and the anatomy of Batson's valveless venous plexus explains how pyelonephritis can seed a spinal epidural abscess.
- Keep the anemia-plus-thrombocytopenia five-cause schema (MAHA, sepsis, reduced production, separate mechanisms, one-causing-the-other) and the AKI schema (prerenal/intrinsic/postrenal, with ATN by base rate and interstitial nephritis/GN as alternatives) as reusable structures, and remember that infections — influenza especially — can themselves immunosuppress and predispose to bacterial and fungal superinfection.
