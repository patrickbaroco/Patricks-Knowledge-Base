## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 32-year-old man with three days of an ordinary-sounding flu-like illness tests positive for influenza A and is essentially ready for discharge — until a nearly-forgotten EKG comes back reading "STEMI." What follows is a careful, disciplined workup (troponin, cardiology consultation, bedside echo) that correctly avoids overtreating a young, chest-pain-free patient for a heart attack he almost certainly isn't having, while never dismissing the alarming tracing outright. The real answer only surfaces once a sharp-eyed cardiology fellow asks about family history: a Brugada ECG pattern, unmasked by fever and coincidentally treated by an antipyretic given for entirely different reasons. The case becomes a meditation on how context reframes the same data, and on the discipline required to let a rare diagnosis emerge without ever skipping the steps that protect against a common, dangerous one.

## Pearls

- A patient's decision to seek emergency care for symptoms nearly everyone has experienced and self-treated (like a flu-like illness) is itself a data point worth interrogating — asking directly what specifically prompted the visit, and whether the patient is a frequent presenter or someone who rarely seeks care, helps calibrate how seriously to weigh the presentation.
- Muscle symptoms can be organized into three overlapping categories — myalgia (pain without inflammation or weakness), myositis (inflammation of the muscle, which may be autoimmune, drug-induced, infection-induced, or endocrine-induced), and myopathy (true weakness) — and a creatine kinase level is a useful branch-point test to determine whether a myalgia-predominant presentation also involves myositis.
- Polymyalgia rheumatica is a useful example of a disease that can present with prominent myalgia and some myositis but no true myopathy/weakness — patients experience disabling stiffness limiting daily activities without measurable strength loss on exam.
- Even when a presentation is atypical for a common process, that atypicality should be weighed against how much more common the common process is relative to any less common alternative — a somewhat atypical presentation of a common disease remains statistically more likely than a textbook presentation of a rare one.
- A confirmatory positive test for one plausible diagnosis (e.g., influenza) does not exclude a second, unrelated process — a "case closed" reflex after a single positive result should not override other pending or ordered data (like a still-outstanding EKG) that were obtained for independent reasons.
- Systematic EKG interpretation benefits from a consistent structure: first assess rhythm (narrow or wide, fast or slow, regular or irregular), then establish the isoelectric baseline (using the PR interval or TP segment) before assessing ST segments for elevation or depression, organized by anatomic lead groupings (anterior, lateral, inferior).
- ST-segment elevation does not equal ST-elevation myocardial infarction — a true STEMI requires both troponin elevation and a mechanism consistent with dying myocardium, and the morphology of the elevation (a reassuring "happy face"/J-point pattern versus a concerning "tombstone" pattern) provides additional, though imperfect, discriminating information.
- Reciprocal ST depression in leads anatomically opposite the leads showing ST elevation is part of the diagnostic criteria for true STEMI and helps distinguish it from other causes of diffuse ST elevation (such as pericarditis, which classically shows elevation without a reciprocal depression pattern).
- The most useful evaluation of possible acute coronary syndrome synthesizes four data points together — history (especially the presence or absence of chest pain), the EKG, the troponin, and the echocardiogram — and a patient with only one of these four abnormal (an alarming EKG alone) should prompt continued caution rather than either dismissal or full activation of an emergency cardiac pathway.
- STEMI response pathways are designed to tolerate a meaningful false-positive rate — activating a full STEMI response and being wrong is an acceptable, expected cost of a system built for speed; in atypical or confusing presentations, directly consulting a cardiology fellow for urgent focused input is a reasonable intermediate step short of full activation.
- A large observational body of evidence links viral syndromes (particularly influenza and RSV) and sepsis to a higher incidence of true STEMI, likely via a hyperadrenergic, systemically inflamed physiologic state — meaning troponin elevation in this context should not be automatically dismissed as pure demand ischemia, even though the pretest probability calculation still depends heavily on age and risk factors.
- Sustained tachycardia is atypical in a patient having a large STEMI without underlying heart failure, since significant myocardial infarction — particularly involving the AV node's blood supply — more classically produces bradyarrhythmia rather than tachycardia; tachycardia in this context is a soft but real point against a large evolving infarct.
- The Brugada EKG pattern in leads V1-V2 resembles a right bundle branch block (with an R and R' morphology) but critically lacks the deep S waves seen in the lateral leads (V5-V6) that true RBBB produces — this distinction (deep lateral S waves present in RBBB, absent in Brugada) is a key discriminator between the two patterns.
- The identical EKG finding can be interpreted completely differently depending on the clinical context it is encountered in: a Brugada-like pattern found incidentally during a workup for a febrile illness is unlikely to be recognized as such, while the same pattern found during a syncope workup with a family history of sudden cardiac death is immediately suggestive of Brugada — underscoring how the framing a clinician enters a case with shapes which findings register as diagnostically significant.
- "Brugada pattern" (the EKG finding alone) and "Brugada syndrome" (the EKG finding plus a clinical history of arrhythmic events or high-risk features) are distinct: most patients with the EKG pattern never experience clinical arrhythmic consequences, and the two exist on a spectrum of underlying sodium channel dysfunction severity.
- Fever is a well-described trigger that can unmask or exacerbate the Brugada EKG pattern and precipitate a transition from asymptomatic to a potentially lethal arrhythmia — making aggressive fever avoidance and treatment one of the few clinical circumstances where treating fever itself (rather than only its underlying cause) is an important, specific intervention.
- Implanting an ICD in a young patient with a Brugada pattern but no confirmed arrhythmic event requires a high burden of proof; ambulatory monitoring (an implantable loop recorder) and close electrophysiology follow-up, rather than immediate device placement, is the appropriate next step when the diagnosis remains "putative."
- Recognizing an uncommon diagnosis mid-case should never come at the cost of skipping the essential, potentially life-saving steps required to evaluate the far more common and time-sensitive possibility (here, ACS) — the correct sequence is to rule out the dangerous common diagnosis using the standard, disciplined workup, and let the rarer diagnosis emerge from that same rigorous process rather than jumping to it prematurely.

## Problem Representation

A 32-year-old man with three days of an otherwise unremarkable flu-like illness (confirmed influenza A) found incidentally to have an alarming EKG with ST elevation in V1-V2 and diffuse ST/T-wave changes, initially worked up and reasonably de-escalated as a possible STEMI given a negative troponin and normal bedside echocardiogram, ultimately identified — once family history of sudden cardiac death and nocturnal dyspneic episodes was elicited — as a fever-unmasked Brugada ECG pattern (type 1 evolving to type 2 as the fever resolved), managed with fever avoidance counseling, medication-avoidance guidance, ambulatory monitoring, and electrophysiology follow-up rather than immediate defibrillator placement.

## Case Narrative

Rabih presented a 32-year-old man at the VA whose triage chief complaint was "flu-like illness." Reza, working from the label alone before further data, reasoned that flu-like symptoms are near-universal human experiences typically self-managed at home (NyQuil, Tylenol, fluids, rest) over three to four days without ever prompting an ED visit — making the decision to actually present for care itself a meaningful data point. His first question would be why this particular patient, apparently young and previously healthy, chose to come in: whether he was a frequent presenter for whom this was routine, or someone who rarely sought care and had been genuinely tipped over some threshold. Absent a red flag in the history, exam, or labs, he would not extend the working diagnosis beyond a routine viral illness, and would send a nasopharyngeal swab for flu, COVID, and RSV, favoring history over exam as the more informative tool at this stage.

Rabih confirmed the history: three days of sore throat, rhinorrhea, cough, and body aches — with myalgias notably more prominent than any prior illness the patient could recall, concerning enough to him that it factored into his decision to seek care. He denied shortness of breath, abdominal pain, vomiting (mild nausea only), rash, or dysuria, and reported multiple family members with a similar illness, some diagnosed with "the flu" (used colloquially rather than confirmed influenza) at urgent care. Exam showed mild pharyngeal erythema and rhinorrhea, otherwise unremarkable, but vital signs were notable: temperature 38.6°C and heart rate 124, with normal respiratory rate and oxygen saturation; the patient appeared mildly fatigued.

Reza focused on the prominence of the myalgias as the single most informative detail, framing muscle symptoms along three overlapping categories — myalgia, myositis, and myopathy — and recommended a creatine kinase level as a key branch-point test to assess for an inflammatory myositis component (which can be autoimmune, drug-induced, infection-induced, or endocrine-induced, not only rheumatologic). He referenced a related VMR case discussion about infection triggering an autoimmune process, and cited polymyalgia rheumatica as an example of a disease with prominent myalgia and some myositis but no true myopathy. Given the absence of any exam finding suggesting myopathy, he did not think a full neuromuscular workup was indicated yet, but recommended a CBC, BMP, CK, and — since IV access was already being placed — a chest X-ray given the cough, even without tachypnea or hypoxemia. He remained explicit that he was not yet dissuaded from a routine, self-limited viral illness as the most probable explanation, reiterating that an atypical presentation of a common process is still more likely than a typical presentation of an uncommon one.

Given the patient's fatigue, myalgia severity, and tachycardia, Rabih opted to bring him to a bed for IV fluids rather than oral antipyretics, ordered the respiratory viral swab, and — "out of an abundance of caution" — also ordered an EKG. The flu swab returned positive for influenza A within about 45 minutes, and Rabih, satisfied the case was essentially resolved, planned to let the fluids finish and discharge the patient. Roughly an hour later, a visibly alarmed nurse brought him the EKG — which had been technically completed but nearly overlooked amid the perceived resolution of the case — reading "STEMI."

Reza worked through the EKG using a structured approach: first the rhythm (narrow, regular, normal rate — normal sinus rhythm based on upright P waves in leads I and II), then the ST segments, using the PR interval/TP segment to establish the isoelectric baseline. He identified ST elevation in V1-V2 (more than 2mm, without a reassuring "happy face"/J-point morphology), normal V3-V4, and what appeared to be ST depression in V5-V6; he specifically checked for reciprocal depression — a required feature distinguishing true STEMI from diffuse-elevation mimics like pericarditis — finding ST depression and T-wave inversion in the inferior leads (II, III, aVF) and a subtly elevated aVR (concerning, if present, for left main disease). He summarized the tracing as normal sinus rhythm with concerning ST elevation in V1-V2, likely reciprocal depression in the inferior and lateral leads, and mild aVR elevation.

Both hosts converged on caution rather than alarm, given the clinical mismatch: a healthy 32-year-old with no chest pain (the near-universal expected symptom of a true STEMI, absent underlying neuropathy) having a silent large infarct would be unusual. Reza recommended a stat troponin and direct engagement of a cardiologist to review the tracing, citing a NEJM review linking viral syndromes (specifically influenza and RSV) and sepsis to a genuinely higher observed incidence of true STEMI via a hyperadrenergic, systemically inflamed state — meaning the troponin/EKG findings could not be automatically dismissed as pure demand physiology, even in a young patient. He weighed this against the patient's age and complete absence of symptoms as reasons not to immediately activate a full STEMI pathway.

Rabih described his real-time, reflexive actions under time pressure: he ordered aspirin 325mg and a statin (reasoning the potential benefit outweighed the minimal risk if this were truly ACS), told the patient his EKG showed "unexpected" findings without alarming him, and confirmed the patient remained completely asymptomatic. Rather than activating the full STEMI pager, he opted to directly involve a cardiology fellow. A point-of-care troponin returned normal within three minutes, followed by unremarkable routine labs and chest X-ray. After a brief delay without a callback, Rabih found the cardiology fellow already at the bedside performing a stat echocardiogram — visibly concerned by the EKG's appearance, but finding a normal ejection fraction and no focal wall motion abnormality on a "pristine" echo.

Reza affirmed the aspirin-and-statin decision as correct given its minimal downside and potentially life-saving upside, and raised myocarditis (potentially subclinical, without overt heart failure signs) as an alternative explanation for ST changes in the setting of a viral illness. He introduced a four-data-point framework for evaluating possible ACS — history, EKG, troponin, and echocardiogram — noting that in this patient, only one of the four (the EKG) was abnormal, which substantially lowered the probability not just of STEMI from plaque rupture, but of the broader category of causes for infarct-pattern EKG changes (vasospasm, MINOCA, embolus, spontaneous coronary artery dissection). Working through remaining explanations for the ST changes, he set aside pericarditis and aneurysm as poor fits, considered myocarditis unlikely to produce this degree of finding without any corroborating labs or echo abnormality, and flagged Takotsubo as a formal possibility — while noting the tracing's morphology looked more concerning than the reassuring patterns sometimes seen incidentally in thin patients. He recommended repeating the EKG (confirming correct lead placement) and rechecking troponin, adding a specific pearl: sustained tachycardia argues somewhat against a large evolving STEMI in the absence of heart failure, since major infarction (particularly involving the AV node's blood supply) more classically produces bradyarrhythmia. His conclusion: he would not activate the STEMI pager, but would repeat testing and continue engaging cardiology.

One hour later, with the patient defervesced and his tachycardia resolved after fluids and Toradol, a repeat troponin was again negative, and cardiology had independently reached the same conclusions. A repeat EKG showed the inferior lead ST depression and T-wave inversions had normalized, and the V1-V2 changes were far less dramatic (a subtle, isolated ST elevation in V2). Reza used this to distinguish clinically meaningful "dynamic" EKG changes (as would occur with true pseudo-normalization during ongoing ischemia accompanied by chest pain and troponin rise) from this patient's benign-context evolution, in which no chest pain or troponin elevation accompanied the change.

The diagnostic turn came when the cardiology fellow, examining the serial EKGs together, took a more targeted history: the patient was of Southeast Asian descent, and two older family members (an uncle and aunt) had died of sudden cardiac death; he also reported three prior episodes of profound difficulty breathing that woke him from sleep, and occasional palpitations, without any history of syncope. Reza immediately recognized the reframed significance of the V1-V2 findings: a Brugada-pattern morphology, resembling a right bundle branch block (R, R' complex) but critically lacking the deep S waves in V5-V6 that true RBBB produces (a distinction rooted in the different depolarization vector), consistent with either a type 1 or type 2 Brugada pattern. He emphasized that this same EKG, encountered in the context of a febrile illness, would essentially never be recognized as Brugada — but the identical pattern, encountered during a syncope workup with this family history, would be the very first thing that came to mind, illustrating how clinical framing shapes which findings a clinician notices and weighs.

Rabih confirmed the retrospective interpretation: the first EKG showed a type 1 Brugada pattern, and the repeat EKG (obtained after the fever resolved with Toradol) showed a type 2 pattern — meaning the EKG's evolution had been driven not by any cardiac treatment, but incidentally by the antipyretic given for symptomatic relief. He distinguished "Brugada pattern" (the isolated EKG finding, common enough that most affected individuals never have a clinical event) from "Brugada syndrome" (the EKG finding paired with a clinical history of arrhythmic events or other high-risk features), framing the two as points along a spectrum of underlying cardiac sodium channel dysfunction severity. Given this patient's combination of a Brugada EKG pattern with a concerning family history and personal history of nocturnal dyspneic episodes and palpitations, he was flagged for likely (though still "putative") Brugada syndrome — with implantation of an ICD in a 32-year-old requiring a high burden of proof of an actual arrhythmia before proceeding.

Practical management followed accordingly: the patient was given a copy of his baseline EKG and a written explanation to carry to any future medical encounter, specifically to prevent unnecessary future STEMI activations; he was referred to a medication-avoidance resource (brugadadrugs.org) for drugs known to unmask or worsen the pattern; he was counseled to minimize or avoid alcohol, another known trigger; and — most specifically — counseled that fever itself is a well-described trigger capable of transitioning the pattern from asymptomatic to a potentially lethal arrhythmia, making aggressive fever avoidance and treatment a genuinely important, targeted intervention in this population (a notable exception to the usual practice of not aggressively treating fever for its own sake). He was discharged with an ambulatory loop recorder and urgent electrophysiology follow-up, with explicit return precautions for any presyncope or syncope.

Both hosts closed by drawing an explicit parallel to a previously discussed conference example in which an ambiguous priming image (a tick versus a cactus) shown before a case presentation caused audience members to diagnose the identical case as either Lyme disease or coccidioidomycosis depending on which image they had seen — illustrating how a clinician's prior framing shapes interpretation of genuinely ambiguous data. Reza's central takeaway was that recognizing a rare, "cool" diagnosis (Brugada) must never come at the cost of skipping the essential steps required to evaluate the more common, more time-sensitive possibility (STEMI) — the correct sequence is to rigorously rule out the dangerous common diagnosis first, and let the rarer diagnosis emerge through that same disciplined process. Rabih reflected that his own growth edge, years into practice, is precisely this: avoiding both the trap of anchoring prematurely on a rare diagnosis and the trap of missing a common, dangerous one — and reframed his own (in-the-moment reflexive) choice to give Toradol as something he would now deliberately repeat with the added intention of using it diagnostically, to see whether fever resolution alone could explain a dynamic EKG change.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| Young, previously healthy patient presenting for typical flu-like symptoms | Prompts questioning why care was sought at all; raises suspicion something atypical is present |
| Prominent myalgias, disproportionate to the patient's prior illness experience | Raises consideration of an inflammatory myositis component alongside simple viral myalgia |
| Positive influenza A test | Confirms one plausible explanation for the presentation but does not exclude a separate, coincidentally ordered finding (the EKG) |
| EKG with ST elevation in V1-V2, inferior/lateral ST depression, subtle aVR elevation | Raises concern for STEMI, but must be weighed against the complete absence of chest pain |
| Normal point-of-care troponin, normal echocardiogram with no wall motion abnormality | Substantially lowers probability of an acute plaque-rupture STEMI or other ischemic explanation for the EKG changes |
| EKG normalizes as fever resolves with antipyretic treatment | Reframes the EKG changes as fever-dependent rather than ischemia-dependent |
| Family history of sudden cardiac death in two relatives; personal history of nocturnal dyspneic episodes and palpitations | Reframes the V1-V2 EKG morphology specifically as a Brugada pattern rather than a nonspecific or ischemic finding |
| EKG lacks deep S waves in V5-V6 | Distinguishes the pattern from true right bundle branch block, supporting Brugada over RBBB |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Brugada-unmasked-by-fever illness script: a Brugada EKG pattern can be entirely silent at baseline and become apparent only during a febrile illness, meaning the finding may first surface in a context (an ED workup for infection) where it is least likely to be recognized as such — deliberately eliciting family history of sudden cardiac death and personal history of nocturnal dyspnea/palpitations is what reframes an incidentally alarming EKG into a recognizable syndrome.

The case is an explicit, doubled example of context-dependent interpretation bias: the same EKG finding is essentially invisible as Brugada in the context of a febrile illness, but would be the first pattern sought in the context of syncope with a concerning family history — a phenomenon the hosts explicitly compare to a documented priming-bias demonstration (identical case, different diagnosis, depending on an incidental image shown beforehand). The case also models disciplined sequencing as an antidote to premature closure: correctly working through the common, dangerous possibility (STEMI) using a standard four-data-point framework before allowing the rarer diagnosis to emerge, rather than skipping steps once a "more interesting" answer becomes plausible.

## Teaching Discussion

The central teaching point is the disciplined, staged evaluation of an alarming EKG in a young, chest-pain-free patient: using history, EKG, troponin, and echocardiogram together (rather than any single alarming data point in isolation) to calibrate the actual probability of ACS, and choosing an intermediate response (direct cardiology consultation, aspirin/statin, repeat testing) over either dismissal or full emergency activation.

A second teaching point is the Brugada pattern itself — its EKG morphology and distinction from RBBB, its fever- and alcohol-triggered unmasking, the pattern-versus-syndrome distinction, and the practical, low-tech but high-value interventions (patient EKG documentation, medication-avoidance counseling, aggressive fever treatment) that follow from a "putative" diagnosis pending further proof of arrhythmia.

A third teaching point, made explicit by both hosts, is the danger and seduction of a "cool," rare diagnosis mid-case: recognizing Brugada should never have come at the expense of skipping the essential ACS rule-out steps, and the case's actual safety came from following the standard sequence rigorously rather than from recognizing the rare pattern quickly.

The final teaching point is the demonstrated power of context and framing on interpretation of identical data — explicitly connected to a documented priming-bias example — reinforcing that clinicians should actively interrogate whether their working hypothesis is shaping which findings they notice, rather than assuming their read of ambiguous data is context-independent.
