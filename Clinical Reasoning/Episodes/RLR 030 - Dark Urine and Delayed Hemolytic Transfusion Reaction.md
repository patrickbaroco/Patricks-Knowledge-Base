## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 76-year-old man with a dense cardiac history (CAD with a prior NSTEMI and stent, HFpEF, atrial flutter on apixaban) and two recent hospitalizations for post-polypectomy GI bleeding — four units of packed cells during the second — calls the triage line about one day of cola-colored urine, plus ten days of fatigue, lightheadedness, and exertional chest/neck tightness. In the ED the urine is Coca-Cola dark, the [[Test Interpretation|UA shows large occult blood but only 4 RBCs]], hemoglobin sits at his post-bleed baseline of ~9, and a *static* high-sensitivity troponin (~300–320) accompanies diffuse ST depression with AVR elevation. Rabih applies the [[Law of Proportionality|law of proportionality]]: heme pigment with too few red cells is not [[Hematuria|hematuria]], and with a normal CK it is not [[Rhabdomyolysis|rhabdo]] — so it is [[Intravascular Hemolysis|intravascular hemolysis]], with a "normal" hemoglobin held up by compensation. The heart is [[Acute Coronary Syndrome|ACS]] until proven otherwise, but the static troponin plus the emerging hemolysis point to [[Type 2 Myocardial Infarction|type 2 demand ischemia]]. Overnight fever, scleral icterus, LUQ tenderness, [[Splenomegaly|splenomegaly]], new pulmonary hypertension, indirect hyperbilirubinemia, LDH 1100, [[Haptoglobin|haptoglobin]] <8, retic 8%, and [[Spherocytes|spherocytes]] confirm hemolysis. The master schema is [[Immune-Mediated Hemolysis|immune-mediated hemolysis]] — **auto, allo, or drug** — and a positive [[Direct Coombs Test|direct Coombs]] with alloantibody testing lands it: a [[Delayed Hemolytic Transfusion Reaction|delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction]] from alloimmunization during the earlier transfusions, driving hemoglobinuria, symptomatic anemia, demand NSTEMI, and pigment-plus-prerenal AKI. An intern who had taken a lab-medicine elective made the call within 12 hours; prednisone resolved it. Lessons: proportionality turns "dark urine" into hemolysis rather than hematuria; don't collapse immune hemolysis into "autoimmune"; and it is an AND, not an OR.

## Pearls

- **[[Law of Proportionality|Proportionality]] on the dipstick: "large blood" with only a few RBCs on micro is heme *pigment*, not [[Hematuria|hematuria]].** Split it into [[Intravascular Hemolysis|intravascular hemolysis]] (free hemoglobin) versus [[Rhabdomyolysis|rhabdomyolysis]] (myoglobin) and let the CK and smear decide. Getting this right is what keeps you *giving* [[Acute Coronary Syndrome|ACS]] therapy rather than withholding it for a misread "hematuria."
- **Hemolysis can run with a normal or baseline hemoglobin.** Brisk reticulocytosis can hold the count up, so a normal Hgb does not exclude hemolysis — and hemolysis-with-normal-Hgb is far more plausible than rhabdo-with-a-normal-CK, because the marrow has a compensatory lever the muscle does not.
- **The master schema for [[Immune-Mediated Hemolysis|immune-mediated hemolysis]] is auto / allo / drug — do not reflexively collapse it to autoimmune.** A recent transfusion should raise *alloimmune* ([[Delayed Hemolytic Transfusion Reaction|delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction]]); a culprit drug whose withdrawal stops the hemolysis raises *drug-induced*. Rabih names anchoring on [[Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia|autoimmune]] as the very mistake he nearly made.
- **[[Spherocytes|Spherocytes]] narrow hemolysis to a short list — [[Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia|AIHA]], [[Hereditary Spherocytosis|hereditary spherocytosis]], [[Paroxysmal Nocturnal Hemoglobinuria|PNH]], and [[Babesiosis|Babesia]] — and the [[Direct Coombs Test|direct Coombs]] is the splitter.** Coombs-positive points to an immune process (AIHA or alloimmune); Coombs-negative sends you toward HS, PNH, or infection.
- **[[Delayed Hemolytic Transfusion Reaction|Delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction]] hides its trigger in a prior admission.** An antigen on cells transfused weeks earlier drives alloantibody formation; the antibodies then hemolyze *after* the index hospitalization. Low-titer alloantibodies can be missed on type-and-screen and "wake up" on re-exposure — so future crossmatch must screen for the known antibodies.
- **A *static* troponin argues against a classic dynamic-rising ACS and, with a new anemia, points to [[Type 2 Myocardial Infarction|type 2 (demand) MI]].** Read the whole sequence — transfusion → alloimmune hemolysis → anemia and hemoglobinuria → demand ischemia and [[Pigment Nephropathy|pigment]]/prerenal AKI — as one cascade ([[Hickam's Dictum|AND, not OR]]), not a pile of separate problems.

## Problem Representation

A 76-year-old man with coronary artery disease (prior NSTEMI, drug-eluting stent, a non-intervened 70% RCA lesion), HFpEF with severe diastolic dysfunction, atrial flutter on apixaban, stage 3 CKD (baseline creatinine 1.4), diabetes, and a remote gastric carcinoid — and two hospitalizations in the preceding month for post-polypectomy hematochezia requiring four units of packed cells, one of FFP, and andexanet — presents with one day of cola-colored urine and ten days of fatigue, lightheadedness, and exertional chest/neck tightness. He is afebrile and hemodynamically stable with a hemoglobin of 9.1 (post-bleed baseline ~9), a creatinine risen to 2.1 from 1.4, a urinalysis showing large occult blood with only 4 RBCs, 28 WBCs, mild protein, and positive urobilinogen, a normal CK, and a *static* high-sensitivity troponin around 300–320 with diffuse ST-segment depression and AVR elevation. Over the first day he develops fever, scleral icterus, left-upper-quadrant tenderness, marked tachypnea without hypoxemia, splenomegaly, a new pulmonary hypertension (RVSP 67, normal a month earlier), indirect hyperbilirubinemia, LDH 1100, haptoglobin <8, reticulocytosis (8%), and spherocytes on smear — with a positive direct Coombs and positive alloantibody screen — yielding a delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction as the unifying diagnosis, with intravascular hemolysis driving hemoglobinuria, symptomatic anemia, a type 2 NSTEMI, and pigment/prerenal acute kidney injury.

## Case Narrative

Recorded as a slide-driven video case (with deliberate "stop and think" prompts), Reza presents a 76-year-old man who calls the triage line to report one day of brown urine, without dysuria, frequency, or suprapubic pain, on a background of ten days of fatigue, lightheadedness, and exertional dyspnea. Rabih opens on what the complaint *means*: pigmented urine grabs attention and is reflexively translated into [[Hematuria|hematuria]], but base rate aside, many things pigment urine. He offers a conceptual model — endogenous versus exogenous pigments — with exogenous being simple (rifampin, beets) and endogenous spanning the heme pathway from synthesis (acute intermittent porphyria) through heme products (hemoglobin, myoglobin) to breakdown products (bilirubin, urobilinogen). Flagging the coexisting dyspnea, he warns against collapsing the problem into "hematuria and dyspnea," and predicts that an early UA looking for red cells will make a large diagnostic leap.

Reza loads the background: a remote gastric carcinoid under surveillance, insulin-dependent diabetes, stage 3 CKD (baseline creatinine 1.4), CAD with a prior drug-eluting stent to a 95% circumflex and an untreated 70% proximal RCA, HFpEF with severe diastolic dysfunction and a normal EF, hypertension, atrial flutter, and OSA. Crucially, two hospitalizations in the past month: a screening colonoscopy with polypectomy complicated by hematochezia and a hemoclip, during which he developed flutter and was started on apixaban (aspirin was begun for CAD then stopped per the AFIRE trial); then a massive rebleed requiring four units of packed cells, one of FFP, and andexanet to reverse the apixaban, with the clip replaced and hemostasis achieved.

In the ED within the hour, vitals are normal and he sats 100% on room air. Hemoglobin is 9.1 (baseline ~9), platelets and INR near-normal, creatinine 2.1 from 1.4, BUN 35, CK normal. The high-sensitivity troponin is 321, then 320, 323, 305 over nine hours — *static*, not dynamic. The urine is Coca-Cola colored; the UA shows large occult blood, only 4 RBCs, 28 WBCs, mild protein, negative bilirubin, and positive urobilinogen. The EKG shows diffuse ST depression with AVR elevation (global ischemia); the chest film is clear.

Rabih notes the case is still in the phase of *clarifying data* rather than solving, and clarifies the urine first with the [[Law of Proportionality|law of proportionality]]: large occult blood means heme pigment, and the differential is free hemoglobin, hemoglobin inside intact red cells, or myoglobin. Four RBCs cannot account for "large" blood, so intact-RBC [[Hematuria|hematuria]] is out, leaving [[Intravascular Hemolysis|intravascular hemolysis]] versus [[Rhabdomyolysis|rhabdomyolysis]]. The tension: the hemoglobin is *normal* and the CK is *normal*. Could there be hemolysis with a normal hemoglobin? Maybe — a compensatory reticulocyte response can hold the count up. Could there be rhabdo with a normal CK? That would be very strange. He bets heavily on hemolysis and orders the confirmatory panel — smear, bilirubin, LDH, reticulocytes, haptoglobin — while conceding operator/technique error on the RBC count as a distant alternative he refuses to anchor on. Turning to the chest, the picture is cardiac: EKG changes, a troponin elevation, and non-revascularized coronary disease make this [[Acute Coronary Syndrome|ACS]] until proven otherwise — but two wrenches complicate it. The troponin is *static* (raising the rare myocarditis, or, far more usefully, [[Type 2 Myocardial Infarction|demand ischemia]]), and a hemolytic process is brewing that could itself drive demand injury. Reza pauses to name the meta-lesson: the [[Schema-Based Reasoning|schema]] and the law of proportionality are what let Rabih read the UA correctly and keep ACS therapy on the table rather than withholding it for a misdiagnosed "hematuria."

On day one the patient is diagnosed with a type 1 NSTEMI and admitted to cardiology on apixaban, aspirin, ticagrelor, and heparin. Overnight he becomes tachypneic (RR 30) with mild desaturation that improves on oxygen; the film suggests possible interstitial edema; the troponin stays ~296. Cardiology, worried about active bleeding, recommends stopping ACS therapy. In the morning the intern, Dr. Victoria Starks, examines him for the first time: temperature 100°F, scleral icterus, left-upper-quadrant (and mild right-upper-quadrant) tenderness, increased work of breathing but clear lungs. Rabih reads the new jaundice as a strong clue for hemolysis — especially with dyspnea and tachypnea *out of proportion* to hypoxemia — while cautioning that heart-plus-jaundice most commonly means [[Congestive Hepatopathy|congestive hepatopathy]], so decompensated heart failure with pulmonary edema and hepatic congestion must be excluded. He invokes the RLR maxim against mutually exclusive diagnoses — [[Hickam's Dictum|it can be an AND, not an OR]] — and notes that if this is hemolysis it is the rarer, narrower *intravascular* variety. Asked how CKD/AKI colors things, he says the troponin could be attributed to kidney disease but only as an end-of-journey conclusion, and that the hemolysis–kidney overlap is broad: the morbid [[Microangiopathic Hemolytic Anemia|MAHA]] he hopes to avoid, the direct [[Pigment Nephropathy|pigment nephropathy]] from hemoglobinuria this patient likely has, and simple prerenal disease from under-hydration.

The confirmatory labs land: hemoglobin drifting to 8 then 7, AST 58 with a normal-ish ALT, total bilirubin 3.8 with a direct of only 0.6 (indirect-predominant), reticulocytes 8% (index >2%), LDH 1100, [[Haptoglobin|haptoglobin]] <8, CT [[Splenomegaly|splenomegaly]], and a TTE showing severe diastolic dysfunction *and* a new RVSP of 67 (normal a month earlier). Rabih anchors the hemolysis diagnosis in cell destruction (very high LDH) plus red-cell–specific signs (indirect hyperbilirubinemia, low haptoglobin, reticulocytosis, anemia), then reads the splenomegaly and new pulmonary hypertension as hints that the process may be *subacute-to-chronic* rather than purely acute — chronic hemolysis being inherently hypercoagulable and a recognized driver of pulmonary hypertension (as in sickle cell and thalassemia). The [[Spherocytes|spherocyte]]-heavy smear then narrows the field: a large spherocyte burden points to [[Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia|AIHA]] or [[Hereditary Spherocytosis|hereditary spherocytosis]] first, with [[Paroxysmal Nocturnal Hemoglobinuria|PNH]] and [[Babesiosis|Babesia]] behind them, and the [[Direct Coombs Test|direct Coombs]] as the splitter — with recent transfusion making a post-transfusion immune process loom large.

The Coombs is positive, and an alloantibody screen returns positive: weeks earlier an antigen on transfused red cells drove alloantibody formation, and those antibodies later hemolyzed the patient's cells, producing hemoglobinuria and demand ischemia — a [[Delayed Hemolytic Transfusion Reaction|delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction]], confirmed by matching the detected alloantibody to the antigens of the previously transfused units. The intern had suspected it on first contact, having learned it in a fourth-year lab-medicine elective — a delayed diagnosis made with *zero delay*. The course turned briefly tricky (hemoglobin to 7 with recurrent chest pain, requiring leukoreduced blood), and he will now need lifelong screening for two specific alloantibodies before any transfusion, since low-titer antibodies can be missed on type-and-screen and reactivate on re-exposure. He was started on prednisone 90 mg (with pantoprazole and Bactrim prophylaxis) on a weekly taper; by discharge the urine cleared, chest tightness resolved, LDH halved, bilirubin normalized, and hemoglobin rose — though the creatinine later crept up again. Both hosts close on the reasoning: Reza on the law of proportionality and the transfusion → hemolysis → anemia → type 2 NSTEMI sequence; Rabih on the correct master schema — [[Immune-Mediated Hemolysis|immune-mediated hemolysis is auto, allo, or drug]] — and on the patient's own triage wisdom in prioritizing the dark urine that turned out to be the key.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Law of Proportionality|The law of proportionality]] as the case-opening move.** "Large blood" with four RBCs is a disconnect, and reading the disconnect — not the headline positive — is what reclassifies the problem from hematuria to hemolysis. Reza explicitly credits the [[Schema-Based Reasoning|schema]], not raw intellect, for enabling it, and notes the stakes: the misread would have made a clinician *withhold* ACS therapy for a nonexistent bleed.
- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|Schema structure over recall]] — and getting the master schema right.** Rabih's closing self-critique is that he drifted toward [[Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia|autoimmune]] hemolysis when the correct top node is [[Immune-Mediated Hemolysis|immune-mediated]] hemolysis with three branches — auto, allo, drug. Choosing the right *level* of the schema is what keeps the recent transfusion (alloimmune) and a culprit drug (drug-induced) in view instead of collapsing to the most familiar branch.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Interpreting the troponin by pattern and host]].** A static troponin is read against its expected dynamic ACS trajectory; combined with a new anemia it is reframed as [[Type 2 Myocardial Infarction|demand]] rather than plaque rupture, with CKD offered as an attribution to reach only at the *end* of the journey, not the start.
- **[[Hickam's Dictum|AND, not OR]] and the [[Center of Gravity|unifying cascade]].** Rather than adjudicate hemolysis *versus* heart failure *versus* AKI, the reasoning threads them: one alloimmune hemolytic process drives hemoglobinuria, anemia, demand ischemia, and pigment/prerenal kidney injury. The explicit warning against mutually exclusive diagnoses is what allows congestive hepatopathy to be held alongside hemolysis instead of instead of it.
- **[[Base Rate|Base rate]] used and tempered.** Pigmenturia is hematuria by base rate until proportionality overrides it; a static troponin makes myocarditis technically possible but base rate keeps it low; spherocytes are read AIHA/HS-first before the rarer PNH/Babesia — frequency setting the prior, specific data adjusting it.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Humility and distributed expertise]].** The diagnosis is made by an intern reaching *out of* cardiology into hematology on the basis of a lab-medicine elective — "a delayed diagnosis with zero delay" — and both hosts flag hemolysis-with-a-normal-hemoglobin as a pattern they had each seen only once, a candid marker of how thin even an expert's script for it can be.

## Teaching Discussion

- Meet pigmented urine with the endogenous/exogenous pigment schema and the law of proportionality: "large blood" on the dipstick with few RBCs on microscopy is heme pigment, so split it into intravascular hemolysis (free hemoglobin) versus rhabdomyolysis (myoglobin) using the CK and smear, and do not let a misread "hematuria" scare you off indicated ACS therapy.
- Diagnose hemolysis in two steps — establish cell destruction (a high LDH), then prove the cells are red (anemia plus indirect hyperbilirubinemia, low haptoglobin, and reticulocytosis) — and remember that compensation can keep the hemoglobin normal, so a normal count does not exclude brisk hemolysis.
- Structure immune-mediated hemolysis as auto / allo / drug rather than collapsing to autoimmune: recent transfusion raises the alloimmune delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction, and a drug whose withdrawal stops the hemolysis raises drug-induced disease; a positive direct Coombs confirms an immune mechanism but does not by itself distinguish auto from allo.
- Read spherocytes as a short differential (AIHA, hereditary spherocytosis, PNH, Babesia) and let splenomegaly and new pulmonary hypertension raise the possibility of a subacute-to-chronic hemolytic process, since chronic hemolysis is hypercoagulable and a recognized cause of pulmonary hypertension.
- Recognize the delayed hemolytic transfusion reaction: alloantibodies formed against an antigen on cells transfused weeks earlier hemolyze on re-exposure, often after discharge; because low-titer antibodies can be missed on type-and-screen and reactivate, the patient needs the specific alloantibodies flagged for all future crossmatches.
- Interpret a static troponin against the dynamic trajectory of type 1 ACS and, in the setting of a new anemia, favor type 2 demand ischemia; attribute a lingering troponin to CKD only as an end-of-workup conclusion, not a starting assumption.
- Hold the whole picture as a cascade rather than mutually exclusive diagnoses — transfusion → alloimmune hemolysis → anemia and hemoglobinuria → demand NSTEMI and pigment/prerenal AKI — and keep congestive hepatopathy in the differential for any patient with heart disease and jaundice.
