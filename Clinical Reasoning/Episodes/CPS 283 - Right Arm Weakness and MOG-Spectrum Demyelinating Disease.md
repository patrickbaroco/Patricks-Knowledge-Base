## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A Neurology VMR (an ~8-year-old case at the time of presentation) built around a **44-year-old right-handed woman** with **six days of progressively worsening right arm and grip weakness**, who over the same days also develops **confusion, disorganized thinking, and a depersonalization-like sense that her arm "wasn't part of her,"** superimposed on roughly a year of night sweats and weight gain. Discussants use **[[Illness Tempo|"think fast, think slow"]]** — naming the pattern-matched stroke hypothesis, then deliberately widening outward through Berkowitz's **[[Anatomical Approach|whole motor pathway]]** (cortex, brainstem, cervicomedullary decussation, cord, root, brachial plexus, nerve, NMJ, muscle) — and consider **Parsonage-Turner syndrome** (inflammatory brachial plexitis) before the emerging confusion relocalizes the case squarely to the brain. Exam shows **no objective confirmation of the family-reported fluctuating confusion**, a subtle inconsistent facial droop, **no formally demonstrable arm weakness** despite the chief complaint, right-arm clumsiness on rapid alternating movements, an unsteady gait, and a **right Babinski with an equivocal left** — a picture the group works hard to interpret, using the **crossed anatomy of the cerebellum** to argue that the apparent "ataxia" is more likely pseudo-ataxia from weakness than a true second, cerebellar lesion. Brain MRI shows **two ring-enhancing left-hemisphere lesions**, prompting a broad differential (toxoplasmosis, metastases, primary CNS lymphoma, tumefactive demyelination) that HIV testing, body imaging, and CSF studies gradually narrow. The clincher is a spine MRI: a **longitudinally extensive (T7/8-to-conus) thoracic cord lesion** without enhancement — a pattern that points toward **[[Neuromyelitis Optica Spectrum Disorder|NMO]]**/**[[MOG Antibody Disease|MOG-antibody disease]]**, with the combined brain-plus-cord picture read as an **[[ADEM|ADEM]]**-spectrum fulminant demyelinating process. Worked up before MOG serology was clinically available, the case was ultimately treated as multiple sclerosis, on a spectrum from ADEM to tumefactive MS — with the year-long night sweats and weight gain left genuinely, explicitly unresolved.

## Pearls

- **"Think fast, think slow" as an explicit two-step differential habit.** Name the first, pattern-matched diagnosis honestly, then deliberately slow down and work outward anatomically — other intracranial causes, demyelinating disease, spine, plexus, muscle, even a vascular event isolated to the limb — before committing to a plan.
- **A single limb can be weak from a cortical lesion alone**, because the hand has an outsized representation in the motor **[[Anatomical Approach|homunculus]]** — don't assume "only the arm" rules out the brain; look for subtle associated findings (a whisper of facial weakness, a gaze preference) before moving the localization more peripheral.
- **Attention is the doorway to the rest of cognitive testing.** An inattentive patient cannot reliably demonstrate an intact or impaired memory or language, because attention is what holds anything in working memory long enough to test it — check attention (serial 7s, spelling backward) before drawing conclusions from the rest of the mental status exam.
- **A weak limb can masquerade as an ataxic one — and the crossed anatomy of the cerebellum can prove it.** Because cerebellar output communicates with the *contralateral* cerebral hemisphere (which then governs the *ipsilateral*-to-that-hemisphere body), a lesion in one cerebellar hemisphere should produce a Babinski sign on the *opposite* side from the ataxia it causes. A same-sided Babinski and "ataxia" argues for one corticospinal lesion looking clumsy, not two separate lesions.
- **Longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis (a cord lesion spanning more than three vertebral segments) is a specific red flag**, distinct from a typical short-segment MS plaque, and should trigger testing for **[[Neuromyelitis Optica Spectrum Disorder|NMO (anti-aquaporin-4)]]** and **[[MOG Antibody Disease|MOG]]** antibodies rather than being filed as just "another demyelinating lesion."
- **Describe an image before you diagnose it.** Reading a scan in neutral radiology terms first — signal characteristics, location, enhancement pattern — rather than jumping straight to a diagnosis, is good discipline against letting a favored hypothesis color the read itself.

## Problem Representation

A 44-year-old right-handed woman with chronic migraines and hypothyroidism presents with six days of progressively worsening right arm and grip weakness, evolving over the same period to include fluctuating confusion, slowed responses, and a depersonalization-like sense of the affected limb, superimposed on roughly a year of night sweats and unintentional weight gain. Exam is notable for a normal formal mental status assessment (no objective confirmation of the family-reported fluctuating confusion), a subtle and inconsistent right facial droop, no clearly demonstrable arm weakness on formal strength testing despite the chief complaint, right-arm clumsiness on rapid alternating movements, an unsteady tandem gait, a right Babinski sign, and an equivocal left Babinski. Brain MRI shows two ring-enhancing left-hemisphere lesions without midline shift or disproportionate edema; an extensive infectious, autoimmune, and malignancy workup (HIV, VDRL, hepatitis, ANA, rheumatoid factor, CSF cell count/protein/oligoclonal bands, body CT) is unrevealing aside from a mildly positive ANA and an incidental, non-concerning pulmonary nodule. Spine MRI reveals a longitudinally extensive (T7/8-to-conus) thoracic cord signal abnormality without enhancement or cord expansion — a pattern that, together with the ring-enhancing brain lesions, is most consistent with a fulminant CNS demyelinating process on the ADEM/tumefactive-MS/MOG-antibody-disease spectrum, worked up and treated (roughly eight years before MOG antibody testing was routinely available) as multiple sclerosis.

## Case Narrative

Ravi presents a case roughly eight years old at the time: a 44-year-old right-handed woman with six days of right arm and right-hand grip weakness. Yasmin's first thought is vascular — a stroke — and she asks about coagulopathy and procoagulant medications like oral contraceptives. Sridhara introduces **"think fast, think slow"**: the fast, pattern-matched diagnosis is vascular, but slowing down opens other intracranial causes, demyelinating disease, the cervical spine, the brachial plexus, muscle, and even a vascular event confined to the limb itself (an embolic axillary artery occlusion from atrial fibrillation, for instance) — with tempo and associated symptoms as the key discriminators.

Berkowitz builds the full motor pathway for arm and hand weakness — precentral gyrus, subcortical white matter, anterior brainstem, decussation at the **cervicomedullary junction**, descending spinal cord, anterior horn lower motor neurons, ventral roots, brachial plexus, individual nerves, neuromuscular junction, muscle — and reasons through localization. Isolated arm and hand weakness could still be cortical, because the hand has an outsized representation in the motor **homunculus**; the patient could have subtle, unnoticed facial weakness too. Truly isolated arm weakness is probably not brainstem (too much anatomy packed tightly there — other findings would be expected). It could be cervical spine, brachial plexus, one or more roots or nerves. A neuromuscular junction process or a muscle disease affecting only one limb doesn't fit well, since both are usually more diffuse. The group settles on brain, cervical spinal cord, or periphery (root, plexus, or nerve), deferring granular peripheral localization until it's confirmed the process is truly peripheral.

On tempo, Berkowitz distinguishes sudden-onset-then-static weakness (favoring stroke) from a process that has been progressively worsening over six days (favoring infectious, inflammatory, demyelinating, or toxic-metabolic causes at any tempo). He raises a first presentation of MS (or a repeat event, if there's a prior undiagnosed episode like optic neuritis), an infectious lesion like a brain abscess (broadened considerably if the patient turns out to be immunocompromised, opening toxoplasmosis), and on the peripheral side, Guillain-Barré syndrome (fast, but not typically this focal) and **Parsonage-Turner syndrome** — an inflammatory brachial plexitis he flags as underappreciated: a prodrome of severe axillary or shoulder pain with a largely unremarkable exam, resolving over days and leaving behind arm weakness (sometimes bilateral and asymmetric), with particular nerves classically affected — scapular winging from the long thoracic nerve, or difficulty making the "OK sign" from anterior interosseous nerve involvement — often following heavy exertion of the arm the day before. Mononeuritis multiplex from vasculitis (sudden nerve infarcts) is also raised. He concludes that a six-day progressive tempo makes the periphery somewhat less likely outside of an inflammatory plexopathy, leaning the group back toward brain or cord, with stroke as the not-to-miss diagnosis.

Ravi reveals more history: over the same six days, the patient also developed confusion and difficulty organizing her thoughts; her family noted two to three days of being slow to respond, "distant," and confused by simple matters; she described a depersonalization-like sense that her right arm "wasn't part of her," alongside a long-standing generalized headache. Additional history: a year of worsening night sweats and roughly fifteen pounds of weight gain; migraines since childhood or adolescence, treated with topiramate, metoclopramide, and intermittent NSAIDs; a past medical history of hypothyroidism; a family history of lung and breast cancer; and a modest smoking history (five cigarettes a day since age twenty), occasional alcohol, and no other substance use.

Yasmin's first read of the new information: progressive mental status change plus a year of night sweats raises a slowly expanding mass, and her leading thought is **CNS lymphoma** (which can cause focal deficits, headache, and symptoms from rising intracranial pressure) — though she flags this as a jump ahead of imaging. Sridhara refines the localization: confusion and disorganized thinking point to the frontal lobe, particularly the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and should prompt checking for a gaze preference on exam; slowed responses localize more medially, and severe bilateral involvement can produce **[[Abulia|abulia]]**. He notes the tempo (worsening over days, not the classic stroke pattern) shifts weight toward infection, inflammation, or malignancy; the night sweats reinforce an inflammatory read; the weight gain is probably from the hypothyroidism, though "patients can have as many diagnoses as they please." He raises whether the chronic headache history could be part of a broader syndrome — as in CADASIL or certain mitochondrial/TREX1-related conditions, which can produce both migraine-like headaches and stroke- or mass-mimicking lesions — or be an unrelated comorbidity, and notes the smoking history keeps vascular, inflammatory, infectious, and malignant causes all in play.

Berkowitz builds out **abulia** — a profound, bilateral-frontal-circuit slowing of response and motivation, illustrated with a mentor's anecdote about a patient who finally answered a question only after the team had moved on to several other patients — and wonders whether a single left-hemisphere lesion could really account for this much mental status impairment, since that degree of abulia typically implies a bifrontal or otherwise bihemispheric process. Reframing the timeline, he observes the patient appears to be **accumulating deficits** over the six days — arm weakness worsening, then days later confusion and an odd sensory/depersonalization phenomenon that "might be some type of seizure" — rather than presenting once and staying static. He works through what could cause accumulating deficits: a rapidly expanding mass (atypical for most tumors over days, though possible for an abscess); a fulminant demyelinating syndrome — **acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM)**, more common in children, sometimes associated with anti-MOG antibody or a preceding infection, or **tumefactive demyelination** (a large, actively expanding lesion mimicking a tumor); or a series of strokes from a source like subacute endocarditis. He notes the year of night sweats doesn't fit demyelinating disease well and is hard to reconcile with any leading hypothesis, and that the arm's focality argues for a genuine structural lesion over a purely toxic-metabolic or endocrine process. He settles on either one large left-hemisphere lesion or a multifocal process, with the exam left to decide.

Asked what to look for on exam, Sridhara lists gaze preference, frontal release signs (a snout reflex, or lip contraction with thumb stimulation), language (a core frontal-lobe function), inattention (serial 7s or spelling backward — noting that if the patient is inattentive, most other cognitive testing becomes unreliable), the standard cranial nerve/motor/sensory/reflex/cerebellar exam, cortical sensory testing (two-point discrimination, or placing an object in the hand to see if the patient can identify it by touch alone — intact sensation with an inability to identify the object localizes to cortex), the pattern of motor involvement (a cortical lesion typically spares part of the face, arm, or leg unless it's very large; equal face-arm-leg weakness argues against a small cortical lesion), and peripheral vision (a parietal extension can produce a visual field cut that's easy to miss without specifically testing for it). Berkowitz adds that hyperreflexia isolated to one limb localizes to the corticospinal tract without proving a cortical, as opposed to subcortical, origin.

Berkowitz elaborates that attention is "the doorway" to the rest of cognitive testing — an inattentive patient can't reliably demonstrate memory or hold a conversation, which points toward a more generalized (toxic-metabolic) process, whereas preserved attention with a focal language deficit localizes more precisely, and preserved-but-slow, confused-in-content language suggests a bihemispheric, particularly bifrontal, process. On gaze deviation, he notes he's mainly seen it with acute vascular events or seizure, rather than large, slower-growing non-vascular lesions; Sridhara offers a possible explanation — gaze deviation may reflect a lack of time for the brain to adapt, more likely with something sudden (stroke, hemorrhage, seizure) than with a lesion that has had time to accommodate.

Ravi reports the exam: normal vitals; normal general and eye exam. Neuro exam: normal attention and concentration, alert and fully oriented, good recent, remote, and short-term memory, adequate fund of knowledge, normal speech and language, cranial nerves intact aside from a questionable mild right facial droop at rest that was symmetric with smile, normal sensation, normal bulk and tone, no drift, and — notably — **no appreciable right upper extremity weakness** on formal testing despite the chief complaint. Coordination and gait: negative Romberg, right-arm clumsiness on rapid alternating movements, and an unsteady tandem (heel-to-toe) gait. Reflexes: an upgoing (Babinski) toe on the right and an equivocal Babinski on the left. Berkowitz specifically confirms with Ravi that none of the family-reported mental status change was objectively demonstrated on this exam — the family describes a fluctuating state, and the patient looked at her baseline to the examining team.

Yasmin reads the upgoing right toe (with an equivocal left) as an upper-motor-neuron sign, which she'd expect to travel with increased tone and some demonstrable weakness in that distribution, and raises MS, stroke, a mass, or something simpler like B12 deficiency (though that would typically bring other findings). She also flags the clumsiness and unsteady gait as potentially localizing to the cerebellum — vascular or compressive.

Berkowitz works through the cerebellar hypothesis carefully. Clumsiness on rapid alternating movements does suggest cerebellar dysfunction, but a weak, slow limb can look ataxic without truly being ataxic — the two are hard to distinguish clinically when a limb isn't fully paralyzed. Critically, a cerebellar lesion shouldn't itself produce an upgoing toe, since that's a corticospinal (pyramidal) sign, and because cerebellar output crosses to communicate with the *contralateral* cerebral hemisphere before that hemisphere controls the ipsilateral-to-the-cerebellum body, a right cerebellar lesion large enough to also involve the right corticospinal tract should logically produce a *left*-sided, not right-sided, Babinski. A same-sided finding therefore argues either that the "ataxia" is really just weakness from a single left corticospinal lesion, or that there are genuinely two separate lesions — a left corticospinal lesion and a right cerebellar lesion — in which case the group is dealing with a multifocal process. The absence of objective confusion on exam, set against the family's report of fluctuating altered behavior, raises the possibility of an intermittent process such as a non-convulsive seizure superimposed on the picture; since seizures arise from gray matter, a cerebellar lesion alone wouldn't explain that either, pushing further toward multifocal disease.

Before seeing the imaging, Berkowitz asks Sridhara to predict what they'll find. Sridhara predicts a left cortical process — a mass or abscess — given no other cranial nerve or long-tract findings beyond the corticospinal signs, and no other features of true ataxia (speech, eye movements). Berkowitz agrees this is reasonable, and separately reasons that if the "clumsiness without appreciable weakness" is taken at face value, it would argue for a genuine right cerebellar lesion that wouldn't explain the right Babinski — implying either a remote, separate old lesion accounting for the toe sign, or a new acute demyelinating process (a new MS-type plaque in the right cerebellar white matter, for instance) layered on an old one. He notes this still wouldn't explain the fluctuating cognition, since seizures shouldn't arise from an acute white-matter lesion alone, though the overlying gray matter could in principle be affected.

Labs return unremarkable (hemoglobin 14, no anemia, no leukocytosis). Brain MRI shows two left-hemisphere lesions; Berkowitz has Yasmin describe them in neutral radiology language first — T2/FLAIR hyperintense, T1 dark, possibly diffusion-restricting, though without a corresponding ADC map to confirm true restriction versus T2 shine-through — before she offers a working diagnosis. She reads them as two ring-enhancing masses and, drawing on her training, favors toxoplasmosis over neurocysticercosis (which typically shows more numerous, smaller lesions), while explicitly flagging that she may be **anchored by her own training** and asking about animal exposure or immunocompromise. Berkowitz praises the discipline of describing images like a radiologist blinded to the history before naming a diagnosis, agrees the lesions don't look like classic infarcts, and notes they could be infectious or a fulminant acute demyelinating process, while not quite matching the classic "cyst with a dot" appearance of neurocysticercosis.

Post-contrast images (which the group doesn't see directly, but which Ravi confirms) show the two lesions are indeed ring-enhancing — and are the only lesions, with nothing in the cerebellum. Berkowitz notes this retrospectively supports pseudo-ataxia from a weak limb over a true separate cerebellar lesion. The group works through the two-ring-enhancing-lesion differential: toxoplasmosis (though the lesions show less surrounding edema than expected and no midline shift, arguing somewhat against it without excluding it), metastases (odd to have two lesions confined to one hemisphere and none elsewhere, though not impossible; the patient is relatively young with only a modest smoking history), and demyelinating disease (these lesions lack the classic ovoid morphology, though it remains possible), with Sridhara raising primary CNS lymphoma (which can also show diffusion restriction from densely packed cells) given the year of night sweats, though two lesions confined to one hemisphere is also somewhat atypical for lymphoma. HIV testing is flagged as an important branch point.

HIV returns negative. A body/pan-CT performed to look for an occult malignancy shows only a small, non-concerning pulmonary lingular lesion attributable to the smoking history. The case is discussed at a departmental neurology conference. CSF studies are unremarkable — no significant inflammatory signal, negative infectious studies (toxoplasmosis, Lyme, VZV), nothing specifically supportive of MS, and negative oligoclonal bands. Serum autoimmune workup shows a mildly positive ANA (1:640), a low/normal CRP, a rheumatoid factor of 15, a non-reactive VDRL, and negative hepatitis serologies.

Spine MRI is then obtained: a normal cervical spine, but a subtle T2 signal abnormality in the mid-to-lower thoracic cord spanning from about the T7/8 disc level down to the conus medullaris, without cord edema, expansion, or enhancement — read as concerning for demyelinating disease. Sridhara identifies this as **longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis** (a cord lesion spanning more than three vertebral segments), a pattern that should prompt testing for MOG-antibody-associated disease, neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, and other LETM causes (Sjögren syndrome, antiphospholipid syndrome); he favors MOG antibody disease as the leading suspect. Berkowitz agrees, situating **neuromyelitis optica** as a biomarker-defined carve-out of the MS spectrum (anti-aquaporin-4 antibody) and **MOG-antibody disease** as a further carve-out identified in some antibody-negative NMO-like presentations; other LETM causes include Sjögren's, radiation myelopathy, and a dural arteriovenous fistula (which can mimic transverse myelitis through venous congestion), but LETM extending all the way to the conus is, in his experience, particularly characteristic of MOG disease. He notes the brain lesions look more ADEM-like than the periependymal pattern classically described for NMOSD, and lands on a combined encephalitis-plus-myelitis picture — literally ADEM — while observing that MOG antibody testing wasn't clinically available at the time of this roughly-eight-year-old case, so it would likely be checked, and potentially reclassify the case, if the patient were presenting today.

Ravi confirms that, at the time, the case was worked up and treated along the spectrum from ADEM to tumefactive MS, ultimately labeled clinically as multiple sclerosis, without a specific MOG result available; he doesn't have details of longer-term follow-up. Asked about the unexplained systemic symptoms — the year of night sweats and weight gain — Ravi doesn't have a confirmed explanation, though he notes the patient may have been non-compliant with her hypothyroidism medication, which Berkowitz acknowledges as plausible but unproven, leaving that thread genuinely unresolved. Sridhara closes with a visual teaching point: MOG-associated encephalitis can show a distinctive cortical/juxtacortical gray matter enhancement pattern that, from the actual images in this case, he likens to a Star Wars "TIE fighter" — a memorable visual cue for recognizing the pattern on imaging.

Berkowitz closes by reflecting that this was a case of testing whether one lesion or multiple lesions, one hemisphere or both, could explain the full picture, and of returning to first principles once the systemic red herrings were set aside: a young, previously healthy woman with an acute-timeframe CNS process, once HIV is excluded, points strongly toward demyelinating disease, even though the ring enhancement and the lack of CSF inflammation were both mildly atypical. He notes, with some self-awareness, that he's glad the group didn't spend too long down the brachial plexus branch of the differential once the confusion emerged, and closes on how quickly the field has moved — a case that would have been called ADEM eight years ago would now prompt MOG antibody testing, with old cases potentially reclassified as the biomarker landscape evolves.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Anchoring Bias|Explicit, self-caught anchoring.]]** Yasmin names her own likely anchoring toward toxoplasmosis "because of my training" the moment she sees ring-enhancing lesions — flagging the bias in real time rather than only in retrospect, and asking a discriminating question (immunocompromise, animal exposure) to test it rather than confirm it.
- **[[Illness Script|Correctly pursuing, then abandoning, a branch.]]** Berkowitz explicitly welcomes having raised Parsonage-Turner syndrome and other peripheral possibilities early, then says he's "glad I didn't go off on the brachial plexus" once the confusion emerged — a model of updating a differential rather than anchoring on wherever the discussion started.
- **[[Unifying Diagnosis|Testing "one lesion or many" as an explicit checkpoint.]]** Rather than assuming either a single unifying lesion or a multifocal process, the group repeatedly asks whether one left-hemisphere lesion can explain both the arm weakness and the degree of cognitive change, using that question to decide how hard to look for a second lesion.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Describing images before diagnosing them.]]** Berkowitz has Yasmin describe the MRI findings in neutral radiology terms (signal characteristics, location, enhancement) before naming a diagnosis — a deliberate technique to reduce the risk that a favored hypothesis colors the read of the image itself.
- **[[Diagnostic Humility|Comfort with genuine uncertainty.]]** Berkowitz explicitly says he "appreciates not being sure" partway through the case, and the systemic-symptom thread (night sweats, weight gain) is never resolved — modeling tolerance for an incomplete picture rather than forcing every finding into the leading diagnosis.

## Teaching Discussion

- Use "think fast, think slow" explicitly: name the pattern-matched first diagnosis honestly, then deliberately widen the differential outward anatomically (other intracranial causes, demyelinating disease, spine, plexus, muscle, even a vascular event isolated to the limb) before settling on a plan.
- Remember that an isolated limb weakness can still be cortical, because of the hand's outsized representation in the motor homunculus; look for subtle associated findings (a whisper of facial weakness, a gaze preference) before assuming the lesion must be more peripheral.
- Distinguish sudden-onset-then-static weakness (favoring stroke) from weakness that worsens over days (favoring infectious, inflammatory, demyelinating, or toxic causes), and use that distinction to weight the differential.
- Recognize Parsonage-Turner syndrome (inflammatory brachial plexitis) by its classic pattern: severe axillary or shoulder pain with an unremarkable exam, resolving over days and leaving behind arm weakness, sometimes with scapular winging (long thoracic nerve) or an inability to make the "OK sign" (anterior interosseous nerve), often following heavy arm exertion.
- Treat attention as the gateway to the rest of the cognitive exam: an inattentive patient cannot reliably demonstrate an intact or impaired memory or language, so test attention (serial 7s, spelling backward) before drawing conclusions from the rest of the mental status exam.
- Use the crossed anatomy of the cerebellum to check an "ataxia": because cerebellar output communicates with the contralateral cerebral hemisphere (which then controls the ipsilateral-to-that-hemisphere body), a lesion in one cerebellar hemisphere should produce a Babinski sign on the opposite side from where it causes limb ataxia — a same-sided Babinski and apparent ataxia argues for a single corticospinal lesion causing pseudo-ataxia through weakness, not a true cerebellar lesion.
- Recognize longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis (a cord lesion spanning more than three vertebral segments) as a specific red flag pattern that should prompt testing for neuromyelitis optica (anti-aquaporin-4) and MOG antibodies, rather than being filed as a routine demyelinating lesion.
- Describe imaging findings in neutral radiology terms — signal characteristics, location, enhancement pattern — before naming a diagnosis, both as good practice and as a check against letting the leading clinical hypothesis color the read.

## Handbook Opportunities

- A **frontal lobe / mental status exam** card — gaze preference, frontal release signs, attention-first testing order, and the abulia spectrum.
- A **cerebellar vs. weakness "ataxia"** card — the crossed-pathway Babinski-laterality check for distinguishing true ataxia from a weak, slow limb.
- A **longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis** card — the >3-segment rule and its focused antibody workup (AQP4, MOG).
- A **ring-enhancing brain lesion** differential card, with the imaging features (edema, midline shift, enhancement completeness, lesion count) that help discriminate among the causes.
