## Pearls

- When a patient's chief concern is a rash, examine the rash before taking a detailed history — let the visual pattern guide which history questions actually matter, rather than the reverse.
- A rash that begins in, or is concentrated in, the lower extremities should prompt you to ask whether it extends elsewhere, since gravity-dependence is itself a diagnostic clue (small vessel processes are classically worst in the dependent legs).
- Petechiae, purpura, and ecchymosis are distinguished purely by lesion size, and can coexist within a single eruption; use a simple bedside blanch test (finger pressure, or pressing a clear plastic surface against the skin) to determine whether a rash is blanching (vessel-normal, from vasodilation) or non-blanching (blood has actually left the vessel into the skin).
- Frame a non-blanching rash the same way you would frame any bleeding problem, just relocated to the skin: is the defect in hemostasis (a clotting/platelet problem) or in the vessel wall itself (inflammation, infiltration, or fragility)?
- Palpability is a genuinely powerful physical exam sign: palpable purpura strongly supports leukocytoclastic (small-vessel) vasculitis, but its absence does not exclude vasculitis — like most physical exam findings, it's far more useful when present than when absent.
- Small-vessel vasculitis affecting the lower extremities preferentially reflects a "two-hit" mechanism: fragile, inflamed vessels plus the added hydrostatic stress of gravity pooling blood in the dependent legs.
- Always screen for hemorrhagic herpes zoster as a purpura mimicker when evaluating a non-blanching rash — look specifically for vesicles, crusting, or a dermatomal distribution, since bleeding into zoster vesicles (especially in a coagulopathic or immunosuppressed patient) can closely resemble vasculitic purpura.
- An ecchymotic lesion overlying the flank or posterior trunk should prompt consideration of a retroperitoneal bleed manifesting on the skin (Grey Turner sign for the flank, Cullen sign periumbilically) as part of the differential for an unexplained bruise-like lesion in that region.
- When small-vessel vasculitis is suspected, actively screen beyond the skin: a urinalysis with microscopy and a spot urine protein-to-creatinine ratio for glomerulonephritis, a pulmonary and hypoxemia assessment for capillaritis/diffuse alveolar hemorrhage, and a neurologic exam (specifically for something easy to miss, like a foot drop from vasculitic neuropathy) that can be overshadowed by an "impressive" skin finding.
- Diffuse, fluffy, bilateral alveolar opacifications without a convincing heart failure story or a multifocal-pneumonia pattern should broaden the differential beyond edema and infection to diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (remembering roughly 30% of DAH patients lack hemoptysis) and to metastatic or infiltrative malignancy.
- Distinguish vasculitis (a genuinely inflammatory process, confirmable with ESR/CRP) from vasculopathy (structural vessel disease, like atherosclerosis, that everyone accumulates to some degree) — inflammatory diseases can themselves accelerate atherosclerosis, so the two concepts are related but distinct and shouldn't be conflated.
- "Cannonball metastases" (multiple large, well-circumscribed, round pulmonary masses without air bronchograms) is a classic but narrow radiographic pattern, with a differential dominated by choriocarcinoma, renal cell carcinoma, and sarcomas — recognizing this pattern should immediately shift your reasoning from an inflammatory/infectious framework toward malignancy.
- When a mass appears to originate outside the classic visceral organs (lung, GI tract, prostate), consider a mesenchymal (soft tissue or bone) primary — sarcomas are aggressive, can disseminate widely, and deserve consideration whenever a solid mass doesn't fit a more common visceral cancer's typical spread pattern.
- Framework for any vessel-centric process: is the abnormality at the vessel wall itself (inflammatory, i.e. vasculitis), in the vessel lumen (occlusive, i.e. thrombotic microangiopathy or thromboembolism), or is the vessel wall/tissue actually replaced or infiltrated by an abnormal (e.g., malignant) process — a third category distinct from both inflammation and occlusion.
- Angiosarcoma is a well-described mimicker of both cutaneous vasculitis (its skin metastases can look exactly like purpura) and hematoma (its markedly vascular, hyperenhancing soft tissue deposits are frequently mistaken for a simple bruise or bleed on imaging) — a genuinely difficult diagnosis to reach without tissue confirmation.
- Malignancy can mimic vasculitis or vasculopathy through more than one mechanism: paraneoplastic true vasculitis, DIC-related bleeding, or — as increasingly recognized — direct vascular infiltration or occlusion by cancer cells themselves (e.g., a recently described case of metastatic breast cancer causing livedo racemosa via intravascular tumor cell occlusion), a mechanism worth keeping in mind whenever "vasculitis" doesn't quite fit.
- Creating genuine progress on a hard case sometimes requires being deliberately pushed by a colleague through successive rounds of reasoning rather than being allowed to stop at an initial plausible answer — a teaching technique worth using intentionally with trainees and colleagues alike, paired with making it safe to say "I don't know" along the way.

## Problem Representation

An 89-year-old man with two weeks of fatigue and an evolving, non-blanching, non-palpable purpuric rash — initially closely resembling small-vessel vasculitis with pulmonary capillaritis given a strikingly abnormal chest x-ray — is ultimately found on CT to have bilateral cannonball pulmonary metastases and a hyperenhancing shoulder mass, confirmed as metastatic angiosarcoma with cutaneous metastases mimicking purpura and a soft tissue deposit mimicking a hematoma.

## Case Narrative

Robbie, visiting Reza in Los Angeles at the end of a vacation, presented a case using photos and imaging sent directly to Reza's phone, deliberately minimizing upfront history so Reza would reason primarily from what he could see. The case: an 89-year-old man brought in by his son, with two weeks of significant fatigue and low energy, no shortness of breath, fevers, or chills — and a striking rash.

Reza opened by emphasizing that with a rash as the chief concern, the exam should come before detailed history-taking, letting the visual findings direct subsequent questioning. From the first image, he identified a predominantly lower-extremity distribution, with confluent erythema in some areas (like the dorsum of the foot) and pinpoint lesions elsewhere, consistent with a non-blanching eruption containing petechiae, purpura, and ecchymosis together. He flagged one scabbed area on the right shin as a possible sign of ischemia/infarction requiring exclusion, and proposed confirming non-blanching status with a bedside clear-plastic or finger-pressure blanch test. Robbie confirmed via video that the rash did not blanch with direct pressure.

Reza framed the non-blanching finding the same way he'd frame any bleeding problem — a hemostasis defect versus a vessel wall problem — and proposed checking for palpability next, since palpable purpura is a strong (though not fully exclusionary, when absent) sign of small-vessel (leukocytoclastic) vasculitis. He explained the lower-extremity predominance of small-vessel vasculitis as a "two-hit" phenomenon: fragile, inflamed vessels compounded by gravity's added hydrostatic stress in the dependent legs. Reviewing a second image showing lesions on the right abdomen and an ecchymotic lesion near the right scapula, he raised hemorrhagic herpes zoster (referencing a prior RLR case) as a mimicker to exclude — looking specifically for vesicles or crusting — and flagged that an ecchymotic lesion in that posterior/flank region should also raise consideration of a retroperitoneal bleed (Grey Turner/Cullen sign).

Robbie reported the rash was not palpable at all, and shared a better-angled image of the scapular lesion. Reza reiterated that non-palpability doesn't rule out vasculitis, described the scapular lesion as genuinely ecchymotic-appearing, and continued weighing vasculitis against hemorrhagic zoster (some petechial lesions appeared possibly post-vesicular/scabbed). He recommended a CBC, PT, and PTT; screening the urine (UA with microscopy, spot protein-to-creatinine ratio) for glomerulonephritis given the vasculitis concern; asking about easily overlooked hypoxemia/dyspnea and performing a careful neurologic exam (specifically for a missed foot drop); and obtaining a CT scan to evaluate for retroperitoneal pathology and for subclinical diffuse alveolar hemorrhage/capillaritis.

Robbie then filled in the additional history and results: the rash was mildly painful, not pruritic; the shoulder lesion started as a small bump about two weeks earlier that had progressively enlarged; there was no relevant past medical history or medications; vitals were normal; exam was unremarkable apart from the rash and the patient appearing tired; CBC, coagulation studies, and UA were all normal. A representative chest x-ray (not the actual patient's image, shown for illustration) demonstrated diffuse, fluffy, bilateral alveolar opacifications.

Reza reasoned through the x-ray: not a convincing pulmonary edema pattern (no heart failure story), not a convincing multifocal pneumonia pattern, leaving diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (noting roughly 30% of DAH patients lack hemoptysis, though he'd expect more anemia than was seen given how extensive the opacification appeared) or metastatic malignancy as leading considerations. Combined with the skin findings, he leaned toward vasculitis with pulmonary capillaritis, and recommended ESR and CRP to support an inflammatory process — explicitly distinguishing vasculitis (inflammatory, confirmable serologically) from vasculopathy (structural vessel disease like atherosclerosis, which inflammatory conditions can themselves accelerate).

Robbie revealed ESR/CRP had not actually been obtained, then shared the CT scan of the chest — described as the "last major aliquot" before the diagnosis would be revealed. Scrolling through the lung windows, Reza identified multiple round, sharply marginated, dense masses scattered throughout both lungs, some pleural-based, without air bronchograms — a pattern entirely inconsistent with his prior alveolar-hemorrhage/vasculitis framing and immediately suggestive of metastatic disease. On the soft tissue window, he specifically examined the right shoulder (as Robbie had directed) and identified a markedly hyperdense, seemingly hyperenhancing mass-like structure, asymmetric compared to the normal left shoulder.

Reza reasoned live: if shown this CT scan with zero other context, he would call it metastatic cancer without hesitation, given the round masses' lack of air bronchograms — a pattern liquid, pus, or edema does not produce. He named "cannonball metastases" and worked through its narrow differential (choriocarcinoma, renal cell carcinoma, and sarcomas prominently), reasoning that the shoulder likely represented the primary lesion. Since the process didn't localize to a classic visceral organ (lung, GI tract, prostate), he favored a mesenchymal (soft tissue or bone) primary, considering rhabdomyosarcoma (would check a CK) versus osteosarcoma, and explicitly cross-referenced a recent case discussion (from an Emory grand rounds session on cryofibrinogenemia) that had taught him to ask whether a vessel-centric process reflects wall inflammation, luminal occlusion, or something else entirely.

Robbie confirmed the radiology report used the literal phrase "cannonball metastases" and described a large, hyper-enhancing soft tissue mass, concerning for a metastatic sarcoma primary in the shoulder. Reza, pushed to commit to a specific subtype, settled on osteosarcoma or rhabdomyosarcoma — acknowledging afterward that he'd come remarkably close to the vascular category outright ("I swear I almost said vessel") before landing elsewhere.

The final diagnosis was angiosarcoma — a rare, aggressive vascular soft tissue tumor. Robbie explained the full picture retrospectively: the ecchymotic-appearing scapular lesion had never actually been a bruise or a bleed at all — it was the tumor itself, an intensely vascular mass whose hyperenhancing, hyperdense appearance on CT is a classically described mimicker of hematoma. Skin biopsy confirmed cutaneous angiosarcoma throughout the "purpuric" areas, including the non-palpable lesions — meaning the entire rash, not just the scapular lesion, represented tumor infiltration of the skin and its vasculature rather than true hemostatic or inflammatory extravasation, alongside extensive pulmonary metastatic spread. The radiologist had reached "metastatic sarcoma" directly from the CT pattern (a mesenchymal-appearing focus plus cannonball metastases) before any biopsy confirmation.

Given the patient's age and disease burden, he was ultimately transitioned to inpatient hospice care after a prolonged hospitalization; the case was roughly a month old at the time of the recording, with biopsy results having taken considerable time to return.

Robbie closed by connecting this case to a very recent NEJM report of metastatic breast cancer causing livedo racemosa through direct intravascular occlusion by cancer cells — reinforcing that malignancy can mimic vasculitis or vasculopathy through more than one mechanism (paraneoplastic true vasculitis, DIC-related bleeding, or direct vascular infiltration/occlusion by tumor cells themselves), and that "is the problem the vessel wall, the lumen, or something outside the vessel entirely" needs a fourth possibility in cases like this: the vessel itself infiltrated or replaced by malignancy. Reza reflected on the value of being deliberately pushed through successive rounds of reasoning by a colleague rather than stopping at an initially plausible answer, connecting it to a recent teaching talk he and Robbie had given to chief residents about creating psychological safety for trainees to say "I don't know" while still being pushed to reason further.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Impact |
|---|---|
| Non-blanching lower extremity petechiae/purpura/ecchymosis, gravity-dependent distribution | Supports a vessel-wall (rather than purely hemostatic) process; consistent with small-vessel vasculitis |
| Normal CBC, PT, PTT, UA | Argues against a primary hemostatic disorder and against glomerulonephritis at that point in the workup; keeps vasculitis/vessel-wall pathology as the leading hypothesis |
| Ecchymotic scapular lesion with underlying firmness | Initially raised concern for a hematoma or retroperitoneal-type bleed; in retrospect, reflected the underlying vascular tumor mass itself |
| Diffuse bilateral fluffy alveolar opacifications, no heart failure story, atypical pneumonia pattern | Shifted the differential toward diffuse alveolar hemorrhage/capillaritis (supporting vasculitis) or, alternatively, metastatic disease |
| CT: multiple round, sharply marginated pulmonary masses without air bronchograms ("cannonball" pattern) | Decisively overturns the vasculitis/DAH framework in favor of metastatic malignancy |
| Hyperdense, hyperenhancing right shoulder soft tissue mass on CT | Localizes a likely primary lesion; its vascular-appearing enhancement pattern is a specific clue toward a vascular tumor, though initially read as possibly hematoma-like |
| Radiology report explicitly naming "cannonball metastases" and a mesenchymal-appearing primary | Narrows the diagnosis toward sarcoma as a category |
| Skin biopsy showing angiosarcoma throughout the "purpuric" lesions | Confirms the entire cutaneous eruption was tumor infiltration, not true hemostatic/inflammatory extravasation |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Reasoning from images alone, updated stepwise as new data arrives**: this case's format is itself a deliberate exercise in updating a working diagnosis (vasculitis) completely once disconfirming imaging (cannonball mets) arrives, rather than trying to force the new finding into the old framework.
- **A single powerful image (the CT) overturning an entire prior hypothesis**: explicitly modeled — Reza states that if shown the CT scan alone, with zero other context, he would call it cancer immediately, illustrating how a late, high-yield piece of data can and should dominate the final synthesis over earlier, now-secondary findings.
- **"Almost got there" as a teaching moment, not a failure**: Reza's near-miss (almost saying "vessel" before settling on osteosarcoma/rhabdomyosarcoma) is treated as evidence of good reasoning, not as a mistake to be embarrassed by.
- **Deliberate pushing as a stated pedagogical technique**: explicitly named and connected to a separate teaching session on creating psychological safety for trainees to admit uncertainty while still being pressed to reason further — pushing and honesty are framed as compatible, not opposed.
- **A vasculitis mimicker recognized and explicitly cross-referenced against a second recent mimicker (a prior cryofibrinogenemia case) and a third (the NEJM breast cancer/livedo racemosa case)** — building a running mental catalog of "things that look like vasculitis but aren't" as a distinct diagnostic skill.

## Teaching Discussion

This episode is structured as a live demonstration of visual, stepwise clinical reasoning under progressively revealed information, showing both the strength of a well-built framework (the blanching/palpability/distribution workup for a purpuric rash) and the necessity of being willing to abandon that framework entirely once a single decisive piece of data (the cannonball-pattern CT) arrives. It also expands the standard "vessel wall vs. lumen vs. hemostasis" triage framework with a fourth category — a vessel replaced or infiltrated by malignancy — and reinforces, via two cross-referenced mimicker cases (cryofibrinogenemia and metastatic breast cancer with livedo racemosa), that "this looks like vasculitis" always deserves a deliberate pause to consider non-inflammatory vascular mimickers before treatment is committed to.
