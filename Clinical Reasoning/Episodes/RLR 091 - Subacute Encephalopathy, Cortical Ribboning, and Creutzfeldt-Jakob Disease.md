## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 76-year-old woman declines over three months from anxious insomnia after a home robbery to a subacute encephalopathy — unable to follow commands, eating and speaking less, with a normal general medical workup. A methodical framework for subacute encephalopathy and rapidly progressive dementia leads to an MRI showing subtle cortical ribboning on diffusion-weighted imaging, raising concern for Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease; deliberate skepticism about the nonspecificity of both cortical ribboning and the 14-3-3 protein delays diagnostic closure until CSF RT-QuIC testing confirms sporadic CJD weeks later.

## Pearls

- A dramatic life stressor (like a home robbery) preceding a cognitive or psychiatric decline is a tempting causal anchor, but as symptoms progress beyond what the stressor alone would explain, deliberately reconsider whether the stressor was truly causal, merely contributory, or coincidental — patients (and clinicians) tend to retroactively search for an explanation once something feels wrong.
- In a patient who cannot participate meaningfully in a history or exam, actively look for a systemic "fingerprint" the patient cannot verbalize (pain, nausea, altered output) and review the medicine cabinet for toxins — an isolated neurological process should be a diagnosis reached only after excluding a systemic disease that has simply outstripped the patient's ability to communicate it.
- When approaching a communication disturbance, distinguish a "hardware" problem (impaired arousal/attention — encephalopathy) from a "software" problem (a focused language deficit, like aphasia, that can be mistaken for confusion by an untrained examiner) before assuming the whole picture reflects global cognitive impairment.
- Subacute presentations are especially deceptive along two axes: a nominally "acute" process (like a strategic stroke or embolic shower) can smolder into a subacute-appearing decline, while a nominally "chronic" neurodegenerative disease can suddenly accelerate — so always ask whether an apparently subacute course is actually a mistimed acute or chronic process before committing to a primary subacute framework.
- True rapidly progressive dementia (decline from independent to non-independent in activities of daily living within roughly one to two years, often faster) is itself an uncommon presentation; when genuinely this fast, the differential includes Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, an atypically aggressive course of a common neurodegenerative disease (Alzheimer's, frontotemporal dementia, Lewy body disease), and antibody-mediated autoimmune or paraneoplastic encephalitis — a category worth pursuing aggressively because, unlike CJD, many of these are treatable.
- Cortical ribboning on diffusion-weighted MRI is a classic but non-specific finding for CJD — it can also occur with seizure, HSV encephalitis, hypoxic-ischemic injury, and hyperammonemia, and predominantly limbic (rather than diffuse cortical) involvement should push the differential toward limbic encephalitis (autoimmune, paraneoplastic, or infectious) instead.
- The 14-3-3 CSF protein is a nonspecific marker of brain tissue breakdown, not a CJD-specific biomarker (it was also found in the original validating study in stroke and HSV encephalitis patients); the CSF RT-QuIC assay, which directly detects the abnormally folded prion protein, is the high-specificity test that should drive diagnostic certainty before delivering a fatal, untreatable diagnosis to a family.

## Problem Representation

A 76-year-old woman with a history of hypertension develops anxiety and insomnia after being robbed in her home, followed over three months by progressive decline in oral intake, arousal, and the ability to understand or communicate with her family, with two prior "weakness" and "stroke" workups yielding no diagnosis; a normal general medical evaluation, non-diagnostic first-pass MRI sequences, and subtle cortical ribboning with restricted diffusion on DWI/ADC ultimately lead — after deliberate exclusion of mimicking causes and confirmatory CSF RT-QuIC testing — to a diagnosis of sporadic Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease.

## Case Narrative

A 76-year-old woman with a history of hypertension is robbed in her home roughly three months prior to presentation. Understandably distressed, she becomes increasingly anxious, sleeps poorly at night, and spends progressively more time in bed — sleeping more overall but remaining visibly anxious while awake. Her family takes her to an outside hospital for vague "weakness" (with a mention of a rarely-invoked diagnosis, mesenteric panniculitis, that isn't clearly substantiated), and she is given prednisone and discharged. Symptoms continue to worsen; another outside workup for "weakness" yields nothing. Over the three weeks before the current presentation, she sleeps more, eats less, and her family reports she no longer seems to understand them and speaks very little. A separate hospital visit documents a negative "stroke workup." By the time she is seen for this case, she has not tolerated any oral intake and hasn't had a bowel movement in weeks.

The presenting discussant frames this as fundamentally not a localization mystery — something is clearly wrong in the brain — but emphasizes that the real diagnostic challenge in cases like this is simply identifying what the actual problem is, since encephalopathic patients often cannot reliably communicate systemic symptoms (weakness, oral intake, GI output) that might otherwise redirect the workup. He advises tracking every mentioned symptom even while pursuing the leading hypothesis, attaching diagnostic significance only after a focused, one-to-two-minute physical exam looking for a corroborating "fingerprint" of disease.

One discussant's early reasoning notes that none of the described "weakness" is objectively documented, and that the consistent thread across three months is a progressive disturbance of cognitive function — starting with an anxiety and insomnia picture plausibly (initially) explained by the robbery, but becoming implausible as a purely psychological reaction once it progresses to impaired comprehension and reduced speech output. He recalls a prior teaching framework — is a communication problem "hardware" (impaired arousal/attention, encephalopathy) or "software" (an isolated language problem, like aphasia, that can look like confusion to an untrained eye) — and lands on a subacute (with a possible acute component) encephalopathy as the working frame, prioritizing a brain MRI given the exam's likely limitations in a poorly cooperative patient.

A second discussant agrees, but flags an important cognitive trap: it's tempting to assume the robbery is causally related to everything that follows, when in fact it may be fully causal, partially contributory, or entirely coincidental — a very human tendency to retroactively search for an explanation (analogous to blaming the last thing you ate for an unrelated stomach bug). He advises approaching a poorly communicative patient with particular humility toward hidden systemic disease: the patient cannot report muscle pain, abdominal pain, nausea, or other localizing systemic symptoms, so a broad medical evaluation — including a careful review of medications and potential toxins at home — must accompany, not follow, the neurological workup.

The presenting discussant confirms the framing: this is a subacute encephalopathy, and the central question is whether it represents a global encephalopathy or an isolated, severe language disturbance being mistaken for confusion. On exam: normal vital signs; prior general medical and emergency evaluations were unremarkable; she does not appear acutely medically ill; eyes closed, sleeping, arms flexed on her chest; opens eyes to voice without clearly purposeful tracking; not English-speaking, making unintelligible sounds via interpreter/family, not following commands; tone appears increased but is difficult to characterize precisely — spasticity, rigidity, or paratonia/gegenhalten (a resistance to passive movement classically seen in encephalopathic patients) are all considered, without clear asymmetry; a noxious stimulus (nail bed pressure) produces symmetric withdrawal.

One discussant notes that with such a limited exam, imaging and labs will likely carry more diagnostic weight than the neurological exam itself (reflex testing, for instance, is unreliable in a patient who cannot relax), while recalling a case of profound extended-leg rigidity and visible fear ultimately diagnosed as paraneoplastic stiff person syndrome from a thymoma — flagged as one possibility to keep in mind without anchoring on it here. The other discussant reiterates that the working hypothesis (a brain-centered process) has now been reasonably corroborated by the exam, but repeats his caution to remain open to an occult systemic driver — including antibody-mediated paraneoplastic disease, which itself is often triggered by a subtle, easily overlooked systemic process — and to specifically screen the patient's medications and home environment for subacute toxic causes of altered mental status, distinct from the more familiar hyperacute toxidromes.

Asked to expand the differential for subacute encephalopathy, the presenting discussant offers: toxic/metabolic/medical causes (the most common reason for a neurology consult from an inpatient medicine service), and paraneoplastic/autoimmune encephalitis (invoked often in this context but genuinely uncommon in his personal experience). One responding discussant adds rapidly progressive dementia (RPD) as the major additional concern — most often an atypically fast presentation of a common neurodegenerative disease (Alzheimer's "on steroids"), but with a differential that includes Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, thyroid-related encephalopathy (Hashimoto's/SREAT), certain vasculitides (excluding primary CNS vasculitis specifically), and other autoimmune encephalitides; he considers the tempo too subacute for a seizure-driven process (referencing the prior episode's discussion of status epilepticus) and raises a patchy embolic phenomenon (recalling a dialysis patient with valvular calciphylaxis and subacute cognitive decline from embolic showers) as a rare stroke-adjacent mimicker. The other discussant offers a complementary four-part framework for subacute presentations: (1) a nominally acute process (like a strategic stroke) smoldering into a subacute-appearing course, (2) a nominally chronic process (like Alzheimer's) suddenly accelerating, (3) genuine rapidly progressive dementia as its own intellectually distinct category, and (4) localizing by anatomic compartment — parenchyma, vasculature, or meninges — raising subacute meningoencephalitis (from causes like sarcoidosis or tuberculosis, echoing a prior RLR case) as another possible site of disease, distinguishable by whether the CSF is inflammatory.

The presenting discussant expands on rapidly progressive dementia: true RPD (progression from independent function to non-independence in roughly one to two years or less) is itself an uncommon presentation. When genuinely this fast, Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (roughly one in a million to two million, untreatable, usually fatal within a year) is the classically taught but fortunately rare answer; far more common is an atypically aggressive course of Alzheimer's disease, frontotemporal dementia, Lewy body disease, or corticobasal degeneration — diseases that normally progress along a predictable bell curve (Alzheimer's, for example, loses roughly three MMSE/MoCA points per year on average) but occasionally "fall off a cliff" for reasons not fully understood. He also highlights autoimmune, antibody-mediated encephalitis as an emerging and important category — not common, but critical not to miss, since cell-surface antibody syndromes (such as anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis) are often treatable, while intracellular-antigen ("anti-Hu"-type) paraneoplastic antibodies are typically less treatable and more strongly associated with underlying malignancy. Medical, toxic, metabolic, endocrine, and drug causes remain broadly relevant throughout, and he offers illustrative alternative explanations (a sodium of 106, sepsis, an ammonia of 200, prolonged urinary retention) that would immediately deprioritize the RPD workup if present.

Workup: a general medical evaluation including urine toxicology, plus the best available brain imaging. Results: BMP, CBC, TSH, ammonia, B12, syphilis, HIV, and urine toxicology are all normal; CRP 46 and ESR 38 (not dramatically abnormal for a 76-year-old); CT chest is unremarkable; CT abdomen/pelvis shows a large stool burden and osteoporosis, with nothing acute.

The first MRI sequence shown is momentarily mistaken for a T1 image because the ventricles are not bright, but is explained to be a T2 FLAIR sequence — T2 with the CSF signal subtracted out, which is why it can visually resemble a T1 image; this sequence is typically reviewed first because it captures most acute and chronic pathology at a glance. On this sequence, there is only mild periventricular white matter disease consistent with age-related small vessel disease; the brain is not atrophied and the ventricles are not enlarged — nothing definitively abnormal is identified by either responding discussant.

A DWI/ADC sequence is shown next, explained as the correct next step to assess for true diffusion restriction (versus a "T2 shine-through" artifact that would also appear bright on DWI but would not be dark on the corresponding ADC map). On this sequence, subtle cortical signal is identified in the right temporal-occipital region on one slice, and — on a second slice — more broadly, with the right frontal region most pronounced but also present on the left in the temporal-frontal region; the caudate/basal ganglia region also shows subtle diffusion restriction. One discussant recognizes this "ribbon" pattern as a classic buzzword for Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease and connects it back to the earlier ambiguous increase in tone, wondering whether it in fact represented myoclonus.

The presenting discussant confirms this is diffusion restriction in a cortical ribbon pattern across multiple gyri, with additional subtle basal ganglia (caudate) involvement, and notes that cortical ribboning is far more specific for CJD on DWI/ADC than on FLAIR, where it is much subtler.

Before accepting the diagnosis, one discussant explicitly resists closure: a diagnosis of CJD cannot be made on MRI alone, and the widely taught 14-3-3 CSF protein is not truly disease-specific (recalling a prior case where a positive result was initially exciting but later understood to be nonspecific); a specific sequencing- or conversion-based CSF assay exists and should be pursued, and an EEG could offer supportive (not diagnostic) evidence. The other discussant agrees, adding that cortical ribboning itself is not specific to CJD — he has personally seen it once in a patient with a migraine, and it is also described in seizure, HSV encephalitis, and autoimmune encephalitis — so his confidence in a CJD diagnosis is "dampened" pending further data. He recommends confirming the absence of ongoing (including non-convulsive) seizure activity with EEG, obtaining a non-inflammatory CSF profile as a supportive criterion, considering empiric coverage for HSV despite the prolonged three-month course (unless a distinct, more acute process has been superimposed), sending a paraneoplastic antibody panel, and specifically naming the CSF RT-QuIC (real-time quaking-induced conversion) assay — a test he knows only by its excellent reported performance characteristics, not personal experience — as the most informative next step.

The presenting discussant praises the depth of this reasoning as "gourmet neurology" beyond what most learners bring to this topic, and provides additional detail: the 14-3-3 protein is a ubiquitously expressed intracellular protein found in all eukaryotic cells; the original study proposing it as a CJD biomarker also found it positive in 4 of 4 stroke patients and 11 of 12 HSV encephalitis patients tested as controls, meaning it simply reflects nonspecific brain tissue breakdown rather than a CJD-specific process. In the right clinical context (a compatible rapidly progressive dementia with a suggestive MRI), its specificity rises into the 80% range, but remains imperfect, and its sensitivity against pathology-confirmed cases is only around 50%. Brain biopsy is essentially avoided given the infection-control burden of handling prion-contaminated tissue (specialized incineration protocols for instruments), and definitive pathological confirmation is centralized at a national prion disease pathology surveillance center. The correct high-value test is CSF RT-QuIC, which directly detects the abnormally misfolded prion protein with much greater specificity. He confirms that cortical ribboning, while classically taught for CJD, is also seen in status epilepticus/seizure, hyperammonemia, hypoxic-ischemic brain injury (typically after cardiac arrest, usually a more acute presentation), and HSV encephalitis (though HSV changes are typically accompanied by FLAIR signal change and predominate in the limbic/medial temporal structures) — a predominantly limbic distribution should in fact push the differential away from CJD and toward limbic encephalitis (autoimmune, paraneoplastic, or infectious). He notes that current MRI-based CJD diagnostic criteria, combined with a compatible clinical picture, can technically support the diagnosis even without RT-QuIC, but that these criteria predate many of the more recently characterized antibody-mediated autoimmune encephalitis syndromes — reinforcing the need for a broad, rigorous workup (explicitly likened to the diagnostic rigor expected before delivering an equally grave, fatal diagnosis like ALS) before final closure.

Additional clinical detail emerges: the family had described the patient as "seeming scared all the time," which on closer questioning represented an exaggerated startle response (startle myoclonus), and repeated tone/reflex checks intermittently elicited a brief, whole-body, Moro-like jerk.

CSF is obtained, though technically difficult (an older patient with significant spinal bony disease requiring interventional radiology guidance, causing a diagnostic delay): 1 white cell, protein 23, glucose 80 — a non-inflammatory profile. A paraneoplastic antibody panel is sent (returns later, ultimately negative). RT-QuIC, 14-3-3, and tau are sent together to a specialized reference laboratory and return weeks later: 14-3-3 elevated above the assay threshold, tau (another nonspecific marker of brain breakdown) also elevated, and RT-QuIC positive — confirming sporadic Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease.

In discussion, the presenting discussant reflects on how to deliver this diagnosis: the family had already researched "fast dementia" online and suspected CJD before being told directly; given the absence of any treatment, care shifts to comfort, occasionally alongside an empiric steroid trial in case of an inflammatory contribution (not clearly indicated here). His approach to breaking this kind of news involves naming the disease plainly, pausing to gauge whether the family already has some familiarity with it, and anchoring the explanation in a more familiar reference point (framing CJD as, in effect, "a fast version of Alzheimer's disease"). He reviews prion disease epidemiology: the great majority of cases are sporadic (spontaneous prion misfolding); variant CJD ("mad cow disease," from contaminated meat) is exceedingly rare, numbering only in the low hundreds of cases worldwide; genetic/familial prion disorders exist; and a small number of historical iatrogenic cases occurred from contaminated neurosurgical instruments, cadaveric dural grafts, and cadaveric human growth hormone, predating modern understanding and precautions. He notes that while no confirmed transmission has occurred via routine needle-stick or splash exposure, standard universal precautions (gown, glove, mask, face shield, with a defined bleach-based spill protocol) are still followed for any procedure on a patient with suspected CJD, out of caution.

Closing the episode, one discussant asks about the diagnostic value of myoclonus when no toxic/metabolic or medication cause is identified (his default framework otherwise being toxic-metabolic first, since accumulated medications such as gabapentin in renal impairment are common culprits). The presenting discussant offers a general movement-disorder schema — primary neurologic disease, structural lesion, toxic-metabolic cause, or systemic illness — applicable across tremor, myoclonus, and chorea alike, and notes that myoclonus itself remains fairly nonspecific: it occurs in Alzheimer's disease, corticobasal syndrome (a rare Parkinson-plus disorder), epileptic myoclonus (raising the recurring question of non-convulsive status epilepticus and the value of continuous EEG), and as a recognized side effect of SSRIs and opioids. A passing mention of Hashimoto's encephalopathy (steroid-responsive encephalopathy associated with autoimmune thyroiditis, SREAT) prompts a reference to a paper questioning whether this is a truly distinct entity, given that elevated thyroid peroxidase antibodies are found incidentally in roughly 30% of the general population and the causal relationship to encephalopathy remains uncertain.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Effect on leading hypothesis |
|---|---|
| Progressive decline in arousal, comprehension, and speech over three months following a home robbery, with two prior nondiagnostic "weakness" and "stroke" workups | Shifts away from a purely psychological/PTSD explanation toward an organic subacute encephalopathy as symptoms progress and generalize |
| Normal vitals, unremarkable prior medical workups, ambiguous increased tone without clear focality, symmetric response to noxious stimulus | Supports a diffuse, brain-centered process (encephalopathy) over a focal structural lesion; limits the diagnostic yield of the neurological exam itself |
| Normal BMP, CBC, TSH, ammonia, B12, syphilis, HIV, urine toxicology; unremarkable CT chest/abdomen/pelvis | Down-weights common medical, toxic, metabolic, and infectious causes of subacute encephalopathy |
| MRI FLAIR: only mild age-related periventricular white matter change | Does not support a primary vascular, atrophic, or grossly structural explanation |
| MRI DWI/ADC: cortical ribboning with true diffusion restriction across multiple gyri, subtle caudate/basal ganglia involvement | Raises specific concern for Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, while remaining explicitly non-diagnostic given the finding's differential (seizure, HSV, autoimmune encephalitis) |
| Startle myoclonus and intermittent whole-body jerks on exam | Adds a supportive clinical feature for CJD, though still nonspecific on its own |
| CSF: 1 WBC, protein 23, glucose 80 (non-inflammatory); paraneoplastic panel negative | Argues against an inflammatory meningoencephalitis or paraneoplastic/autoimmune encephalitis as the driver |
| CSF 14-3-3 and tau elevated above assay threshold; RT-QuIC positive | Confirms sporadic Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease with high specificity |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The case models deliberate resistance to two forms of premature closure: attributing the initial symptoms entirely to the antecedent robbery (a compelling but potentially misleading causal narrative), and accepting cortical ribboning on MRI as diagnostic of CJD without first excluding its several mimics (seizure, HSV, autoimmune encephalitis) and pursuing a definitive confirmatory test.

Both discussants explicitly model calibrated uncertainty in a high-stakes, irreversible diagnostic context: despite a highly suggestive MRI and supportive clinical findings (startle myoclonus), they withhold diagnostic certainty and continue pursuing CSF confirmation (RT-QuIC) before communicating a fatal, untreatable diagnosis to the family — drawing an explicit parallel to the rigor expected before diagnosing ALS.

The discussion of the 14-3-3 protein's history illustrates a recurring theme: an early, uncontrolled observation (a proposed biomarker later found in the very control groups it was meant to be tested against) can become entrenched as a diagnostic shortcut long after its actual specificity has been shown to be poor.

## Teaching Discussion

Subacute cognitive or behavioral decline in a patient recently exposed to a plausible psychological stressor should be reassessed for an organic cause once symptoms progress beyond what the stressor alone would explain; a compelling causal narrative should not be allowed to substitute for ongoing reassessment.

In a poorly communicative patient, always screen for a systemic "fingerprint" of disease the patient cannot verbalize, and review medications and the home environment for subacute toxic causes, before concluding that a purely neurological process is isolated.

Subacute presentations are deceptive along two axes — an acute process smoldering into an apparently subacute course, and a chronic process suddenly accelerating — and can also be organized by anatomic compartment (parenchyma, vasculature, or meninges), with CSF inflammatory markers helping to distinguish a meningoencephalitic process from one confined to brain tissue.

True rapidly progressive dementia is uncommon; when present, the differential includes Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, an atypically aggressive course of a common neurodegenerative disease, and antibody-mediated autoimmune or paraneoplastic encephalitis — the last of which deserves aggressive pursuit because, unlike CJD, many of these syndromes are treatable, particularly the cell-surface antibody subtypes.

Cortical ribboning on diffusion-weighted MRI is a classic but nonspecific finding that also occurs in seizure, HSV encephalitis, hypoxic-ischemic injury, and hyperammonemia; a predominantly limbic distribution of MRI changes should redirect the differential toward limbic encephalitis (autoimmune, paraneoplastic, or infectious) rather than CJD.

The CSF 14-3-3 protein is a nonspecific marker of brain tissue breakdown rather than a CJD-specific biomarker; CSF RT-QuIC, which directly detects the misfolded prion protein, is the appropriate high-specificity confirmatory test and should be obtained before delivering a diagnosis with this degree of certainty and finality to a family.

Myoclonus without an identifiable toxic-metabolic or medication cause should prompt a broader movement-disorder framework (primary neurologic disease, structural lesion, toxic-metabolic, or systemic illness) and consideration of epileptic myoclonus/non-convulsive status epilepticus, since myoclonus itself is seen across a wide range of neurodegenerative and metabolic conditions and is not specific to any single diagnosis.
