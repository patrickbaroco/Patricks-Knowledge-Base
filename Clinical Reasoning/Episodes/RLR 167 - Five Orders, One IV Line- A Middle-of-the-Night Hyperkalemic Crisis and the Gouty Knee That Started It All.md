Reza manages the crisis entirely from a distance — sight unseen — walking through pseudohyperkalemia, the "D's" of hyperkalemia management (diuretic, diarrhea/binders, dextrose-insulin, dialysis), and the distinction between targeted and supportive treatment, while Robbie juggles five simultaneous orders through a single unreliable IV line. ## Pearls

- Hyperkalemia is dangerous specifically because it's usually clinically silent until a sudden dysrhythmia, with concern rising sharply once the potassium climbs much above 6 — there's no reliable early-warning sign to wait for, which argues for prompt empiric stabilization once a critical value is confirmed or strongly suspected.
- Before treating a critical potassium as real, consider pseudohyperkalemia from ex vivo cell lysis during or after the draw; sending a concurrent AST can help confirm true hemolysis (it will also be elevated), but a lab's own "possibly hemolyzed" flag shouldn't be relied on alone — repeat the test.
- A patient's renal function calibrates how much to trust a reported potassium: with normally functioning kidneys, severe hyperkalemia is very difficult to sustain (loosely analogous to how a negative ANA makes lupus unlikely) outside of an overwhelming acute potassium load like tumor lysis syndrome; with advanced renal failure, a critically high potassium becomes biologically expected and should be believed.
- When a nurse reports a stable, conversant patient with no signs of acute decompensation, it can be appropriate — and even more time-efficient — to enter stabilizing orders immediately from a distance rather than walking to the bedside first, since electrolyte emergencies are typically silent killers rather than overt ones; this is a situational exception to, not a replacement for, the general instinct to see the patient first.
- Organize hyperkalemia management by separating targeted treatment (definitively fixing the underlying cause — here, dialysis for renal failure) from supportive treatment (temporizing measures: membrane stabilization, transcellular shifting, and elimination), and pursue the targeted treatment in parallel with supportive care rather than treating supportive measures as a sufficient endpoint on their own.
- A practical ordering discipline for hyperkalemia: never give insulin/dextrose without also giving calcium if there's real concern for cardiac instability, and never give calcium without also initiating some form of potassium elimination (a diuretic or a binder like Lokelma) — calcium buys time, but only elimination or shifting therapy actually uses it.
- Diuretic effectiveness for hyperkalemia depends on functioning nephron mass — a rough dosing heuristic is that the effective IV furosemide dose scales to roughly 20 times the serum creatinine, meaning diuretics are often nearly useless in advanced renal failure even at high doses.
- Insulin given for hyperkalemia must be intravenous, not subcutaneous — subcutaneous dosing has unpredictable, delayed onset and offset that can produce severe, prolonged hypoglycemia, especially dangerous in renal failure where insulin clearance is already impaired.
- For an acutely inflamed single joint, the two diagnoses that must always be actively excluded are septic arthritis and crystalline arthritis (gout/pseudogout) — arthrocentesis with cell count/differential, gram stain, and crystal analysis remains the definitive way to distinguish them, though a rapid in-house turnaround (as fast as 30 minutes in some settings) can allow real-time, results-driven decisions about empiric antibiotics rather than treating blindly.

## Problem Representation

A 76-year-old woman with end-stage renal disease on Monday/Wednesday/Friday hemodialysis presents by ambulance to the VA emergency department after missing two consecutive dialysis sessions because severe right knee pain and swelling prevented her from traveling, and is found on a point-of-care blood gas to have a critical potassium of 7.9 — reasoned through and managed in real time by Reza working entirely blind from a single lab value, while Robbie is physically mid-procedure draining a different patient's knee, before the crisis resolves with aggressive supportive therapy and the patient's own knee is ultimately found to be an acute gout flare rather than septic arthritis.

## Case Narrative

Robbie sets up the case: working a solo overnight shift, he's in the middle of draining a large knee effusion in a patient with a history of osteoarthritis — the joint had no erythema, the fluid looked reassuringly light-colored and non-infectious, and he'd proceeded straight to therapeutic aspiration without sending it for formal analysis. He's mid-procedure, switching out a full 30cc syringe for a second one, when a nurse knocks with news that can't wait: an ambulance patient who arrived about ten minutes earlier has a point-of-care blood gas printout showing a critical potassium of 7.9. Robbie poses the scenario to Reza exactly as he lived it — alone, overnight, with a needle still in another patient's knee — and asks him to reason through it blind.

Reza's first move is to credit the nurse for interrupting, and to draw a distinction between ordinary and truly dangerous hyperkalemia: a potassium of 5.6 no longer excites him after a decade of practice, but anything approaching or above 6, and especially a value like 7.9, is genuinely worrisome. He immediately flags two challenges. First, the value itself might not be real — pseudohyperkalemia from cells lysing outside the body (ex vivo hemolysis) is common enough that he'd want to repeat the test, ideally sending a concurrent AST in the same draw, since a falsely elevated potassium from hemolysis is often accompanied by a falsely elevated AST as well; even if the lab flags the sample as possibly hemolyzed, he'd still want to confirm with a fresh repeat rather than simply trusting that read. Second, and more urgent: hyperkalemia is a silent killer — it frequently produces no symptoms at all until a sudden, potentially fatal arrhythmia appears with no warning. Given the practical problem of a needle in someone else's knee, Reza suggests either withdrawing it or having a nurse hold it in place sterile while Robbie steps away, and lays out his immediate plan: treat the potassium as real until proven otherwise, have calcium gluconate (2 grams) drawn up for cardiac membrane stabilization regardless of whether the value turns out to be a lab error, get an EKG looking for peaked T waves and other classic changes (while cautioning that their absence doesn't rule out danger), and place the patient on telemetry.

Robbie sharpens the exercise with a paired hypothetical before revealing more data: given the same critical potassium, how would Reza's confidence change if the creatinine came back as 0.5 versus 9? Reza credits a nephrologist mentor, Manny, with the underlying principle — if the kidneys are working well, it's extremely difficult to become meaningfully hyperkalemic, loosely comparable to how a negative ANA makes lupus unlikely. The only real exception is an acute potassium load large enough to overwhelm even normal renal clearance, as in tumor lysis syndrome. Absent that scenario, he says plainly that he simply wouldn't believe a potassium of 7.9 paired with a creatinine of 0.5 — but a creatinine of 9 makes the same potassium entirely plausible, most likely reflecting a patient with end-stage renal disease who has missed dialysis.

The full chemistry panel confirms the more concerning branch: sodium 128, potassium 7.9, CO2 12, pH 7.21, and a creatinine of 9 — a picture of severe renal failure with a significant metabolic acidosis, all of it consistent with markedly impaired excretion. Robbie removes the needle from the original knee patient, who is satisfied with the partial drainage she's already gotten and opts to leave rather than wait, and pivots fully to the new crisis — noting, as an aside, that the case has a "full circle" element still to come. He then poses a logistical question that turns out to be a genuine teaching point: his computer sits roughly halfway between the low-acuity area where he'd been working and the high-acuity bay where the new patient is — should he walk to the bedside first, or go straight to the computer and start entering orders? Reza's answer is immediate and confident: go straight to the computer. If the nurse's report is that the patient is talking normally and not in any overt distress, that's reassuring enough, because electrolyte derangements like this are silent killers rather than overt ones — there's essentially nothing Robbie could accomplish at the bedside in that moment that isn't better accomplished by getting stabilizing treatment ordered immediately. Both hosts note this is a rare exception to their usual instinct to see the patient before doing anything else, made necessary by the specific spatial and time constraints of a solo overnight shift.

With the diagnosis effectively confirmed, Reza walks through his full hyperkalemia treatment schema, organized as a series of "D's." Diuretic is technically first alphabetically, but its usefulness depends entirely on functioning nephron mass — citing a dosing heuristic from mentor Joel Topf, the effective IV furosemide dose scales to roughly 20 times the serum creatinine, meaning a creatinine of 9 would require an impractically large dose to have any real effect, so he doesn't expect much benefit here. Diarrhea — really, GI potassium binders — is next: where kayexalate and lactulose were once standard (with historical caution around bowel surgery/perforation risk), Reza now reaches for Lokelma (sodium zirconium cyclosilicate) as a more effective, better-tolerated option, ordering 10 grams. Dextrose, paired with insulin, temporarily shifts potassium intracellularly rather than eliminating it, and requires real caution in renal failure since impaired insulin clearance raises the risk of prolonged hypoglycemia; Reza is explicit that this must always be given intravenously, never subcutaneously, recalling a personal case where a subcutaneous dose caused severe hypoglycemia and landed a patient in the ICU in a coma. He typically favors a modest dose (around 5 units of IV insulin with dextrose) rather than a larger one, and isn't a routine user of inhaled beta-agonists for this indication. The final D, and the one this patient actually needs, is dialysis — the only truly definitive treatment when the underlying renal failure can't be corrected any other way.

Robbie names the organizing principle underneath all of this explicitly: separate targeted treatment, which addresses the actual root cause (here, dialysis for renal failure), from supportive treatment, which only temporizes (membrane stabilization, transcellular shifting, and elimination). Targeted treatment should always be pursued in parallel with supportive care, not deferred until supportive measures run their course. Within the supportive options, he frames the choice as a return-on-investment-versus-risk calculation — Lokelma and diuretics carry a favorable ratio, while insulin and dextrose carry a more questionable one given the hypoglycemia risk Reza just described. He also names two hard ordering rules that both hosts confirm they've never violated in practice: insulin/dextrose should never be given without calcium if there's genuine concern the patient could be in extremis, since calcium's job is immediate membrane protection while insulin/dextrose is only a temporary parking measure; and calcium should never be given without also initiating some form of elimination or binding therapy, since stabilizing the membrane without also working to remove the potassium accomplishes little on its own. Lokelma or a diuretic alone, without calcium, remains appropriate for lower-acuity, non-emergent hyperkalemia — Reza gives the example of bumping up a patient's home Lokelma dose for a chronic kidney disease patient with a potassium of 5.6, no calcium needed.

The chart fills in the clinical picture: the patient is 76 years old, has end-stage renal disease on a Monday/Wednesday/Friday dialysis schedule, and takes potassium binders, a vitamin D analog, and 80mg of oral furosemide twice daily. The emergency department tracking board lists her chief complaint as knee pain with two missed dialysis sessions. On exam, her tunneled dialysis catheter in her left chest looks appropriate and functional, and aside from a large, swollen, hot right knee, the rest of her exam is unremarkable. She explains that severe right knee pain and swelling over the preceding week made her unable to get to dialysis on Monday or Wednesday, and she only came in now by ambulance because the pain had become too severe to walk, worried about what would happen if she also missed Friday's session.

With five separate orders now in hand — calcium gluconate, IV insulin, dextrose, IV furosemide (Bumex), and Lokelma — and only a single, poor-quality IV line in the patient's right arm, the bedside nurse asks which to prioritize. Reza's sequencing: calcium gluconate first, given the acuity; dextrose next, immediately followed by IV insulin (minimizing any gap that could risk hypoglycemia); Lokelma can go at any point since it doesn't require IV access at all and can simply be handed to the patient; and the diuretic goes last, both because it doesn't need to compete for the line as urgently and because Reza doesn't expect it to be particularly effective in this degree of renal failure anyway. On the arthritis side, Robbie frames the swollen knee using a parallel to the show's familiar "top differentials" reflex device — just as chest pain should immediately trigger consideration of ACS, PE, and dissection, an acutely inflamed single joint should immediately trigger consideration of septic arthritis and crystalline arthritis. Given the patient's tunneled catheter and dialysis-dependent, relatively immunocompromised state, septic arthritis is a real concern; a prior personal history of gout would support empiric treatment without further workup in some cases, but absent that, the standard approach is arthrocentesis with cell count and differential, gram stain, and crystal analysis, with empiric antibiotics considered pending results.

Nephrology is called and updated in real time as the interventions are given — not held off until a recheck — and about an hour and a half later, at roughly 3am, the repeat potassium comes back at 5.4, confirmed concordant between the point-of-care and formal lab values. The dramatic response allows the on-call nephrologist to defer emergent overnight dialysis in favor of the patient's next scheduled session rather than initiating middle-of-the-night dialysis right away. Reza specifically notes that calling nephrology immediately, in parallel with treatment rather than after seeing how the recheck turned out, was the right move regardless of the outcome — had the repeat instead come back closer to 6.5-7 (his own predicted range), the team would have already lost valuable preparation time by waiting.

The case closes with its promised full circle: Robbie performs a second arthrocentesis, this time on the crashing patient's own swollen knee, drawing off fluid with the same technique he'd used earlier in the shift on an unrelated patient. A nurse's playful "gotcha, doc — nothing, everything's fine, don't worry" delivers the reassuring read: gout, not infection. She receives an intra-articular steroid injection (administered, by Robbie's own admission, only after he allowed himself a two-hour nap first) and is scheduled for dialysis the next day. The gout flare that had kept her from two consecutive dialysis sessions turns out to be the same problem — closing the loop on both threads of the night's work.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

The episode's clearest cognitive teaching is the distinction between targeted and supportive treatment, applied here to hyperkalemia but framed by Robbie as a general habit for any acute derangement: identify and pursue the actual corrective treatment (dialysis) in parallel with — never instead of — temporizing measures, rather than treating supportive care as a sufficient endpoint on its own. The "should I go see the patient or go straight to orders" moment is explicitly flagged by both hosts as a rare, situation-specific exception to their near-universal instinct to lay eyes on a patient first — useful precisely because it's bounded (a reassuring secondhand report, and no action available at the bedside that isn't better accomplished remotely) rather than a general principle to overapply. The creatinine-calibrates-potassium heuristic is a good example of using one data point to judge how much to trust another, rather than evaluating each lab result in isolation. Finally, the decision to call nephrology before knowing the repeat potassium result is a clean instance of a recurring show theme: a decision's validity is judged by the reasoning and information available at the time it was made, not by how favorably things happened to turn out afterward.

## Teaching Discussion

This episode works as a fully worked, immediately usable hyperkalemia management schema, made memorable by the genuinely practical constraint that forces it into the open — a solo overnight physician mid-procedure, with a single unreliable IV line and five simultaneous orders to prioritize. The organizing move of separating targeted treatment (dialysis, since the root problem is renal failure) from supportive treatment (calcium, shifting, and elimination) gives a durable mental model that generalizes well beyond hyperkalemia to other acute derangements with both a fixable cause and a need for interim stabilization. The specific ordering rules demonstrated — never insulin/dextrose without calcium when instability is a real concern; never calcium without also initiating elimination; diuretic effectiveness scaling with functioning nephron mass via the "20x creatinine" heuristic — are concrete and bedside-usable. The real-world detail of sequencing orders across one poor IV line is an operational teaching easy to omit from a purely pharmacologic discussion, but one that matters enormously in practice.

The knee-arthritis subplot, while smaller, reinforces the show's recurring "two-diagnosis reflex" device (chest pain → ACS/PE/dissection; monoarticular arthritis → septic versus crystalline) and closes with a satisfying structural symmetry: the same overnight shift that opens with Robbie draining one patient's osteoarthritic knee ends with him draining a second patient's gouty knee — which turns out to be the actual root cause of the entire hyperkalemic emergency by way of two missed dialysis sessions.
