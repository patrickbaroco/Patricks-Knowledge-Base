## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Two patients present with fatigue within thirty minutes of each other. The first, a 73-year-old with a bioprosthetic aortic valve, is rapidly worked up for endocarditis-driven decompensated heart failure — a leukocytosis of 27,000 becomes the standout clue — and within the hour develops an embolic stroke that a suspicion for endocarditis correctly halts from receiving tPA moments before it would have been given. Riding the high of that catch, Rabih goes to discharge the second patient, a dialysis-dependent man with three days of fatigue and an entirely unremarkable workup — only to have the man's daughter reveal he can't walk. He had only ever been examined seated in a wheelchair. The second half of the episode traces that miss to a rare, painless diagnosis: dialysis-induced spinal cord ischemia from vascular steal. The episode is as much about the discipline of walking every fatigued patient as it is about either diagnosis.

## Pearls

- Fatigue is a highly nonspecific symptom whose single most useful branch point is whether it worsens with exertion — exertional fatigue prioritizes the cardiopulmonary and hematologic systems, while non-exertional, at-rest fatigue broadens the differential toward depression, endocrine disease (e.g., hypothyroidism), and other systemic causes.
- Applying the exertional-fatigue framework requires actually observing the patient exert themselves — a patient too fatigued to walk, or examined only while seated, cannot have this key branch point meaningfully assessed from history alone; physically walking a fatigued patient is an essential, easily skipped step.
- An unexpectedly high white blood cell count (e.g., 27,000) in a patient with a plausible diagnosis (such as endocarditis) should prompt reflection on whether that magnitude of leukocytosis is actually typical for the working diagnosis — when personal experience or base rates suggest it usually isn't, it's worth actively searching for a second or alternative process (such as an occult abscess) rather than assuming it's simply a more severe version of the leading hypothesis.
- New atrial fibrillation in a patient with systemic illness should prompt the question of directionality: is the arrhythmia the primary driver of the patient's decompensation, or is it a secondary consequence of an underlying systemic process (infection, inflammation, ischemia) affecting the myocardium? A markedly elevated white count alongside new AFib favors the latter, and argues for caution with beta-blockade, since the tachycardia may be a compensatory response to reduced cardiac output.
- Objective heart failure assessment benefits from tracking a defined set of data points over time — weight trend, JVP, pulmonary and lower-extremity edema on exam, chest X-ray, BNP (ideally trended, including at discharge), and creatinine — rather than relying on any single data point in isolation.
- Decompensated heart failure with a markedly elevated white count should prompt consideration of an inflammatory or infectious driver of the cardiac decompensation itself: sepsis, catecholamine/stress-induced cardiomyopathy, thyrotoxicosis (a hypermetabolic rather than truly inflammatory mechanism), and — especially in a patient with a prosthetic valve — infective endocarditis, which can present subacutely with fatigue, edema, and a new arrhythmia potentially reflecting myocardial or conduction system involvement from a vegetation.
- In suspected endocarditis, a negative transthoracic echocardiogram should not end the workup — transesophageal echocardiography and, increasingly, PET-CT (which can detect vegetations missed on TEE) should be pursued when clinical suspicion remains high, alongside blood cultures obtained with a low threshold in any patient with a prosthetic valve or other foreign vascular material.
- In a patient with a prosthetic valve and suspicion for infective endocarditis who develops an acute embolic-appearing stroke, thrombolysis (tPA) should be paused and reconsidered: infected embolic material (essentially septic debris rather than simple thrombus) does not respond to thrombolytics the way bland clot does, and endocarditis-associated septic emboli are specifically associated with mycotic (infection-related) microaneurysms that substantially raise the risk of intracranial hemorrhage with thrombolytic therapy.
- The core code-stroke checklist condenses to one piece of history (time last known normal), one piece of past medical history (anticoagulation status), one vital sign (blood pressure), and one lab (glucose) — a compact framework for rapidly triaging thrombolysis candidacy before more detailed workup is available.
- Embolic phenomena from a cardiac source can affect multiple vascular territories simultaneously (a "shower" of emboli) — in a patient with suspected endocarditis and new focal neurologic deficits, both cerebral (e.g., MCA territory) and ocular (e.g., retinal/ophthalmic artery) embolization should be considered together as part of a single unifying process, rather than evaluated as separate, coincidental findings.
- In evaluating acute bilateral lower extremity weakness, tempo and associated bowel/bladder findings are key localizers: hyperacute onset with bowel/bladder dysfunction strongly implicates a cord (or cauda equina/conus) process, and reflexes being acutely diminished (rather than increased) does not argue against an acute cord lesion — hyperreflexia in myelopathy typically develops only after some weeks, once spinal inhibitory interneurons are lost ("spinal shock" in the interim).
- A patient's risk factors matter for localizing a suspected cord process: in a patient with vascular access for dialysis (bacteremia risk) presenting with acute paralysis, an epidural abscess should be considered alongside more classically vascular or compressive causes, while a hyperacute (rather than gradually progressive) tempo argues against a slower-building infectious or degenerative process and favors a vascular mechanism instead.
- Gadolinium-based MRI contrast can and should be used when diagnostically necessary in patients with end-stage renal disease on dialysis, despite historical caution around nephrogenic systemic fibrosis, when the alternative is missing a time-sensitive compressive or inflammatory cord process.
- Spinal cord ischemia from a dialysis-related vascular steal phenomenon is a well-described but underrecognized entity, particularly in patients with atherosclerotic disease of the vessels supplying the cord (e.g., a diseased artery of Adamkiewicz in a patient with an aortic aneurysm), precipitated by recurrent intradialytic hypotension — MRI is frequently normal in acute spinal cord ischemia, since this modality has limited sensitivity for the diagnosis.
- Unlike most other causes of spinal cord ischemia, which are classically painful, the specific entity of dialysis-induced spinal cord ischemia from vascular steal has been described in the literature as uniformly painless — an important, counterintuitive exception worth knowing when evaluating acute painless paralysis in a dialysis patient.
- A simple, high-yield mental checklist for quickly triaging whether a patient is safe when a clinician feels overwhelmed or time-pressured is to confirm the patient can eat, walk, and talk — inability to do any one of the three should prompt immediate further evaluation, and this checklist explicitly includes physically observing the patient walk, not simply asking about it.
- A missed finding should be judged by the flaws in the diagnostic process that allowed it to be missed, not retrospectively excused by a fortunate outcome — even when the actual underlying cause proves to be something that could not have been treated differently, a different (potentially reversible) cause could easily have been present instead, and time-sensitive processes are harmed by any unnecessary delay in recognition regardless of how the specific case ultimately resolves.

## Problem Representation

Two patients presenting with fatigue within roughly thirty minutes of each other: a 73-year-old man with a bioprosthetic aortic valve and marked leukocytosis, found to have subacute endocarditis complicated by new atrial fibrillation, decompensated heart failure, and an embolic stroke (with thrombolysis appropriately withheld given endocarditis risk of hemorrhagic transformation); and a dialysis-dependent man with a normal initial workup who, once actually examined standing, was found to have acute bilateral lower extremity paralysis with bowel/bladder involvement, ultimately attributed to painless dialysis-induced spinal cord ischemia from vascular steal.

## Case Narrative

**Case 1.** Rabih presented a 73-year-old man with a recent (roughly six-week-old) inguinal hernia repair and a history of aortic stenosis status post bioprosthetic transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) in 2019, presenting with three weeks of progressive fatigue and malaise, without respiratory, abdominal, or leg symptoms.

Reza immediately applied the exertional-versus-non-exertional framework to the nonspecific symptom of fatigue: exertional worsening would prioritize the cardiopulmonary and hematologic systems, while non-exertional fatigue would broaden the differential toward depression and endocrine causes like hypothyroidism. Given the bioprosthetic valve, he emphasized the cardiovascular exam — both at rest and with exertion, assessing heart rate and oxygen saturation response to ambulation.

Rabih reported the patient was too fatigued to walk at baseline, making the exertional question difficult to assess directly. Vitals were normal; exam showed a tired-appearing man with a BMI of roughly 22, a mechanical valve click without other murmurs, and a slow but otherwise unremarkable gait when he did stand. Given his degree of fatigue, he was brought to a bed for workup. Results returned over the following hour: a new EKG finding of atrial fibrillation, a chest X-ray showing mild pulmonary edema, a BNP that had risen from the 300s to the 600s, a white count of 27,000, hemoglobin 11, normal platelets, and normal liver and kidney function.

Reza flagged the white count as the standout, unexpected finding, and reiterated a lower threshold for basic diagnostics (EKG, chest X-ray) in any patient with significant cardiac history — connecting this to an earlier RLR teaching point about obtaining an EKG even in ambiguous presentations like falls. He framed the new AFib as raising a chicken-or-egg question: was the arrhythmia driving the decompensation, or was it a consequence of an underlying process affecting the myocardium? The markedly elevated white count, he reasoned, argued for the latter — the AFib being secondary rather than causal. He described using a defined set of heart failure data points (weight trend, JVP, pulmonary/lower-extremity edema on exam, chest X-ray, BNP — including at discharge — and creatinine) rather than any single value in isolation, crediting this framework to a mentor ("Garpreet") from his UCSF training. The combination of mild pulmonary edema, new AFib, and elevated BNP was, in his view, sufficient to diagnose left-sided heart failure (elevated LV end-diastolic pressure) even without directly assessing JVP. The unexpected leukocytosis then reframed this as likely inflammatory decompensated heart failure, with a differential spanning sepsis, catecholamine/stress-induced cardiomyopathy, and thyrotoxicosis (a hypermetabolic rather than strictly inflammatory mechanism) — but with infective endocarditis, given the bioprosthetic valve, as the leading concern, potentially explaining the fatigue, edema, and new AFib (possibly reflecting a vegetation affecting the conduction system). He recommended blood cultures (citing a low threshold for any patient with a prosthesis or foreign vascular material), a transthoracic echocardiogram with explicit intent not to stop there if negative (favoring TEE and, if needed, PET-CT, having seen TEE-negative/PET-positive cases previously), and cautious avoidance of beta-blockade given the AFib might be a compensatory response.

Rabih confirmed he had independently reached a similar conclusion, sending three sets of blood cultures and starting empiric vancomycin and ceftriaxone. But he paused on the white count specifically: reflecting that in his own limited personal experience with endocarditis cases, he had never seen one present with a white count this high, he became concerned for a second, occult process — specifically a possible prosthetic or intra-abdominal abscess, given the patient's recent hernia repair — and sent a CT of the abdomen. Using teleradiology self-read skills he described as having improved substantially, he reviewed the scan himself while awaiting the formal read (which later confirmed no significant abdominal pathology).

While this was unfolding, a second patient with the same chief complaint — fatigue — arrived roughly thirty minutes later (detailed as Case 2, below). Rabih returned to Case 1's patient and found him now reporting he "can't see" on his left side, with his left arm dangling; full exam revealed complete left-sided arm and leg paralysis and left facial involvement.

Reza immediately connected this to an embolic process from a cardiac source, given the constellation of bioprosthetic valve, heart failure, and marked leukocytosis: a probable shower of emboli to the right cerebral hemisphere (explaining left-sided weakness, likely MCA territory) and, separately, to the retina or ophthalmic circulation (explaining the visual loss, while remaining open to an occipital cortical explanation as well). He recommended immediately activating a code stroke, obtaining a point-of-care glucose to exclude a hypoglycemic mimic, and expediting CT/CTA or MRI.

Rabih relayed the compact code-stroke checklist used by neurology: one piece of history (time last known normal), one piece of past medical history (anticoagulation status), one vital sign (blood pressure), and one lab (glucose) — all reassuring here (minutes since last known normal, no home anticoagulation, BP 130/75, normal glucose). CTA confirmed a distal MCA occlusion; the neurology team, hypothesizing a shower of emboli, prepared to administer tPA.

Reza flagged an important safety concern before this proceeded: given the strong suspicion for infective endocarditis, thrombolysis was relatively contraindicated — infected embolic material behaves differently from simple thrombus and does not respond meaningfully to thrombolytics, and septic emboli from endocarditis are specifically associated with mycotic microaneurysm formation (referencing a prior RLR case involving Bartonella), substantially raising the risk of intracranial hemorrhage with tPA. Rabih intercepted the stroke team just before tPA was to be administered, raised the endocarditis concern and the white count of 27,000, and the plan was halted.

**Case 2.** While Case 1 was still being worked up, Rabih saw a second patient: also presenting with fatigue, but for exactly three days, beginning immediately after a dialysis session. His history included end-stage renal disease, an aortic aneurysm under surveillance (not yet requiring intervention), and extensive coronary artery disease. He described an identical episode six to seven months prior. He reported feeling too unwell at rest to attempt exertion, so the exertional-fatigue question could not be meaningfully assessed. Vitals were normal; he was examined comfortably seated in a wheelchair, with normal heart, lung, abdominal, and leg findings on that exam. Given his fatigue, he too was moved to a bed for a similar workup: CBC, renal panel (as expected for a dialysis patient), liver panel, COVID test, and chest X-ray — all unremarkable.

With Case 1's endocarditis-stroke sequence successfully navigated, Rabih — by his own account, still riding the momentum of that catch — went to discharge Case 2's patient, telling him the workup was reassuring and he was safe to go home. The patient's daughter, present in the room, immediately corrected him: her father could not walk. Rabih realized he had examined the patient only while seated in the wheelchair and had never asked him to stand or walk — the same exertional-fatigue step Reza had emphasized for Case 1, and one Rabih himself endorsed as a standard practice, had been skipped here under the pressure of managing the parallel stroke code. A full neuro exam revealed complete bilateral lower extremity paralysis, with normal cranial nerves and normal arm strength.

Reza worked through the localization of acute bilateral lower extremity weakness: muscle-level processes (inflammatory myositis, rhabdomyolysis) rarely produce this degree of bilateral, symmetric, complete paralysis; peripheral neuropathy (e.g., Guillain-Barré) typically produces hyporeflexia and usually spares the bladder, sometimes with an ascending pattern; but the primary concern, especially with bowel or bladder involvement, was a cord-level (or cauda equina/conus) process. He recommended assessing for a sensory level and reflexes, and specifically noted that tempo mattered for the differential: hyperacute onset should prompt concern for cord compression or a vascular event until proven otherwise, while a more subacute course (better fitting a roughly three-day evolution) would raise consideration of an inflammatory process — including, given the patient's dialysis access, an epidural abscess seeded from bacteremia — or, if more chronic, degenerative spondylotic disease. He recommended an MRI with gadolinium contrast, explicitly noting his usual caution about gadolinium in renal impairment did not apply here given the diagnostic necessity in a dialysis-dependent patient with no native renal function to protect, framing the leading concerns as compressive myelopathy or an abscess, weighted by base rate.

Rabih supplied further history: the paralysis began acutely during dialysis itself — the patient walked in and could not walk out; he denied back pain; a bladder scan showed 450cc of retained urine with no urination in three days, and he had been unable to have a bowel movement in three days (unusual for him); his reflexes were diminished (hyporeflexic) on exam.

Reza responded that the combination of urinary retention and constipation strongly localized the process to the cord or the cord-nerve root transition zone, and specifically addressed the hyporeflexia: rather than arguing against an acute cord lesion, diminished reflexes are actually expected early in an acute myelopathic process ("spinal shock"), with hyperreflexia typically only developing after some weeks as spinal inhibitory interneurons are lost. He remained open to a cauda equina process (which can also present with hyporeflexia alongside bowel/bladder dysfunction) but felt the hyperacute tempo argued against a more slowly evolving process like an abscess, favoring instead a vascular mechanism — candidly acknowledging uncertainty about the specific pathophysiology of any dialysis-fistula-related vascular steal syndrome affecting the cord, but recommending evaluation of the vasculature supplying the spinal cord as the leading hypothesis.

Neurology was consulted and obtained an MRI, which was normal — a finding Rabih noted actually reinforced rather than refuted the ischemia hypothesis, since spinal cord ischemia is notoriously difficult to visualize on MRI. The patient was admitted with a working diagnosis of dialysis-induced spinal cord ischemia from a vascular steal phenomenon: given his atherosclerotic disease (including his aortic aneurysm), the artery of Adamkiewicz supplying his cord was presumed diseased and vulnerable, with recurrent intradialytic hypotension episodes precipitating ischemia. Rabih referenced a neurology review article on this entity, highlighting a striking and counterintuitive teaching point: while spinal cord ischemia is classically painful, every reported case of dialysis-steal-induced spinal cord ischemia in that review was painless.

As of recording, Case 1's patient remained on empiric endocarditis treatment with cultures still negative (raising the possibility of culture-negative endocarditis), a negative TTE, and a planned TEE. Case 2's patient had not yet recovered lower extremity function, with a repeat MRI planned later (given improved diffusion-weighted imaging yield with time) to look for cord infarct signal.

Rabih closed with an unprompted, detailed self-reflection: for Case 1, he had followed the exertional-fatigue framework as intended; for Case 2, overwhelmed by the simultaneous stroke code, he skipped that same fundamental step — despite knowing better — and was saved only by the daughter's advocacy. He explicitly declined to let the (unpreventable) underlying vascular etiology excuse the flawed process, noting that a different, potentially reversible cause (such as a compressive lesion) could easily have been present instead, and that any delay in recognition matters given how time-sensitive cord-compressing processes can be. Reza validated this with his own admission that he could easily see himself making the same omission under similar pressure, reinforcing the broader lesson (echoing RLR's prior teaching on falls and EKGs) that certain basic steps — walking a fatigued patient chief among them — should never be skipped regardless of how busy or confident a clinician feels. Rabih offered a simplified mental checklist for exactly these high-pressure moments: confirm the patient can eat, walk, and talk — physically observed, not merely reported — as a fast, high-yield safety screen.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Subacute prosthetic-valve endocarditis illness script: fatigue, new arrhythmia, and heart failure in a patient with a prosthetic valve — especially with an unexpectedly high white count — should trigger blood cultures and echocardiography with explicit willingness to escalate beyond a negative TTE (to TEE and PET-CT), and should specifically inform downstream management decisions (such as caution with thrombolysis) if an embolic stroke subsequently develops.

Dialysis-induced spinal cord ischemia illness script: acute, painless bilateral lower extremity paralysis with bowel/bladder involvement, occurring in temporal relationship to dialysis (particularly with hypotension) in a patient with atherosclerotic vascular disease, should raise suspicion for spinal cord ischemia from vascular steal even when MRI is normal, given the modality's poor sensitivity for this diagnosis.

The case's central bias lesson is procedural rather than purely diagnostic: a fundamental, previously internalized step (walking a fatigued patient) was skipped not from ignorance but from cognitive overload during a competing emergency — a reminder that even well-established clinical habits are vulnerable to being dropped under pressure, and that external advocates (here, a patient's daughter) can be the actual mechanism that catches such gaps. Rabih's explicit refusal to let a benign outcome retroactively excuse a flawed process models outcome-independent evaluation of clinical reasoning quality.

## Teaching Discussion

The central teaching point of Case 1 is the disciplined pursuit of infective endocarditis in a patient with a prosthetic valve and an unexpectedly high leukocytosis — including the willingness to escalate imaging beyond a negative TTE, and the specific, high-stakes management implication that a strong endocarditis suspicion should pause thrombolysis in a subsequent embolic stroke given infected-embolus non-responsiveness to tPA and mycotic-aneurysm-related hemorrhage risk.

The central teaching point of Case 2 is the counterintuitive, easily missed diagnosis of painless dialysis-induced spinal cord ischemia from vascular steal — and the specific reassurance that a normal MRI does not exclude this diagnosis, given the test's known limited sensitivity for spinal cord ischemia.

The overarching, connecting teaching point across both cases is procedural: the exertional-fatigue framework (specifically, physically walking a fatigued patient) is only useful if actually applied, and cognitive overload from a competing emergency is a real and dangerous threat to consistently applying even well-known, previously internalized clinical habits — a lesson reinforced by the fact that the miss was caught not through self-correction but through a family member's advocacy.
