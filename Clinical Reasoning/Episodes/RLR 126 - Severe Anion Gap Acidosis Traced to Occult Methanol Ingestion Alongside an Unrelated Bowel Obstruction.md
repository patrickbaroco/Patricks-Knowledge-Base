## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> With Robbie out sick, guest discussant Brandon (a PA in critical care and RLR's "favorite intensivist," despite his own correction that he isn't one) presents a 50-year-old man with alcoholic cirrhosis and a prior TIPS who arrives with two days of vague GI distress and new dyspnea, found to have a devastating triple acid-base disorder: severe anion gap metabolic acidosis (bicarb 6, gap 46), a concomitant metabolic alkalosis unmasked only by delta-delta calculation, and respiratory acidosis from fatigue (pH 6.9, PCO2 28). Reza works the case live, building an anion-gap framework around four buckets, applying Winter's formula, and using the "law of proportionality" to argue the lactate (27) is too severe to be explained by a normotensive patient's perfusion alone. A CT scan reveals a small bowel obstruction from prior hernia-repair adhesions — but a toxic alcohol panel, sent almost as an afterthought, comes back positive for methanol, unmasked only because the osmolar gap had already closed by the time of presentation. The episode becomes a study in resisting premature closure once one plausible diagnosis (the bowel obstruction) is found, and in reasoning about how two seemingly unrelated diagnoses in one patient are usually connected rather than coincidental.

## Pearls

- The only acid-base disorder diagnosable from a chem panel alone (without an ABG/VBG) is an anion gap acidosis — the body never compensates for an alkalosis by generating an anion gap.
- Reframe the anion gap acidosis differential into four buckets instead of memorizing MUDPILES: lactate, ketones, renal insufficiency (unmeasured anions — phosphates/sulfates), and toxins (ethylene glycol, isopropyl alcohol, acetaminophen, methanol).
- Multiple drivers of an anion gap acidosis can and often do coexist — the question isn't "is it X or Y," it's whether each candidate (lactate, ketones, renal failure) is proportionally sufficient to explain the entire gap.
- Toxic-alcohol pearl: as ingested alcohol is metabolized, the osmolar gap falls while the anion gap rises — a patient presenting late after ingestion may have a normal osmolar gap despite ongoing toxic-metabolite-driven anion gap acidosis.
- Isopropyl alcohol is the one common toxic alcohol that does NOT cause an anion gap acidosis (it's metabolized to acetone/a ketone, raising the osmolar gap without a corresponding anion gap).
- Winter's formula (expected PCO2 = 1.5 × bicarb + 8 ± 2) predicts appropriate respiratory compensation for a metabolic acidosis; a measured PCO2 above that range signals a superimposed respiratory acidosis — the patient is tiring out, not just failing to compensate.
- Lactic acidosis splits into type A (impaired oxygen delivery/tissue hypoperfusion — note a patient can be normotensive and still have inadequate tissue perfusion) and type B (adequate perfusion but impaired cellular oxygen utilization, from toxins like metformin, alcohols, propofol, albuterol, caffeine, cocaine, or thiamine deficiency, or genetic mitochondrial disease).
- "Law of proportionality": before accepting a lactate as explained by hypoperfusion, ask whether the hemodynamic picture (here, normotensive with a good blood pressure) is severe enough to produce a lactate this high (27) — a large disconnect should prompt a search for a type B cause instead.
- In decompensated/critically ill cirrhotics, checking an ammonia level has essentially no diagnostic utility — the level doesn't correlate with the clinical picture of encephalopathy, positively or negatively, and shouldn't be used to anchor management (e.g., empirically treating with lactulose/rifaximin based on the number alone).
- TIPS procedures are relatively contraindicated in patients prone to hepatic encephalopathy, since bypassing the liver removes its capacity to convert ammonia to urea — but this doesn't mean the ammonia level itself should drive diagnostic reasoning in a TIPS patient with altered mental status.
- Clinical appearance should override an alarming lab value when they conflict: this patient's pH of 6.9 would traditionally prompt intubation, but his comfortable, non-labored breathing argued for deferring intubation, since patients often ventilate more effectively on their own than on a ventilator when they can sustain a rapid respiratory rate.
- Large-volume paracentesis can be a respiratory intervention, not just a comfort measure — decompressing ascites gives the diaphragm more room to move and can meaningfully improve a tachypneic patient's mechanics.
- Delta-delta gap calculation: subtract the (albumin-corrected) expected normal anion gap from the measured gap, then add that difference back to the measured bicarbonate; if the result is well above a normal bicarbonate, it implies an underlying metabolic alkalosis was present before the anion gap acidosis was superimposed on it.
- A bowel obstruction with significant vomiting classically produces a chloride-responsive metabolic alkalosis via volume depletion and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone activation and hydrogen ion excretion — not a primary acidosis; this is a useful check on whether a bowel obstruction alone can explain a severe acidemic picture.
- A serum toxic-alcohol assay with essentially no false positives (per poison control) should be treated as real and acted on even when barely above threshold and even without corroborating osmolar gap elevation, especially with a plausible delayed-presentation explanation.
- Methanol toxicity classically causes visual symptoms/CNS toxicity via its toxic metabolite (formate) and carries a risk of spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage; fomepizole blocks further alcohol dehydrogenase-mediated conversion to toxic metabolites, but dialysis is the primary way to clear metabolites already formed once conversion is well underway.
- Concurrent ethanol ingestion can blunt/delay methanol toxicity by competitively inhibiting alcohol dehydrogenase, potentially producing an atypically indolent or delayed clinical picture rather than the classically dramatic methanol presentation.
- (Bereg et al., JGIM) When a patient has two seemingly unrelated diagnoses, the relationship usually falls into one of four categories: an incidental, irrelevant finding; a known chronic comorbidity that's simply part of the clinical background; a truly independent but contributing diagnosis (coincidence); or a genuine unifying connection between the two — and a unifying connection is statistically the most common outcome.
- Personal ICU heuristic (Brandon, offered as a corollary to Occam's razor): when a patient has two seemingly coincidental diagnoses, one is often truly acute while the other is subacute or chronic — a predisposition or vulnerability that gets triggered, unmasked, or exacerbated by the acute event.
- Don't let one confirmed diagnosis close the differential prematurely — testing broadly even after finding a plausible explanation (here, the bowel obstruction) can look "unintelligent" or redundant in the moment but is what catches the second, more dangerous diagnosis.

## Problem Representation

A 50-year-old man with alcoholic cirrhosis and a prior TIPS presents with two days of nausea, vomiting, and abdominal discomfort progressing to dyspnea and confusion, found to have a profound anion gap metabolic acidosis with severe lactic acidosis (pH 6.9, bicarb 6, anion gap 46, lactate 27) out of proportion to a normotensive hemodynamic picture, ultimately found to have two coexisting diagnoses: a small bowel obstruction from prior hernia-repair adhesions, and an occult methanol ingestion revealed by a delayed-presentation toxic alcohol panel with a normal osmolar gap.

## Case Narrative

Brandon, a PA in critical care and a recurring RLR guest discussant, stepped in for Robbie (out with the flu) to present a case from a 2 a.m. ICU shift. A 50-year-old man with a history of alcoholic cirrhosis — previously severe enough to require a TIPS procedure — presented with ongoing ascites and known varices but was otherwise generally functional. He reported two days of abdominal discomfort, nausea, and several episodes of bland, non-bloody vomiting without diarrhea, which had progressed to a subjective sense of shortness of breath that finally brought him to the emergency department. He stated he had remained sober since his TIPS, though he admitted to drinking a single shot of vodka the night before presentation, reasoning at the time that it would help settle what he thought was food poisoning.

On exam he was alert but clearly confused, tachypneic (respiratory rate around 25) but not visibly laboring, and normotensive to mildly hypertensive with a sinus tachycardia in the 130s. Initial chemistries revealed a serum bicarbonate of 6 with an anion gap of 46; a venous blood gas showed a pH of 6.9 and a PCO2 of 28.

Reza walked through the case live. He framed the tachypnea with a normal oxygen saturation as a compensatory attempt to blow off acid and maintain pH, since cellular metabolism depends on a pH near 7.4 — the same physiology, he noted, that drives the abdominal pain and rapid presentation seen in DKA. He emphasized that interpreting an acid-base disorder requires both a chemistry panel and a blood gas, and that an anion gap acidosis is the only disorder that can be diagnosed from the chemistry panel alone, since the body never compensates for an alkalosis by generating an anion gap. Rather than reciting MUDPILES, he organized the differential into four buckets: lactate, ketones, renal insufficiency (unmeasured anions like phosphates and sulfates), and toxins (ethylene glycol, isopropyl alcohol, and even acetaminophen). He stressed that these drivers aren't mutually exclusive and that the task is to determine whether each candidate is proportionally sufficient to explain the entire gap, then walked through Winter's formula (1.5 × bicarb + 8, or roughly 16–18 here) to show the measured PCO2 of 28 was higher than expected compensation — evidence the patient was tiring out and developing a superimposed respiratory acidosis, edging toward needing intubation. He also flagged a nephrology pearl: as an ingested toxic alcohol is metabolized, the osmolar gap falls while the anion gap rises, so measuring both the calculated osmolality and the actual serum osmolality would be important to catch a toxic ingestion.

Brandon returned with labs drawn about four hours after initial presentation: lactate 27, beta-hydroxybutyrate 2.1, creatinine 2.3, AST 190, bilirubin 4.9, ammonia 133, and a normal glucose. He noted that clinically the patient looked far better than the numbers suggested — not the classic Kussmaul-breathing DKA picture — creating a disconnect that argued against intubation, since patients often ventilate themselves more effectively than a machine can when they're able to sustain a rapid rate.

Reza used the "law of proportionality" to work through each lactate driver: the modest beta-hydroxybutyrate reflected starvation ketosis from poor oral intake, not enough on its own to explain the acidosis. The lactate, split into type A (hypoperfusion — possible even in a normotensive patient, since normal blood pressure doesn't guarantee adequate tissue oxygen delivery) versus type B (impaired aerobic metabolism despite adequate perfusion, from toxins like metformin, alcohol, propofol, or a genetic defect), was judged too severe for a normotensive patient to be purely type A. On the ammonia of 133, Reza taught that despite TIeliever being a relative contraindication in hepatic encephalopathy risk (because it bypasses the liver's ability to convert ammonia to urea), the ammonia level itself has no proven correlation with the clinical picture in cirrhotics and shouldn't be used to anchor toward hyperammonemic encephalopathy as the cause of the altered mental status — a point Brandon strongly endorsed as standard hepatology teaching. Initial management: fluids, thiamine (given empirically without waiting for a level), phosphorus monitoring, and glucose to suppress ketogenesis by stimulating endogenous insulin release, isolating the lactate as the remaining unexplained problem.

Brandon then revealed the osmolar gap, checked a few hours in, was only about 10 — not elevated. Acetaminophen and salicylate levels were negative. Serum ethanol was positive at 25 (normal under 10), consistent with the reported vodka shot. An old EMR note from ten years prior showed the same patient had once tested positive for propylene glycol and acetone on a toxic alcohol panel — an ambiguous historical red herring with no available context. Bedside echo showed a hyperdynamic heart with no dysfunction, and ample ascites was confirmed.

Reza reasoned through the unrevealing osmolar gap: isopropyl alcohol is the one toxic alcohol that does not cause an anion gap acidosis, but that didn't fit this picture of a large gap with a normal osmolar gap. He hypothesized the ingestion likely occurred days before presentation, meaning the osmolar gap contribution had already resolved into the anion gap by the time of testing. He remained skeptical the lactate represented pure type A physiology given the good blood pressure, favoring a type B process from an as-yet-unidentified toxin, and recommended bicarbonate plus dialysis to both correct the acid-base status and clear a potential toxin.

Brandon then described the management pivot: with no in-house toxicologist, he consulted poison control for diagnostic input. He performed a paracentesis, removing four liters of ascites (with no evidence of peritonitis) to give the diaphragm more room to work. A CT scan revealed a near-complete small bowel obstruction in the right lower quadrant. On exam, the surgical team noticed a prior umbilical hernia repair with mesh, suggesting adhesions as the likely cause. Given the patient's cirrhosis and severe physiologic derangement, and the absence of high-risk features like a closed loop, surgery elected medical management: an NG tube to suction, which drained significant fluid and gas.

Just as the team felt they were reaching diagnostic closure, the toxic alcohol panel returned positive for serum methanol at 11 (undetectable is normal; poison control considers values over 10 clinically significant — this barely qualified).

Reza revisited the acid-base picture and corrected an earlier assumption. Recalculating the delta-delta — subtracting an albumin-corrected expected normal anion gap (roughly 6, given this patient's low albumin from cirrhosis) from the measured gap of 46 yields about 40; adding that 40 back to the actual bicarbonate of 6 predicts a baseline bicarbonate of about 46 in the absence of the anion gap process. Since a bicarbonate that high implies an underlying metabolic alkalosis rather than a simple normal picture, Reza corrected his earlier read: this was not just an anion gap acidosis plus respiratory acidosis, but a third process — a concurrent metabolic alkalosis, well explained by vomiting-driven volume depletion and RAAS activation from the bowel obstruction. He connected the bowel obstruction to the patient's prior surgery, citing the prior RLR episode's teaching that small bowel obstructions are overwhelmingly caused by adhesions or hernias rather than malignancy — but noted this did nothing to explain the profound lactic acidosis.

Brandon went back to the bedside for history: the patient, still somewhat confused, denied any ingestion beyond the store-bought vodka, but his family reported he had recently been complaining of visual difficulties — a detail consistent with methanol's classic CNS/visual toxicity. Poison control reiterated that this assay essentially has no false positives and should be treated as real regardless of magnitude, and that a late presentation with a resolved osmolar gap but persistent toxic metabolites (formate/formic acid, not directly measurable) was a plausible and expected pattern. They recommended fomepizole to block further metabolism (acknowledging most of the parent alcohol was likely already converted), a CT head to rule out the methanol-associated risk of spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage, and dialysis as the primary means of clearing the toxic metabolites, since no other clearance mechanism exists.

As the patient became more confused and tachypneic, he was intubated, a dialysis catheter was placed, and he was dialyzed. His lactate cleared briskly, and he was extubated within one to two days. The bowel obstruction resolved with conservative management alone, and his LFTs and AKI resolved. A serum PEth level returned markedly elevated (300–400), supporting heavy recent alcohol use by the team's rubric (roughly four or more drinks daily on multiple days per week) despite the patient's own account.

The discussion closed on how to connect the two seemingly unrelated diagnoses. Brandon cited a JGIM paper (Bereg et al.) reviewing how multiple diagnoses in one patient typically relate: as an irrelevant incidentaloma, a known chronic comorbidity, an independent-but-contributing coincidence, or — most commonly, per their findings — a genuine unifying connection. Brandon offered his own ICU corollary to Occam's razor: when two diagnoses appear coincidental, one is often truly acute while the other is subacute or chronic, representing a predisposition that gets triggered or unmasked by the acute event. He speculated the methanol ingestion was the more subacute, lower-grade process — possibly blunted by concurrent ethanol competitively inhibiting its metabolism — while the bowel obstruction was the truly acute precipitant that, through dehydration and cirrhotic impairment of lactate clearance, tipped the picture into crisis. Reza pushed back gently, using the "liver clears lactate" mnemonic and the absence of any surgical evidence of bowel ischemia (the surgeons never rushed to the OR) to argue the bowel obstruction alone, even accounting for cirrhosis, could not explain a lactate of 27 — reinforcing that the methanol was doing most of that work. Neither host could identify a known physiologic mechanism directly linking methanol toxicity to bowel obstruction in the literature, and the episode closed acknowledging the connection between the two diagnoses remains genuinely unresolved.

### Bayesian Updates

|---|---|

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Anchoring risk avoided**: after the bowel obstruction was found and seemed to explain the clinical picture, both discussants resisted treating it as a complete explanation, since it did not account for the severity or type of acidosis present — a deliberate check against premature closure once "a" diagnosis is found.
- **Law of proportionality** as an anti-anchoring tool: repeatedly asking whether a given abnormality (lactate, ketones, hemodynamics) is quantitatively sufficient to explain the full picture, rather than accepting a qualitatively plausible explanation.
- **Occam's razor vs. Hickam's dictum**, operationalized via the Bereg et al. taxonomy (incidentaloma / known comorbidity / independent-but-contributing / unifying connection) rather than treated as a binary choice.
- **Acute-plus-chronic pairing heuristic**: Brandon's personal framework that of two seemingly coincidental diagnoses, one is often the acute trigger and the other a subacute/chronic vulnerability it unmasks — a structured way to avoid dismissing either diagnosis as pure coincidence.
- **Testing broadly even after finding one explanation** was explicitly framed as looking "unintelligent" in the moment (why keep testing when you already found the obstruction?) but was what ultimately caught the second, more dangerous diagnosis — a direct argument against reflexively narrowing workups once a first plausible answer appears.
- **Ammonia-anchoring pitfall named explicitly**: checking ammonia in cirrhotics is common practice but has essentially no diagnostic yield and risks anchoring clinicians toward hepatic encephalopathy as a default explanation for AMS.

## Teaching Discussion

The episode is structured as two interlocking teaching threads. The first is a rigorous, stepwise acid-base workup — anion gap bucket differential, Winter's formula, osmolar gap physiology, and (crucially, as a late correction) the delta-delta calculation revealing a third, initially hidden metabolic alkalosis. The second is a higher-order discussion of diagnostic reasoning once two real diagnoses are confirmed in the same patient: how to avoid stopping at the first plausible answer, and how to think structurally (via the Bereg et al. taxonomy and Brandon's acute-plus-chronic heuristic) about whether multiple diagnoses are coincidental, background, or causally connected — while being honest that, in this case, no definitive unifying mechanism could be identified.
