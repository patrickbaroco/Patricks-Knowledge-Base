## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Robbie presents two of his own ED cases from a 48-hour stretch, both built around the same unusual pairing: severe limb pain together with true weakness. A 34-year-old woman's dropped-iPhone story confirms real weakness (not pain-limited effort), and a sensory deficit alongside triceps, wrist extensor, and finger extensor weakness localizes the lesion proximal to the elbow — ultimately a compressive brachial plexopathy from being trapped in a window for 25 minutes, not the simple radial mononeuropathy it first resembled. The key discriminator Robbie almost missed: compressive mononeuropathies don't typically hurt this much, but plexopathies do. The very next day, a 75-year-old man with two months of worsening "sciatica" turns out to have a lumbosacral plexopathy from severe, undiagnosed diabetes (A1c ~11) — diabetic amyotrophy — with dramatic pain relief on venlafaxine despite persistent weakness. Both cases converge on the same teaching point: severe pain paired with true weakness is an unusual and highly localizing combination that should immediately raise plexopathy on the differential.

## Pearls

- Distinguishing true weakness from pain-limited effort (asthenia) is a critical first step — a patient who can't grip an object well enough that it falls from an otherwise painless hand demonstrates genuine weakness, unlike a patient whose apparent weakness is really just pain preventing full effort (e.g., someone with bad osteoarthritis who "can't" stand due to pain, not muscle weakness).
- When weakness, sensory loss, and pain all localize to the same single limb, the sensory findings do a lot of localizing work: a pure muscle disease (dermatomyositis, polymyositis) doesn't cause sensory symptoms, so their presence shifts suspicion toward the peripheral nerve, plexus, or nerve root rather than the muscle or neuromuscular junction.
- Use parallel neuroanatomic reasoning across limbs: the same localization logic used for a lower-extremity foot drop (distinguishing femoral, sciatic, peroneal, and tibial nerve lesions) applies to the upper extremity — map which specific muscles are weak to determine how proximal or distal along the brachial plexus and its branches the lesion must be.
- Weakness in muscles supplied by multiple different peripheral nerves (e.g., radial-, ulnar-, and median-innervated muscles together) localizes proximal to where those nerves diverge — to the brachial plexus itself, not to any single peripheral nerve.
- Most isolated compressive mononeuropathies are painless or only mildly uncomfortable — carpal tunnel syndrome, tarsal tunnel syndrome, and peroneal nerve compression are classic examples. A patient in severe, excruciating limb pain should prompt reconsideration of a simple mononeuropathy diagnosis.
- A useful mnemonic: "plex = painful." Plexopathies are characteristically excruciatingly painful, unlike most compressive mononeuropathies distal to the plexus.
- Cervical or lumbosacral radiculopathy is the single most common cause of severe one-limb pain, but there's a specific natural-history pearl: as a compressive radiculopathy progresses to the point of causing weakness, the pain typically resolves and is replaced by numbness — loss of pain with new numbness is itself an alarm sign of advanced nerve root compression. In plexopathy, by contrast, severe pain and weakness occur simultaneously rather than pain fading as weakness appears.
- A fast clinical rule of thumb: one limb with a lot of pain plus true weakness should make you think plexus; classic radiculopathy causes significant pain but is much less commonly associated with prominent weakness (and if weakness does develop, the pain usually fades by then).
- Prolonged positional nerve/plexus compression (a limb trapped or pinned in an awkward position for an extended period, analogous to a "Saturday night palsy") is a recognized, usually self-limited cause of an acute compressive plexopathy — the history may be absent or minimized if the patient was intoxicated or otherwise unable to clearly recall the mechanism.
- Building a broader differential for "weak AND in severe pain": diabetic myonecrosis, necrotizing myopathies, ischemic neuropathies (including dialysis-steal-associated ischemic neuropathy), ischemia to the spinal cord itself, and plexopathies — as a general rule, ischemia to any part of the peripheral or central nervous system tends to hurt, and so do plexopathies.
- Classic sciatica has a specific distribution (posterior thigh, buttock, radiating toward the foot) reflecting L5-S1 nerve root impingement — pain confined to an isolated region like the anterior/lateral thigh, without that radicular distribution, and especially pain that steadily worsens over months despite treatment, should prompt reconsidering the diagnosis rather than assuming refractory sciatica.
- Diabetes doesn't only cause a symmetric, length-dependent (distal, "stocking-glove") polyneuropathy — it can also present as an asymmetric plexopathy, historically called diabetic amyotrophy (now more often termed diabetic lumbosacral radiculoplexus neuropathy), typically in patients with poorly controlled or newly severe diabetes.
- When a plexopathy has no compressive or traumatic explanation, the differential broadens to non-compressive causes: vasculitis, infiltrative or granulomatous disease (including sarcoidosis), and malignant infiltration of the plexus — but in an older patient with severe uncontrolled diabetes, diabetic plexopathy is by far the more likely diagnosis, and imaging (MRI of the plexus, not just the spine) is reasonable to exclude a mass or infiltrative process, especially given institutional recall bias from prior cases of malignant plexus infiltration.
- Neuropathic pain in a plexopathy may respond dramatically to agents like gabapentin or venlafaxine even when the underlying weakness persists — pain control and functional/motor recovery can follow very different timelines.

## Problem Representation

Two sequential cases seen by the same emergency physician within 48 hours, each combining severe, localized limb pain with true focal weakness: a 34-year-old woman with an acute compressive brachial plexopathy from prolonged positional entrapment, and a 75-year-old man with a subacute, progressively worsening lumbosacral plexopathy from previously undiagnosed severe diabetes (diabetic amyotrophy) — both cases converging on the teaching principle that severe pain combined with true weakness in a single limb should prioritize plexopathy over simple mononeuropathy or radiculopathy.

## Case Narrative

Robbie set up the episode as a two-part guessing game: he'd tell each story with one deliberately omitted detail — first a piece of the history of present illness, then a piece of past medical history — and Reza's job was to infer the missing piece from the reasoning alone.

The first patient was a 34-year-old woman seen on a Friday with severe pain, numbness, and weakness in her right upper extremity, otherwise well, with normal vital signs. Her own words: her phone had fallen out of her hand while she was trying to pick it up.

Reza immediately flagged the diagnostic value of that detail: of the three symptoms (pain, weakness, sensory loss), weakness is usually the most concerning, but it's also the hardest to assess reliably in a patient who's also in pain, since pain itself can limit effort and mimic weakness — the way a patient with bad osteoarthritis might seem unable to stand not because of true muscle weakness but because pain prevents them from trying. The dropped phone, though, argued against a pain-limited effort problem; Reza said he'd specifically ask whether the phone fell because of discomfort or because she simply couldn't hold onto it. Robbie confirmed: she wasn't in pain when it happened, she just couldn't grip it — true weakness, not asthenia.

With true weakness established, Reza reframed the case as a localization exercise along the neuromotor axis — cortex, spinal cord, nerve root/plexus, peripheral nerve, neuromuscular junction, muscle. He noted that weakness usually shows up without pain or sensory symptoms, but here the sensory findings did significant localizing work: a pure muscle process like dermatomyositis or polymyositis wouldn't cause sensory symptoms, so their presence shifted his suspicion toward the sensory-motor nerve rather than the muscle, junction, or (given the unilateral, non-symmetric pattern) the spinal cord. He focused on where the nerve exits the spinal cord — nerve root or brachial plexus — as the most likely lesion site, while flagging that if additional, non-contiguous nerve territories developed weakness over the following hours or days, that would open up a different differential (mononeuritis multiplex).

Robbie then shared his own reflexive instinct in the moment: as an ED physician, his default response to a patient in this much pain would normally be reflexive ketorolac (Toradol) — but before doing the exam, he paused to reflect on how many patients with weakness he'd given Toradol to in his career, a thought he wanted to return to. He then asked Reza how the presence of pain, specifically localized to the same extremity (not a headache or neck pain), changed his calculus.

Reza distinguished nociceptive pain (from pain receptors specifically) from the broader category of sensory symptoms (light touch, temperature, proprioception), and suggested the character of the pain mattered: if it sounded like classic neuropathic pain (pins and needles, tingling), that would support a straightforward sensory-motor nerve process; if the pain sounded different — more mechanical or deep — that would raise concern for involvement beyond the nerve alone (muscle, joint, or vessel).

The exam: a completely normal neuro exam and general affect aside from the right upper extremity, where there was no muscle atrophy, no skin breakdown, normal vasculature, and no bony or joint involvement — but weak triceps, weak wrist extensors, weak finger extensors, and sensory loss in that same distribution, without a true wrist drop (weak extension, not absent).

Robbie asked Reza to localize using the same logic applied to a lower-extremity foot drop (femoral, sciatic, peroneal, tibial nerve distinctions): mapping which specific muscles were weak (triceps, wrist extensors, finger extensors) meant the lesion had to be proximal — likely at or near the brachial plexus, since a lesion distal to the triceps couldn't explain triceps weakness. With no systemic features and no muscle atrophy, Reza's leading hypothesis was a compressive mononeuropathy — he estimated roughly 90% of presentations like this are due to simple nerve compression or trauma, drawing the analogy to a limb "falling asleep" after sitting cross-legged or on a toilet seat too long, and specifically wondered about compression near the axilla, where the brachial plexus is particularly vulnerable (as seen with crutch-related injuries).

The withheld history detail: the patient had been trapped trying to climb out of a bathroom window when the door wouldn't open, with her arm jammed between the windowsill and the closing window for about 25 minutes.

Robbie's initial read — triceps, wrist extension, and finger extension weakness — pointed to a compressive radial mononeuropathy. But he then returned to the reflection he'd flagged earlier: across many prior cases of compressive mononeuropathy causing foot drop, he had never once given a patient ketorolac for the pain, because those presentations simply aren't that painful. This patient's severe pain didn't fit that pattern, and he asked Reza how to think about compressive mononeuropathy paired with severe pain.

Reza, wishing aloud for a specialist's input, said his instinct was not to question the compressive-nerve diagnosis itself, but rather to focus on management — favoring a neuropathic pain agent like gabapentin over NSAIDs (which he considered generally unhelpful for neuropathic pain) and expecting spontaneous resolution over time.

Robbie pushed back: the diagnosis should be questioned. He revealed that when he'd curiously consulted neurology (mostly out of interest rather than necessity), the exam found weakness not just in the radial nerve distribution but also in the ulnar and median nerve distributions. Reza, working through the anatomy, recognized that all three of these nerves originate from the brachial plexus, and that an injury near the plexus itself could selectively affect some branches more than others — though he admitted he still wasn't fully questioning the working diagnosis. Robbie clarified the real distinction at stake: not whether a nerve was compressed, but whether it was a single nerve or the whole plexus that had been compressed. With that framing, Reza immediately recognized it as a plexopathy, applying the same localization logic as before — findings in multiple nerve distributions push the lesion proximal to where those nerves diverge, to the plexus itself.

Robbie then delivered the pearl that had actually solved the case for him after the fact: plexopathies are excruciatingly painful, unlike the peripheral compressive mononeuropathies (carpal tunnel, tarsal tunnel, peroneal compression) that typically aren't. He paired this with a second pearl about radiculopathy, the most common cause of single-limb pain: by the time a compressive cervical (or lumbosacral) radiculopathy progresses to cause true weakness, it has usually already knocked out the sensory nerve as well, and the patient's pain resolves, replaced by numbness — loss of pain with new numbness is itself a red flag for advanced nerve root compression. In radiculopathy, pain and weakness tend to occur sequentially (pain fading as weakness sets in); in plexopathy, they occur simultaneously. His practical rule of thumb for non-specialists: one limb with a lot of pain plus true weakness should prioritize plexus; classic radiculopathy causes real pain but is usually not associated with prominent weakness.

Management for this patient was conservative — wrist splinting, with resolution over a couple of weeks, consistent with a self-limited compressive plexopathy from prolonged positional entrapment (analogous to a "Saturday night palsy," where history may be unreliable if the patient was intoxicated at the time).

Robbie then presented the second case, seen the very next day: a 75-year-old man arriving in a wheelchair, diaphoretic, tachycardic, and hypertensive, reporting excruciating left thigh pain that had progressively worsened over two months despite an outside diagnosis of "sciatica" and its associated treatment. Exam showed severe proximal left thigh weakness with a fully intact distal left leg, and patchy sensory loss confined to the thigh.

Reza applied the pearls from the first case directly: the diaphoresis, tachycardia, and hypertension he attributed to a pain-driven sympathetic surge, setting those aside as secondary findings. The focal, severe pain plus true weakness again prioritized a nerve-based process for him over a muscle disease (noting that severe muscle diseases like myonecrosis can also cause significant pain and weakness, but the patchy sensory loss made a pure muscle process less likely). He questioned the outside "sciatica" diagnosis directly: classic sciatica reflects L5-S1 nerve root impingement with a specific distribution — pain down the back of the thigh, buttock, and into the foot — not isolated, non-radiating thigh pain, and the fact that this pain had only worsened despite treatment further undermined the original diagnosis. He also considered meralgia paresthetica (lateral femoral cutaneous nerve entrapment, given the patchy lateral thigh sensory pattern) but correctly noted it wouldn't explain the patient's weakness or deep discomfort.

Neurology, consulted after the patient received two doses of intravenous hydromorphone, confidently diagnosed a lumbosacral plexopathy and ordered MRI specifically of the lumbosacral plexus (not just the lumbar spine) to exclude another cause, given no history of trauma or falls.

Robbie then asked Reza to guess the patient's relevant past medical history. Reza reasoned through the anatomy of the plexus (multiple nerve roots converging, as opposed to a single nerve root in radiculopathy) and argued that pathology affecting multiple nerve roots simultaneously (rather than one) shifts the differential toward non-compressive causes — but cautioned against reflexively jumping to the more exotic-sounding options (vasculitis, infiltrative or granulomatous disease, sarcoidosis, malignancy). The much more common explanation, he said, is diabetes: diabetes doesn't only cause the classic symmetric, distal polyneuropathy, but can also present as a plexopathy. He noted that in a patient without diabetes, this presentation would become considerably more concerning for an infiltrative or inflammatory process.

Robbie confirmed: A1c was 10.8–11.2%, reflecting severe, previously uncontrolled diabetes. MRI of the lumbar spine and lumbosacral plexus was normal — reassuring against the specific concern the neurology team had carried into the case from recent recall bias (a recent case at their institution involved prostate cancer infiltrating the lumbosacral plexus). The diagnosis was diabetic lumbosacral plexopathy — historically called diabetic amyotrophy — and the patient was admitted to the neurology service specifically for pain control. Per the discharge summary, he had near-complete resolution of pain on venlafaxine, going from requiring repeated IV hydromorphone to being fully controlled off oral narcotics, though his weakness persisted.

Robbie closed by synthesizing both cases into a working schema for "weak and in severe pain": diabetic myonecrosis, necrotizing myopathies, ischemic neuropathies (including dialysis-steal-related ischemic neuropathy, referencing a prior RLR case), ischemia to the spinal cord itself (referencing another prior RLR case), and plexopathies — broadly, ischemia to the peripheral or central nervous system tends to hurt, and so do plexopathies. He reflected candidly that despite years of ED practice, he'd never before encountered patients with true weakness whom he also needed to treat aggressively for pain, and that this pattern — rather than expert-level neuroanatomic localization, which he admitted isn't his strength — was what tipped him off. His take-home: it's unusual to be both weak and in severe pain at once, and clinicians can use that combination as a localizing tool in the same way sensory symptoms help localize a pure weakness presentation.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Both cases share a common near-miss: an initially plausible, narrower diagnosis (radial mononeuropathy; sciatica) that fit the majority of the findings well enough to feel settled, until an atypical feature — pain severity disproportionate to the presumed diagnosis — prompted reconsideration. Robbie's explicit habit of tracking his own practice pattern ("how many times have I given Toradol to someone with weakness — the answer was zero") functioned as a personal calibration tool, letting an unusual therapeutic instinct (reaching for strong analgesia in a weak patient) serve as a signal that the working diagnosis might be incomplete, even without formal neuroanatomic expertise. Reza's reflex to reach for AI or a specialist for the more granular neuroanatomy, paired with his confidently correct application of the plexus concept once reframed, illustrates that the big-picture "what category of structure is failing" reasoning can be robust even when detailed anatomic recall is imperfect.

## Teaching Discussion

The unifying teaching point across both cases is that severe pain combined with true weakness in a single limb is an unusual and highly informative combination — most compressive mononeuropathies are relatively painless, and radiculopathy-associated pain tends to fade as weakness sets in, so pain and weakness occurring together should prioritize a plexus-level lesion. The episode also reinforces a general localization discipline: use every available modality (motor, sensory, and now pain) to narrow the anatomic level of a neurologic complaint, and apply the same systematic logic used for well-known patterns (like foot drop) to less familiar ones (like brachial or lumbosacral plexopathy). Finally, the diabetic amyotrophy case is a reminder that diabetes has heterogeneous neurologic presentations well beyond the classic distal symmetric polyneuropathy, and that severe, unexplained proximal weakness and pain in an older patient warrants an A1c check alongside imaging to exclude infiltrative disease.
