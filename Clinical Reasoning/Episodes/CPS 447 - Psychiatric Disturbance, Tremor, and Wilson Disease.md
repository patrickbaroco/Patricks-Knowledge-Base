## Pearls

- **Acute psychiatric disturbance works like [[Altered Mental Status|altered mental status]]: medical / neurologic / psychiatric.** "Psychiatric disturbance" (psychosis, agitation, behavior) rather than plain confusion still localizes to the brain and runs the same differential — most often **medical/toxic-metabolic**, with the can't-miss **neurologic** causes (stroke, seizure, meningitis/encephalitis) worked up by tempo, and a young person with new psychiatric symptoms + a movement disorder raising **[[Anti-NMDA Receptor Encephalitis|anti-NMDA]]** encephalitis.
- **In [[Movement Disorder Approach|movement disorders]], phenomenology beats localization.** Most don't localize (hemiballismus→subthalamic nucleus is the rare exception); the key is naming the movement — [[Tremor|tremor]], [[Chorea|chorea]], [[Dystonia|dystonia]], tic, myoclonus — then applying the **four-bucket cause schema**: primary neurologic disease, structural lesion, systemic condition/state, or medication/drug/toxin.
- **Characterize a [[Tremor|tremor]] by body parts, amplitude, frequency, and activation — and when it's mixed, "look at the legs."** *Rest* → [[Parkinsonism|parkinsonism]] (low freq/amplitude, re-emergent); *posture + action, no dysmetria* → [[Essential Tremor|essential]] (± head/voice); *action + intention with dysmetria* → [[Cerebellar Tremor|cerebellar]]; *rest + posture + action, high amplitude* → [[Holmes Tremor|rubral/Holmes]] (red nucleus/midbrain — combined parkinsonian-cerebellar). A patient rarely has two movement disorders, so a confusing high-amplitude "chorea-like" limb often resolves when a quieter body part shows the true tremor.
- **[[Wilson Disease|Wilson disease]] is the [[Unifying Diagnosis|unifying diagnosis]] for a young patient with movement disorder + liver disease + psychiatric change.** Onset is typically the teens–20s; the movement disorder can be almost anything (the classic **wing-beating tremor**, but also dystonia, parkinsonism, or tremor of any activation), and neuropsychiatric presentations are common (this patient was first worked up as "index psychosis"). A unilateral movement disorder is a stretch for a toxic-metabolic cause (aside from **[[Hyperglycemic Hemichorea|hyperglycemic hemichorea]]**), pushing toward a structural or degenerative process.
- **Confirm Wilson disease by [[Test Interpretation|the right tests]]: low [[Ceruloplasmin|ceruloplasmin]], high 24-hour urine copper, [[Kayser-Fleischer Rings|Kayser-Fleischer rings]] on slit lamp, ATP7B genetics, and hepatic copper on biopsy.** KF rings usually need a **slit-lamp** exam (easily confused with arcus senilis by naked eye); MRI may show putaminal/basal-ganglia T2 signal and the **[[Face of the Giant Panda Sign|face-of-the-giant-panda]]** or split-thalamus signs. Treatment is chelation (penicillamine, trientine) or zinc — and neurologic symptoms can **transiently worsen** after starting therapy.
- **[[AI as a Diagnostic Tool|AI is a "consultant in your pocket" — only as good as your clinical assessment.]]** Feeding a large-language-model tool "20-year-old, confusion, RUQ pain" yields noise; feeding it "**unilateral rest-posture-action tremor + new liver disease in a young adult**" yields Wilson disease. The physician's job — the precise phenomenology and the key positives/negatives — is what makes the tool (or a Google search, or an expert) useful; garbage in, garbage out.

## Problem Representation

A previously healthy 20-year-old man presents with one to two weeks of acute neuropsychiatric change — confusion, talking to himself, insomnia, low mood, and odd behavior — accompanied by a new right-sided tremor and intermittent right-upper-quadrant pain, with a normal general exam except mild hepatomegaly (later hepatosplenomegaly). His neurologic exam shows a unilateral rest-, posture-, and action-tremor of high amplitude with otherwise normal cranial nerves, power, tone, and reflexes and mild disorientation. Labs show thrombocytopenia and mildly elevated transaminases with normal renal, thyroid, B12, HIV, and syphilis studies, a bland CSF, and normal CT and EEG; MRI reveals putaminal T2 hyperintensity with subtle midbrain change. The unifying diagnosis — a young patient with a new movement disorder, liver disease, and psychiatric change — is Wilson disease, confirmed by Kayser-Fleischer rings on slit-lamp examination and a markedly elevated 24-hour urine copper, with liver biopsy pending.

## Case Narrative

A CP Solvers Neurology VMR with **Aaron Berkowitz** teaching, **Zachariah** presenting (from Johannesburg, South Africa), and **Sebastian** discussing. The chief concern: **acute psychiatric disturbance and tremor**.

**Framing the two problems.** Sebastian works the tempo — acute → intoxication/withdrawal (alcohol/DTs the real-world favorite), infection (including rhombencephalitis), vascular, and, if more subacute, autoimmune/[[Paraneoplastic Neurologic Syndrome|paraneoplastic]] encephalitis. Berkowitz reframes **acute psychiatric disturbance** as a cousin of [[Altered Mental Status|altered mental status]] — his simple **medical / neurologic / psychiatric** buckets (not [[MIST Mnemonic|MIST]]) — noting most such presentations are **medical/toxic-metabolic**, with the can't-miss neurologic causes (stroke, seizure, meningitis/encephalitis) and, in a young person with new psychiatry plus a movement disorder, **[[Anti-NMDA Receptor Encephalitis|anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis]]**. Then he pivots to the **[[Movement Disorder Approach|movement-disorder method]]**: unlike the rest of neurology, movement disorders turn on **phenomenology, not localization** (hemiballismus→subthalamic nucleus is the rare localizer). Name the movement — [[Tremor|tremor]], [[Chorea|chorea]], [[Dystonia|dystonia]], tic, myoclonus — then run the **four-bucket cause schema** (primary neurologic, structural, systemic, drug/toxin), and read a tremor by **body parts, amplitude, frequency, and activation** (rest / posture / action / intention).

**HPI.** A **20-year-old man**, previously well with no psychiatric or family history and on no medications, has one to two weeks of **confusion, talking to himself, poor sleep, and a right hand/arm tremor**, with **intermittent right-upper-quadrant pain**. He is a thin, tall student; vitals are normal; the systemic exam is unremarkable except **mild hepatomegaly** (liver 2 cm below the costal margin). Neurologically he has a **unilateral right-sided tremor**, normal cranial nerves, 5/5 power, normal tone and reflexes, GCS 14 (disoriented to place/time), and a mental state notable for agitation, self-directed speech, and **low mood**. The triad that reorganizes everything: **new movement disorder + new liver disease + psychiatric change in a 20-year-old**.

**Reading the tremor.** A shared (open-source) video shows a **mixed, high-amplitude, coarse** movement — the arms look almost choreoathetoid and restless, which Sebastian flags as hard to classify. Berkowitz teaches the disambiguation: a patient rarely has **two** movement disorders, so **"focus on the legs"** (cover the confusing arms) — and the legs show a clear **rest tremor** (lower frequency, lower amplitude, Parkinsonian-looking) that, once established, reveals the arms as the **same rest-and-postural tremor at very high amplitude** (with the relaxed hands "flopping" and mimicking chorea). He defines tremor (involuntary rhythmic oscillation), distinguishes it from **[[Chorea|chorea]]** ("the atrial fibrillation of movement disorders — irregularly irregular"), and — given Zachariah's report that the real patient's tremor was **rest + posture + action, high amplitude, unilateral** — walks the tremor differential: rest → [[Parkinsonism|parkinsonism]]; posture+action without dysmetria → [[Essential Tremor|essential]]; action+intention with dysmetria → [[Cerebellar Tremor|cerebellar]]; and **rest+posture+action, high amplitude → [[Holmes Tremor|rubral/Holmes tremor]]** (red nucleus/midbrain, a combined parkinsonian-cerebellar tremor he's seen only twice in ~15 years). (See the **[[Tremor]]** hub for the full frequency/amplitude/activation table.)

**The unifying diagnosis, and the anchor.** Movement disorder **plus liver** in a young patient pulls both Berkowitz and Sebastian irresistibly to **[[Wilson Disease|Wilson disease]]** — and both name their **[[Anchoring Bias|anchoring]]** (and "VMR bias") explicitly. They generate the honest alternatives — **[[Neuroacanthocytosis|neuroacanthocytosis]]** (chorea + organomegaly), Niemann-Pick (too old), **[[Acute Intermittent Porphyria|acute intermittent porphyria]]** (Sebastian's prior case: abdominal pain + psychiatric + neuropathy), inborn errors of metabolism, and a toxin causing combined hepatic-plus-neurologic injury — while noting that a **unilateral** movement disorder is a stretch for a toxic-metabolic cause (aside from [[Hyperglycemic Hemichorea|hyperglycemic hemichorea]]) and favors something structural or degenerative.

**Work-up.** Labs show a **platelet count of 90** (thrombocytopenia) with otherwise normal counts, normal chemistries/thyroid/B12/B6, negative HIV and RPR, low inflammatory markers, and **mildly raised transaminases**; urine dipstick and a drug screen are negative. **CT brain (pre/post-contrast), EEG, and LP (acellular, normal protein)** are normal, as are CXR, EKG, and echocardiogram. Berkowitz and Sebastian, anchored, name the Wilson work-up with candid humility about the details: **serum [[Ceruloplasmin|ceruloplasmin]]** (low), **24-hour urine copper** (high), **[[Kayser-Fleischer Rings|Kayser-Fleischer rings]]** on **slit-lamp** exam (hard to see by naked eye — easily confused with arcus senilis), **ATP7B** genetics, **hepatic copper** on liver biopsy, and the MRI **[[Face of the Giant Panda Sign|face-of-the-giant-panda]]** and split-thalamus signs. Berkowitz plugs the source of his fresh Wilson knowledge — an upcoming *Continuum* "Neurology of Systemic Disease" issue he guest-edited (neurology of cardiac, renal, and liver disease).

**Resolution.** The **MRI** shows **putaminal T2 hyperintensity with subtle midbrain changes**; a **slit-lamp exam reveals Kayser-Fleischer rings**, and the **24-hour urine copper is markedly elevated** — confirming **Wilson disease** (liver biopsy pending), with GI agreeing. Treatment is chelation/zinc, with the caveat that neurologic symptoms can **transiently worsen** after initiation; the patient is improving. Zachariah's teaching arc doubles as a lesson in tools: the case was first labeled "index psychosis" until a colleague caught the **hepatomegaly**, prompting LFTs and ultrasound — and he used an **[[AI as a Diagnostic Tool|AI tool (OpenEvidence)]]** to generate the differential and next tests. Berkowitz endorses it with the crucial caveat (and Marty Samuels' "why do you need me, just Google it" anecdote about copper-deficiency myeloneuropathy): the tool is a "consultant in your pocket" **only if you feed it the precise clinical assessment** — the unilateral rest-posture-action tremor plus new liver disease — rather than raw, unprocessed symptoms.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Movement Disorder Approach|Phenomenology-first]] movement-disorder reasoning.** Naming the movement (a rest-posture-action tremor, not chorea) via the "look at the legs" trick, then running the four-bucket cause schema, is what made the tremor interpretable — the same method used in [[CPS 247 - Tremors, Myoclonus, and Steroid-Responsive Autoimmune Encephalopathy|CPS 247]] and [[CPS 276 - Lethargy, Myoclonus, and Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome|CPS 276]] and anchored in the [[Tremor]] hub.
- **[[Unifying Diagnosis|Unifying diagnosis]] via the Venn diagram.** The decisive move was intersecting *movement disorder* with *liver disease* in a *young* patient — a single diagnosis ([[Wilson Disease|Wilson]]) over a coincidental collection, the Occam counterpart to the Hickam reasoning of [[CPS 323 - Visual Hallucinations and Occipital Seizures|CPS 323]].
- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring]], named and (this time) vindicated.** Berkowitz and Sebastian announce their anchor on Wilson and their "VMR bias," keep generating alternatives (neuroacanthocytosis, porphyria, Niemann-Pick), and acknowledge they "anchored the ship in the right harbor" — modeling that anchoring is a risk to name, not always an error.
- **[[Base Rate|Base rates]] and the unilateral clue.** A unilateral movement disorder is improbable for a toxic-metabolic cause (hyperglycemic hemichorea aside), which correctly nudged the reasoning away from generic encephalopathy toward a structural/degenerative substrate.
- **[[Test Interpretation|Test interpretation]] with candid humility.** The Wilson panel (ceruloplasmin low, urine copper high, KF rings on slit lamp not naked eye, ATP7B, hepatic copper) and the MRI signs are named with explicit uncertainty about specifics — a model of knowing *which* tests to send even when the fine print needs looking up.
- **[[AI as a Diagnostic Tool|AI as an amplifier of clinical reasoning]].** The presenter's use of an LLM tool and Berkowitz's Marty Samuels "just Google it" anecdote make the same point: the tool's output is only as good as the physician's phenomenologic framing — the reasoning, not the retrieval, is the scarce skill.

## Teaching Discussion

- Treat acute psychiatric disturbance like altered mental status, sorting causes into medical, neurologic, and psychiatric, expecting most to be medical/toxic-metabolic, working up the can't-miss neurologic diagnoses by tempo, and considering anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis in a young person with new psychiatric symptoms plus a movement disorder.
- Approach movement disorders by phenomenology rather than localization: name the movement (tremor, chorea, dystonia, tic, myoclonus) and then apply the four-bucket cause schema (primary neurologic, structural, systemic, drug/toxin), since most movement disorders do not localize.
- Characterize a tremor by affected body parts, amplitude, frequency, and activation state — rest (parkinsonism), posture-plus-action without dysmetria (essential), action-plus-intention with dysmetria (cerebellar), and rest-plus-posture-plus-action at high amplitude (rubral/Holmes) — and, when a limb's movement is ambiguous, examine a quieter body part because a patient rarely has two distinct movement disorders.
- Intersect a new movement disorder with new liver disease and psychiatric change in a young patient to reach Wilson disease; expect onset in the teens to twenties, a movement disorder of almost any type (classically the wing-beating tremor), and frequent neuropsychiatric presentations (often first labeled primary psychosis), and remember that a unilateral movement disorder argues against a toxic-metabolic cause aside from hyperglycemic hemichorea.
- Confirm Wilson disease with serum ceruloplasmin (low), 24-hour urine copper (high), Kayser-Fleischer rings on slit-lamp examination (not reliably seen by naked eye), ATP7B genetics, and hepatic copper on biopsy; look for putaminal/basal-ganglia T2 signal and the face-of-the-giant-panda or split-thalamus MRI signs; treat with chelation or zinc, anticipating possible transient neurologic worsening after starting therapy.
- Name anchoring when it happens: announce the leading hypothesis, keep generating alternatives (neuroacanthocytosis, acute intermittent porphyria, inborn errors of metabolism, toxin-induced hepatic-plus-neurologic injury), and treat the anchor as a risk to manage rather than an automatic error.
- Use AI and search tools as a "consultant in your pocket," but recognize their output is only as good as the clinical assessment fed in — the physician's phenomenologic framing and selection of key positives and negatives is what turns a tool, a search, or an expert into a precise answer.
