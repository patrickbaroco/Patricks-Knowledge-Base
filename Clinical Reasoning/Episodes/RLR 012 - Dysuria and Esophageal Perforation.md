## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 65-year-old man with BPH is brought from urology clinic to the ED with dysuria and urinary frequency two days after a cystoscopy with biopsies (workup of hematuria attributed to BPH). He looks well, is afebrile, and has no CVA tenderness; the UA shows florid [[Pyuria|pyuria]] (~180 WBC) without hematuria, a normal bladder scan, and normal labs — a clean [[Sequence of Events|post-procedural]] cystitis story. But relentless nausea and vomiting sit oddly beside a "simple cystitis," and a CT done to chase the nausea is normal except for bladder-wall thickening and, glossed over, *mild [[Esophagitis|esophagitis]]*. He is discharged on antibiotics and returns two days later still nauseated, dysuria only partly improved, now with chest discomfort. A pan-sensitive *E. coli* confirms the UTI is real — yet a repeat CT is unremarkable, and while sitting comfortably he abruptly (over ~15 seconds) becomes tachycardic and hypoxemic; a resident hears absent right-sided breath sounds and a chest x-ray shows a large right **[[Hydropneumothorax|hydropneumothorax]]** without tension. Reza fixes the [[Center of Gravity|center of gravity]] on explaining *air plus fluid* together, unleashes a [[Schema-Based Reasoning|pneumothorax schema]] (trauma vs spontaneous → lung vs esophagus), and when the chest tube drains dark food particulate the answer declares itself: **[[Boerhaave Syndrome|Boerhaave-type esophageal perforation]]** at the GE junction from forceful vomiting, confirmed by a sky-high [[Pleural Fluid Amylase|pleural fluid amylase]]. The [[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive autopsy]]: filing the symptom as "nausea" rather than "vomiting" muted the very cue — repeated vomiting — that would have put perforation on the board.

## Pearls

- **"UTI" is as coarse as "respiratory tract infection" — take an [[Anatomical Approach|anatomical approach]] down the tract before you treat.** Urethra, prostate, bladder, ureter, kidney each localize differently: [[Pyelonephritis|pyelonephritis]] brings fever, rigors, and CVA tenderness in a sick patient; [[Cystitis|simple cystitis]] gives isolated dysuria/frequency/suprapubic tenderness. In a man with lower urinary tract symptoms keep a very low threshold for a prostate exam ([[Prostatitis|prostatitis]] clues: burning with ejaculation, tenderness on sitting).
- **When a "simple" diagnosis grows a symptom it can't explain, the diagnosis — not the symptom — is what's wrong.** Nausea and vomiting do not belong to simple cystitis; the discordant feature was the case's true engine and deserved to reframe it rather than be smoothed over.
- **Read nausea by its *tempo*: peaks-and-valleys points to a mechanical/obstructive cause (partial SBO), a low-grade constant hum points to a toxin, drug, or endogenous derangement (e.g., hyponatremia).** Tempo narrows an otherwise head-to-toe differential before imaging.
- **A pan-sensitive urine culture confirms the UTI is real but never certifies it as the *whole* story** — [[Sterile Pyuria|sterile pyuria]] (and, here, a genuine coexisting infection) can sit beside a second, dominant process. Concordant data on the minor problem must not anchor you off the major one.
- **A [[Hydropneumothorax|hydropneumothorax]] must be explained as a *pneumothorax first* — the air is the hard constraint.** Effusion differentials (heart, liver, kidney, cancer, autoimmune, infection) don't generate air; a [[Schema-Based Reasoning|pneumothorax schema]] (trauma vs spontaneous → lung parenchyma vs esophagus) does, and in a vomiting patient it walks you straight to esophageal perforation. Dark **food particulate** from a chest tube and a high [[Pleural Fluid Amylase|pleural fluid amylase]] seal it.
- **[[Boerhaave Syndrome|Boerhaave]] from vomiting alone is rare and humbling** — most esophageal perforations follow instrumentation (endoscopy, dilation, NG placement, surgery) — and it is a *vicious cycle*: a partial tear drives more vomiting, which drives more tearing. The day-one CT's "mild esophagitis," dismissed at the time, was the tear's first faint signature.

## Problem Representation

A 65-year-old man with BPH, undergoing urologic workup of hematuria (attributed to BPH) with a cystoscopy and bladder biopsies three days prior, presents with two days of dysuria and urinary frequency plus prominent nausea and vomiting; he is well-appearing, afebrile, without CVA tenderness, with florid pyuria (~180 WBC) but no hematuria, a normal bladder scan, and normal CBC/renal panel — initially framed as post-procedural cystitis (later a pan-sensitive *E. coli*) with incidental mild esophagitis on CT, who re-presents with persistent nausea, partially improved dysuria, and new chest discomfort, then acutely develops tachycardia and hypoxemia from a large non-tension right hydropneumothorax, ultimately diagnosed as a Boerhaave-type esophageal perforation at the GE junction (dark food particulate on chest-tube drainage, markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase, CT-confirmed) driven by forceful vomiting.

## Case Narrative

Rabih frames the case as "a journey" and sets an unusual scene: the patient is walked into the ED by a urology provider who signs him out directly. He is 65 with BPH, seen in urology for hematuria initially attributed to the BPH but worked up with a cystoscopy three days ago, including biopsies of what may have been a polyp or diverticulum. Two days after the scope he called clinic with dysuria and urinary frequency, prompting the ED visit. His vitals are all normal, he is afebrile, appears very well, and has no CVA tenderness.

Reza immediately notes there are effectively *two problems*: the index problem that led to the cystoscopy (the hematuria) and the current problem that brought him in (dysuria and frequency). These may share one etiology or be entirely unrelated, and he chooses to work the acute ED problem while keeping the hematuria in the back of his mind. Dysuria and frequency are classic for urinary tract infection, but he cautions that "UTI" is as broad as "respiratory tract infection" — you must take an **[[Anatomical Approach|anatomical approach]]** along the tract (urethra, prostate, bladder, ureters, kidneys), because the site explains the presentation. **[[Pyelonephritis|Pyelonephritis]]** classically brings a sick, febrile patient with rigors and CVA tenderness; **[[Cystitis|simple cystitis]]** gives isolated urinary symptoms and suprapubic tenderness without systemic signs. Because this is a man, he keeps **[[Prostatitis|prostatitis]]** in play — clues being burning with ejaculation or tenderness on sitting — and argues for a low threshold to examine the prostate in any man with lower urinary tract symptoms.

Crucially, the patient just had a procedure, so Reza applies the principle of a **[[Sequence of Events|sequence of events]]**: a foreign object passed up the urethra readily translocates or manipulates bacteria upward, and while post-cystoscopy infections often show only transient, self-limited bacteremia, he can easily imagine ascending organisms seeding a cystitis. He wants a urinalysis (pyuria, nitrites, leukocyte esterase), a prostate exam, and a fuller history of the hematuria (gross vs microscopic, painful vs painless).

Rabih loads the data. Basic bloodwork (drawn only because of the recent procedure) and the UA return: florid **[[Pyuria|pyuria]]**, ~180 white cells, *no* hematuria, a normal bladder scan (no retention), and normal renal panel and CBC. But for the two hours he sat in the ED he was constantly nauseated and vomiting. An EKG, troponin, and chest x-ray were all normal; puzzled by the nausea, the team obtained a CT that was likewise normal except for bladder-wall thickening (supporting cystitis) and *mild esophagitis*. He was discharged on an antibiotic. Two days later he returns still nauseated and vomiting, with dysuria somewhat but not fully improved, and now a little chest discomfort.

Reza flags the discord: nausea and vomiting are *not* consistent with simple cystitis, and that mismatch should drive further investigation. Nausea is a head-to-toe symptom, so he leans on two tools — look for any other localizing pathology to explain it, and read its **tempo**: peaks and valleys suggest a partial small-bowel obstruction, whereas a low-grade constant nausea suggests a toxin, a drug effect, marijuana, or an endogenous derangement like hyponatremia. The prior CT appropriately excluded obstruction and colonic wall thickening. Given the persistence, he now re-interrogates the pyuria itself: did the culture actually grow a bacterium, or is this **[[Sterile Pyuria|sterile pyuria]]** pointing to a process *around* the tract (e.g., an inflamed colon abutting the bladder)? He would check the nitrite and the urine culture. For the chest, he again takes an anatomical approach — imagining an arrow through the center of the chest and cataloguing what it strikes (heart, lung, mediastinum, pleura) — and holds a low threshold to repeat the CT, this time adding the chest with contrast, because a simple cystitis that neither resolves on antibiotics nor explains the chest suggests something was missed or is still too early to have left a radiographic signature.

Rabih tightens the seatbelts. The urine culture grows *E. coli* pan-sensitive to everything. Repeat evaluation head to toe: EKG mild sinus tachycardia, chest x-ray normal, repeat labs showing a mild leukocytosis of 12 (up from 8), unchanged renal panel, normal liver enzymes; the repeat contrasted CT is again unremarkable. He is given hydration and anti-emetics, is sitting comfortably and improving — and then, over roughly 10–15 seconds, becomes markedly tachycardic, markedly hypoxemic, and in severe pain, with the EKG still only sinus tachycardia. A resident, Dr. Rob Weber, auscultates and hears *no breath sounds on the right*; a chest x-ray shows a large right-sided **[[Hydropneumothorax|hydropneumothorax]]** without tension physiology.

Reza builds the collapse cleanly: the tachycardia is explained by acute hypoxemia, and the hypoxemia by the hydropneumothorax collapsing the lung and impairing gas exchange. The **[[Center of Gravity|center of gravity]]** now becomes *why there is both fluid and air in the pleural space* — and he insists on approaching it as a **pneumothorax**, not an effusion, because effusion etiologies (heart, liver, kidney, cancer, autoimmune, infection) don't account for the air, whereas whatever explains the air can explain the fluid. Practically, before any schema, he wants CT surgery: even without tension the lung is down and the patient hypoxemic, so a chest tube is needed. Then he unleashes a **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|pneumothorax schema]]** (credited to pulmonologist Dr. Michelle Johnson, who happens to be the ICU attending). Is it traumatic or spontaneous? Not trauma here. Is there underlying lung or esophageal pathology? An esophageal-pulmonary fistula can produce a pneumothorax, but there's been no dysphagia or aspiration signal. Most non-traumatic pneumothoraces in an older patient are lung-driven: obstructive disease with ruptured blebs (COPD) — but that won't explain the *hydro*; cystic disease (lymphangioleiomyomatosis, cystic fibrosis); interstitial disease (pleuroparenchymal fibroelastosis); or metastatic disease to the lung, which could explain the effusion but struggles to explain the pneumothorax. That last branch pulls his mind back to the **hematuria**: bladder or renal cell carcinoma in an elderly man, possibly with lung metastasis tying pyuria, hematuria, and hydropneumothorax together — though he is hesitant given the total absence of cancer signal (no weight loss, fatigue, or smoking history offered). He notes candidly that he has considered a hydropneumothorax exactly once in his career — a catamenial case from thoracic [[Endometriosis|endometriosis]] — underscoring how far off the beaten path this is.

Rabih delivers the pivot. CT surgery places a chest tube and the answer arrives instantly: **dark food particulate** drains out. This prioritizes an esophageal lesion — a tear or perforation venting esophageal contents into the pleural space. Reza reassembles it: the patient's forceful nausea and vomiting produced a **[[Boerhaave Syndrome|Boerhaave-type perforation]]**, food tracked into the pleural space causing irritation and effusion, and air escaped to create the pneumothorax. The confirmatory test he reaches for is a **[[Pleural Fluid Amylase|pleural fluid amylase]]**. It is markedly elevated, and a CT chest confirms an esophageal perforation at the level of the GE junction.

Rabih closes on how humbling it is: most esophageal perforations follow instrumentation — endoscopy, surgery, NG placement, esophageal dilation — and a spontaneous perforation from vomiting alone is rare. Looking back, the day-one CT's *mild esophagitis*, mentioned in passing and moved past, was the tear's earliest signature; most mild esophagitis never perforates, but this one did. He describes the mechanism as a vicious cycle — once enough esophagus tears, the nausea and vomiting worsen, driving still more tearing toward a complete perforation. The patient had a hard ICU course. Reza offers a **[[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive autopsy]]**: his attention had fixed on the word *nausea* rather than *vomiting*, and had he registered "repeated vomiting" at the lung-versus-esophagus fork, Boerhaave would have surfaced. Both hosts land on the lesson — for problems you rarely see, don't trust gestalt; pull up the **[[Illness Script|script]]/[[Schema-Based Reasoning|schema]]** and read it deliberately ("don't break the pasta when you look at the schema").

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Framing Effect|Framing]] — the episode's headline miss, surfaced in the [[Cognitive Autopsy|cognitive autopsy]].** Reza's mind encoded the complaint as *nausea* rather than *vomiting*, and that single word choice muted the mechanical cue (repeated forceful vomiting) that would have activated the Boerhaave script at the lung-versus-esophagus fork. How a symptom is labeled determines which illness scripts it can retrieve.
- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring]] on the concordant minor problem.** The post-procedural cystitis story was internally coherent — plausible mechanism (ascending bacteria), florid pyuria, bladder-wall thickening, and ultimately a pan-sensitive *E. coli* — and that very concordance risked anchoring the team on a benign explanation while the dominant process (a tearing esophagus) advanced. A confirmed minor diagnosis does not certify it as the whole story.
- **[[Premature Closure|Premature closure]] / [[Search Satisficing|search satisficing]] on the day-one CT.** "Mild esophagitis" was read, named, and moved past because most mild esophagitis is inconsequential — a search that stopped at the first satisfying (urinary) explanation. In hindsight it was the perforation's first radiographic signature; the base rate was right but this instance was the exception.
- **[[Availability Heuristic|Availability]] and a rare-diagnosis schema.** Reza notes he has considered a hydropneumothorax exactly once before (a catamenial [[Endometriosis|endometriosis]] case), i.e., it is barely *available*. His remedy is explicit: for low-frequency problems, distrust gestalt and instead "unleash the schema" and read it deliberately — the [[Schema-Based Reasoning|pneumothorax schema]] is what carried him from air-in-the-pleura to esophagus.
- **[[Center of Gravity|Center of gravity]] and constraint-first reasoning.** Facing both air and fluid, Reza refuses to let the broad effusion differential lead and instead anchors on the *harder constraint* (the air), because only an air-producing mechanism can unify the picture — a disciplined choice of which finding organizes the differential.
- **Keeping two problems live.** From the outset Reza tracks the index problem (hematuria) and the acute problem (dysuria) separately, resisting a forced unifier; when the hematuria tempted a metastatic-cancer unifier for the hydropneumothorax, he tempered it against the absent cancer signal rather than anchoring.

## Teaching Discussion

- "UTI" is a location-agnostic label; localizing along the tract (urethra → prostate → bladder → ureter → kidney) predicts the syndrome, and in men a prostate exam belongs on the table for lower urinary tract symptoms. Post-instrumentation, the sequence-of-events principle makes ascending, procedure-seeded infection the natural first hypothesis.
- The single most portable move here is treating a discordant symptom as a signal about the *diagnosis*: nausea and vomiting do not fit simple cystitis, and that mismatch — not the pyuria — was the case's center of gravity waiting to be recognized.
- Nausea is best triaged by tempo (episodic peaks → mechanical/obstructive; low-grade constant → toxin, drug, or metabolic derangement) alongside a search for any co-localizing pathology, which keeps a head-to-toe symptom tractable.
- A positive, pan-sensitive urine culture confirms infection but does not exclude a second dominant process; sterile pyuria in particular should redirect attention to structures adjacent to the tract.
- A hydropneumothorax is explained pneumothorax-first: effusion etiologies cannot generate air, so the air is the binding constraint. The non-traumatic pneumothorax schema (spontaneous vs traumatic → lung parenchyma vs esophagus, with bleb/cystic/interstitial/metastatic sub-branches) is the tool of choice for a problem clinicians rarely see.
- Esophageal perforation is overwhelmingly iatrogenic (endoscopy, dilation, NG placement, surgery); spontaneous Boerhaave from vomiting is rare and self-amplifying (tear → more vomiting → more tear). Chest-tube food particulate and a high pleural fluid amylase are the fast confirmatory cues.
- For low-frequency, high-stakes problems, deliberate schema use outperforms gestalt — and a cognitive autopsy that traces a miss back to a framing choice ("nausea" vs "vomiting") is how the next case gets caught.

## Handbook Opportunities

- A **hydropneumothorax** card — approach air-first, the trauma-vs-spontaneous and lung-vs-esophagus schema, and immediate management (chest tube, CT surgery).
- A **Boerhaave / esophageal perforation** card — iatrogenic vs spontaneous causes, the vomiting-driven vicious cycle, and confirmatory workup (chest-tube food particulate, pleural fluid amylase, CT with contrast/esophagram).
- A **"UTI" localization** card — the urethra→kidney anatomic ladder mapped to syndromes, plus the low threshold for a prostate exam in men.
- A **nausea by tempo** quick reference — episodic (mechanical/obstructive) vs constant low-grade (toxin/drug/metabolic), with the head-to-toe co-localization search.
- A **sterile pyuria** differential card — and when a positive culture still isn't the whole story.
