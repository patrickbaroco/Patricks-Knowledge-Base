## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Reza presents a medicine-consult case from an inpatient psychiatric unit: a 65-year-old man with chronic catatonic schizophrenia and a flat, unreliable history, referred for nurse-noted abdominal distension he himself never complained of. Robbie builds a "phase of matter" framework (solid/liquid/gas) for abdominal distension in real time, argues that an unreliable historian lowers the diagnostic threshold for imaging, and works through an unexpected CT finding — unilateral left hydronephrosis with a mid-ureteral stricture and two incidental renal lesions — using a luminal/mural/extraluminal framework borrowed directly from biliary-obstruction reasoning. A deep chart review turns up decades of stable, thoroughly worked-up "chronic benign eosinophilia," and a UA prompts empiric pyelonephritis treatment despite a poor clinical fit. After a ureteroscopy (obtained only because the team pushed urology past a "just treat and move on" recommendation) shows complete resolution with no visible cause, Robbie reasons his way to the real answer: the whole picture — constipation on a CT scan, a psychiatric patient likely on constipating medications, and a painless, spontaneously-resolving left-sided obstruction — is far more consistent with stool compressing the ureter than with a passed stone, the diagnosis the urologists themselves settled on.

## Pearls

- When building a differential for abdominal distension, first triage by "phase of matter": solid (organomegaly/mass), liquid (ascites), or gas (obstruction/constipation) — then use three variables (time course, focality, associated findings) to discriminate between them.
- Gas-driven distension is typically hyperacute (true obstruction) or chronic (longstanding constipation); liquid-driven distension is typically subacute, diffuse, and often accompanied by lower extremity edema; solid-driven distension (organomegaly, a mass) is typically subacute to chronic and characteristically focal/asymmetric rather than uniformly distributed.
- The absence of a supporting finding (no tympani, no leg edema, no focality) lowers but does not eliminate the corresponding category — in a patient who cannot reliably corroborate a timeline, none of the three categories can be confidently excluded on exam alone.
- Diagnostic threshold should be explicitly tailored to the reliability of the history: an unreliable historian (flat affect, minimal engagement, cognitive/psychiatric limitation) should lower the bar for pursuing imaging rather than relying on serial symptom-based reassessment.
- The single most sensitive historical red flag for a mass effect large enough to matter is early satiety — a distending abdominal process compresses the stomach specifically, so a change in appetite/food tolerance is often the earliest clue, though this sign is unusable in a poor historian.
- Nonspecific lab abnormalities (AKI, elevated liver enzymes, leukocytosis) in the setting of abdominal distension are collectively "alarm features" that argue for cross-sectional imaging even without a specific localizing symptom.
- A negative diagnostic test that was ordered for a legitimate, alarm-feature-driven indication should not be second-guessed as "the wrong test" — the value of the test is judged by the appropriateness of the indication at the time, not retrospectively by the result (the seatbelt analogy: a good outcome without an intervention doesn't mean the intervention wasn't warranted).
- Unexpected incidental findings on imaging deserve their own independent workup rather than being folded into the original clinical question — treat findings like renal lesions plus ureteral dilation as potentially two separate problems (directly analogous to a patient found to have both bile duct dilation and separate liver lesions).
- Painless, slowly progressive unilateral hydronephrosis is classically silent because the contralateral kidney compensates for lost GFR — unlike the biliary system (a single common bile duct), the paired-ureter anatomy of the urinary tract allows a unilateral obstruction to go undetected for a long time without triggering renal dysfunction.
- Bilateral hydronephrosis should raise suspicion for a bladder outlet-level process; unilateral hydronephrosis localizes the problem to that side's ureter specifically.
- Reusable framework for a ureteral (or biliary) stricture: is the process intraluminal (e.g., a stone), within the wall itself (e.g., IgG4-related disease, urothelial carcinoma with metastasis), or extraluminal/external compression? When good-quality imaging shows nothing intraluminal or extraluminal, the probability shifts toward a mural process — the same reasoning used for an unexplained biliary stricture pointing toward primary sclerosing cholangitis when no stone or mass is visualized.
- Eosinophilia is a commonly overlooked but meaningfully sensitive clue for IgG4-related disease — roughly 60% of patients with IgG4 disease have eosinophilia, and the disease can cause both post-renal (ureteral/retroperitoneal) and intrarenal parenchymal lesions simultaneously.
- A long-standing, previously thoroughly worked-up finding (here, eosinophilia investigated and labeled "chronic benign" a year earlier) should generally be treated as unrelated to an acute new problem — unless the newly suspected unifying diagnosis is itself one with a similarly long, indolent natural history.
- A thorough chart review is consistently underrated as a diagnostic tool: recognizing that this patient's white count and eosinophil count have fluctuated for years without fever or clinical decompensation can prevent a reflexive "tachycardia + leukocytosis = sepsis" pathway that would otherwise trigger an unnecessary transfer, IV access, and antibiotics.
- Different specialties can reasonably use different diagnostic thresholds for the same working diagnosis: a generalist wants to be roughly 70-80% confident before committing to and communicating a diagnosis, while a proceduralist about to instrument a potentially non-sterile space (e.g., before a ureteroscopy) may reasonably treat even a 5-10% risk of infection empirically, since the downside of a procedural complication in an infected field is disproportionate.
- The dogma that acute hydronephrosis must always be emergently decompressed is worth holding with some humility — while urgent decompression remains the default approach for acute, symptomatic, or infected obstruction, chronic/subacute, non-infected, minimally symptomatic hydronephrosis does not necessarily require emergent intervention, and the underlying evidence base for reflexive urgency is not always as strong as commonly assumed.
- When consulting a specialist, the generalist's job is not finished once the consult is placed — remaining the integrator of the patient's full picture (and being willing to push a consultant to explain the "why," not just deliver a "what to do") is an active, ongoing responsibility, not something that can be fully outsourced.
- Framework for spontaneous resolution of a ureteral abnormality: an intraluminal process (a passed stone, a sloughed renal papilla, epithelial sloughing from a vitamin deficiency) can plausibly resolve spontaneously; a mural/wall process (fibrotic, static) rarely does; a true external compressive process is very unlikely to resolve spontaneously — with one notable exception: constipation, since a decompressing bowel regimen can directly relieve extrinsic compression on the ureter.
- The sigmoid colon's anatomic position makes the left ureter more susceptible than the right to compression from a heavily stool-loaded colon — a distinguishing clue when hydronephrosis is unilateral and left-sided in a constipated patient.
- Constipation is broadly under-recognized as a cause of clinically significant urinary tract compromise — it can cause bladder outlet-level urinary retention and, less appreciated, direct mechanical compression of one or both ureters severe enough to cause hydronephrosis and, if chronic and severe, meaningful renal function loss (distinct from the separate risk of prerenal injury from poor fluid intake in constipated patients).
- Hypodense renal lesions found incidentally alongside hydronephrosis are frequently benign or artifactual, and can even represent intrarenal edema from the obstructive process itself rather than a distinct structural lesion — their disappearance after the obstruction resolves supports this explanation retrospectively.

## Problem Representation

A 65-year-old man with chronic catatonic schizophrenia and an unreliable history, referred for nurse-observed (not patient-reported) abdominal distension with leukocytosis and longstanding fluctuating eosinophilia, is found on CT to have unexplained unilateral left hydronephrosis with a mid-ureteral stricture and incidental renal lesions, ultimately resolving completely on ureteroscopy and attributable — despite an initial "passed stone" explanation from urology — most plausibly to constipation-related extrinsic compression of the left ureter.

## Case Narrative

Reza presented a consult case from an inpatient psychiatric unit — a community-care-like setting without IV access, functioning more like a skilled nursing facility than a traditional medical ward. The patient, a 65-year-old man with a three-month psychiatric admission for catatonic schizophrenia, was referred for abdominal distension noted by nursing staff; the patient himself voiced no concerns at all. On evaluation by the intern, the patient offered minimal history — flat affect, yes/no answers only — with vitals notable only for a heart rate of 111 (temperature, blood pressure, and respiratory rate all normal) and a comfortable overall appearance. He denied belly pain and urinary symptoms and reported his last bowel movement was three days prior. Exam showed a distended but soft abdomen, non-tympanic to percussion, without tenderness or guarding. Labs showed a normal CBC apart from a white count of 14,000 with an eosinophil count of 1,200; a year-long chart review showed the white count fluctuating between 10,000 and 13,000 and the eosinophil count fluctuating between 1,000 and 4,000 over that time. Liver chemistries and lipase were normal.

Robbie built a structured approach to abdominal distension live: triage first by "phase of matter" — solid (organomegaly or a mass), liquid (ascites), or gas (obstruction or constipation) — then use time course, focality, and associated findings to discriminate. Gas-driven distension tends to be either hyperacute (true obstruction) or chronic (constipation); liquid-driven distension tends to be subacute, diffuse, and accompanied by leg edema; solid-driven distension tends to be subacute to chronic and focal rather than symmetric. He acknowledged that in this patient, the absence of tympani, leg edema, and focality each modestly lowered but did not eliminate their respective categories, leaving genuine diagnostic uncertainty. He framed the practical question as whether to "look inside," and argued the answer hinges on red flags — historically, early satiety is the single most sensitive symptom (since a distending mass compresses the stomach specifically), though this was unusable given the patient's limited communication; on labs, any combination of abdominal distension with AKI, liver injury, or leukocytosis constitutes an alarm feature warranting cross-sectional imaging. Given the leukocytosis and an unreliable historian, Robbie recommended a CT scan over ultrasound, since ultrasound would only be preferable if history/exam had already pointed toward a liquid (ascites) process specifically.

Reza reinforced the point that diagnostic threshold should be explicitly tailored to how much the history can be trusted, and cited a prior RLR case (metastatic disease with ascites, ultimately diagnosed by CT) as a parallel example of imaging catching something an unreliable or limited history would have missed. Robbing offered his own analogy for not second-guessing a reasonably-indicated negative test: like a seatbelt, its value is judged by the soundness of the decision to use it, not retrospectively by whether it happened to "catch" anything that time.

The CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast showed no ascites, no organomegaly, no obstruction, and only mild-to-moderate nonspecific stool burden — but also an unexpected finding: narrowing at the mid-left ureter with distal dilation, mild bladder distension suggestive of possible bladder outlet obstruction, and accompanying left hydronephrosis (the right kidney was entirely normal), plus two hypodense lesions in the left kidney. The radiologist floated pyelonephritis as one possible explanation for the constellation.

Robbie treated this as effectively two separate problems bundled together — hydronephrosis/ureteral stricture and distinct renal lesions — analogous to a patient found to have both bile duct dilation and separate liver lesions. He reasoned that the absence of significant pain argued for a subacute-to-chronic tempo rather than an acute process like an obstructing stone (which is classically painful), and explained the clinical silence of a unilateral obstruction through the paired-kidney anatomy: unlike the single common bile duct, two ureters allow the unaffected kidney to compensate for lost GFR, letting a unilateral obstruction progress slowly with little to no detectable renal dysfunction. He noted bilateral hydronephrosis typically points to a bladder-outlet-level process, while unilateral hydronephrosis (as seen here) localizes disease to the ureter itself, and organized the differential for a ureteral stricture into intraluminal (e.g., a stone), mural/wall (e.g., IgG4-related disease, urothelial carcinoma with metastasis to the kidney), or extraluminal/external compression — explicitly modeled on the same reasoning used for an unexplained biliary stricture, where nothing visible on imaging shifts probability toward a wall process like primary sclerosing cholangitis. Since the radiologist saw nothing clearly intraluminal or extraluminal, Robbie leaned toward a mural process, highlighting IgG4-related disease (which can cause both post-renal obstructive and intrarenal parenchymal lesions, and for which eosinophilia is a commonly overlooked but roughly 60%-sensitive clue) alongside urothelial carcinoma with renal metastasis. Given the risk of losing functional kidney tissue, his immediate recommendation was to involve urology (anticipating interventional radiology as well) to decompress the kidney — either definitively at the level of the stricture or temporarily via percutaneous nephrostomy — alongside pursuing MRI for further characterization and screening for extrarenal disease manifestations.

Reza modeled a deep chart review: this patient's eosinophilia dated back to 1994, and roughly a year earlier had already been thoroughly worked up by hematology-oncology, ranging from 2,000 to 10,000, including a peripheral flow showing only eosinophilia, a bone marrow biopsy with next-generation sequencing and FISH (both negative), and a search for end-organ damage in the heart, skin, GI tract, and lungs (none found) — culminating in a diagnosis of chronic benign eosinophilia with no further workup recommended. Robbie's response was that a finding stable since 1994 is unlikely to be causally linked to a brand-new process decades later, unless the disease being considered as the unifying diagnosis (like IgG4-related disease) itself has a comparably long, indolent time course.

Reza and the team judged the leukocytosis and tachycardia not concerning for sepsis, given the patient's years-long fluctuating white count and the absence of fever — deliberately avoiding a reflexive "tachycardia plus leukocytosis equals sepsis" transfer-and-antibiotics pathway. Urology was consulted and recommended a UA and urine culture. The UA showed roughly 30 white cells, a few red cells, and positive ketones, with a normal creatinine; prior UAs had shown intermittent pyuria for years, but this was the first time nitrites were positive. Urology recommended treating for pyelonephritis with a 14-day course of Bactrim, a recommendation echoing the radiologist's earlier mention of possible pyelonephritis — despite the patient having no fever, no flank pain, and no dysuria, a poor fit for the classic pyelonephritis illness script.

Robbie explained why the generalist and proceduralist perspectives diverged here: a generalist typically wants roughly 70-80% confidence in a diagnosis like pyelonephritis before committing to treat, while a proceduralist about to instrument a potentially non-sterile urinary tract (as urology and IR were contemplating) reasonably treats even a small risk (5-10%) empirically, given the outsized downside of introducing infection during a procedure. Reza, coining the term "possible radiographic subclinical pyelonephritis" for his own note, agreed the antibiotic decision was reasonable given the low bar for treating in this context. Urology did not recommend emergent decompression given the mild degree of hydronephrosis. Reza offered a reflective aside questioning how strong the evidence base actually is behind the reflexive urgency to decompress acute hydronephrosis (recalling a prior personal case where decompression could not be arranged promptly with no adverse effect on creatinine), while explicitly clarifying he was not advocating against timely action in genuinely acute or symptomatic cases.

Robbie, given the low urgency implied by the subacute tempo and urology's own lack of emergent action, proposed treating the presumed pyelonephritis, then obtaining repeat imaging after the antibiotic course to reassess, with outpatient urology follow-up — while candidly acknowledging genuine uncertainty over whether a repeat CT (for direct before/after comparison) or an MRI (for more detailed characterization) would be the better follow-up study.

Reza then referenced a prior NEJM Clinical Problem-Solving case (attributed to a mentor, "Garpreet"/Uncle Bob) describing sloughing of ureteral epithelium related to a vitamin deficiency (referenced once in this discussion as vitamin A and once as vitamin K, so the exact vitamin implicated was not fully reconciled between the hosts) leading to ureteral obstruction — a case that had taught Reza about the procedure of ureteroscopy. Applying that lesson here, Reza pushed back on urology's initial recommendation, which addressed only the immediate treatment question without addressing the root cause of the stricture (eosinophilic disease? cancer? IgG4 disease?). The team explicitly advocated for urology to pursue further characterization, and urology agreed, scheduling a ureteroscopy.

At the time of the procedure, the patient's eosinophil count remained elevated and his white count was mildly elevated, with no SIRS criteria met; he had completed the Bactrim course, and the urine culture had returned completely negative. On ureteroscopy, there was no stricture, no evidence of hydronephrosis, and normal ureteral reflux/efflux dynamics — the entire process had spontaneously resolved.

Robbie's closing reflection emphasized that consulting a specialist does not end the generalist's responsibility to keep integrating the whole patient picture — the broad, cross-organ perspective of a generalist is exactly what identified that this needed further characterization rather than a simple treat-and-move-on disposition. He revisited his intraluminal/mural/extraluminal framework through the lens of spontaneous resolution: an intraluminal process (a passed stone, a sloughed renal papilla, or epithelial sloughing from a vitamin deficiency) can plausibly resolve on its own; a mural process, being typically fibrotic and static, rarely does; and a true external compressive process is usually implausible to resolve spontaneously — with one notable exception, constipation, since bowel decompression directly relieves extrinsic pressure on the ureter. Robbie recalled a personal case where an aggressive bowel regimen alone resolved a similar hydronephrosis on repeat ultrasound, and noted the sigmoid colon's anatomic position makes the left ureter specifically more vulnerable to stool-related compression than the right — a detail that fit this patient's left-sided, painless, spontaneously-resolving picture. Given the patient's schizophrenia (likely on constipating psychotropic medications), the stool burden visible on the original CT, and the lack of any stone-type symptoms, Robbie's final assessment favored constipation-related transient ureteral compression over a passed stone, recommending only adequate hydration, a reliable bowel regimen, and interval repeat imaging (proposed at roughly six months) to confirm durable resolution.

Asked to reconcile the incidental hypodense renal lesions, Robbie speculated they were most likely either imaging artifact or intrarenal edema related to the hydronephrosis itself, particularly plausible given their location matched the obstructed kidney — a hypothesis supportable if the lesions resolve alongside the hydronephrosis.

Reza revealed that although urology's own final impression was a passed ureteral stone, he actually favored Robbie's constipation hypothesis: the patient's resolution followed a bowel regimen of laxatives alone (without any specific push for increased hydration and without ever reporting stone-type symptoms), a therapeutic pattern more consistent with relief of extrinsic compression than with spontaneous stone passage. He reflected on whether the entire subsequent workup — from Bactrim through ureteroscopy — could theoretically have been avoided by trying laxatives first and reassessing, but defended the actual sequence of decisions: with a leukocytosis, a strikingly distended exam, and a patient unable to reliably report worsening or new symptoms, maintaining a lower diagnostic threshold and pursuing full imaging was the appropriate, deliberate choice rather than a retrospective misstep.

The episode closed on the broader teaching point that constipation is significantly under-recognized as a cause of serious urinary tract compromise — capable of causing bladder outlet obstruction and, less appreciated, direct mechanical compression of one or both ureters severe enough to threaten renal function, distinct from the separate risk of prerenal injury from poor oral intake in constipated patients. Robbie shared a strikingly parallel personal case from around the same time: an ED patient on high-dose opioids with diffuse abdominal pain, found to be severely constipated with bilateral hydronephrosis that urology attributed directly to stool compressing both ureters, reinforcing just how little pressure the thin-walled, low-pressure-tolerant ureters can withstand.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Reflexive "tachycardia + leukocytosis = sepsis" pattern explicitly avoided**: a thorough chart review revealing years of fluctuating counts without fever prevented an unnecessary transfer and antibiotic cascade.
- **Diagnostic threshold calibrated to historian reliability**: an explicit, named principle — the less trustworthy the history, the lower the bar for imaging — applied consistently throughout the case.
- **Not treating a negative or unrevealing test as a "wrong" decision in hindsight**: the seatbelt analogy directly counters outcome bias in evaluating test-ordering decisions.
- **Specialist recommendations treated as a starting point, not an endpoint**: the team explicitly pushed back on urology's initial "just treat" recommendation to pursue root-cause characterization, resulting in the ureteroscopy that ultimately clarified the picture.
- **Avoiding premature attribution of a new eosinophilia-adjacent finding to a decades-old, previously characterized eosinophilia** — an explicit anti-anchoring heuristic (only link a longstanding finding to a new process if the candidate unifying diagnosis is itself comparably indolent).
- **Willingness to disagree with the proceduralists' own final conclusion (passed stone) based on independently reasoning through the pattern of therapeutic response** — modeling continued primary-team ownership of the diagnostic narrative even after a specialist has closed their differential.
- **Explicit acknowledgment that the full workup might, in retrospect, have been avoidable with an earlier trial of laxatives — paired with a clear defense of the original decision-making given the information available at the time** — a calibrated, non-punitive style of diagnostic reflection.

## Teaching Discussion

This episode threads together three reusable skills: a "phase of matter" framework for undifferentiated abdominal distension; a directly transferable luminal/mural/extraluminal framework for any tubular-structure stricture (ureteral here, explicitly modeled on the same logic used for biliary strictures); and a broader lesson about the limits and responsibilities of consultation — that placing a consult does not discharge the primary team's obligation to keep integrating the whole clinical picture, including questioning a consultant's own final diagnosis when the pattern of clinical response doesn't fully fit. The episode's other throughline, made explicit at the end, is that a common, seemingly mundane condition (constipation) deserves far more diagnostic respect than it typically receives, given its capacity to cause clinically significant — and potentially permanent — urinary tract compromise.
