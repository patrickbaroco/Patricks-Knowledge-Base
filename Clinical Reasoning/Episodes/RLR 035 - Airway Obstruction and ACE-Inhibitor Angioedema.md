## Pearls

- **Acute [[Upper Airway Obstruction|upper airway obstruction]] is an ABC emergency before it is a diagnostic one.** Call a [[Code Blue|code blue]], get ENT and anesthesia and the crash cart to the bedside, sit the patient bolt upright, secure IV access, and prepare to secure the airway; the differential guides drugs but the airway comes first, because a swollen upper airway can be impossible to bag-mask.
- **Examine exactly *what* is swollen — the anatomy reframes the differential.** Normal tongue and lips with a massively swollen **floor of the mouth** (palpable under the chin, pushing the tongue to the palate) is not the classic angioedema picture and pulls in [[Ludwig Angina|Ludwig's angina]] and post-procedural [[Sublingual Hematoma|hematoma]]; precise [[Anatomical Approach|localization]] is the pivot of the case.
- **[[Angioedema|Angioedema]] splits into bradykinin- vs histamine-mediated, and the split changes treatment.** [[Histamine-Mediated Angioedema|Histamine]] (mast cell/allergic) often has urticaria, can progress to [[Anaphylaxis|anaphylaxis]], and responds to epinephrine/steroids/antihistamines; [[Bradykinin-Mediated Angioedema|bradykinin]] (ACE inhibitor, [[C1 Esterase Inhibitor Deficiency|C1-INH deficiency]]) has *no* urticaria, does *not* respond to epinephrine, and is managed by removing the culprit (± icatibant/C1-INH). Tempo helps only loosely — both can be rapid in onset, but "bradykinin is Brady": slower to resolve.
- **When the cost of waiting is death, treat empirically and ask questions later.** If anaphylaxis is on the table and intubation is looming, give **IM epinephrine** (downsides ≈ zero) — but do *not* start an epi *drip* (real downsides). Same threshold logic as giving [[Adenosine|adenosine]] while prepping to cardiovert an unstable SVT: [[Treatment as a Diagnostic Test|treat first, ask later]].
- **Reframe "spontaneous" [[ACE Inhibitor Angioedema|ACE-inhibitor angioedema]] as trigger-provoked.** ~80% of reactions are in the first week and drug-alone; the delayed "can-happen-anytime" cases usually have a **trigger** — an NSAID, an opiate, a topical anesthetic, or local trauma at the site — and most ACE-angioedema *intubations* involve such a second hit. This man had three of four from one dental procedure (opiate, topical anesthetic, local trauma) — the same [[Physiologic Reserve|second-hit]] logic seen in the immobilization and vitamin-D cases.
- **Trust the frantic nurse, and beware the procedural anchor.** A nervous triage nurse is a high-value signal — they live at the [[Return to the Bedside|bedside]] and sense trajectory — while "he just had a procedure" [[Anchoring Bias|anchored]] the team on hematoma/infection and delayed steroids and epinephrine by ~10 minutes until the code leader re-asked the angioedema question.

## Problem Representation

An upper-middle-aged man presents to the ED in acute distress — sitting bolt upright, clutching his neck, gasping and saying he is choking — with massive swelling of the floor of the mouth (palpable beneath the chin, elevating the tongue to the palate) but normal lips and tongue, no urticaria, no wheeze, and normal vital signs. History obtained during resuscitation reveals a dental extraction four hours earlier with clindamycin prophylaxis and post-procedural oxycodone, followed by abrupt swelling beginning 30 minutes before arrival. Contrast CT shows diffuse soft-tissue edema of the floor of the mouth without hematoma or features of infection (no gas, no fat stranding), and the chart reveals hypertension treated with lisinopril — establishing ACE-inhibitor–induced bradykinin-mediated angioedema, provoked by procedural triggers (opiate, topical anesthetic, and local trauma), which improved with steroids and discontinuation of the ACE inhibitor without requiring intubation.

## Case Narrative

Rabih presents a case that "stretched me beyond my ability." A triage nurse — one of the best he's worked with — frantically calls several clinicians into a room, and the urgency in her voice alone conveys the acuity. An upper-middle-aged man sits bolt upright in a chair, gasping, hand around his neck, repeating "I'm choking," with a massively swollen mouth. Reza opens on the meta-point he returns to across the series: when a nurse is nervous, so is he — nurses spend far more time at the [[Return to the Bedside|bedside]] and sense a patient's trajectory before the labs do. Then he triages the emergency: this is [[Upper Airway Obstruction|acute upper airway obstruction]], likely from [[Angioedema|angioedema]], and the first question is whether it is angioedema *plus* [[Anaphylaxis|anaphylaxis]] (check for hypotension, wheeze, GI symptoms → give epinephrine) or isolated angioedema (then the issue is purely the ABCs). Either way he would call a [[Code Blue|code blue]] to bring ENT and anesthesia, because the airway will likely need securing regardless of mechanism.

Rabih slows the case down deliberately and sharpens the exam: the lips are *not* swollen, there is no rash or wheeze, and the tongue is normal in size — what is massively enlarged is the **floor of the mouth**, so swollen it can be felt bulging under the chin, soft, pushing the tongue up against the palate. Reza uses the [[Anatomical Approach|precise anatomy]] to build a differential and a plan for a single provider at the bedside: vitals, continuous pulse oximetry, tele leads and pads ready (a hypoxemic patient like this could arrest, and bag-masking a swollen upper airway may fail), oxygen, and IV access. He names two leading possibilities — angioedema and an infectious process such as [[Ludwig Angina|Ludwig's angina]] (which involves the floor of the mouth) — with the caveat that infection is usually *acute* rather than *hyper-acute*, and readies dexamethasone and an antihistamine while getting a CBC and more history.

The history, taken while the crash cart arrives, supplies the [[Illness Tempo|sequence of events]]: the man had come to the VA that morning entirely well for a dental extraction, took **clindamycin** prophylaxis before and **oxycodone** after, and — still on campus talking with friends — developed abrupt, rapidly progressive swelling 30 minutes ago. Reza applies the sequence: fine until a procedure, then hyper-acute mucosal edema, so the leading possibility is a reaction to an exposure. He then works the [[Angioedema|angioedema mechanism schema]] out loud: [[Bradykinin-Mediated Angioedema|bradykinin]]-mediated (ACE inhibitors; [[C1 Esterase Inhibitor Deficiency|C1-esterase deficiency]]) versus [[Histamine-Mediated Angioedema|histamine]]-mediated (mast-cell/allergic, often cutaneous, can cause anaphylaxis). The *hyper-acuity* nudges bradykinin, but the *exposure* — opiates can directly degranulate mast cells and release histamine — nudges histamine, and with no anaphylaxis he leans toward treating a histamine-mediated angioedema with steroids and antihistamines, doubting epinephrine has a role in isolated angioedema.

The management scene illustrates the [[Anchoring Bias|anchor]]. Anesthesia and oral surgery arrive; oral surgery, having done the procedure, worries about a [[Sublingual Hematoma|hematoma]] or infection and starts trying to incise for blood or pus — and everyone is so focused on the post-procedural frame that no one voices Reza's [[Angioedema|angioedema]]/anaphylaxis analysis until a *separate* code leader arrives and asks, "hold on — could this not be angioedema or anaphylaxis?" About 10 minutes in, the patient finally gets a steroid and **IM epinephrine** (given correctly on the lateral outer thigh, held for at least three seconds). He improves enough over 10–15 minutes to lie flat and get a contrast CT, which shows **diffuse soft-tissue edema of the floor of the mouth without hematoma and without infectious features** (no gas, no fat stranding) — confirming angioedema.

Reza credits the hematoma idea (a fresh procedure can bleed briskly) but reads the CT as clinching mucosal edema, an angioedema from a procedural exposure — and initially favors direct opiate-mediated mast-cell degranulation, planning around-the-clock steroids and antihistamines, avoidance of the culprit opiate, and an allergy/immunology consult (an acute [[Tryptase|tryptase]] could mark mast-cell activation, though he judges the story convincing enough not to need it). Then the chart opens: hypertension, and on the medication list, **lisinopril**. Reza reframes to two possibilities — opiate-mediated histaminergic angioedema, or **[[ACE Inhibitor Angioedema|ACE-inhibitor bradykinin angioedema]]** with some exacerbating factor he cannot yet name — noting the *absence of cutaneous findings* and the *lingering, epinephrine-unresponsive* course both favor bradykinin, and that epinephrine, steroids, and antihistamines have no real role there; the treatment is to stop the culprit (or replace the enzyme in hereditary C1-INH disease). He frames the puzzle with [[Analogical Reasoning|analogical reasoning]]: the sharply demarcated question he is left holding — *what are the triggers for ACE-induced angioedema?* — is one he can only ask because he knows the topic well enough to sense the gap.

Rabih delivers the payoff, praising exactly that: the clarity of what Reza knows and the crisp line of what he doesn't. About **80% of ACE-inhibitor angioedema occurs within the first week** and is attributed to the drug alone; the remaining "can-happen-anytime" cases are not truly spontaneous — a large fraction are **trigger-provoked**, and there are four common triggers: an **NSAID**, an **opiate**, exposure to **topical anesthetics**, and **local trauma at the site**. This patient had *three of four* — the dental trauma, the topical anesthetic, and the opiate — and most patients intubated for ACE angioedema have such secondary triggers; ACE-alone intubation is rare. His course fit: rapid improvement enough to avoid intubation, but swelling lingering a day or two (he couldn't eat or swallow well), with no histaminergic features ever (no rash, diarrhea, or wheeze). He got steroids, *no* antihistamines, *no* epinephrine drip, the ACE inhibitor was removed (with no reflexive switch to an ARB), and he slowly recovered.

Both hosts close on the reasoning. On epinephrine: if you are unsure and the patient may die, **IM epi has essentially no downside** and should be given, whereas an **epi drip does** — so being on the fence should push you to treat, "ask questions later" (Reza's parallel: giving adenosine while preparing to cardiovert an unstable, adenosine-responsive SVT). On the miss: "Brady kind of messed up a System-2 person" — the acuity made even senior clinicians freeze and anchor on hematoma/infection, when thinking *angioedema/anaphylaxis first* and *looking at the medication list earlier* would have been faster. And on the [[Cognitive Autopsy|humbling lesson]]: what had felt "idiopathic" late ACE angioedema is, on closer reading, usually explained by triggers — "how can it be anything else?" once you know them.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **[[Anatomical Approach|Anatomical precision as the pivot]].** The whole differential turns on *which* structure is swollen — normal tongue/lips but a massive floor of the mouth is not typical angioedema, which is exactly why Ludwig's angina and a sublingual hematoma had to be entertained. Slowing down to localize is what makes the reasoning tractable in an emergency.
- **[[Anchoring Bias|Anchoring]] on "he just had a procedure," and [[Diagnostic Momentum|momentum]] toward surgery.** The recent extraction pulled experienced clinicians toward hematoma/infection and an attempt to incise, and it took a *fresh* mind (the code leader) to voice the angioedema question — a ~10-minute delay in steroids and epinephrine. The procedural frame is a powerful, plausible anchor that crowds out the mechanism actually at play.
- **[[Illness Tempo|Tempo]] as a discriminator, used with humility.** Hyper-acute onset nudges bradykinin and away from infection; but Reza (and the literature) note the limits — both bradykinin and histamine angioedema can be rapid in *onset*, and the more useful rule is that "bradykinin is Brady," i.e., slower to *resolve*, which fits the lingering course.
- **[[Schema-Based Reasoning|The angioedema mechanism schema]].** Bradykinin (ACE-i, C1-INH) vs histamine (mast cell/allergic) is the branch point, and it dictates therapy: epinephrine/antihistamines/steroids for histamine and anaphylaxis; culprit-removal (± icatibant/C1-INH) for bradykinin, with no urticaria and no epinephrine response.
- **[[Treatment as a Diagnostic Test|Threshold-to-treat under lethal uncertainty]].** The set-piece management lesson: when anaphylaxis is possible and the airway is failing, give IM epinephrine (near-zero downside) rather than withhold it for certainty — but avoid the epi *drip* (real harm). Reza's [[Analogical Reasoning|analogy]] to adenosine-before-cardioversion in unstable SVT generalizes the "treat first, ask later" rule for do-not-miss emergencies.
- **[[Analogical Reasoning|Knowing enough to ask the right question]], and the trigger reframe.** Rabih praises Reza's sharply drawn line of uncertainty — the ability to land on the single targeted question ("what are the triggers for ACE angioedema?") because he commands the topic. The answer converts a seemingly idiopathic late reaction into a trigger-provoked one, the same [[Physiologic Reserve|second-hit]] logic (a background susceptibility plus a provocation) seen in the immobilization- and vitamin-D–hypercalcemia cases — and a model of [[Diagnostic Humility|humility]] that names the knowledge gap and fills it by reading.

## Teaching Discussion

- Treat acute upper airway obstruction as an airway emergency first: call a code blue for ENT and anesthesia, bring the crash cart, sit the patient upright, get IV access and continuous monitoring with pads ready, and remember that a swollen upper airway can defeat bag-mask ventilation — so the plan is early definitive airway control regardless of the eventual mechanism.
- Localize the swelling precisely, because the structure involved rewrites the differential: normal lips and tongue with a swollen floor of the mouth should raise Ludwig's angina and a post-procedural hematoma alongside angioedema, and a contrast CT distinguishes soft-tissue edema from hematoma and from infection (gas, fat stranding).
- Split angioedema into bradykinin- versus histamine-mediated and let it drive therapy: histamine/allergic disease (often with urticaria, may progress to anaphylaxis) responds to epinephrine, steroids, and antihistamines, whereas bradykinin disease (ACE inhibitors, C1-INH deficiency) lacks urticaria, does not respond to epinephrine, and is treated by removing the culprit (or replacing C1-INH); onset can be rapid in both, but bradykinin angioedema is slower to resolve.
- When a life-threatening diagnosis like anaphylaxis is plausible, give IM epinephrine empirically — its downside is minimal and the cost of waiting can be death — while avoiding an epinephrine infusion, which carries real risk; the same "treat now, confirm later" threshold applies to unstable tachyarrhythmias (adenosine while preparing to cardiovert).
- Recognize that "spontaneous" late ACE-inhibitor angioedema is frequently trigger-provoked: about 80% of cases occur in the first week from the drug alone, but delayed cases commonly follow an NSAID, an opiate, a topical anesthetic, or local trauma at the site, and most intubations for ACE angioedema involve such a secondary trigger — so a dental procedure stacking three triggers on a patient taking lisinopril is a coherent unifying explanation.
- Heed the nervous nurse and resist the procedural anchor: a triage nurse's alarm is a validated early-warning signal, and "the patient just had surgery" can anchor a whole team on bleeding or infection, so deliberately re-ask the do-not-miss question (angioedema/anaphylaxis) and check the medication list early.
