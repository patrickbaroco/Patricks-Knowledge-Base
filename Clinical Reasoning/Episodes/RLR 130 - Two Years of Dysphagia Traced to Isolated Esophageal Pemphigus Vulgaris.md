## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Robbie relays a case chart-reviewed on referral from an emergency department chief, deliberately narrated step by step so Reza can build his diagnostic reasoning live. A 46-year-old, otherwise healthy man presents with progressive dysphagia to solids, framed and localized using Reza's two-dimensional dysphagia framework (tempo, and oropharyngeal-vs-esophageal). Endoscopy reveals multiple esophageal ulcers and a distal stricture; Reza's base-rate approach to ulcers (NSAIDs, H. pylori, alcohol, stress, then infection) comes up empty, and the biopsy is non-diagnostic. Months later the patient develops strikingly ulcerative, exudate-crusted lip lesions, and Reza reasons his way — despite having essentially no personal "reps" with this differential — toward an autoimmune blistering disease, correctly guessing the category but not the specific diagnosis. The biopsy confirms intraepithelial immune complex deposition, and serology confirms pemphigus vulgaris via anti-desmoglein-3 antibodies. The final twist: Robbie reveals the "two weeks" of dysphagia he'd stated was fabricated for teaching purposes — the real timeline was two years of isolated esophageal pemphigus vulgaris, a well-described but exceedingly rare presentation that predates the far more typical simultaneous oral-and-cutaneous disease.

## Pearls

- Break dysphagia down along two independent dimensions: tempo (how quickly it developed) and location (oropharyngeal vs. esophageal) — recognizing this is a spectrum, not a strict binary, but that framing the question this way is usually the most cognitively efficient way to make progress.
- Elicit the dysphagia history with a genuinely open-ended prompt ("tell me what exactly happens when you swallow") rather than a leading, multiple-choice-style question — this maximizes the authenticity and diagnostic yield of the patient's own description.
- Solids-worse-than-liquids dysphagia (especially progressing from solids to soft solids to liquids as it worsens) suggests a structural/obstructive process; dysphagia to both solids and liquids from the outset suggests a motility disorder — a useful but non-absolute heuristic.
- Before ordering anything, mentally pre-load your diagnostic armamentarium (modified barium swallow, EGD, and — in the minority of cases concerning for external compression — CT) so you're ready to act efficiently once the history and exam point you in a direction.
- Not all dysphagia arises from pathology within the esophageal lumen — extrinsic compression from outside the esophagus is an important, if less common, category to keep in mind.
- Choice between EGD and modified barium swallow as the first test is often more about practical access and turnaround time than a hard-and-fast rule (directly analogous to the CT-vs-ultrasound decision in right upper quadrant pain) — if endoscopy will be delayed, a barium study can efficiently localize the lesion in the meantime.
- When working up esophageal ulcers, generalize the problem first: apply a base-rate approach to ulcers as a category (anywhere in the body) rather than jumping straight to an esophagus-specific differential. The most common causes of ulcers broadly are NSAIDs, H. pylori, alcohol, and stress — work through these before escalating to less common causes.
- After the common causes of ulcers are addressed, broaden to infectious etiologies (HSV, CMV, and consider an HIV test/sexual history) and always try to get tissue (biopsy) sent for both routine and special stains/serologies, since a well-obtained biopsy can be the single most powerful tool for eventually cracking a cryptic case.
- Always screen the medication list specifically for agents known to cause pill esophagitis (doxycycline, potassium supplements, iron supplements) when esophageal ulcers are found without an obvious cause.
- Empiric PPI therapy for esophageal ulcers of unclear cause is reasonable, but should be prescribed with explicit acknowledgment of diagnostic uncertainty rather than as a presumed final answer — especially when the severity or the patient's age/demographic makes straightforward GERD an awkward fit.
- Approach any mucosal ulcer (esophageal, oral, or otherwise) with the same underlying framework used for skin ulcers: something is causing trauma to the mucosa, and that "something" can be external (mechanical irritation) or internal (acid, medications, alcohol, infection, or an autoimmune/inflammatory process).
- The number and distribution of oral ulcers changes the differential meaningfully: a single isolated ulcer suggests a simple aphthous ulcer; a few lesions raise HSV; widespread, severe, or systemically-associated oral ulceration should prompt a broader autoimmune workup (IBD/Crohn's, Behçet's, lupus, and less common infectious causes like Mycoplasma-associated mucocutaneous eruption).
- Crohn's disease has a bimodal age distribution — an initial peak in the second decade of life and a second peak in the sixth to seventh decade — worth remembering when considering IBD as a cause of oral or GI mucosal ulceration in an older patient.
- Lupus-associated oral ulcers are classically painless — pain (or its absence) is a genuinely useful discriminating feature to elicit when considering lupus in an oral ulcer differential.
- The most reliable way to crack a genuinely isolated (non-aphthous, non-HSV) oral ulcer case is often to look for the disease's broader systemic signature rather than trying to diagnose from the mouth alone — for example, genital ulcers plus oral ulcers plus pathergy points to Behçet's, while oral ulcers plus diarrhea points toward IBD.
- Distinguishing pemphigus vulgaris from bullous pemphigoid histologically and immunologically: pemphigus vulgaris shows intraepithelial acantholysis with immune complex/antibody deposition between epithelial cells (anti-desmoglein antibodies) with an intact basement membrane; bullous pemphigoid involves antibodies at the dermal-epidermal (basement membrane) junction, producing subepidermal blistering.
- Classic teaching: pemphigus vulgaris typically presents with both oral and cutaneous involvement together, while bullous pemphigoid classically presents with skin involvement alone — but isolated esophageal pemphigus vulgaris that predates oral involvement by a long interval is a rare but well-described phenomenon, and should be added to the differential for cryptic, hard-to-diagnose esophageal ulcers once common causes are excluded.
- Pemphigus vulgaris at an older age of onset can carry a paraneoplastic association and should prompt consideration of an underlying malignancy workup in the right clinical context.
- Applying a generalized framework (working from "ulcers, broadly" down to the specific mucosal site) rather than an organ-specific one from the very first encounter is what would most likely have shortened this patient's actual two-year diagnostic odyssey — a concrete argument for deliberately "zooming out" before zooming back in on a hard-to-diagnose lesion.

## Problem Representation

A 46-year-old, otherwise healthy man with progressive solid-food dysphagia is found on endoscopy to have multiple esophageal ulcers and a distal stricture of unclear cause; after an extensive but unrevealing standard ulcer workup, the eventual development of severe ulcerative lip lesions leads to a diagnosis of pemphigus vulgaris, ultimately revealed to have caused two years of isolated esophageal disease before oral involvement finally declared itself.

## Case Narrative

Robbie presented a case referred to him for chart review by the chief of the emergency department, Jodie Garber — Robbie never personally examined the patient, and deliberately narrated the case step by step to let Reza build his reasoning live. The patient (stated at the time as two weeks of symptoms, a detail later revealed to be altered for teaching purposes) presented with difficulty swallowing.

Reza framed dysphagia along two dimensions: tempo, and whether the problem is oropharyngeal or esophageal — emphasizing this is a spectrum rather than a strict binary, but that distinguishing between the two is usually the highest-yield initial move. He described eliciting the history with a deliberately open-ended prompt ("tell me what exactly happens when you swallow") to avoid leading the patient, and noted that pre-loading his diagnostic armamentarium (modified barium swallow, EGD, and CT for the minority of cases concerning for external compression) lets him move efficiently once red flags or a location come into focus.

Robbie relayed the history: the patient felt food getting stuck just below the sternal notch, had much greater difficulty with solids than liquids, and had progressed from solids to difficulty with soft solids like pureed food. He'd hoped it would improve on its own and delayed seeking care. Exam was entirely unremarkable — vitals, skin, oropharynx, cardiac, and lung exam all normal. A bedside water/ice-chip test (performed routinely, as Robbie does, though he wasn't certain Jodie had done the same) showed the patient tolerated water without difficulty but had significant trouble with ice chips.

Reza interpreted this as esophageal (not oropharyngeal) dysphagia, given the sternal-notch localization, and as more likely structural than a motility disorder, given the solids-first pattern (motility disorders classically affect solids and liquids together from the start). He framed the case as subacute, progressive esophageal dysphagia and outlined the structural differential — webs, rings, tumors, and ulcers within the lumen, plus external compression in a minority of cases — while explicitly not neglecting motility causes like achalasia. He recommended basic labs (CBC, albumin) to look for systemic clues, and framed the choice between EGD and modified barium swallow as often driven by practical access rather than a fixed rule, directly analogous to choosing between CT and ultrasound for right upper quadrant pain.

Labs (including albumin, renal function, and liver function) were normal. The patient was able to get an EGD quickly, which showed multiple esophageal ulcers throughout the esophagus and a dominant stricture in the distal esophagus; the ulcers were biopsied and the stricture was dilated, providing symptomatic improvement, and the patient was discharged.

Reza, recalling a prior RLR case (a gastric ulcer causing gastric outlet obstruction, presented by guest "Uncle Bob") reasoned the stricture was likely a consequence of the ulcers rather than the primary problem, and reframed the real question as what was causing the ulcers themselves. He deliberately generalized: rather than jumping to an esophagus-specific differential, he applied a base-rate approach to ulcers broadly, working through NSAIDs, H. pylori, alcohol, and stress as the most common causes, then broadening to less common infectious causes (HSV, CMV, and considering an HIV test with a sexual history). He recommended reviewing the medication list, taking an alcohol history, sending a stool H. pylori test, and following up the ulcer biopsy results.

The HIV test returned negative. The team acknowledged ongoing diagnostic uncertainty and reviewed the medication list specifically for agents known to cause pill esophagitis (doxycycline, potassium, iron) — none were present, consistent with the patient's overall good health. An empiric PPI was prescribed in case of underlying reflux esophagitis, with explicit acknowledgment that severe GERD would be an unusual explanation at age 46. The ulcer biopsy came back non-diagnostic — notable for something abnormal without a clear pattern the pathologist could name.

Time passed, and the patient developed new lip findings. Robbie shared a representative image (not an actual photo of this patient, as he clarified) showing pinpoint ulcerative lesions across the upper and lower lips interspersed with yellowish exudate, along with cheilitis at the corners of the mouth. Reza approached these mucosal lesions with the same underlying framework he'd use for skin ulcers — something is causing trauma to the mucosa, whether external (mechanical) or internal (acid, medication, alcohol, infection) — and, having already excluded the common causes, expanded into an autoimmune schema: inflammatory bowel disease/Crohn's (noting its bimodal age distribution, with peaks in the second and sixth-to-seventh decades), Behçet's disease (acknowledging the patient's demographic was an imperfect fit), and lupus (classically painless oral ulcers — Robbie confirmed this patient's lesions were painful, arguing against lupus). He also considered infectious causes like Mycoplasma-associated mucocutaneous eruption, while candidly acknowledging he was reasoning past the edge of his confidence.

The lip biopsy proved decisive: it showed fragmentation of the epithelium with immune complex deposition between epithelial cells (intraepithelial) and an intact basement membrane — a pattern of intraepithelial acantholysis. This gave dermatology strong diagnostic confidence, and they sent a confirmatory serologic test. Reza reasoned through the implications live: had the patient presented with skin blistering from the start, autoimmune blistering diseases (bullous pemphigoid, pemphigus vulgaris) would have been on the differential immediately; the absence of cutaneous involvement had made this connection harder to make initially. He correctly identified the category — an autoimmune blistering condition — and reasoned that the site of antibody deposition (intraepithelial vs. at the basement membrane) distinguishes pemphigus vulgaris from bullous pemphigoid. Weighing the patient's age and recalling teaching (credited to a former resident, John Woller, at Hopkins) that pemphigus vulgaris in this age group can carry a paraneoplastic association, Reza guessed bullous pemphigoid — incorrectly, as it turned out, in a lighthearted wager with Robbie.

The confirmatory test was an anti-desmoglein-3 antibody, confirming pemphigus vulgaris. Robbie noted that this same week's New England Journal of Medicine "Images in Clinical Medicine" (published by Steph Sherman) happened to feature a strikingly similar case of pemphigus vulgaris — the image Robbie had shown was, in fact, from that NEJM case, used as a representative picture rather than an actual photo of this patient.

Robbie then delivered the case's central teaching point and its twist. Pemphigus vulgaris classically presents with both oral and cutaneous lesions together, while bullous pemphigoid classically presents with skin involvement alone — but isolated esophageal pemphigus vulgaris that predates oral involvement is an exceedingly rare, though well-described, phenomenon. In this patient's actual history, the esophagus had been "on fire" with ulceration on the initial EGD while his mouth exam was entirely normal — striking enough that no endoscopist would ordinarily perform that EGD without commenting on a florid oral exam, yet none had, because there was nothing to comment on at that time. The true duration of the patient's dysphagia before diagnosis, Robbie revealed, was not two weeks but two years — a detail he had altered earlier in the case purely for teaching purposes, so as not to bias Reza's reasoning process. The patient had originally been evaluated elsewhere; once pemphigus vulgaris was finally diagnosed, his prior pathology slides were retrieved from that outside institution, re-stained, and retrospectively confirmed to show pemphigus vulgaris all along. His true clinical course was two years of isolated esophageal pemphigus vulgaris, followed by the eventual development of oral disease, with cutaneous involvement — the "classic" third leg of the presentation — not yet having occurred.

The episode closed with both hosts reflecting on how Reza's deliberate generalization — building a base-rate approach to "ulcers" broadly before narrowing to the esophagus specifically — is exactly the kind of reasoning move that, applied at the very first presentation two years earlier, could plausibly have shortened this patient's long diagnostic delay. Robbie candidly noted that oral/mucosal ulcers are genuinely difficult for him diagnostically given how infrequently any single clinician encounters the full range of causes, and offered the "systemic signature" strategy (genital ulcers + oral ulcers + pathergy for Behçet's; oral ulcers + diarrhea for IBD) as his best available approach — with isolated, cryptic esophageal or oral pemphigus vulgaris now added to that mental list going forward.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Impact |
|---|---|
| Sensation of food sticking below the sternal notch | Localizes the process to the esophagus rather than the oropharynx |
| Solids-predominant dysphagia progressing to soft solids | Favors a structural/obstructive process over a motility disorder |
| Water tolerated, ice chips poorly tolerated on bedside testing | Reinforces the impression of an esophageal, texture/bolus-dependent process rather than a global motility failure |
| Normal labs (CBC, albumin, renal, LFTs) | Lowers suspicion for a systemic/nutritional process driving the dysphagia; keeps focus localized to the esophagus |
| EGD: multiple esophageal ulcers + distal stricture | Reframes the diagnostic question from "why the stricture" to "why the ulcers," since the stricture is presumed secondary |
| HIV negative, no culprit medications (doxycycline/potassium/iron), stool H. pylori presumably negative, HSV/CMV negative on biopsy | Systematically excludes the most common ulcer etiologies, raising the relative probability of a rarer cause |
| Non-diagnostic initial esophageal ulcer biopsy | Leaves the case genuinely unresolved, motivating watchful waiting/empiric PPI rather than premature closure |
| New severe, exudative lip ulceration months later | Shifts the differential toward an autoimmune mucosal/blistering process (IBD, Behçet's, lupus, autoimmune blistering disease) |
| Painful (not painless) oral lesions | Lowers likelihood of lupus specifically as the cause |
| Lip biopsy: intraepithelial immune complex deposition with intact basement membrane | Distinguishes pemphigus vulgaris (intraepithelial) from bullous pemphigoid (subepidermal/basement membrane) |
| Anti-desmoglein-3 antibody positive | Confirms pemphigus vulgaris definitively |
| Retrospective re-staining of outside pathology slides from two years prior | Confirms the esophageal disease had been pemphigus vulgaris from the very beginning, despite two years of non-diagnostic workup |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Deliberate generalization as a diagnostic strategy**: Reza's explicit move from an esophagus-specific ulcer differential to a general "ulcers, broadly" framework (NSAIDs/H. pylori/alcohol/stress, then infection, then autoimmune) is presented as a transferable habit, not case-specific luck.
- **Absence of an expected co-finding used as an active discriminator**: the lack of cutaneous involvement initially made an autoimmune blistering disease harder to consider, even though it was ultimately correct — a reminder that classic "both X and Y" illness scripts can mislead when a case presents atypically with only one component.
- **Honest modeling of diagnostic uncertainty in real time**: Reza explicitly says "I'm just babbling because I have no clue" while working through the oral ulcer differential — modeling that visible uncertainty during a difficult differential is a legitimate and useful part of the reasoning process, not a failure of it.
- **Teaching-motivated narrative alteration disclosed after the fact**: Robbie's deliberate use of "two weeks" instead of the true two-year timeline, revealed only once the diagnosis was reached, functioned to prevent Reza's reasoning from being anchored by an unusually long, alarm-provoking duration — an explicit case-presentation technique worth noting for future case narrations.
- **Recognizing that a category-correct guess (autoimmune blistering disease) with an incorrect specific diagnosis (bullous pemphigoid instead of pemphigus vulgaris) is still meaningful diagnostic progress**, since the workup pathway (biopsy, confirmatory serology) was already correctly triggered regardless of which specific autoimmune blistering disease was guessed.

## Teaching Discussion

This episode's central lesson is about the diagnostic power of generalization: rather than treating "esophageal ulcers" as a self-contained problem, Reza's habit of stepping back to a broader base-rate framework for ulcers anywhere in the body — and later, for mucosal ulcers anywhere in the body — is shown to be the reasoning move most likely to have shortened this patient's genuine two-year diagnostic delay. The case also offers a clean teaching contrast between pemphigus vulgaris and bullous pemphigoid (intraepithelial vs. basement-membrane antibody deposition) and introduces isolated esophageal pemphigus vulgaris predating oral disease as a rare but well-described entity worth keeping on the differential for cryptic esophageal ulcers once the common causes are exhausted.
