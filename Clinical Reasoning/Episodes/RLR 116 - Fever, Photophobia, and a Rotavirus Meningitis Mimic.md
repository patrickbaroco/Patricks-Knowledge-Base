## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A teaching opener on fever versus hyperthermia sets up a case that seems to confirm every reflex toward bacterial meningitis: a 43-year-old man with three days of severe photophobia, headache, and neck discomfort, tachycardic to 160, otherwise unremarkable. The empiric bacterial-meningitis pathway (blood cultures, steroids and antibiotics before imaging, CT before LP given concern for the optic nerve) is executed correctly — and the lumbar puncture comes back completely normal. An MRI is also normal. The answer turns out to be sitting in a stool sample sent almost by coincidence, moments after the patient interrupted his own LP to ask for a bedpan: rotavirus, with a rare but well-described CNS syndrome that mimics meningitis without producing CSF pleocytosis. The case closes on the idea that the correctness of an empiric approach should be judged by the soundness of the reasoning that produced it, not by whether the final diagnosis matches the reflexive first impression.

## Pearls

- Fever and hyperthermia are physiologically distinct: fever is a rise in the hypothalamic temperature set point (usually driven by an inflammatory stimulus), while hyperthermia is a failure to thermoregulate around a normal, unchanged set point (e.g., an environment like a sauna or steam room that overwhelms the body's ability to dissipate heat) — the body is actively trying to raise its temperature in fever, and actively trying to lower it in hyperthermia.
- A fever that responds to antipyretics (Tylenol, ibuprofen) is confirmatory evidence of a true elevated set point, since these drugs work by lowering the hypothalamic set point itself; a hyperthermic patient's temperature will not respond to antipyretics, because the set point was never elevated in the first place.
- Liebermeister's rule: for every 1°C rise in temperature above baseline, expect the heart rate to rise by roughly 8 beats per minute; tachycardia well in excess of what fever alone would predict should raise concern for an independent driver of sympathetic tone, such as a stimulant (cocaine, methamphetamine), for which supporting clues include hypokalemia (catecholamine-driven intracellular potassium shift) and an elevated lactate.
- Fever plus headache should immediately raise concern for CNS inflammation; adding photophobia sharply increases suspicion specifically for meningitis (inflammation of the meninges, producing neck stiffness and headache) as opposed to encephalitis (inflammation of the brain parenchyma itself, producing confusion and seizure) — the two exist on a spectrum and can evolve from one into the other if treatment is delayed.
- Illness duration helps calibrate the urgency and specificity of empiric coverage for suspected CNS infection: an acute (days-long) presentation like this one is "fair game" for bacterial meningitis, given its high mortality with any delay in treatment, whereas a much longer (e.g., 10-day) course would make bacterial meningitis far less likely and shift the differential toward more indolent processes.
- The correct sequence for suspected bacterial meningitis is: obtain blood cultures, then start empiric antibiotics (plus steroids, ideally before or with the first antibiotic dose, for pneumococcal meningitis) without waiting for imaging or the LP — deciding whether a CT scan is needed before the LP (to exclude elevated intracranial pressure and reduce herniation risk) should not delay antimicrobial administration.
- CT before LP is reserved for patients with a focal neurologic deficit or significant alteration in mental status; photophobia alone is not a focal neurologic deficit, but can still be a reasonable independent trigger to obtain a CT first if there is concern for a process affecting the optic nerve or for elevated intracranial pressure (e.g., papilledema).
- Empiric coverage for suspected bacterial meningitis in this age group: ceftriaxone and vancomycin (chosen for blood-brain-barrier penetration and coverage of resistant pneumococcus) plus acyclovir (HSV can cause meningitis, and can progress to meningoencephalitis if treatment is delayed) — ampicillin (for Listeria coverage) is added only above an age cutoff, discussed in this episode as roughly the mid-50s, and was withheld here given the patient's age of 43.
- A lack of leukocytosis in a patient with a strong clinical picture for bacterial meningitis is a genuinely discordant data point, but on its own is not sufficient to withhold empiric antibiotics or delay the LP — the cost of missing bacterial meningitis is too high to let a single reassuring lab value override a compelling clinical presentation.
- An LP obtained during a workup for one syndrome does not preclude sending other specimens in parallel if a new detail (like an urgent need to defecate, with a reported preceding several days of loose stools) surfaces incidentally during the same encounter — an easily overlooked opportunistic specimen ended up carrying this case's actual answer.
- A completely normal LP (clear fluid, "champagne tap," no red or white cells, normal glucose/protein/opening pressure) in a patient with a strong clinical syndrome for meningitis should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis rather than distrust of the test — CSF analysis is a highly sensitive tool for meningeal inflammation, and a truly normal result argues meaningfully against it (assuming a competent immune system).
- Rotavirus, though best known as a cause of pediatric gastroenteritis, has rare but well-described central nervous system manifestations (headache, photophobia, meningismus-like presentation) that can occur without CSF pleocytosis — a notable exception to the more classically taught GI-to-CNS neurotropic link, which is usually attributed to enterovirus.
- A plausible, well-reasoned hypothesis generated mid-case (here, a proposed "Salmonella gastroenteritis with neurologic, MRI-negative CNS involvement" syndrome in a traveler with diarrhea) can still be wrong — good clinical reasoning is judged by the quality of the process that generated the hypothesis, not by whether that specific hypothesis turns out to be correct.
- Isolated bacterial meningitis without any extra-meningeal focus of infection is exceedingly uncommon; most patients presenting with a meningitis-like syndrome will, on careful evaluation, have (or be found to have) an identifiable source or accompanying process elsewhere.
- The correctness of an empiric treatment decision should be judged by the soundness of the reasoning and probability assessment that produced it at the time, not retrospectively by the final diagnosis — treating this patient empirically for bacterial meningitis was the right decision regardless of the fact that the ultimate cause was a self-limited viral illness with an unusual CNS presentation.

## Problem Representation

A 43-year-old man with three days of severe photophobia, headache, and neck discomfort without other localizing symptoms, sinus tachycardic to 160 that resolves with fluids and antipyretics, treated empirically for bacterial meningitis pending workup, ultimately found to have a normal lumbar puncture and MRI and a positive stool culture for rotavirus — representing a rare, non-pleocytic rotaviral CNS syndrome rather than bacterial or herpetic meningitis.

## Case Narrative

Before presenting the case, Reza used a physiology teaching aliquot to distinguish fever from hyperthermia. Fever reflects a genuine upward shift in the hypothalamic temperature set point, usually in response to an inflammatory stimulus, with the body actively working to reach that new, higher set point. Hyperthermia, by contrast, involves no change in the set point at all — it is a failure to thermoregulate around a normal target, as when a person cannot adequately dissipate heat in an environment like a sauna or steam room. History is often the most useful discriminator between the two, but the heart rate offers another clue: because fever raises sympathetic tone, Liebermeister's rule predicts roughly an 8 beats-per-minute rise in heart rate for every 1°C of temperature elevation. A heart rate substantially higher than this rule would predict should prompt consideration of an independent driver of sympathetic tone, such as a stimulant intoxication, for which hypokalemia (from catecholamine-driven intracellular potassium shifting) and an elevated lactate would be supporting clues.

Rabih then presented a 43-year-old man, evaluated at the San Diego VA while on a family trip to San Francisco, with three days of severe photophobia, headache, and neck discomfort, and no cough, shortness of breath, abdominal pain, dysuria, nausea, or diarrhea. He took no daily medications. On exam he was slumped in a near-fetal position, severely uncomfortable, with photophobia but no conjunctival injection, and was able to range his neck reasonably well despite holding his head in obvious pain. His only abnormal vital sign was a sinus tachycardia to 160, which resolved along with his fever after two liters of fluid and Tylenol. His CBC, renal panel, LFTs, and urinalysis were all normal, and blood cultures were pending.

Reza reacted to the combination of fever, headache, and photophobia as an immediate, near-reflexive trigger for CNS inflammation, specifically meningitis rather than encephalitis, given the absence of confusion or seizure. He distinguished the two conceptually — meningitis as inflammation of the meningeal layers (explaining the neck stiffness and headache), encephalitis as inflammation of the brain parenchyma itself (explaining altered mentation) — while noting they exist on a spectrum and one can evolve into the other. The three-day time course made bacterial meningitis "fair game" given the mortality cost of delayed treatment; a substantially longer course would have made him far less concerned about a bacterial process. He flagged the fever's response to Tylenol as itself informative, arguing it confirmed a true elevated set point (true fever) rather than hyperthermia, since antipyretics only work by lowering the hypothalamic target.

With bacterial meningitis activated as the leading concern, Reza laid out an explicit sequence: obtain blood cultures, then start empiric treatment — dexamethasone (ideally before or with the first antibiotic dose, standard of care for pneumococcal meningitis), vancomycin and ceftriaxone (chosen for blood-brain-barrier penetration), and acyclovir (to cover HSV, which can cause meningitis and progress to meningoencephalitis if treatment is delayed) — without waiting for a CT scan or the LP itself. He reasoned through the CT-before-LP decision: it exists to protect the small subset of patients with elevated intracranial pressure at risk of herniation with an LP, and is generally reserved for patients with a focal neurologic deficit or significant mental status alteration. Photophobia itself, he clarified, is not a focal neurologic deficit, but he still favored obtaining a CT first in this patient out of concern that the photophobia could reflect a process affecting the optic nerve or elevated intracranial pressure with early papilledema. He also raised HIV testing to inform the differential and coverage, and specifically discussed the age cutoff for adding ampicillin (Listeria coverage), initially uncertain whether it was 50 or a bit higher. One data point gave him pause: the patient's basic labs showed no leukocytosis, which he found genuinely discordant with his working diagnosis of bacterial meningitis — but not discordant enough to change his plan, given the cost of missing the diagnosis.

Reza's plan was executed essentially as described: dexamethasone, vancomycin, ceftriaxone, and acyclovir were given (ampicillin was withheld, with the age cutoff for Listeria coverage in this discussion settled around the mid-50s, above this patient's age of 43); given the patient's age and lack of any focal deficit, no CT was obtained before proceeding directly to LP. As the LP was being set up, the patient interrupted to say he urgently needed to defecate, and on questioning reported four to five loose, foul-smelling bowel movements over the preceding three to four days that had not otherwise come up in his history. A stool sample was sent to the lab essentially as an afterthought, in parallel with the LP.

The LP itself was unremarkable: a clear, atraumatic "champagne tap," no red or white cells, and normal glucose, protein, and opening pressure. Both hosts found this genuinely surprising given how strong the clinical picture had seemed, and reasoned that — assuming an immunocompetent patient capable of mounting some CSF response — a truly normal LP argued meaningfully against active meningeal inflammation, prompting an MRI brain with and without contrast to look further. Reza predicted the MRI would not show leptomeningeal enhancement, given the absence of any CSF evidence of an active meningeal process, but specifically flagged concern for possible optic nerve enhancement given the prominence of the photophobia. The MRI came back completely normal, including the optic nerve, and the patient's HIV test also returned negative.

The diagnostic turn came from the stool sample sent almost as an afterthought during the LP. Reasoning through how a GI process could connect to a CNS syndrome, Reza worked through acute infectious diarrhea's usual causes — viral (norovirus, rotavirus, and others, often never specifically identified) and bacterial (E. coli species, C. diff, Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter) — and, in a traveler with diarrhea and a CNS syndrome, proposed Salmonella (specifically S. typhi, capable of disseminating to the gallbladder, liver, bone, and skin) as his leading hypothesis, describing it as a "classic Salmonella gastroenteritis neurologic MRI-negative syndrome." The stool culture instead grew rotavirus — a result Rabih initially assumed was a lab error until confirming that rotavirus, though best known as a pediatric GI pathogen, has rare, well-described case reports of CNS manifestations, including headache and photophobia, occurring without a corresponding CSF pleocytosis.

Antibiotics were discontinued, and the patient was admitted for observation to ensure nothing further was being missed. Once he improved and was afebrile, he described a history of migraines that had never previously been formally diagnosed or brought to medical attention, and he was treated empirically for migraine as well — leaving some genuine uncertainty about how much of his residual headache reflected the rotaviral illness itself, an unmasked or triggered migraine, or both.

## Reasoning Breakdown

### Bayesian Updates

| Finding | Diagnostic Implication |
|---|---|
| Fever, headache, and photophobia together, acute (3-day) tempo | Strongly raises probability of CNS inflammation, specifically bacterial meningitis, warranting empiric treatment before confirmatory testing |
| Fever normalizes with Tylenol | Supports true fever (elevated hypothalamic set point) over hyperthermia |
| Tachycardia to 160, well above what Liebermeister's rule predicts for the degree of fever | Prompts consideration of an independent driver of sympathetic tone (though not pursued further once fever/tachycardia improved with fluids and antipyretics) |
| No leukocytosis on initial CBC | Discordant with the working diagnosis of bacterial meningitis, but insufficient alone to withhold empiric antibiotics |
| No focal neurologic deficit, young/otherwise healthy patient | Supports proceeding directly to LP without a preceding CT |
| LP completely normal (no pleocytosis, normal glucose/protein/opening pressure) | Substantially lowers probability of active bacterial (or typical viral) meningitis |
| MRI brain with/without contrast completely normal, including optic nerve | Further lowers probability of an active CNS inflammatory or structural process |
| Incidentally elicited 3-4 day history of loose, foul-smelling stools | Reframes the case toward a systemic/GI process with possible extra-CNS manifestations |
| Stool culture positive for rotavirus | Identifies rotavirus, via a rare non-pleocytic CNS syndrome, as the most probable explanation for the presentation |

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Bacterial meningitis illness script: fever, headache, and photophobia in a patient with an acute (days-long) tempo should trigger empiric treatment (blood cultures, steroids and antibiotics, HSV coverage, and CT-before-LP only if there is a focal deficit or altered mentation) before diagnostic confirmation, because the cost of a delayed diagnosis is severe — this case shows the illness script correctly triggering appropriate empiric action even though the final diagnosis proved to be something else entirely, reinforcing that empiric decisions should be judged by the reasoning that produced them, not by the eventual answer.

A secondary bias theme is the value of not dismissing incidental data: a symptom volunteered almost apologetically mid-procedure (needing to defecate) led to a specimen that solved the case, illustrating that opportunistic sampling and attentiveness to tangential complaints can outperform a narrowly scoped, hypothesis-driven workup. Reza's mid-case Salmonella hypothesis — logical, well-reasoned, and ultimately wrong — is presented as a model of confidently generating and then abandoning a hypothesis without defensiveness once new data (the actual culture result) arrived.

## Teaching Discussion

The central teaching point is the distinction between fever and hyperthermia, and the practical tools (antipyretic responsiveness, Liebermeister's rule) for telling them apart at the bedside — a foundational physiology concept the hosts felt was underemphasized relative to how often it comes up in practice.

A second teaching point is the disciplined empiric-treatment sequence for suspected bacterial meningitis: blood cultures, then antibiotics and steroids, with the CT-before-LP decision made independently and without delaying antimicrobial therapy — including the clarification that photophobia alone does not meet criteria for a focal neurologic deficit, even though it can still be a reasonable independent trigger for imaging.

A third teaching point is the rare but real capacity of rotavirus to produce a meningitis-mimicking CNS syndrome without CSF pleocytosis, positioned as a notable exception to the more classically taught enterovirus GI-to-CNS neurotropic link.

The closing and arguably most important teaching point is philosophical: the empiric approach to this patient was correct regardless of the final diagnosis, because it was grounded in sound probabilistic reasoning given the information available at the time — reinforcing the mentorship principle, attributed to a colleague ("Grappreet"), that isolated meningitis without any extra-meningeal focus is almost never found, and that good clinical reasoning is about the journey and the discipline of the process, not merely about landing on the correct final label.
