## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> Two men, both 60, both presenting with abdominal distension and vomiting, become a deliberately paired teaching exercise in bowel obstruction. The first has an incarcerated umbilical hernia causing a small bowel obstruction — reduced, almost unbelievably, at the bedside by a skilled surgeon overnight. The second has a large bowel obstruction with no identifiable cause on colonoscopy, decompressed with a simple rectal tube after a hyperacute, severely painful presentation that defies the usual gradual tempo of bowel obstruction. Rabih narrates both cases live from CT images, teaching air-fluid-level mechanics, hernia reducibility assessment, acid-base interpretation, and the VIPO framework for abdominal emergencies along the way — while modeling, in real time, how differently small and large bowel obstructions present, get worked up, and get managed.

## Pearls

- Finding both abdominal distension and a palpable hernia on exam does not automatically mean the hernia is the source of the obstruction — an alternative, easily overlooked sequence is that a separate space-occupying process (such as ascites) has raised intra-abdominal pressure and pushed a previously subclinical hernia into prominence, only for it to recede once the underlying process resolves.
- A hernia's reducibility and consistency provide bedside clues to whether it is the primary problem: an incarcerated (irreducible) hernia resists gentle manual pressure, while a soft, reducible hernia lacks the firmness and venous engorgement characteristic of incarceration or strangulation — borrowing the same firm-versus-soft heuristic used to distinguish malignant from benign masses.
- Bedside reduction of an incarcerated hernia can be attempted with analgesia (to relax the abdominal musculature) and reverse Trendelenburg positioning (head down, using gravity to draw abdominal contents away from the hernia defect) before committing to advanced imaging, if the hernia is judged likely to be the actual source of the obstruction.
- Dark urine is often simply concentrated urine, but should still prompt consideration of direct hyperbilirubinemia and scleral icterus, which can be genuinely difficult to detect on exam — an overlooked hepatic process could, in principle, drive ascites that secondarily displaces bowel into an umbilical defect, changing the causal sequence of an abdominal exam finding.
- When interpreting a near-normal pH with multiple competing acid-base abnormalities (e.g., an elevated PCO2, an elevated bicarbonate, and an elevated lactate), reasoning through which abnormality is the "driver" versus which are compensatory or camouflaging requires tracking how many processes are pulling the pH in each direction — if it takes two acidotic processes together to nearly counterbalance one alkalotic process, the alkalotic process is very likely the primary driver.
- Vomiting classically produces a primary metabolic alkalosis (from loss of gastric acid) with compensatory respiratory acidosis (hypoventilation); a superimposed lactic acidosis (e.g., from hypoperfusion or early bowel ischemia) can "camouflage" this classic picture by pulling the pH back toward the acidotic side, without being the primary process.
- Air-fluid levels on imaging are a strong, near-diagnostic marker of bowel obstruction: normally, fluid secreted into the bowel keeps moving and gets reabsorbed, so it does not pool; a mechanically obstructed and dysfunctional segment of bowel fails to keep that fluid moving, causing it to pool and layer against retained gas — the same principle by which nasogastric tube output signals either bleeding or obstruction.
- On cross-sectional imaging, small bowel obstruction is characteristically identified by centrally located, dilated loops, while large bowel obstruction is characteristically identified by dilated loops at the periphery of the abdomen — a simple but useful heuristic for quickly orienting to which segment is involved.
- In a patient without prior abdominal surgery, a hernia should be considered the leading cause of small bowel obstruction — removing the (typically dominant) adhesive-disease population from consideration meaningfully raises hernia's relative probability, since adhesive disease is by far the most common cause of SBO only in the aggregate population that includes previously operated patients.
- Some hernias (femoral, obturator, and other pelvic hernias) can be genuinely difficult or impossible to detect on physical exam despite being the cause of an obstruction — a sufficiently high pretest probability for a hernia (e.g., an unoperated abdomen with distension and vomiting) justifies specifically directing the radiologist to scrutinize these subtle sites, even without a palpable bulge on exam.
- The VIPO mnemonic (vascular, inflammation, perforation, obstruction) is a useful framework for organizing abdominal emergencies; when a presentation that otherwise fits an obvious diagnosis (like adhesive small or large bowel obstruction) includes atypical features — specifically, hyperacute onset and disproportionately severe pain — this should prompt reconsideration of the more dangerous vascular and perforation categories rather than anchoring on the more common, better-fitting diagnosis.
- Acute mesenteric ischemia can itself cause abdominal distension by inducing an ileus (loss of bowel peristalsis from energy failure of the bowel wall), which can mimic mechanical bowel obstruction on presentation — the key discriminating feature is the hyperacute tempo of ischemia-induced ileus compared with the many more gradual and common causes of ileus.
- Explicitly telling a radiologist that mesenteric ischemia is a specific concern meaningfully increases the likelihood that subtle imaging findings of ischemia will actually be identified — a documented example of how communicating a specific pretest concern directly to a radiologist changes diagnostic yield.
- The most common cause of large bowel obstruction is malignancy, followed by sigmoid volvulus; distinguishing between these on CT involves looking for an endoluminal mass (malignancy) versus a twisted mesenteric configuration ("whirl" pattern, volvulus) — sigmoid volvulus is a high-priority diagnosis to actively rule in or out because its first-line treatment (endoscopic detorsion via GI) differs substantially from the surgical management most other large bowel obstruction causes require.
- Gallstone-induced large bowel obstruction is a rare but real entity requiring a cholecysto-colonic fistula (since the ileocecal valve normally protects the colon from a large gallstone reaching it from the small bowel) — worth specifically flagging to a radiologist in a patient with a relevant surgical history, even when it remains a low-probability explanation.
- Large bowel obstructions are far more likely than small bowel obstructions to require definitive surgical management, since the probability of a mechanically obstructed segment spontaneously resolving decreases substantially as the diameter of the obstructed structure increases.
- Universal supportive care for both small and large bowel obstruction includes aggressive IV fluid resuscitation (given substantial fluid sequestration in the obstructed bowel) and decompression, ideally as proximal to the obstruction as possible — nasogastric tube efficacy is inversely related to its distance from the obstruction, making it most effective for gastric outlet obstruction and progressively less useful the more distal the obstruction is.
- In practice, nearly all small bowel obstruction patients receive an NG tube on diagnosis, while large bowel obstruction patients often do not, both because NG decompression is less effective for a distal obstruction and because these patients are frequently taken more directly to definitive surgical or endoscopic management, where an NG tube (if still needed) can be placed under sedation for greater patient comfort.
- Even a confident, well-supported working hypothesis (in this case, a low-probability but conceptually interesting rare diagnosis) can become disproportionately appealing simply because it is a compelling story — actively noticing and naming this pull is a useful check against over-attachment to an elegant but under-supported hypothesis.

## Problem Representation

Two sequential cases of 60-year-old men presenting with abdominal distension and vomiting: one, with a history of cocaine use and prior asymptomatic umbilical hernia, found to have a small bowel obstruction from an incarcerated umbilical hernia successfully reduced at the bedside; the other, with alcohol use disorder and a prior subtotal cholecystectomy, presenting with hyperacute, severe, belt-like pain and found to have a large bowel obstruction of unclear etiology (no mass, no volvulus on colonoscopy) successfully decompressed with a rectal tube.

## Case Narrative

**Case 1.** Reza presented a 60-year-old man with hypertension, cocaine use disorder (last use four days prior), and a significant smoking history, brought in by ambulance with four days of intractable vomiting, abdominal pain, and distension — a large volume of non-bloody vomitus was documented in the chart. He had a loose bowel movement two days prior and reported darker urine. Vitals were normal; exam showed a distended, tympanic (hyper-resonant) abdomen with an umbilical hernia visibly containing protruding content, with otherwise clear lungs and an unremarkable remaining exam.

Rabih immediately flagged the risk of connecting the two exam findings too quickly: while it is tempting to conclude the umbilical hernia is directly causing the obstruction, an alternative sequence is equally plausible — a separate process causing abdominal distension (as classically seen with cirrhotic ascites) can push a previously subclinical hernia into visible prominence, with the hernia receding once the underlying process resolves. He emphasized taking the exam to a more surgical level of nuance: is the hernia reducible, or incarcerated? Given the severity of the presentation, he recommended parallel, rapid evaluation: an EKG (to avoid missing an acute cardiac process), a bladder scan (to exclude urinary retention as a distension mimic), labs including a point-of-care creatinine, and prompt CT — unless bedside assessment suggested the hernia itself was the obstruction's true source and reducible, in which case he would attempt reduction first (analgesia to relax the abdominal wall, reverse Trendelenburg positioning to use gravity, and manual reduction) before committing to imaging.

Asked how to make this distinction at the bedside, Rabih explained that an incarcerated hernia resists gentle attempts at manual reduction, and lacks the softness of a simple reducible hernia — instead feeling firm, with venous engorgement, analogous to the firm-versus-soft heuristic used to distinguish malignant from benign masses. Prompted further on the mechanism behind a purely "pressure"-driven hernia, Rabih connected the patient's dark urine to a possible (if unlikely, given how commonly dark urine simply reflects concentration) missed direct hyperbilirubinemia and underlying ascites — raising the key diagnostic question of whether the abdominal distension was caused by bowel trapped and growing within the umbilical defect itself, or by a separate space-occupying process (like ascites) that had simply displaced a piece of otherwise-reducible bowel into the umbilicus.

An EKG was normal sinus rhythm; a bladder scan showed only 200cc of urine, excluding retention. Labs returned before CT: hemoglobin 19 (marked hemoconcentration), normal platelets and white count, creatinine 1.7 (baseline 1.3), bicarbonate 31, anion gap 15, AST 62 and ALT 98 (mild transaminitis), and a VBG lactate of 2.9 with pH 7.37 and PCO2 56.

Rabih walked through the acid-base picture in detail: with a pH of 7.37 (technically normal), the analytic question was whether this reflected an absence of problems or multiple competing problems balancing out. The elevated PCO2 alone would predict a respiratory acidosis pulling pH down, while the elevated bicarbonate reflected a metabolic alkalosis pulling pH up; a third abnormality, a mild lactic acidosis, complicated this picture further. Reasoning that it took two acidotic processes (respiratory acidosis and lactic acidosis) together to nearly counterbalance the single alkalotic process (metabolic alkalosis), he concluded the metabolic alkalosis was the primary driver — consistent with a straightforward, physiologically expected picture of vomiting-induced metabolic alkalosis with compensatory respiratory acidosis, camouflaged by superimposed lactic acidosis from hypoperfusion, further supported by the absence of any obstructive lung disease (from cocaine-related bronchospasm, OSA, or obesity hypoventilation) that might otherwise explain the PCO2 elevation. Both hosts noted, with some self-deprecating humor, that this granular real-time analysis was of limited practical consequence — the actionable step being simply to give the patient fluids.

Rabih then interpreted the CT scan live: no pneumoperitoneum; dilated loops of bowel with air-fluid levels, a near-diagnostic finding for bowel obstruction; centrally located loops (as opposed to peripheral), consistent with small bowel involvement; and, scrutinizing the abdominal wall specifically (his preferred strategy for hernia detection, since he described himself as less confident distinguishing adhesive disease from malignancy on imaging alone), small bowel entering the abdominal wall at the umbilicus. The absence of any other space-occupying process (no mass, no ascites) supported the hernia as the causative lesion, and zooming in on the hernia itself showed edematous, hyperemic bowel wall with air-fluid levels within the sac — findings consistent with strangulation/incarceration. Based on his personal track record (zero successful bedside reductions out of three or four prior CT-confirmed cases like this), he predicted the patient would require operative reduction.

Contrary to that prediction, general surgery was consulted with an initial plan of NG tube, IV fluids, and antiemetics with possible surgery — but the surgeon successfully reduced the hernia at the bedside, and by the next morning the obstruction had resolved: abdominal pain resolved, the patient tolerated clear liquids, and was passing gas. He was scheduled for elective umbilical hernia repair two weeks later, framed by Reza as analogous to an incidentally found gallstone — not addressed until it causes a problem, at which point definitive repair becomes appropriate. Rabih explained the mechanism behind air-fluid levels: bowel secretes fluid that normally continues moving and gets reabsorbed; a dysfunctional, obstructed segment fails to keep that fluid moving, causing it to pool and layer against gas — the same physiologic principle by which fluid return through an NG tube signals either obstruction or bleeding.

Reflecting on the broader teaching point of the case, Rabih emphasized that in an unoperated abdomen (no prior abdominal surgery), a hernia should be considered the leading cause of small bowel obstruction — removing the large population of previously operated, adhesion-prone patients from the denominator substantially raises hernia's relative probability. He specifically flagged that femoral, obturator, and other pelvic hernias can be genuinely difficult or impossible to palpate on exam, meaning a sufficiently high pretest probability (an unoperated abdomen with distension and vomiting) justifies directing the radiologist to specifically scrutinize these hard-to-detect sites regardless of exam findings.

**Case 2.** Reza then presented a second 60-year-old man, this one with alcohol use disorder, presenting with 13 hours of acute-onset abdominal pain, distension, nausea, and vomiting — he could precisely time the onset (4pm) and characterized the pain as sharp, 7 out of 10, in a belt-like distribution across his lower abdomen. His last bowel movement, the day prior, was also loose. His surgical history included a subtotal cholecystectomy years earlier, leaving a gallbladder remnant (Rabih looked up the indications for a subtotal versus total cholecystectomy: severe local inflammation with high injury risk to surrounding structures, intraoperative patient decompensation requiring rapid closure, or a fistula). Vitals were normal; his abdomen was distended, hyper-resonant to percussion, and tender in all four quadrants.

Rabih organized his approach using the VIPO mnemonic (vascular, inflammation, perforation, obstruction) for abdominal emergencies. Obstruction, given the distension and history of prior abdominal surgery (risk for adhesive disease), was the leading candidate — but he flagged two important cautionary features that didn't fit the typical picture: most bowel obstructions build gradually rather than presenting abruptly (an adhesive band typically progresses to complete obstruction rather than blocking suddenly), and pain in a typical bowel obstruction tends to be comparatively mild and slowly building, unlike the case's earlier lactate elevation from strangulation-related ischemia. This patient's hyperacute onset and disproportionately severe pain raised specific concern for the more dangerous VIPO categories: acute mesenteric ischemia (vascular) and perforation. He explained that mesenteric ischemia can itself cause abdominal distension through an early ileus phase (loss of bowel peristalsis from bowel wall energy failure, producing a flaccid, dilated bowel that can mimic mechanical obstruction), with the hyperacute tempo of this specific type of ileus serving as the key distinguishing feature from the many more common, gradually developing causes of ileus. Given this concern, he recommended explicitly telling the radiologist that mesenteric ischemia was a specific worry — citing evidence that doing so meaningfully increases (he cited roughly 25%) the likelihood the radiologist successfully identifies it, since it is otherwise commonly missed.

Labs (CBC, BMP, LFTs) were normal. The CT scan showed a massively dilated stomach and dilated bowel loops with air-fluid levels — this time located peripherally rather than centrally, indicating large bowel involvement. Rabih worked through the large bowel obstruction differential in probability order: malignancy (most common cause; he looked specifically for an endoluminal mass and saw none), sigmoid volvulus (second most common; he looked for mesenteric twisting and did not see a configuration consistent with volvulus), and — given the patient's unusual surgical history — the rare possibility of gallstone-induced large bowel obstruction (requiring a massive gallstone and a cholecysto-colonic fistula, since the ileocecal valve normally protects the colon from stones migrating from the small bowel), which he flagged for the radiologist to specifically consider despite its low probability. He reasoned that the hyperacute tempo made malignancy comparatively less likely (absent additional history suggesting a more chronic process, such as fatigue, iron deficiency anemia, or a change in stool caliber), and that ruling sigmoid volvulus in or out was the single most actionable next step, since its first-line treatment (endoscopic detorsion by GI) differs substantially from the surgical management most other large bowel obstruction causes require. He also taught that large bowel obstructions are far more likely than small bowel obstructions to ultimately require surgery, reasoning that the probability of spontaneous resolution decreases as the diameter of the obstructed structure increases.

Discussing supportive management, Rabih described universal principles for both SBO and LBO: aggressive IV crystalloid resuscitation (given substantial fluid loss into the obstructed bowel) and decompression, ideally as proximal to the obstruction as possible. He explained that NG tube efficacy is inversely related to its distance from the obstruction — maximally effective for gastric outlet obstruction, and progressively less useful the more distal the obstruction lies. In practice, he places an NG tube in nearly every small bowel obstruction patient at the time of diagnosis, but often withholds it in large bowel obstruction, both because it is less effective that distally and because these patients are typically taken more directly to definitive (often operative) management, where an NG tube — if still needed — can be placed under sedation for greater comfort. He invited comparison of the two cases' CT stomach appearances: markedly dilated in Case 1 versus compressed/collapsed in Case 2, illustrating how much decompression potential existed relative to each obstruction's location.

The patient was admitted to general surgery, but once further history revealed roughly 70 pounds of unintentional weight loss over the preceding year, a STAT GI consult was obtained for colonoscopy rather than proceeding directly to the operating room. Endoscopy found no mass and no volvulus at the point of obstruction; the bowel was decompressed endoscopically (analogous to the proximal-venting principle discussed for NG tubes, but from below), and a rectal tube was left in place. Given suboptimal bowel prep, a repeat colonoscopy was planned for several weeks later to ensure nothing was missed. Imaging before and after rectal tube placement showed dramatic decompression, and no NG tube was ever required.

Rabih reflected on the case's unresolved etiology, openly wondering what had actually caused the large bowel obstruction, since no mass or volvulus was found. He raised the possibility of atypical adhesive disease (despite the patient's surgical history being limited to a gallbladder procedure rather than direct bowel surgery) and admitted he remained drawn to his earlier gallstone-fistula hypothesis specifically because it made for an interesting, memorable case — explicitly naming this as a bias worth being aware of, given how low its actual pretest probability was without confirmed fistula on imaging. He also noted that, in his experience, large bowel obstruction patients typically describe a gradual, insidious symptom evolution rather than a hyperacute onset like this patient's — making it notable when Reza revealed that the surgical team's own documentation characterized this presentation as a "slowly progressing, subacute process," in apparent tension with the patient's own hyperacute description of onset.

Both hosts closed by reaffirming the default framework — abdominal distension with vomiting equals bowel obstruction until proven otherwise — while highlighting how differently the two presentations, workups, and management pathways unfolded despite that shared starting point, underscoring the value of deliberately comparing similar-looking cases side by side.

### Illness Scripts and Bias

Incarcerated hernia SBO illness script: an unoperated abdomen with distension and vomiting should prioritize hernia (including hard-to-palpate femoral, obturator, and pelvic hernias) as the leading cause, distinguished from adhesive disease by the absence of prior abdominal surgery, and confirmed on CT by bowel entering the abdominal wall with surrounding edema/hyperemia signaling strangulation risk.

The pain-severity-and-tempo mismatch as a red flag: when a presentation superficially fits a common, expected diagnosis (adhesive bowel obstruction) but includes atypical severity or hyperacute onset, this mismatch should actively prompt reconsideration of more dangerous alternatives (mesenteric ischemia, perforation) via a structured framework like VIPO, rather than being absorbed into the leading, better-fitting diagnosis.

The case also models an explicit anti-bias habit: Rabih's own acknowledgment that he remained drawn to the rare gallstone-fistula hypothesis specifically because it was an interesting story, despite thin supporting evidence — naming this pull directly as a way of guarding against over-investment in an elegant but under-supported hypothesis, rather than either suppressing or unconsciously acting on it.

## Teaching Discussion

The central teaching point of Case 1 is the disciplined distinction between a hernia as the primary cause of obstruction versus a hernia made secondarily prominent by another process, resolved through bedside reducibility/consistency assessment and, when imaging is needed, specific attention to the abdominal wall and to anatomically subtle hernia sites in patients with a high pretest probability (unoperated abdomen).

A second teaching point, illustrated through live acid-base interpretation, is a reasoning strategy for near-normal pH values with multiple competing acid-base processes: tracking how many processes are needed to counterbalance the true primary driver.

The central teaching point of Case 2 is the VIPO framework for reassessing an otherwise obvious-seeming abdominal diagnosis when pain severity or symptom tempo doesn't fit — and the practical, evidence-supported habit of explicitly communicating a specific suspicion (mesenteric ischemia) directly to the interpreting radiologist to improve detection.

A shared teaching point across both cases is the differing management logic of small versus large bowel obstruction — NG tube efficacy by proximity to obstruction, differing likelihoods of spontaneous resolution by luminal diameter, and differing default management pathways (NG tube and possible surgery for SBO; GI-first evaluation and often more direct escalation to intervention for LBO) — reinforced by directly comparing the two cases' CT findings and courses side by side.
