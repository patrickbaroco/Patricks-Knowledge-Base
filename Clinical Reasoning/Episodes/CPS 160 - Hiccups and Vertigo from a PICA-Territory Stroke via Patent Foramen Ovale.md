## Summary

> [!summary] Scan Summary
> A 49-year-old engineer develops sudden vertigo and tinnitus after exercise, is diagnosed with vestibular neuritis and treated with steroids — but the symptoms never resolve. Over the following weeks he develops a progressively worsening, pulsatile global headache, gait instability, vomiting, and finally hiccups. The discussion works vertigo through **Aaron Berkowitz's** timing-and-triggers framework (acute vestibular syndrome: [[Stroke|posterior circulation stroke]], vestibular neuritis, or intoxication) before the unrelenting month-long course — atypical for vestibular neuritis's expected recover-then-taper arc — pushes the group toward stroke. Exam reveals bilateral nystagmus, a right adduction (medial rectus) palsy from an **[[Internuclear Ophthalmoplegia|internuclear ophthalmoplegia]]**, ataxia, dysmetria, and gait instability — cerebellar and brainstem findings without any body weakness, localizing to the lateral lower brainstem. MRI confirms a **right PICA-territory infarct** (restiform body, medial cerebellar peduncle, cerebellar olives) — larger than a "pure" lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome, extending to involve the MLF — and transesophageal echocardiography identifies a **[[Patent Foramen Ovale and Paradoxical Embolism|patent foramen ovale]]**, implicating exercise-provoked paradoxical embolism as the likely mechanism.

## Pearls

- **Before building a differential for "dizziness," clarify which of four types the patient means**: true room-spinning **[[Acute Vestibular Syndrome|vertigo]]**, presyncopal lightheadedness, imbalance ("dizzy in your head or dizzy in your feet"), or a psychogenic/anxiety-related sensation. Patients are notoriously inconsistent describing "room spinning" from one exam to the next, so David Newman-Toker's timing-and-triggers approach (acute vs. chronic; continuous vs. episodic; provoked vs. unprovoked) is a more reliable second axis than symptom quality alone.
- **Acute vestibular syndrome (sudden, persistent vertigo) has a short, high-value differential: posterior circulation stroke, vestibular neuritis, or intoxication.** The HINTS exam helps separate them: nystagmus that changes direction with gaze, or that is vertical, points central; nystagmus that beats in only one direction regardless of gaze (Alexander's law) points peripheral.
- **Vestibular neuritis has a characteristic arc — brutal for the first 24 hours, clearly improving by 48–72 hours, then a longer tapering tail.** Symptoms that persist unremittingly (or worsen) for a month, as here, should prompt revisiting an existing outside diagnosis rather than accepting it at face value, even after steroids were already given.
- **Distinguish a primary medial rectus/third-nerve adduction failure from an [[Internuclear Ophthalmoplegia|internuclear ophthalmoplegia]] (INO).** INO is a lesion of the medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF) disrupting the coordination between one eye's lateral rectus (CN VI) and the other eye's medial rectus (CN III) during conjugate gaze — a brainstem localizer, not a muscle or isolated cranial-nerve problem, and (by convention) named for the side of the weak adduction/medial rectus.
- **A stroke provoked by exercise or Valsalva should prompt a hunt for a right-to-left shunt.** A patent foramen ovale allows paradoxical embolism during maneuvers that transiently reverse or increase right-to-left flow (Valsalva, straining, heavy lifting); the alternative to keep in mind is an exercise-induced blood pressure surge rupturing an underlying vascular malformation.
- **Cerebellar and brainstem signs (nystagmus, ataxia, dysmetria, INO) without any body weakness, plus a strange add-on symptom like hiccups, is the signature of a small, laterally-placed brainstem/cerebellar lesion** — here a right PICA-territory infarct extending beyond the "pure" lateral medullary syndrome to involve the MLF. Hiccups themselves localize loosely to the brainstem/medullary visceral centers, though the exact pathway to the phrenic nerve remains genuinely unclear even to the discussion's own neurologist.

## Problem Representation

A 49-year-old engineer with no past medical history develops sudden vertigo and tinnitus after exercise, is diagnosed with vestibular neuritis and treated with steroids, but the vertigo never resolves. Over the next several weeks he develops a progressively worsening, pulsatile, holocephalic headache (initially occipital), nausea and one episode of vomiting, gait instability, and, in the 72 hours before presentation, hiccups. He has no hearing loss and no facial weakness. Exam shows bilateral nystagmus, diplopia from a right internuclear ophthalmoplegia (impaired right eye adduction), ataxia, an abnormal finger-nose-finger test, and an unsteady gait, with normal strength and no meningeal signs — a cerebellar and brainstem picture without any long-tract motor findings. MRI shows a right PICA-territory infarct (restiform body, medial cerebellar peduncle, and cerebellar olives) without acute diffusion restriction, consistent with a stroke that occurred weeks earlier; transesophageal echocardiography identifies a patent foramen ovale, implicating exercise-provoked paradoxical embolism as the probable mechanism. Labs show hyponatremia attributed to SIADH.

## Case Narrative

The recording opens with the same standing introduction used across this early run of the series — Aaron Berkowitz's framing of the Clinical Problem Solvers' mission to **[[Neurophobia|end neurophobia]]**, previously captured in full in [[CPS 146 - Right-Sided Weakness and Numbness and Medial Medullary Syndrome from a Vertebral Artery Dissection|CPS 146]] — plus a request from new team member Doug Pett for listeners to submit their own cases.

**Chief complaint: hiccups and vertigo.** Discussant Elena Vasti, a second-year internal medicine resident, opens by admitting she lacks a clean illness script for hiccups, and instead works vertigo first: distinguishing true room-spinning vertigo from other kinds of "dizziness," then splitting vertigo into central (brainstem/cerebellum) versus peripheral (eighth nerve, inner ear) causes. Berkowitz builds on this with **David Newman-Toker's** four-type taxonomy of dizziness — vertigo, presyncopal lightheadedness, imbalance ("dizzy in your head or dizzy in your feet"), and psychogenic/anxiety-related sensations — and Newman-Toker's finding that patients describe "room spinning" inconsistently from one encounter to the next, which is why he emphasizes **timing and triggers** over symptom quality: is this the acute vestibular syndrome (sudden-onset, persistent vertigo), for which the differential is essentially three items — posterior circulation stroke, vestibular neuritis, or intoxication — or is it chronic, and if chronic, continuous or episodic, and if episodic, provoked (orthostasis, BPPV with rolling over) or unprovoked (migrainous vertigo, Ménière's disease)? On hiccups, discussant Drew offers the standard mechanism — irritation anywhere along a pathway that repetitively stimulates the phrenic nerve and diaphragm — while Berkowitz admits he doesn't know the precise brainstem circuitry connecting the two, only that hiccups can occur with brainstem lesions. If the group wants one lesion to explain both hiccups and vertigo, he reasons, it should be a brainstem or cerebellar lesion, since the inner ear and eighth nerve wouldn't produce hiccups.

**History deepens the picture.** The patient is a 49-year-old engineer whose vertigo and tinnitus began abruptly after exercise a month earlier and never resolved; he was diagnosed with vestibular neuritis and treated with steroids. Fifteen days before presentation the vertigo persisted and he developed nausea and an occipital headache, which progressed over subsequent days to a global, 8–10/10, occasionally pulsatile headache, accompanied by gait instability. Seventy-two hours before presentation he developed hiccups, along with one episode of vomiting, prompting hospitalization. Drew asks specifically whether the headache or associated nausea/vomiting has an early-morning predominance — a screen for elevated intracranial pressure Berkowitz calls **"brain orthopnea"** (just as lying flat increases venous return and worsens cardiac orthopnea, lying flat increases CSF return to the brain and can worsen a mass-related headache overnight or on waking) — but the answer is negative: no morning or nighttime pattern. The group also asks about hearing loss (none, which argues against a peripheral/eighth-nerve process like a slowly growing vestibular schwannoma) and facial weakness or numbness (none), with Drew noting from personal experience that a cerebellopontine-angle tumor can present with facial numbness once it's large enough to compress the trigeminal nerve, though this is atypical for an acoustic neuroma given how slowly such tumors usually grow — and this patient's month-long, progressively worsening course is itself atypical for a slow-growing tumor. Elena raises Ramsey Hunt syndrome (VZV of the seventh and eighth nerves) as a possible infectious cause, and questions whether the steroids given for the presumed vestibular neuritis could have "unmasked" a worsening infectious process; Berkowitz finds the infectious hypothesis hard to sustain absent other cranial neuropathies, and is more persuaded by the sheer **persistence** of the vertigo: vestibular neuritis has a predictable arc (severe for 24 hours, clearly improving by 48–72 hours, then a longer taper), and a month of unremitting, worsening symptoms argues strongly against it and toward stroke — raising the question of why a young, healthy man would have a stroke, and pointing back toward the post-exercise onset as a clue (vertebral artery dissection from heavy lifting, or a patent foramen ovale from Valsalva).

**Exam: cerebellar and brainstem findings without body weakness.** The patient is slightly confused but oriented, with normal speech. He has bilateral nystagmus, diplopia from a right adduction failure (later characterized as a right medial rectus/internuclear ophthalmoplegia), ataxia, an abnormal finger-nose-finger test, and an unsteady gait, with normal strength and no meningeal signs. Elena localizes this constellation to the cerebellum, wondering about a posterior circulation stroke versus, less likely in a young patient, a mass or an encephalitis with cerebellar predilection. Berkowitz walks through the diplopia in detail: the affected eye cannot **adduct** — move toward the midline — which could reflect a primary medial rectus problem, a third-nerve lesion (unlikely to isolate just the medial rectus fiber), or, most likely here, an **internuclear ophthalmoplegia**: a lesion of the MLF, which normally couples one eye's lateral rectus (CN VI) to the other eye's medial rectus (CN III) so the eyes move together in conjugate gaze. An INO is, by convention, named for the side of the weak adduction. Combined with the bilateral nystagmus (treated as direction-changing, and therefore central, per the HINTS framework) and the cerebellar signs, the localization moves to the lateral lower brainstem — Berkowitz's working hypothesis is a **lateral medullary syndrome**, though he flags that the INO doesn't neatly fit that "pure" syndrome and that the lesion is probably "a little bigger than that." He favors PICA territory (a branch of the vertebral artery) and, given the post-exercise onset, again raises **vertebral artery dissection** as a candidate mechanism, alongside a differential-diagnosis reminder for cranial nerve trivia: CN IV (superior oblique) and CN VI (lateral rectus) each do one specific action, while CN III does "everything else."

**One more test, then confirmation.** The group anticipates a non-contrast head CT, brain MRI, MR angiography to define the vascular territory, and a hypercoagulable workup given the patient's age. Labs return hyponatremia (attributed, via serum/urine osmolality and urine sodium, to SIADH) and a mildly elevated fibrinogen, otherwise unremarkable, including a reassuring blood count. MRI shows a **right PICA-territory infarct** — restiform body (inferior cerebellar peduncle) with extension into the medial cerebellar peduncle and cerebellar olives and surrounding white matter — without acute diffusion restriction, indicating the stroke was already weeks old by the time of imaging, consistent with the month-long clinical course. Berkowitz confirms the localization was correct even without pinning the exact eponym: a lateral lower-brainstem syndrome producing prominent cerebellar and cranial-nerve findings without body weakness. Etiology testing (transesophageal echocardiography) identifies a **patent foramen ovale**, closing the loop on the exercise-associated onset: Berkowitz names the general pearl that a stroke occurring after exercise or any Valsalva-type maneuver (straining, heavy lifting) should prompt a hunt for a PFO enabling paradoxical embolism, with the caveat — since roughly 30% of the general population has a PFO, comparable to stroke prevalence — that the combination needs a supporting overall picture rather than PFO discovery alone. The alternative he raises (an exercise-induced blood pressure surge rupturing a vascular malformation) is set aside given the imaging findings. The hyponatremia is treated as a reactive SIADH from the brain injury itself, not an independent unifying diagnosis.

**Synthesis.** The closing discussant reprises the case: dizziness first sorted by type and by timing-and-triggers; the acute vestibular syndrome's three-item differential; the HINTS red flags (direction-changing and vertical nystagmus both point central); cerebellar findings (nystagmus, ataxia, dysmetria) localizing to the cerebellum; diplopia parsed by which eye movement fails (adduction versus abduction) rather than by muscle name alone, with internuclear ophthalmoplegia as the key brainstem-localizing diagnosis; hiccups localized loosely via the same central-nervous-system schema; brain orthopnea as an elevated-ICP screening question; and the case's signature pearl — exercise-associated stroke should trigger a search for a PFO (or, alternatively, a ruptured vascular malformation from an exertional blood pressure surge).

### Illness Scripts and Bias

- **Timing-and-triggers as the master vertigo schema.** Newman-Toker's approach (acute vs. chronic; continuous vs. episodic; provoked vs. unprovoked) is used explicitly in preference to symptom-quality descriptions, given his own research finding that patients are inconsistent describing "room spinning" — a direct argument for structuring history around timing rather than a potentially unreliable symptom report.
- **Revising an outside diagnosis against its expected natural history.** Vestibular neuritis carries a specific expected arc (severe, then clearly improving by 48–72 hours, then a long taper); a month of unremitting, worsening symptoms is treated as disconfirming evidence against the existing diagnosis rather than accepted at face value — an exercise in **[[Diagnostic Humility|diagnostic humility]]** applied to someone else's prior call, not just one's own.
- **A negative screening question is kept, not discarded.** Asking about morning/nocturnal headache pattern ("brain orthopnea") returned a negative answer; rather than being dropped from the discussion, it's folded into the overall picture as one data point among several red flags — modeling honest **[[Test Interpretation|calibration]]** rather than only reporting confirmatory findings.
- **Holding a localization hypothesis loosely enough to widen it.** Berkowitz's working diagnosis of a "pure" lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome doesn't neatly explain the INO; rather than discard the general territory (lateral lower brainstem/PICA), he explicitly widens the hypothesis ("maybe it's a little bigger than that") — the same discipline of revising scope rather than abandoning a broadly correct localization seen with the crossed-signs reasoning in [[CPS 146 - Right-Sided Weakness and Numbness and Medial Medullary Syndrome from a Vertebral Artery Dissection|CPS 146]].
- **Exercise/Valsalva as an etiologic illness script, named explicitly late in the case.** The PFO-paradoxical-embolism pattern is presented as a specific, learnable trigger-to-mechanism link — with an explicit base-rate caveat (PFO prevalence ≈ 30% of the general population, comparable to stroke prevalence) against over-crediting an incidental PFO without a supporting clinical picture.

## Teaching Discussion

- When a patient reports "dizziness," first clarify which of four types is meant — true vertigo, presyncopal lightheadedness, imbalance, or a psychogenic sensation — since patients describe these inconsistently, and lean on timing-and-triggers (acute vs. chronic; continuous vs. episodic; provoked vs. unprovoked) as the more reliable organizing axis.
- For acute, persistent vertigo, keep the working differential to three items — posterior circulation stroke, vestibular neuritis, or intoxication — and use the HINTS exam (direction-changing or vertical nystagmus favors central; unidirectional nystagmus independent of gaze favors peripheral, per Alexander's law) to help discriminate.
- Treat an existing outside diagnosis as testable against its expected natural history: vestibular neuritis should clearly improve within 48–72 hours and taper thereafter, so ongoing or worsening symptoms a month out are grounds to revisit the diagnosis, even after treatment (steroids) was already given.
- A positional or nocturnal headache pattern ("brain orthopnea," analogous to cardiac orthopnea) is a quick screen for elevated intracranial pressure, though its absence doesn't exclude a central process when other red flags are present.
- Distinguish a primary medial rectus or third-nerve adduction failure from an internuclear ophthalmoplegia — a lesion of the MLF that disrupts the coupling between one eye's abduction (CN VI) and the other eye's adduction (CN III) — since INO is a brainstem localizer in its own right, not simply "a weak muscle."
- Absent hearing loss and a rapidly progressive, non-improving course both argue against a slow-growing cerebellopontine-angle tumor (e.g. vestibular schwannoma) even when tinnitus and diplopia are both present.
- A stroke occurring after exercise or a Valsalva maneuver should prompt evaluation for a right-to-left shunt (patent foramen ovale) causing paradoxical embolism, while keeping an exertional blood-pressure surge rupturing a vascular malformation as an alternative explanation.
