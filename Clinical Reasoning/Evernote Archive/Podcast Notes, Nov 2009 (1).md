# Podcast Notes, Nov 2009 (1)

Atypical HUS from NEJM (dx\_5)

"Atypical" meaning not associated with Shiga-like toxin, produced by certain strains of Ecoli (O157:H7) and some other bacteria including Strep pneumo.  Much more common in kids.  Atypical HUS has a poor prognosis, with mortality of around 25% of patients and ESRD in about 50% of survivors. 

Fewer than 20% of atypical HUS cases are familiar.  These cases have mortality rates as high as 50-80%.

Sporadic forms are more common.  Triggers include HIV, cancer, organ transplantation, pregnancy, certain anticancer drugs, calcineurin inhibitors, and antiplatelet drugs like clopidogrel.

Excellent review of the complement system.  In the alternative pathway, C3 hydrolyzes spontaneously and C3b is deposited on nearly all plasma-exposed surfaces.  On host cells, CFI inactivates C3b and the C3 and C5 convertases are broken down quickly.  Thus, the typical pattern is low C3 (it's being used up) and normal C4 (not involved)

Numerous mutations have been identified that increase risk.  CFH, the plasma regulator of the alternative pathway, is a big one.  Auto-Ab against CFH have also been reported.  Mutations in membrane cofactor protein (MCP), which is a cofactor to CFI, have also been implicated, as have mutations in CFI itself.

All of these are bad, and CFH mutations are the worst; MCP mutations are the best, with 80% of patients remaining free of dialysis.  Treatment is traditionally plasma exchange, though studies are limited.  Combination of plasma exchange with immunosuppression using steroids, MFM and rituximab has resulted in dialysis-free survival in 60-70% of patients.  Transplantation can help, but relapses after renal transplant are common.  Liver-kidney transplant cures the disease, but mortality is high.  Aggressive plasma exchange pre-op and post-op may help by achieving adequate CFH levels before surgery.

Finally, there is a new drug, eculizumab, that is a monoclonal Ab against C5 that has shown some efficacy in case series.  Clinical trials are ongoing.

 

RENAL Trial (dx\_4)

1508 patients in the ICU with AKI.  All got CVVHD, some at 25ml/kg/hr and some at 40.  Of the 1464 who completed the study, there was a 90-day mortality of 44.7, and was not statistically different between the two arms.  If you survived 90 days, only 5.6% still required dialysis--again, no difference between groups.

As editorial points out, this is similar to the results of the ARF Net study, though the studies were different in many ways.  Namely, the RENAL trial was almost entirely CVVHD whereas the ARF Net study used CVVHD only in unstable patients.  Finally, editorialist reminds us that although high-intensity dialysis does not add anything, this does not mean that dialysis dose does not matter, rather that there is a threshold above which there is no added benefit.  But it is essential to reach that threshold.

 

Statins and treatment of infection (dx\_19)

Meta-analysis looking at statin use in prevention and treatment of infectious diseases.  Authors point out large heterogeneity in the studies and some quality issues, as well as potential for publication bias.  Nonetheless, there was an effect estimate of 0.55 for the treatment cohorts and 0.57 for the prevention cohorts.  Both were significant, with CI of 0.36-0.83 for the treatment cohort and 0.43-0.75 for the prevention cohort.  Postulated mechanisms for this include the apparent immunomodulatory effects of statins: they reduce IFN-gamma-induced MHC II expression, and decrease migration of leukocytes through the endothelial layer.  Statins have been shown to inhibit the inflammatory response to S. aureus alpha toxin in rats, and reduce TNF and IL-1beta at LPS exposure.  Also, statins are known to improve endothelial function, and the pathophysiology of sepsis involves endothelial dysfunction.  Finally, statins exert direct effects on pathogens.  Lovastatin reduces intracellular growth of Salmonella in cultured macrophages, and reduces HIV viral load and raises CD4 counts in patients with chronic infection.  They reduce CMV DNA and viral particles.  And they appear to have direct antifungal activity.  Despite the limitations of the study, the effect was signficant and there is some biologic plausibility, suggesting it is time for some prospective trials.

 

Clinical course of dementia (dx\_18)

Depressing article reminding us that dementia is a progressive disease associated with a high mortality rate, increased risk of infections and eating problems, and that distressing symptoms are common as death approaches.  These distressing symptoms are defined as dyspnea, pain, pressure ulcers, aspiration, and agitation.  A significant take home is that patients who have health-care proxies who understand the poor prognosis are more likely to avoid burdensome medical interventions and have a focus on comfort measures at the end of life.

 

Meta-analysis of ventilation strategies in ARDS and ALI (dx\_17)

A 21-page article from the Annals that reports what I thought was already well-established: low tidal volumes are beneficial in ARDS, and high-PEEP should only be used if needed to obtain adequate oxygenation.

 

Bacterial Diarrhea from NEJM (dx\_16)

\- 4 major causes of dysentery, in decending order incidence, are shigella, campy, nontyphoid salmonella, and Shiga-toxin producing E.coli

\- 80% of travellers diarrhea is bacterial, and cases should be tx'd with abx

\- Prophylaxis of travellers diarrhea can be rifaxamin 200mg once or twice daily, or bismuth subsalicylate 2 tabs with each meal and at bedtime

\- Treat diarrhea caused by invasive or inflammatory bacteria with abx.  The 2 cases deserving special consideration are nontyphoid salmonellosis and Shiga-toxin producing E.coli.  In the case of salmonella, 8% of healthy adults get bacteremic after intestinal salmonella.  Things that increase this risk include extremes of age (under 3months or over 65yrs), steroid use, IBD, immunosuppression, hemoglobinopathies, and dialysis.  These folks should be treated even for intestinal salmonella.  Patients with prosthetic valves or aortic aneurysms should also be treated.  In the case of Shiga-toxin producing E.coli, there is a theoretical risk that some abx, including quinolones and Bactrim, could induce phage-mediated production of Shiga toxin.  That this increases HUS-risk has not been established in studies, and azithromycin actually inhibits Shiga-toxin-induced inflammatory response in mice, preventing death.  Nonetheless, most patients with Shiga-toxin producing E.coli are treated supportively.

 

Postexposure Prophylaxis in HIV (dx\_13)

Prevents seroconversion in about 80% of cases, and is most effective if started within 36 hours and certainly within 72 hrs.

Rates of conversion: 0.3% in occupational exposure from needle stick.  Splash exposures 0.09%.  For receptive anal intercourse, 1-30%; 0.1-10% for insertive anal intercourse and receptive vaginal intercourse; and 0.1-1% for insertive vaginal intercourse.  In needle-sharing, 0.67% per episode.

Whom to prophylax?  Anybody exposed from known HIV(+) contacts and those exposed by selected high-risk populations: MSM, commerical sex workers, IVDU, hx of incarceration, people from a country with a seroprevalence of 1% or greater, and those with a sexual partner in one of these groups.  Also prophylax victims of sexual assault.

Treat for 28 days.  Optimal regimen is unclear; typically 2 nucleoside analogues are used.  In resistence is likely, a ritonivir-boosted PI can be added.

Don't forget to test for immunity to Hep B and to test for both Hep B and Hep C as well.  Follow-up HIV testing in the exposed patient is at 4-6 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months.

 

Post-op ileus (Clev Clinic) \[dx\_11\]

Divided into gastroparesis, small bowel, and colonic.  In abdominal surgery, some ileus is the norm--the small bowel recovers first, in several hours, the stomach next in 1-2 days, and the colon last, in 3-5 days.  Colonic ileus is especially seen in elderly patients, even in non-abdominal operations.  Be judicious with narcotics, and use non-narcotic analgesia when possible.  Nasogastric tubes do not prevent post-op ileus, but may be useful for managing intractable vomiting.  Early enteral feeding decreases infectious complications and length-of-stay.  As far as drugs, erythromycin is effective for gastroparesis, but patients develop tachyphylaxis due to down-regulation of motilin receptors.  Metoclopramide is a dopamine receptor antagonist and a serotonin antagonist/agonist.  It is also effective in gastroparesis, but its dopamine antagonism limits usefulness--it can't be used in patients with Parkinsonism, and it has a black box warning suggesting its use be limited to 3 weeks for fear of tardive dyskinesia.  Neostigmine is an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, and is first line therapy for colonic ileus.  Patients require monitoring for bradycardia and bronchospasm.  Lastly, alvimopan is a peripherally acting mu-opioid antagonist, and appears effective for both small and large bowel ileus, and can help block effects of opiates on the gut, but its use is restricted to hospitals enrolled in an ongoing study.

 

Dialysis in Frail Elders

Study examining all nursing home residents who began dialysis between 1998 and 2000.  Outcomes were abysmal: 58% died within a year, 29% had decreased functional status, and only 13% maintained functional status.  An editorial suggests that discussions about conservative tx of ESRD is appropriate in these patients.
