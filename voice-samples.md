## Sample 1 — Chapter prose, attending to an NP (HFrEF, cardiorenal syndrome)

*Register: chapter. The reference passage for handbook drafting, from
[[Heart Failure with Reduced Ejection Fraction]]. The comparison table is kept — it is
the one table header on file.*

> Cardiorenal syndrome is the bidirectional interplay between cardiac and renal dysfunction. It is one of the most clinically important and most often mismanaged phenomena in HFrEF. In LTC, the most common scenario is this: a volume-overloaded resident with HFrEF has a rising creatinine, and the clinical reflex is to hold or reduce diuretics to “protect the kidneys”. This reflex is frequently wrong, and acting on it causes harm.
>
> In a volume-overloaded HFrEF patient, a rising creatinine is often not a sign of too much diuresis but rather of too little. When the heart fails to pump effectively, pressure backs up through the venous system all the way to the kidneys. That elevated venous pressure compresses the renal circulation from the “outflow” side, reducing filtration in the same way that pinching a garden hose at the nozzle end reduces flow through the whole system. The kidney is being harmed by venous congestion, not by the diuretic. The treatment is more decongestion, not less. 
>
> Of course, true diuretic-induced prerenal AKI exists and must be distinguished. The clinical features that help:
>
> | Feature | Suggests Venous Congestion Driving AKI | Suggests Diuretic-Induced Prerenal AKI |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | Volume status | Overtly volume overloaded — edema, elevated JVP, weight gain, orthopnea | Volume depleted or euvolemic — dry mucous membranes, orthostasis, weight loss |
> | Urine sodium | >20 mEq/L | <20 mEq/L |
> | Response to diuresis | Creatinine stabilizes or improves as congestion clears | Creatinine worsens with continued diuresis |
> | Clinical trajectory | Dyspnea and congestion predominate | Thirst, lightheadedness, reduced urine output without congestion |
>
> In a resident who is visibly volume overloaded (edematous, dyspneic, with weight gain and orthopnea) a rising creatinine is a signal to reassess the adequacy of decongestion, not to reflexively reduce diuretics. Backing off diuresis in this setting prolongs venous hypertension, worsens renal perfusion, and leads to a vicious cycle. Push through the congestion, monitor closely, and allow the kidneys to recover as the hemodynamics improve.
>
> A modest creatinine rise during active, appropriate decongestion of a volume-overloaded patient is expected and acceptable. It is not a reason to stop. The kidney that is clearing congestion is doing its job. A creatinine of 1.8 in a resident who has lost 8 pounds of fluid and can breathe again is a success, not a complication. And it is important to recognize the “dry weight” creatinine as the true baseline creatinine for the patient. 
>
> On the other hand, signals of a true primary acute kidney injury include: creatinine rising without clinical improvement in congestion, rising potassium, oliguria without volume overload, or creatinine that continues to climb despite adequate decongestion. These warrant reassessment of the diagnosis, consideration of intrinsic renal injury, and possibly transfer.
>
> If the treatment goals are more focused on comfort and you are forced to choose between volume status and renal function, choose volume status. A patient who is volume overloaded and dyspneic is suffering now. Renal function that worsens transiently with aggressive diuresis in a volume-overloaded patient usually recovers: the kidney tolerates appropriate decongestion better than it tolerates chronic venous hypertension. Undertreating congestion to protect a creatinine leads to worse outcomes, both clinical and quality-of-life.

## Sample 2 — Clinical teaching (SSRIs for agitation in dementia, 14 July 2025)

*Register: peer.*

*Full piece archived separately; the composed section is what matters for voice.*

> **Thoughts and Takeaways**
>
> I hate to be wrong, and for that reason I hated these studies: for years I have
> used (and suggested others use!) escitalopram as a front-line agent in my attempts
> to manage agitation associated with dementia.
>
> There had been some weak evidence supporting the use of citalopram, but there were
> adverse cognitive and cardiac effects with citalopram. I assumed escitalopram might
> be safer, equally (even if poorly) effective, and that the general safety profile
> of SSRIs was good.
>
> These studies suggest it is not effective, and it is not safer. Worse, the Swedish
> study suggests the risks are a class effect, and not limited to citalopram or
> escitalopram.
>
> These are not perfect studies. The S-CitAD study was fairly small and underpowered.
> It is possible it missed some small benefit, but it is unlikely there is a true
> clinically meaningful effect.
>
> The authors of the Swedish study made heroic efforts to control for confounding
> variables, but it would be impossible to account for all of them. However, the
> consistency of the findings — and the apparent dose-response relationship —
> suggests that the harms are real and likely attributable, at least in significant
> part, to the drugs themselves.
>
> Unfortunately, these two studies, together with multiple other negative trials of
> SSRIs for agitation associated with dementia, suggest that SSRIs in these patients
> are neither effective nor safe.
>
> 🎯 **Clinical pearl:** The ~30% placebo response in S-CitAD is a reminder that when
> we see improvement, we cannot simply assume it's due to the medication. Combined
> with the safety concerns from the Swedish study, this is a strong argument for
> attempting gradual dose reductions (GDRs) — even when we believe the patient has
> benefited.

[Counterexample: the "🎯 Clinical pearl" block belongs to Bedside Notes. Chapters take no callouts.]

## Sample 3 — Clinical teaching (thickened liquids and fluid restriction, 2025)

*Register: peer.*

> **Thoughts and Takeaways**
>
> Thickened liquids are pretty unpalatable. I tried drinking nectar thick liquids for
> a week, and it was awful. So many things that you've always enjoyed become just
> gross — ready for your morning cup of coffee jelly?
>
> The only evidence we have of benefit is on swallow studies, where we find they do
> reduce aspiration. But we have evidence that they don't improve clinical outcomes.
> Why is that? My hypothesis is that perhaps you aspirate less frequently, but when
> you do aspirate the event is worse. Clearing water from the airway isn't very hard.
> Good luck clearing honey.
>
> In my practice, I rarely use thickened liquids, and I find most SLPs are now in
> agreement on this. If they are used, I monitor very closely for harms and am quick
> to discontinue.
>
> As for fluid restriction in CHF, this was always a weak recommendation in the
> guidelines. There have been recent studies in hospitalized patients showing worse
> outcomes — specifically, higher rates of rehospitalization. This makes sense to me:
> you want to dose your diuretic based on their typical volume intake at home. One by
> one, inpatient restrictive diets have gone by the wayside: restricted carbohydrate
> diets for diabetes, restricted fluid for CHF, restricted protein for CKD, etc. We
> are now accumulating evidence that these are not helpful in the ambulatory setting
> either.

## Sample 4 — Clinical teaching, drug monograph variant (tramadol, 15 August 2025)

*Register: peer.*

*Drug monograph variant — plain headings, no callouts. Composed section below.*

> **Clinical bottom line**
>
> I essentially never prescribe tramadol. A PharmD once described it as handing a
> patient a bowl of oxycodone and venlafaxine and asking them to take three random
> pills. That may make for a great Saturday night in college, but it is a dumb way to
> practice medicine.
>
> I have personally admitted three patients to the ICU on dextrose drips from
> tramadol-induced hypoglycemia. And far too many of my patients have structurally
> abnormal brains for me to be comfortable with the seizure risk.
>
> If I want to give a patient a low-dose opioid, I use 2.5 mg of oxycodone. If they
> need something scheduled, I use 5 mcg/hr of transdermal buprenorphine.

## Sample 6 — Casual correspondence (email to Mike, med administration)

*Register: peer.*

> Thanks, Mike. In my experience, most of these issues get resolved just in the nick
> of time. Avardis will throw a ton of resources at it, that's for sure.
>
> Focusing on med administration will be very helpful. One, if we get the numbers
> down, there is statistically a lower chance of med error (and a lower chance of
> drug-drug interactions!). I'd recommend searching PCC for a few good candidates to
> dc or adjust—find and stop all docusate (no better than placebo), for example. H2
> blockers have quite small benefit when used chronically (rapid tachyphylaxis), and
> so I tend to change them all to PRN and then stop them in 2 weeks if not used. I'm
> pretty ruthless with PPIs. Etc.
>
> Then a big opportunity is to simplify complicated orders. DC corrective insulin
> unless it is truly needed (brittle diabetics, primarily) and just use scheduled
> prandial insulin, even sometimes just once a day. Corrective insulin or SSI is a
> med error waiting to happen. Same with complex pain management, where there is
> acetaminophen for one range, oxy 5mg for another, oxy 10mg for another. That leads
> to ODs and/or med errors. Look at orders, consider the "least effective" nurse at
> the facility, and think "Can he interpret this order and execute it reliably on a
> day when he has med pass for 40 residents?" If not, change the order. ;)

[Counterexample: "brittle diabetics" and "leads to ODs" are condition-as-noun. Person-first in the handbook. The winking emoticon is peer-register only.]

## Sample 7 — Leadership, reflective (departure of Dr. King, first CEO)

*Register: team.*

> By now I imagine all of you have heard that Dr. King has departed Eventus. It is a
> true testament to what he accomplished with Eventus that many will feel this as a
> personal loss. After the challenges of the PGS/Eventus merger, Dr. King was a strong
> advocate for Virginia. He gave us a lot of autonomy to allow the development of a
> culture that I think has become not just a positive, but even serves as a model (and
> a "proving ground") for the rest of Eventus. I am full of gratitude to him.
>
> In the Navy, officers aboard a ship typically serve 2–3 years before rotating to a
> new assignment. At first, that seemed short to me—just as you were getting settled,
> you were already moving on. But by the time I left my first operational command, I
> understood what my Commanding Officer meant when he said that "getting settled" can
> become the problem. New leaders arrive with fresh eyes: they see issues others have
> learned to overlook and bring energy to fixing them. Over time, familiarity dulls
> that edge.
>
> More importantly, I learned that a ship is not defined by its Captain. It is defined
> by its Chiefs, its Petty Officers, and its deckplate sailors. It's not that a ship
> gets a "good" or "bad" CO—it's that a CO inherits a "good" or "bad" ship.
>
> Within Eventus, we are the Chiefs, the Petty Officers, and the deckplate sailors. We
> define the culture of Eventus, and we have defined it very well. When a new CEO is
> hired, that CEO is going to inherit something great, and Virginia will continue to
> set the pace. We will have fresh eyes at the top, and an opportunity to innovate and
> grow. But we also have a strong culture that will not change.
>
> Meanwhile, as we recruit a new CEO, we have many strong leaders within Eventus: Dr.
> Patel, Will Sampson, Laura Hardcastle, and more. In particular I single out Cary
> Trainor, our COO. In the time I've worked with her she has proved to be one of the
> finest leaders I've worked with. She is entirely capable, but she is also curious,
> open-minded, straightforward, and most importantly, kind. She is exactly the person
> we want at Eventus. I say this without in any way diminishing my respect for Dr.
> King, but I am excited for the future.
>
> As always, I am available to chat anytime.

## Sample 9 — Family-facing (autumn respiratory vaccines)

*Register: family-facing.*

> Autumn is my favorite season. By late August, I am ready for a respite from the
> heat, and looking forward to the show of colors that Nature brings us in the next
> couple months. Throughout history, autumn is also the last chance to prepare for
> winter: as the squirrels stash away the acorns that begin falling, we begin to put
> up the last of what our garden has to offer.
>
> In that spirit of preparing, it is also time to prepare ourselves against the
> respiratory viruses of winter. [...] RSV hospitalizes around 160,000 older adults
> each year, and takes the lives of about 10,000. Those estimates may even be low, as
> we only recently began routinely testing for it. The new RSV vaccine prevents severe
> disease in about 85% of those who receive the vaccine, and the effect is durable.
> [...]
>
> These vaccines will all be available this autumn. I highly recommend them as safe
> and, while not at all perfect, effective at preventing severe disease. If you have
> questions about whether this is right for you as an individual, I am always happy to
> discuss it.
>
> Enjoy these final weeks of summer as we move toward the beauty of autumn with a
> spirit of community and preparation.

## Sample 10 — Compressed elements

*Register: compressed. Headings, bullets, and run-in labels — where the voice is
known to fail. Each pair shows the written version first, then the same element
drifted into institutional default.*

**Section headings.** Written:

```
Falls Are Multifactorial: Retiring the "Mechanical Fall"
Cardiorenal Syndrome: When the Kidneys Look Worse but Need More Diuresis
Medications: The Highest-Yield Modifiable Risk
CHF, HFrEF, and HFpEF Are Not the Same Thing
The Physiology in One Paragraph
```

The same headings, drifted:

```
Falls Overview
Cardiorenal Considerations
Medication Management
Types of Heart Failure
Pathophysiology
```

**Common Errors bullets.** Written:

> - **Labeling a fall "mechanical" and closing the workup:** the label is a decision to stop thinking; trauma is the mechanism of injury, not the cause of the fall, and the causal columns go unexamined.
> - **Withholding anticoagulation because of fall risk:** the hemorrhage math does not favor stopping in atrial fibrillation, and "he's a fall risk" is not an evidence-based reason to leave a resident unprotected from stroke.
> - **Chronic meclizine for dizziness:** it blocks the central compensation that resolves peripheral vertigo and becomes a fall-risk drug in its own right.
> - **Reading a low fall rate as good care:** immobility produces clean fall logs and its own harms; the fall rate is only interpretable against how active and functional the residents are.

The same bullets, drifted:

> - **Mechanical falls:** avoid premature closure of the workup
> - **Anticoagulation:** balance bleeding risk against fall risk
> - **Meclizine:** use with caution in the elderly
> - **Fall rates:** interpret quality data in context

Each written bullet carries a claim and its warrant and could be lifted into a
paragraph without rewriting. Each drifted bullet names a topic and gestures at a
principle, and could appear in any organization's newsletter.

**Run-in labels, Talking with Families.** Written:

> **On why a fall is not just bad luck:**
> **On restraints and bed rails:**
> **On the balance between safety and living:**

The same labels, drifted:

> **Family education:**
> **Restraint discussions:**
> **Balancing safety and autonomy:**

**Manage in place / Consider transfer.** Written:

> **Manage in place:** The uninjured or minor-injury fall in a resident not on anticoagulation, with a plausible and modifiable cause identified such as orthostasis, a sedating medication, or a treatable positional vertigo, and adequate monitoring available. Most falls belong here, and most of the value of the response is the in-facility workup and the medication changes that follow it.

Drifted:

> **Manage in place:** Stable residents with minor injuries and an identified cause.

The written version is prose that happens to follow a bold label. The drifted version
is a table cell.
