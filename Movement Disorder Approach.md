The movement disorder approach is a deliberate inversion of how most neurologic complaints get worked up. Weakness, numbness, and diplopia are localized first — corticospinal tract, brainstem, cord, nerve, or muscle — and the cause follows from the site. An abnormal movement is localized last, if at all: nearly every hyperkinetic and hypokinetic movement disorder traces back to the same basal ganglia–thalamocortical circuitry regardless of which specific nucleus or pathway is involved, so precise anatomic localization is rarely the productive first question the way it is for weakness ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]). The productive first question instead is *what* the movement looks like at the bedside — its phenomenology — because there is no confirmatory test that names a movement disorder; the phenotype **is** the diagnosis ([[CPS 247 - Tremors, Myoclonus, and Steroid-Responsive Autoimmune Encephalopathy|CPS 247]]; [[CPS 276 - Lethargy, Myoclonus, and Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome|CPS 276]]). This is the parent framework behind this vault's individual movement-disorder notes — [[Tremor]], [[Myoclonus]], [[Parkinsonism]], [[Dystonia]] — each of which applies this same characterize-first method to one phenotype.

## Phenomenology before localization

The method runs through a fixed sequence of branch points, taught across the CP Solvers Neurology VMR by Aaron Berkowitz:

- **Seizure vs. movement disorder.** Any complaint of involuntary movement first splits into abnormal cortical electrical activity (seizure — tonic, tonic-clonic, or myoclonic) versus a movement disorder proper ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]). The two can look identical at the bedside — a bilateral jerking movement read as [[Myoclonus|myoclonus]] turned out on EEG to be rhythmic hemispheric delta activity, i.e., subtle myoclonic seizures, in a cancer patient with posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome ([[CPS 276 - Lethargy, Myoclonus, and Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome|CPS 276]]) — so the distinction should be held provisionally until EEG settles it when the story doesn't fit cleanly.
- **Hypokinetic vs. hyperkinetic.** Movement disorders then split into hypokinetic — [[Parkinsonism]], defined by bradykinesia and rigidity — and hyperkinetic, which spans [[Tremor]], [[Myoclonus]], tics, [[Chorea]] (with its athetosis-ballism continuum), and [[Dystonia]] ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]; [[CPS 276 - Lethargy, Myoclonus, and Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome|CPS 276]]).
- **Describe before you name.** Rather than asking "what movement disorder is this?" directly, describe the video or the exam finding in plain, non-medical language first — amplitude, rhythm, distribution, purposefulness — ideally while the patient is distracted (e.g., giving history), and only then map that description onto the formal taxonomy. This is a deliberate technique for avoiding premature pattern-matching to a remembered label before the phenomenology has actually been observed ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]; [[CPS 276 - Lethargy, Myoclonus, and Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome|CPS 276]]).
- **A coordination disorder is not a movement disorder proper.** Cerebellar ataxia is better thought of as a disorder of coordination than a true movement disorder — its signature is a kinetic/intention tremor that crescendos as precision demands increase (finger-to-nose testing), accompanied by dysmetria and dysdiadochokinesia, rather than a hyperkinetic or hypokinetic phenotype in its own right ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]).

## The taxonomy of major movement disorder categories

Once a movement is watched, it should sort cleanly into one phenotype — patients rarely have two distinct movement disorders at once, so a confusing limb is often better explained by a quieter body part showing the same movement at lower amplitude (see [[Tremor]] for this bedside trick applied directly).

| Category | Core phenomenology | Rhythm | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[Tremor]] | Rhythmic, oscillatory, roughly sinusoidal | Regular | Read by activation (rest/postural/kinetic), frequency, and amplitude — see [[Tremor]] for the full classification table |
| [[Myoclonus]] | A lightning-fast jerk moving a muscle across a joint, too fast to imitate | Irregular, non-sustained | Distinguish from [[Clonus]] (rhythmic, from hyperreflexia) and [[Fasciculation]] (a single fascicle twitches without moving the joint); in the hospital, usually toxic-metabolic (renal/hepatic failure, hypercarbia) or drug-related (opioids, amantadine) ([[CPS 276 - Lethargy, Myoclonus, and Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome|CPS 276]]; [[CPS 247 - Tremors, Myoclonus, and Steroid-Responsive Autoimmune Encephalopathy|CPS 247]]) |
| [[Chorea]] | Irregular, large-amplitude, non-rhythmic, flowing "dance-like" movement | Irregular | Sits on a continuum: athetosis is slower and writhing, ballism (e.g., hemiballismus) is larger-amplitude and more violent; clearly non-purposeful, unlike some tics ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]) |
| [[Dystonia]] | Sustained, twisting co-contraction of agonist and antagonist into a fixed abnormal posture | Sustained, not oscillatory | Torticollis and writer's cramp are classic examples; can produce a position-dependent "null point" dystonic tremor, and overlaps with dystonia-myoclonus syndromes ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]; [[CPS 247 - Tremors, Myoclonus, and Steroid-Responsive Autoimmune Encephalopathy|CPS 247]]) |
| Tics | Complex, stereotyped movements preceded by a premonitory urge and followed by relief | Repetitive, patterned | Can look almost deliberate or purposeful — the feature that separates a tic from chorea's non-purposeful flow ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]) |
| Ataxia | Incoordination, not a discrete hyperkinetic or hypokinetic movement | — | A cerebellar (or sensory) coordination disorder; its own signature is a kinetic/intention tremor rather than a category of its own — kept conceptually separate from movement disorders proper ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]) |

## Cause schema once the phenotype is named

Every named phenotype then runs through the same short differential, regardless of which movement it is: **neurologic** (a structural lesion, or a primary/degenerative neurologic disease), **systemic**, or **drug/toxin-induced** ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]) — presented in some episodes as an explicit four-column schema that splits "neurologic" into separate primary-neurologic and structural columns ([[CPS 247 - Tremors, Myoclonus, and Steroid-Responsive Autoimmune Encephalopathy|CPS 247]]; [[CPS 276 - Lethargy, Myoclonus, and Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome|CPS 276]]). Worked through chorea as the example: a contralateral subthalamic nucleus lesion is structural; Huntington's disease is neurologic/degenerative; hyperglycemia, pregnancy (chorea gravidarum), and lupus are systemic; cocaine is drug-induced ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]). The same schema applies to hypokinetic disease: [[Parkinsonism]] is a syndrome, not a diagnosis, spanning idiopathic Parkinson disease, drug-induced parkinsonism, and the Parkinson-plus neurodegenerative syndromes ([[CPS 211 - Episodic Transient Loss of Consciousness and Multiple System Atrophy|CPS 211]]). One cause is worth holding across nearly every column and every phenotype in a young patient: [[Wilson Disease]] can present with tremor, dystonia, or a chorea-like picture depending on which basal ganglia circuit predominates, and belongs on the differential of almost any movement disorder under 40.

## Practical bedside sequence

1. **Split seizure from movement disorder first**, and keep EEG in reserve for when a hyperkinetic movement doesn't fit its expected story.
2. **Watch before you ask** — observe the movement, ideally while the patient is distracted by giving history, and describe it in plain language (amplitude, rhythm, distribution, purposefulness) before reaching for a formal name.
3. **Assign hypokinetic vs. hyperkinetic**, then the specific phenotype from the taxonomy above.
4. **Run the cause schema for that phenotype** — neurologic (structural vs. degenerative), systemic, drug/toxin — rather than free-listing diagnoses.
5. **Cross-check with tempo.** Acute-onset favors structural lesion or an acute systemic derangement; subacute favors autoimmune/antibody-mediated or post-infectious disease; chronic favors a primary degenerative disorder ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]).
6. **Hold heuristics provisionally.** Unilateral movement disorders generally suggest a structural lesion and bilateral ones a systemic or degenerative cause — but hyperglycemic hemichorea is a classic, named exception: a systemic metabolic derangement producing a strikingly unilateral movement disorder. Likewise, chorea can be "pseudo-acute" — patients unconsciously camouflage early choreiform movements into voluntary gestures (parakinesia), so a "definitely sudden" onset should be interrogated rather than assumed ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]]).
