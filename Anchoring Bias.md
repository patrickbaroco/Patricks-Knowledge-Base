Evergreen concept note. Definition plus worked examples, drawn from the RLR / Clinical Problem Solvers series and elsewhere. Source episodes are cited per example.

## Definition

Anchoring bias is the tendency to lock onto the first salient piece of information — a number, a symptom, a prior diagnosis, a triage label — and then under-adjust as new, contradicting data arrive. The initial value becomes a reference point the mind stays tethered to; subsequent evidence nudges the estimate a little but rarely enough. It was described by Tversky and Kahneman as "anchoring and adjustment": people start from an anchor and adjust insufficiently, so the final judgment is pulled toward wherever they began.

In clinical reasoning the anchor is usually one of a few things: an eye-catching abnormal lab, a dramatic vital sign, a referring or prior diagnosis, or the frame in which the case was handed off. The danger is not that the anchor is wrong — often it's a reasonable starting point — but that reassuring or contradicting data get absorbed into the anchored frame ("keep testing to confirm") instead of prompting a genuine reframe.

## How it operates

- **Adjustment is insufficient, not absent.** The clinician does update on new data, but the revisions are too small and orbit the original anchor rather than escaping it.
- **It compounds into [[Premature Closure]].** Once anchored, the search narrows; the encounter closes around confirming the anchor rather than staying open to alternatives.
- **It is fed by [[Framing Effect|framing]] and [[Availability Heuristic|availability]].** How a case is *presented* supplies the anchor (framing), and whatever is most top-of-mind — a recent case, an active outbreak — makes a particular anchor easier to grab (availability).
- **Salience drives it.** The more dramatic the datum (a sodium of 123, a heart rate of 27, an outbreak everyone is primed for), the stronger its gravitational pull.

## Examples from RLR episodes

- **Anchoring on a salient history detail.** A prior splenectomy plus headache and photophobia anchored the bedside clinician on ruling out meningitis, to the point of dominating the encounter and not letting the patient volunteer that he had abruptly quit six cups of coffee a day. Reassuring exam and labs prompted *more testing within the frame* (CT, then LP) rather than a return to open-ended history. Real diagnosis: caffeine-withdrawal headache. ([[RLR 001 - New Onset Headache|RLR 001]])
- **Anchoring on a prior diagnosis.** Known chronic pancreatitis, an ERCP stent, and an elevated alkaline phosphatase created momentum toward a biliary/pancreatic explanation, delaying consideration of a primary gastric process even after two normal lipase values. ([[RLR 002 - Nausea, Vomiting, and Hyponatremia|RLR 002]])
- **Anchoring on the eye-catching number.** Both hosts name the internist's trap of letting a low sodium consume the whole encounter — citing a patient whose real diagnosis was osteomyelitis and another whose sodium was incidental to a fungal vasculitis. The counter-discipline: confirm the number is actually worth the 20-minute detour before you take it. ([[RLR 003 - Hypertension and Severe Hyponatremia|RLR 003]])
- **Anchoring on an outbreak / the first positive test.** During an active mpox outbreak, the reflex "MSM + rash = mpox" was a strong anchor; the team deliberately kept HSV, VZV, and secondary syphilis in play, and a positive syphilis test did not close the search — which is how the mpox co-infection was still caught. ([[RLR 004 - Rectal Pain and Proctitis|RLR 004]])
- **Anchoring on a syndrome label.** The ED team locked onto [[BRASH Syndrome]] once a few ingredients lined up (beta blocker, mild AKI, bradycardia) and admitted to the ICU under that label — despite a normal potassium and hemodynamic stability that should have loosened the grip. Real mechanism: reflex bradycardia from urinary retention. ([[RLR 006 - Bradycardia and Urinary Retention|RLR 006]])
- **Anchoring on the disposition frame.** The case was handed off as a simple "replace the leaking G-tube and send him back," and the intern wanted to move on — nearly leaving an asymptomatic calcium of 11.5 unexamined. The whole case only opens once the discussant refuses that frame. ([[RLR 007 - Hypercalcemia|RLR 007]])

## Examples elsewhere

- **The original numeric anchor.** In Tversky and Kahneman's experiments, spinning a wheel of fortune to a number before asking an unrelated estimate (the percentage of African nations in the UN) shifted answers toward that arbitrary number — a pure demonstration that even a plainly irrelevant anchor pulls judgment.
- **Referral and triage labels.** A patient sent in as "rule out PE" or triaged as "abdominal pain" starts the encounter tethered to that frame; the label supplies an anchor before the clinician has seen a single datum.
- **The first abnormal result.** In a broad workup, the first value to come back abnormal often becomes the organizing hypothesis, even when later results fit a different story better.

## Counter-moves (de-anchoring)

- **Confirm the anchor is worth its weight before chasing it.** Ask whether the eye-catching number is severe enough, or the prior diagnosis relevant enough, to actually own the presentation ([[RLR 003 - Hypertension and Severe Hyponatremia|RLR 003]]).
- **When reassuring data arrive, reframe rather than re-test.** Normal labs in a high-prior patient are a cue to step back to open-ended history, not to escalate imaging within the same frame ([[RLR 001 - New Onset Headache|RLR 001]]).
- **Keep the differential explicitly open after the first positive.** Name alternatives out loud and let a positive test co-exist with them rather than end the search ([[RLR 004 - Rectal Pain and Proctitis|RLR 004]]).
- **Name the anchor to defuse it.** Several hosts state the tempting label explicitly *in order to dismiss it* — surfacing the anchor makes it something to test rather than accept ([[RLR 006 - Bradycardia and Urinary Retention|RLR 006]]).
