> [!summary] TL;DR — Water is good!
> - Thickened liquids reduce aspiration events on swallow studies, but do not improve clinically meaningful outcomes such as pneumonia or death, and increase risk for UTIs, dehydration, and hospitalization
> - Fluid restriction for mild-moderate CHF in the ambulatory setting does not improve clinical outcomes

## Things We Do For No Reason: Thickened Liquids

> [!cite] Source
> *J Hosp Med 2019 May;14(5):315-317*

> [!question] What are we talking about?
> Thickened liquids are commonly used to reduce aspiration in patients with dysphagia. But does reducing aspiration on imaging translate into better health outcomes?

> [!info] What is the proposed benefit?
> Videofluoroscopy studies show reduced aspiration rates:
>
> **Stroke/TBI patients:**
>
> | Consistency | Aspiration Rate |
> |---|---|
> | Thin | 21.6% |
> | Nectar | 10.5% |
> | Pudding | 5.3% |
>
> **Neurodegenerative disease:**
>
> | Consistency | Aspiration Rate |
> |---|---|
> | Thin | 16.2% |
> | Nectar | 8.3% |
> | Pudding | 2.9% |

> [!question] Does that translate to clinical benefit?
> Multiple RCTs and meta-analyses show **no reduction** in:
> - Incidence of pneumonia
> - Nutritional status
> - Mortality

> [!danger] Are there harms?
> - Reduced fluid intake → dehydration
> - Increased risk of:
>   - UTIs
>   - Aspiration pneumonia
>   - Hospitalization
> - Decreased quality of life
> - Poor taste (try it for at least a day!)
> - Decreased autonomy
> - Possible issues with medication absorption

## Fluid Restriction in CHF

> [!cite] Source
> Herrmann JJ et al. *Nat Med* 2025 Mar 30

> [!info] The study
> - 504 study participants with chronic CHF, no recent CHF hospitalization, NYHA Class II symptoms
> - Randomized to either guidance to limit fluid (<1.5L/day) or liberal fluid intake (no maximum)
> - Less fluid intake in the intervention group (1.48L/day vs 1.76L/day), but no worsening of heart failure symptoms or hospitalizations

> [!quote] Thoughts and Takeaways
> Thickened liquids are pretty unpalatable. I tried drinking nectar thick liquids for a week, and it was awful. So many things that you've always enjoyed become just gross — ready for your morning cup of coffee jelly?
>
> The only evidence we have of benefit is on swallow studies, where we find they do reduce aspiration. But we have evidence that they don't improve clinical outcomes. Why is that? My hypothesis is that perhaps you aspirate less frequently, but when you do aspirate the event is worse. Clearing water from the airway isn't very hard. Good luck clearing honey.
>
> In my practice, I rarely use thickened liquids, and I find most SLPs are now in agreement on this. If they are used, I monitor very closely for harms and am quick to discontinue.
>
> As for fluid restriction in CHF, this was always a weak recommendation in the guidelines. There have been recent studies in hospitalized patients showing worse outcomes — specifically, higher rates of rehospitalization. This makes sense to me: you want to dose your diuretic based on their typical volume intake at home. One by one, inpatient restrictive diets have gone by the wayside: restricted carbohydrate diets for diabetes, restricted fluid for CHF, restricted protein for CKD, etc. We are now accumulating evidence that these are not helpful in the ambulatory setting either.
>
> Water is life. Let your patients drink it. And let them drink it, well, watery.
