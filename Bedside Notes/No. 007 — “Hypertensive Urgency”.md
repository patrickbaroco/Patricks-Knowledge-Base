> [!summary] TL;DL
> - A hypertensive emergency involves end-organ damage. Examples would include stroke, ICH, or acute coronary syndrome
> - If there is no evidence of end-organ damage (the patient is at baseline) but the BP is >180/110, this used to be called "hypertensive urgency" but is now called "asymptomatic markedly elevated BP"
> - Hypertensive emergency is treated in the ICU
> - Asymptomatic markedly elevated BP does not require urgent treatment, and typically you would adjust routine BP meds

---

> [!question] Is there benefit to treating markedly elevated BP?
> - In the absence of acute symptoms, there is almost no benefit to treatment
> - In a study of 58,836 patients presenting to clinic with SBP >180 and/or DBP >110, 426 were referred to the hospital and only 100 were admitted. The primary outcome measured was MACE (major adverse cardiovascular events: MI, stroke, TIA)
> - At 7 days, rates of MACE were 0.1% in those sent home and 0.5% in those admitted
> - In those with SBP >220, rate of MACE was 0.2% of those sent home and 0% in those sent to the hospital

> [!warning] Is there harm to treating markedly elevated BP?
> - In a study of 47 patients treated acutely for hypertensive emergency with nitroprusside or nicardipine, 57% had overcorrection, and 2 had ischemic episodes associated with acute drop in BP
> - In the setting of chronic severe hypertension, the autoregulation of cerebral blood flow is impaired, and the brain is very sensitive to any drop in blood pressure
> - Treating acutely may lead to low BP at the time of scheduled medication administration, leading to the patient not getting their usual meds and thus becoming hypertensive again

> [!check] So what should you do?
> - In a study of 549 emergency department patients, 32% of them responded with a >20 mmHg drop in BP with just 30 minutes of rest
> - In that same population, after 4 hours the outcomes were the same for those treated with rest as for those treated with telmisartan
> - Look at scheduled BP meds. Consider giving somewhat earlier, and if BP is chronically poorly controlled then increase current regimen

> [!note] Thoughts and Takeaways
> One of my general rules of thumb is that if my patient feels fine, I can't make them feel better. And in the absence of end-organ injury, treating markedly elevated BP (even above 220 systolic) is generally just treating a number, with no benefit to the patient. I prescribe rest and a recheck in an hour. If we are approaching usual medication administration time, I'll give the AM or PM meds early, and if this is a chronic problem I'll escalate the regimen—increase doses or add a new agent. But in general, this is a situation calling for the rule, "Don't just do something, stand there!"
