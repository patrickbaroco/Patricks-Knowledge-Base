> [!summary] TL;DR
> - Consider stopping PPIs and statins in patients with a history of hepatic encephalopathy (HE)
> - Measurement of serum ammonia is not helpful in diagnosis or monitoring of HE
> - Asterixis is a key finding in HE; sustained hand-grip is an easier way to elicit it than the classic arms-out maneuver
> - Though lactulose and rifaximin remain the guideline-standard treatment of HE, polyethylene glycol (PEG, or MiraLax) is also effective and useful in patients who don't tolerate lactulose

## Risk Factors

> [!warning] Risk Factors for Hepatic Encephalopathy
> - Prior history of hepatic encephalopathy
> - Constipation
> - Infection
> - Hyponatremia
> - Type 2 diabetes
> - Medications: PPIs, statins, diuretics, and beta-blockers
>   - Diuretics and beta-blockers can't easily be discontinued in many patients with cirrhosis, but consider stopping PPIs and statins

## Role of Ammonia

> [!info] Ammonia Plays a Role — But Don't Measure It
> Ammonia crosses the blood-brain barrier, where astrocytes detoxify it by combining it with glutamate to form glutamine. Glutamine is osmotically active and draws water into astrocytes, causing cerebral edema. Decreased glutamate levels reduce neuronal firing, leading to depressed mental status or coma. Severely elevated ammonia correlates with worse prognosis, primarily in patients who are comatose.
>
> **However, measuring ammonia is not helpful in the diagnosis of HE:**
> - Ammonia is difficult to measure accurately: a tourniquet or clenched fist can increase levels, samples must be placed on ice immediately, and centrifuged within 15 minutes
> - While ammonia is usually elevated in HE, a normal level should prompt you to question the diagnosis — but you can have Grade 3 HE with a normal ammonia level
> - Patients may improve neurologically despite no change in ammonia levels
> - About two-thirds of patients with cirrhosis and normal mental status will nonetheless have an elevated ammonia level
>
> **HE remains a clinical diagnosis.**

## Asterixis

> [!note] Eliciting Asterixis
> Asterixis is "negative myoclonus": where myoclonus is the sudden, brief, involuntary *contraction* of a muscle at rest, asterixis is the sudden, brief, involuntary *relaxation* of a muscle under tension.
>
> It is classically elicited by asking the patient to hold their arms out with hands dorsiflexed and fingers extended: "hold your hands out like you're stopping a car." That can be very difficult for an encephalopathic patient. An easier approach: ask them to shake your hand, then grip tightly and don't let go. Asterixis will feel like the grip giving way in an almost spasmodic fashion.

## Treatment

> [!example] Treatment of Hepatic Encephalopathy
> **Lactulose** remains the mainstay of treatment: 15–30 mL two or three times daily, titrated to a goal of 2–3 soft bowel movements per day.
>
> **Rifaximin** (550 mg twice daily) is recommended in patients with HE refractory to lactulose. It is safe and well tolerated; the main concerns are antibiotic exposure risk and high cost.
>
> **Polyethylene glycol (PEG, MiraLax)** has been shown in multiple studies to be as effective as lactulose — and perhaps more effective in certain circumstances. Lactulose tastes sickly sweet and causes significant bloating and gas; PEG is a reasonable substitute. Dose at 17 g two or three times daily, with the same goal of 2–3 soft BMs per day. Do not titrate beyond 3 BMs daily — there is no good evidence for benefit, and patients will refuse.
>
> *PEG is not yet guideline therapy, but the evidence supports its use.*

## Thoughts and Takeaways

> [!quote] Takeaways
> HE is a clinical diagnosis. An elevated ammonia is not very informative, especially in settings where proper sample handling is unlikely. Asterixis (negative myoclonus) is genuinely helpful, and best elicited with the hand-grip technique. Lactulose is first-line, but it is difficult to take and causes bloating and gas from gut fermentation. PEG is a well-evidenced substitute. Don't push beyond 3 BMs per day: no evidence of benefit, and your patients will start refusing treatmen, and reasonably so.
