Lately it has seemed to me that medical practitioners are facing a moment similar to that faced by chess grandmasters when Deep Blue defeated Garry Kasparov in 1997. To see how accessible AI has made clinical knowledge, it is hard not to wonder what the role of the physician and our fellow clinicians will be in the not-so-distant future. Will we even have a role?

As a tonic to the anxiety that thought may bring, I recommend Kathryn Montgomery’s book, How Doctors Think. Dr. Montgomery, who is not a physician but rather a bioethicist and literary scholar, makes several arguments about the practice of medicine. In particular, she argues that in many ways the practice of medicine has less in common with science than it does with the practice of the trades (that of an electrician, for example). Medical practice, in her view, is not algorithmic. We do not simply apply rules: we interpret evidence in context, in a state of uncertainty, for individual patients. It is the application of medical knowledge (now more readily available thanks to AI) to the individual patient, and the navigation of the uncertainty inherent in a system as complex as a human being, that makes our role essential even in the face of advancing artificial intelligence.

So what does any of this have to do with curiosity? Everything. If medicine is not the execution of fixed rules but rather the continual interpretation of imperfect information, then curiosity is a prerequisite to clinical practice. It is curiosity that keeps us asking what doesn’t fit, what might be missing, and who this patient is beyond the data. In this era of cheap and instantaneous answers, the more valuable skill may be the disciplined habit of asking better questions.

William Osler wrote, “It is much more important to know what sort of a patient has a disease than to know what sort of a disease a patient has.” AI cannot help us with that. Curiosity will. Sometimes curiosity leads to the question that provides the diagnosis. Sometimes it gives us insight into the human being—the individual person—sitting in front of us. [In an essay by Dr. Faith Fitzgerald]([https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/ibrsrgqf5lmk8663ka3sn/Curiosity-medical-history-and-diagnosis.pdf?rlkey=e3oqlh6000h76rx8i3fc36inh&st=ta6dvrfi&dl=0]), she describes how a physician’s idle curiosity about a “social admit”—a patient deemed uninteresting and parked for the weekend—revealed a life history that reframed the entire encounter. The patient came to life as an individual rather than a disposition problem to solve.

Curiosity saves lives. If we are told a patient has nausea and vomiting and we prescribe ondansetron to treat it, we miss an opportunity for curiosity. Why is this patient vomiting? Perhaps we discover that the patient’s insulin was stopped in the hospital due to hypoglycemia. A chemistry panel then reveals hyperglycemia and acidosis. Now we find ourselves not with a case of nausea and vomiting, but with a patient in diabetic ketoacidosis and in urgent need of our help. It is no exaggeration to say that this patient’s life depends on our curiosity.

AI will continue to improve. It will retrieve facts faster, synthesize evidence more completely, and offer recommendations with increasing sophistication. But it will not replace what lies at the center of medical practice: a curious clinician, sitting with uncertainty, attentive to what does not quite make sense, and focused on the individual patient. The danger before us is not that machines will think better than we do, but that we will stop thinking as deeply because the answers arrive so easily.

If Montgomery is right—and medicine is a practice of judgment rather than a science of rules—then curiosity is not optional. It is how we remain clinicians rather than technicians. It is how we resist premature closure, sustain lifelong learning, and keep clinical judgment alive in the face of increasingly capable tools.

In the end, the question is not whether AI will change medicine; it already has. The question is whether it will make us less curious, or demand that we become more so. I believe it is the latter, and that this is a clarifying moment for medical practice. What will best define clinical excellence in this new era is a curiosity that allows us to navigate uncertainty and to care for patients as individuals, not abstractions.
