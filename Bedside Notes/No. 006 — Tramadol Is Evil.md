*15 August 2025*

> [!abstract] TL;DR
> - Tramadol is a prodrug, converted by CYP2D6 from an SNRI to an opioid.
> - CYP2D6 expression is highly variable: ~2–5% of the population has no CYP2D6 activity (tramadol has no opioid effect), and 5–10% are ultrarapid metabolizers at high risk for overdose.
> - Tramadol increases risk of hypoglycemia — in diabetics and non-diabetics alike.
> - Tramadol lowers seizure threshold and may be the drug most likely to cause new-onset seizures.
> - 50 mg of tramadol is about 3–4 mg of oxycodone. If you want a low-dose opioid, just use 2.5 mg of oxycodone.

## Why Is the Pharmacology of Tramadol Problematic?

> [!info] Prodrug mechanism
> Tramadol in its unmodified form is essentially an SNRI, similar to venlafaxine. It is metabolized in the liver by CYP2D6 to O-desmethyltramadol (M1), which is the opioid-active metabolite. CYP2D6 is the same enzyme that converts codeine — which is also inert — to morphine.

> [!warning] CYP2D6 variability
> CYP2D6 expression is highly variable:
> - Up to 5% of the population (higher in Asian ethnicities) has no CYP2D6 expression — tramadol acts only as an SNRI in these patients.
> - 5–10% are ultrarapid metabolizers, at high risk for opioid overdose.
> - In Ethiopia, approximately 1 in 3 people are ultrarapid metabolizers; codeine is illegal there due to overdose risk, and tramadol is highly abused.
>
> M1 levels at 3 hours post-dose by metabolizer phenotype:
> - Poor metabolizers: 0–5 ng/mL
> - Heterozygotes: 20 ng/mL
> - Normal: 40 ng/mL
> - Ultrarapid metabolizers: 70 ng/mL
>
> In practice, you are giving a patient some degree of an SNRI, some degree of an opioid, or possibly one and not the other — and you have no way to know which.

## Side Effects

> [!danger] Seizure risk
> Tramadol lowers the seizure threshold:
> - Danish study (232 ED seizure patients): 62 were tramadol-induced.
> - Australian study (98 first-seizure patients): 8 were due to tramadol.
> - Iranian study (356 patients hospitalized for new-onset seizures): 33 were due to tramadol.

> [!danger] Hypoglycemia
> In a study of 2,927 patients given tramadol, hypoglycemia occurred in:
> - 46.8% of patients with type 1 diabetes
> - 16.8% of patients with type 2 diabetes
> - 4.7% of patients without diabetes

> [!warning] Serotonin syndrome and drug interactions
> - Tramadol is the opioid most commonly associated with serotonin syndrome when combined with other serotonergic medications. Overall risk is low, but the association is real.
> - SSRIs such as fluoxetine and paroxetine are potent CYP2D6 inhibitors. Starting fluoxetine in a patient on high-dose tramadol can precipitate opioid withdrawal — suddenly the patient is receiving an SNRI instead of an opioid.

## Takeaways

> [!note] Clinical bottom line
> I essentially never prescribe tramadol. A PharmD once described it as handing a patient a bowl of oxycodone and venlafaxine and asking them to take three random pills. That may make for a great Saturday night in college, but it is a dumb way to practice medicine.
>
> I have personally admitted three patients to the ICU on dextrose drips from tramadol-induced hypoglycemia. And far too many of my patients have structurally abnormal brains for me to be comfortable with the seizure risk.
>
> If I want to give a patient a low-dose opioid, I use 2.5 mg of oxycodone. If they need something scheduled, I use 5 mcg/hr of transdermal buprenorphine.
