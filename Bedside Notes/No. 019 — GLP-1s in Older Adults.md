## Wonderful Drugs, Used Wisely

Every few years a class of medications arrives that genuinely changes practice: ACE inhibitors in the '80s, HAART in the '90s, the biologics in the 2000s, checkpoint inhibitors in the 2010s, and GLP-1 receptor agonists may prove to be the class that defines the 2020s. What began as diabetes drugs, and then weight-loss drugs, have become cardiovascular, renal, and heart-failure drugs. Our patients often have any combination of these concerns, and so prescribing in older adults is climbing accordingly.

This is not a "beware the hype" issue. These are excellent medications for many of our patients, and my goal here is not to talk anyone out of them. They help people eat less and lose weight, and from this come their benefits, but also two liabilities that our population is at particular risk for. Both are subtle, both are preventable, and neither is a reason to abandon this genuinely helpful class of medications. They are reasons to prescribe it more attentively.

> [!summary] TL;DR
> - GLP-1s reduce major adverse cardiovascular events (SELECT), slow kidney disease (FLOW), and *improve* symptoms and physical function in obesity-related HFpEF, including in older and frailer patients (STEP-HFpEF). Don't reflexively avoid them in older adults.
> - **Thiamine / Wernicke's encephalopathy** has become a real, if rare, signal. Rapid weight loss plus poor intake and vomiting depletes thiamine. Onset is typically 3–6 months in. Recognize it, and give **IV thiamine before glucose.**
> - **Muscle loss** is universal. 25–39% of the weight lost is lean mass. In a patient already sarcopenic, that matters more than the number on the scale. Protect it with protein, resistance activity, and adequate calories — not by stopping the drug.
> - When muscle loss is the dominant worry but you still want cardiorenal protection, an **SGLT2 inhibitor** may be the better-matched drug for real heart and kidney benefit, minimal weight loss, and no appetite suppression.

## First, Why These Are Good Drugs

> [!success] What GLP-1s do for our patients
> - **SELECT** — in overweight/obese adults *without* diabetes and with established CV disease, semaglutide reduced major adverse cardiovascular events by ~20%.
> - **FLOW** — in type 2 diabetes with CKD, semaglutide reduced kidney disease progression and major kidney events by ~24%.
> - **STEP-HFpEF** — in obesity-related heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, semaglutide improved KCCQ symptom scores, 6-minute walk distance, and weight. Prespecified analyses found the benefit **consistent across the age spectrum and preserved in the frailest patients.**

Even in our patient population–older, functionally limited, frail HFpEF patients– semaglutide *improved* function. So as I move on to some recently published concerns, I am talking about how to use a beneficial drug appropriately, not about a reason to avoid it altogether.

## Weight Loss: A Double-Edged Sword

Both of the cautions in this issue come from the same place. GLP-1s work by suppressing appetite and slowing gastric emptying. The intended result is that people eat less and lose weight. The unintended results are that some people eat *too* little, and effectively become malnourished. This can lead to vitamin deficiencies and to excess loss of muscle mass even to a point of impairing function.

## Thiamine and Wernicke's Encephalopathy

Thiamine stores last only a couple of weeks. Reduced intake, nausea, and especially persistent vomiting can deplete them faster than many clinicians realize. Further, the classic triggers we're trained to look for (alcohol, hyperemesis) aren't the only routes there.

> [!danger] The signal
> A 2026 pharmacovigilance analysis (from VigiBase and published reports) identified 21 cases of Wernicke's encephalopathy in patients on GLP-1s, with odds ratios as high as ~10 for semaglutide and ~11 for tirzepatide. All had persistent vomiting and low serum thiamine. Onset clustered at **3–6 months** after starting or escalating the dose, in the setting of rapid loss (one patient lost 30 kg in 12 weeks).

The absolute risk is low, and 21 cases amongst tens of millions of prescriptions should keep this in perspective. But Wernicke's is a diagnosis you cannot afford to miss, because the window to reverse it is short and the cost of missing it is a permanent and devastating amnestic syndrome.

> [!warning] Why it's easy to miss in our patients
> The classic triad of confusion, ataxia, and ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus is easy to miss in our 84 year-old patient with cognitive impairment. Confusion gets called "her dementia." Unsteadiness gets called "her baseline gait." A patient on a GLP-1 who becomes acutely encephalopathic is a patient in whom "she's just declining" is the non-diagnosis we anchor on. This is a moment for clinical curiosity: ask *why* is this patient confused? What changed? What doesn't fit? (See [[No. 012 — Curiosity]].)

> [!tip] What to do
> - **Treat before you confirm.** If you suspect it, give **IV thiamine before any glucose**. A glucose load in a thiamine-deficient patient can precipitate or worsen Wernicke's. Treatment doses are high: 500mg IV three times daily. A thiamine level will typically take 2-3 days to result, so if you are concerned, don't withhold treatment while waiting for the lab. If IV thiamine cannot be given in your facility, IM thiamine can be given (up to 250mg), but in a patient with severe or progressive neurological symptoms I would consider transfer to hospital.
> - **Prophylaxis for the high-risk patient.** In a patient with persistent vomiting, very rapid loss, or poor baseline intake, a course of oral thiamine is cheap, safe, and reasonable while things settle (after stopping the GLP-1).
> - **Educate up front.** At every start and dose increase, the counseling should include not just "expect some nausea" but "keep eating adequately and stay hydrated even when your appetite is gone." Nausea should improve after a week or two, and appetite should stabilize. If it doesn't, then decrease the dose or discontinue the medication.
> - **Titrate slowly.** Most cases followed aggressive dosing and fast loss. Slower up-titration is not just for GI tolerance; it's a nutritional safeguard.


## Sarcopenia

> [!info] What the body composition data show
> Across semaglutide and tirzepatide studies, lean body mass accounts for roughly **25–39% of total weight lost.** As a percentage this resembles diet-based weight loss, but because GLP-1s produce so much more total loss, the absolute muscle lost is larger. A patient who loses 20%+ of body weight may lose 6–8 kg of lean tissue without intervention.

In a younger patient with abundant muscle, losing some of muscle alongside fat is often fine. In fact, as STEP-HFpEF reminds us, function can still improve despite this muscle loss. The concern is specific to the patient who is *already* sarcopenic. Age-related muscle loss plus drug-associated muscle loss can cross the threshold from losing weight to losing strength, and weakness is what drives falls, dependency, and loss of function.

> [!tip] Protect the muscle, and know when to stop
> Often the answer to muscle-loss risk is not to stop an otherwise beneficial drug, but to prescribe the things that preserve lean mass alongside it:
> - **Protein.** Aim higher in older adults, on the order of 1.4–1.8 g/kg ideal body weight daily, spread across meals rather than loaded once. Involve our dietitian colleagues in higher-risk patients.
> - **Resistance activity.** Even modest strength work meaningfully blunts lean mass loss. In LTC and AL, this is a good time to bring in PT/OT and restorative programs.
> - **Monitor function, not just weight.** Grip strength, sit-to-stand, and gait speed are more sensitive early markers than the scale, and loss of function should be a signal to change course. Therapy can raise the flag early if they are involved.
>
> But protection has limits, and sometimes the answer *is* to stop. A patient who develops true sarcopenia probably should not stay on a GLP-1, and what you do next depends on why they were on it in the first place.

> [!question] Match the tool to the goal
> When sarcopenia is progressing, the next move depends on *why* the patient is on the drug:
> - **Weight loss was the goal:** In a frail patient now losing muscle, the drug is doing net harm. Stop it.
> - **Cardiorenal or heart-failure protection was the goal:** An SGLT2 inhibitor delivers much of the benefit with far less catabolic cost (see below).
> - **Diabetic control was the goal:** Relax the target first. An A1c of 7.5–8.5% is fine in a frail older adult, even up to 9% in more advanced frailty. Hypoglycemia is the real enemy, so easing the target may let you simplify the regimen. If you still need glucose-lowering, trade the GLP-1 for a weight-neutral, hypoglycemia-sparing agent such as a DPP4 or even low-dose insulin, which is *anabolic*, compared with the catabolic nature of GLP-1 agonists.
>
> And before pinning it all on the drug, be curious about the muscle loss itself. Progressive wasting in an older adult has a differential (occult malignancy, depression, thyroid disease, heart failure), and the GLP-1 is a plausible contributor, not an automatic answer.

> [!tip] Consider an SGLT2 inhibitor instead
> If sarcopenia is the real worry but you still want the cardiac and renal protection, an SGLT2 inhibitor may be the better tool. Empagliflozin and dapagliflozin carry robust benefit in HFpEF (EMPEROR-Preserved, DELIVER) and in CKD (DAPA-CKD, EMPA-KIDNEY), and their cardiorenal effect is largely independent of weight. They produce only modest weight loss (~2–3 kg, mostly fluid and fat) and don't suppress appetite, so they sidestep both the thiamine and the muscle problems of GLP-1s. They aren't risk-free in frail elders (watch volume status, fungal genitourinary infections, and euglycemic DKA) but for the patient needing organ protection without the catabolism of GLP-1s, they're often the smarter first choice. The two classes are also complementary; there's no rule that says it has to be one or the other, and using an SGLT2 inhibitor can allow a lower, and perhaps safer, dose of a GLP-1.

## Thoughts and Takeaways

The message here is not to stop prescribing GLP-1s. They treat a range of conditions that afflict our patients with significant morbidity and in the right patients they can improve health and function. The key is patient selection and attention to the potential risks. Be proactive in taking steps to mitigate those risks with dietary interventions, involvement of therapy where indicated, and keeping in mind the very small but important risk of Wernicke’s, especially when the patient becomes ill for other reasons and compounds the risk.

> [!important] Bottom Line
> Keep prescribing GLP-1s where they're indicated. The benefits are real and even in older adults they can be dramatic. But use them the way you'd use any powerful drug in a frail patient: titrate carefully, protect nutrition and muscle actively, watch function rather than just weight, and treat Wernicke's the moment you suspect it.
