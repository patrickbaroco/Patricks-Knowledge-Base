> [!summary]
> - First, decide on the goal based on functional status and prognosis (but **not** solely on age)
> - Start a standard-dose single agent from first-line classes, typically an ARB, CCB, or thiazide
> - If not at goal, add a second agent instead of increasing the dose of the first
> - Remember that losartan has a shorter duration of action, and so substituting a long-acting ARB (olmesartan or telmisartan) may help achieve control without adding new agents
> - If BP is near goal but not at goal yet, consider whether new agents need to be added or if it is safer to adjust the goal
> - Remember to document and code for frailty (ICD-10 code R54) if you are allowing a higher goal due to frailty

## Recommended Goals

> [!success] Robust (IL / high-function AL)
> → Independent, cognitively intact
>
> **Target: <130 mmHg** (if tolerated)

> [!info] Intermediate (typical AL, some LTC)
> → Mild–moderate frailty
>
> **Target: 130–140 mmHg** (≤150 acceptable if high fall risk)

> [!warning] Frail (many LTC)
> → ADL-dependent
>
> **Target: 140–150 mmHg**

> [!danger] Advanced frailty / limited prognosis
> → Bedbound, advanced dementia
>
> **Target: 150–160 mmHg** or symptom-based

## How to Get There

> [!note] First-line medications for essential hypertension
> - Thiazide diuretics (HCTZ, chlorthalidone)
> - ACE inhibitors / ARBs
> - Dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (amlodipine)
> - Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (spironolactone, eplerenone)
>
> All of these address the root cause of essential hypertension, which is arterial stiffening resulting in increased vascular resistance.

> [!caution] Other options
> - **Beta-blockers:** Not first-line as they do not directly address vascular resistance. Do not use unless other classes are contraindicated, or when there is another indication (a-fib, HFrEF).
> - **Hydralazine:** Very short half-life leads to very labile blood pressures. It is a vasodilator and causes significant edema and a reflex tachycardia, and has higher fall risk than other classes.
> - **Clonidine:** Oral clonidine should almost never be used. Its duration of action is as short as 6 hours, and even a single missed dose can result in severe rebound hypertension, tachycardia, and anxiety. As a patch, this problem is avoided, and transdermal clonidine is an option when first-line medications are not tolerated or contraindicated.

> [!tip] Low-dose combinations >> high-dose single med
> From a baseline SBP ~154 mmHg:
> - Standard-dose monotherapy → ↓ 8.7 mmHg
> - Doubling the dose → ↓ additional 1.5 mmHg
> - Adding a second drug → ↓ 14.9 mmHg total
>
> If BP is not controlled on one standard-dose agent, don't increase the dose: add a second low- or standard-dose agent. This not only increases the likelihood of reaching goal BP, but also reduces the risk of adverse drug effects (falls, electrolyte abnormalities, etc.).
>
> Combination pills are available with ACE/ARB + HCTZ, ARB + CCB, and triple combos with olmesartan or telmisartan + amlodipine + HCTZ. Although combination pills cost somewhat more (an extra $10–50 per month), they reduce pill burden and reduce the risk of medication errors.
>
> A good rule of thumb: *if a patient is on 4 medications for hypertension, one of them should always be spironolactone* (unless there is a contraindication).

> [!question] What's the problem with losartan?
> Losartan is the cheapest ARB, but has a fairly short half-life and the duration of action is highly variable: sometimes as short as 10–12 hours. In patients with labile blood pressures, or not controlled on losartan, consider switching to a longer-acting ARB (olmesartan or telmisartan). The cost is modestly higher, but efficacy can be much better.
>
> Given the lower cost of losartan, it is reasonable to start with losartan (especially if using a combination of losartan + HCTZ). But remember the shorter duration of action, and be ready to switch if needed.

## What About Fall Risk?

> [!warning] Fall risk and BP medications
> Fall risk is associated with *initiation* and *escalation* of BP meds, but not as closely associated with final BP. There *may* be a modest increased risk of falls for SBP <130 in frail patients, but the real risk is when you start or increase a medication. That risk persists for at least 2 weeks.
>
> So the question to ask when looking at a BP of 142 is whether it is worth the risk of increasing medications to achieve a BP of 135. In a frail patient at higher fall risk, it may not be.

## ACO Implications

> [!example] Documentation tips
> When documenting blood pressures, don't document "old" blood pressures unless clinically relevant, especially if they are elevated and not reflective of their general control. (A separate newsletter on this is coming later in the year.)
>
> If the goal is liberalized due to frailty and fall risk, ensure that you document frailty in your note and code for it (R54, ICD-10). This will help exclude the patient from the ACO measure.
