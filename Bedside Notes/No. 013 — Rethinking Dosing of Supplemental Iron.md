> [!summary] Summary
> - Iron should be dosed every other day, which improves absorption and reduces GI side effects
> - In patients with iron-deficiency anemia (IDA), wean or discontinue proton-pump inhibitors
> - When oral iron fails, IV iron should be considered early

> [!info] Role of hepcidin in iron dosing
> Though iron is necessary for life, the human body has no way to excrete excess iron. Since it cannot be excreted, excess iron is stored in tissues such as the liver, heart, pancreas, and joints. This leads to cirrhosis, diabetes, cardiomyopathy, and is lethal if not treated (usually with therapeutic phlebotomy). Thus, it is imperative that the body not absorb more iron than is needed. This is managed by a hormone called hepcidin. When iron is absorbed in the GI tract, hepcidin is released by the liver and degrades the transporter needed for GI absorption. Thus, when you eat a meal with a lot of iron in it, you absorb some of that iron and then the body (via hepcidin) shuts down further absorption for at least the next 24 hours.

> [!tip] Iron should be dosed every other day
> The role of hepcidin is meaningful when it comes to dosing oral iron. If you give a dose of oral iron, the body will absorb some of that, and then hepcidin will inhibit further absorption for at least the next day. Thus, dosing iron more than once a day actually *inhibits* overall absorption, and leads to many of the GI side effects of iron (such as constipation). Studies have shown that dosing iron once daily or even on alternate days leads to similar or better repletion of iron stores as dosing twice a day. Further, it is better tolerated, with less constipation in particular.

> [!warning] Iron needs acid for absorption, so stop the PPI
> Iron absorption is also dependent on the acidity of the duodenum. In an acidic environment, iron remains soluble and thus absorbable. Additionally, acid converts iron into a more absorbable form, and increases the activity of the transporter that gets iron from the intestine into the blood. Thus, medications that reduce the acidity of the duodenum—such as PPIs and H2-blockers—reduce the absorption of iron significantly. These should be weaned in dose or discontinued.

> [!note] IV iron should be considered early if oral iron fails
> If oral iron has had no benefit after 4 weeks (increase of Hgb by 1 g/dL), it is reasonable to consider IV iron. Though much more expensive, it is very effective. This is especially useful in patients with slow, chronic GI blood loss, or patients with post-operative anemia who do not improve with oral iron. IV iron is well-tolerated, and though expensive, it is typically covered under Medicare Part B as a medical treatment at an infusion center.

> [!caution] Iron deficiency anemia is never normal
> Lastly, remember that IDA is never normal. Though we often see anemia in older adults in LTC because of chronic illnesses (e.g., CHF and CKD) that can cause anemia, IDA is GI blood loss until proven otherwise, and unless goals of care or overall health state dictate otherwise a GI evaluation should be pursued. Malabsorption is the other common cause of IDA, and in our patient population, PPIs are a major culprit. Always consider discontinuing these, and it may prove the supplemental iron is no longer needed.
