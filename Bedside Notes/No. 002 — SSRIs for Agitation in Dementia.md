>[!tldr] **TL;DR** 
>Escitalopram 15 mg daily did not reduce agitation in patients with dementia any more than placebo. This is the 7th study evaluating SSRIs for agitation in dementia: 5 were negative, and 2 showed modest benefit but came with cardiac and cognitive harm.

>[!danger]
>**The real dangers of SSRIs in dementia:**
> - **Cognitive decline** — ~0.2–0.75 MMSE points lost per year of use
> - **Fractures** — ~18% higher risk at low dose, ~25% higher at high dose
> - **Mortality** — dose-dependent: ~7% increased risk at low dose, ~18% at high dose

## Escitalopram for Management of Agitation in Dementia

S-CitAD trial ([PMID: 31587995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31587995/)), Nature Medicine, March 2025

- 173 patients with Alzheimer's disease and significant agitation who did not respond to psychosocial interventions
- Treated with up to 15 mg of escitalopram or placebo for 12 weeks

> ❌ **No significant difference in clinically meaningful agitation at 12 weeks.** QT prolongation was noted in the escitalopram group, and the placebo response rate was ~30%.

## Risks of SSRI Use in Dementia

Antidepressant Use and Cognitive Decline in Patients with Dementia ([PMID: 39994788](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39994788/)), BMC Medicine, February 2025

- Swedish national cohort study involving 18,740 patients with dementia
- SSRI use resulted in an additional decline of 0.39 MMSE points per year, on average
	- Worst: escitalopram (0.76 points/year)
	- Best: SNRIs and TCAs (no effect), mirtazapine (0.19-point decline/year)

Antidepressant use (primarily SSRIs) was also associated with:

| Outcome | Low Dose | High Dose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mortality | ~7% increase | ~18% increase |
| Fracture | ~18% increase | ~25% increase |
| Severe dementia | — | ~35% increase |

## Thoughts and Takeaways

I hate to be wrong, and for that reason I hated these studies: for years I have used (and suggested others use!) escitalopram as a front-line agent in my attempts to manage agitation associated with dementia.

There had been some weak evidence supporting the use of citalopram, but there were adverse cognitive and cardiac effects with citalopram. I assumed escitalopram might be safer, equally (even if poorly) effective, and that the general safety profile of SSRIs was good.

These studies suggest it is not effective, and it is not safer. Worse, the Swedish study suggests the risks are a class effect, and not limited to citalopram or escitalopram.

These are not perfect studies. The S-CitAD study was fairly small and underpowered. It is possible it missed some small benefit, but it is unlikely there is a true clinically meaningful effect.

The authors of the Swedish study made heroic efforts to control for confounding variables, but it would be impossible to account for all of them. However, the consistency of the findings — and the apparent dose-response relationship — suggests that the harms are real and likely attributable, at least in significant part, to the drugs themselves.

Unfortunately, these two studies, together with multiple other negative trials of SSRIs for agitation associated with dementia, suggest that SSRIs in these patients are neither effective nor safe.

> 🎯 **Clinical pearl:** The ~30% placebo response in S-CitAD is a reminder that when we see improvement, we cannot simply assume it's due to the medication. Combined with the safety concerns from the Swedish study, this is a strong argument for attempting gradual dose reductions (GDRs) — even when we believe the patient has benefited.
