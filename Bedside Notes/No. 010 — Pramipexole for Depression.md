
> [!summary]
> - Pramipexole (a dopamine agonist) has shown efficacy in treatment of depression in Parkinson's disease independent of its benefit on motor symptoms (bradykinesia or tremor)
> - Pramipexole has shown efficacy as adjuvant treatment for unipolar depression
> - Tolerability is less than that of SSRI/SNRI, with nausea, headache, and dizziness being most common

## Pramipexole for Treatment of Depression in Parkinson's

> [!info] Source
> *The Lancet Neurology*, 2010 — double-blind, placebo-controlled

- Patient population all had Parkinson's; approximately 75% were on carbidopa-levodopa and 25% on amantadine
- Intervention: placebo versus pramipexole dosed at 0.125–1mg TID based on tolerability
- Efficacy measure: decrease in Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) score
	- Minimal clinically important difference for BDI is ~5

> [!success] Result
> - Pramipexole group: BDI decreased by 5.9
> - Placebo group: BDI decreased by 4.0

- Was improvement driven by better motor control?
	- Path analysis suggests ~80% of the depression improvement was a direct antidepressant effect, not attributable to motor symptom improvement

> [!warning] Tolerability
> No individual side effects reached statistical significance between groups, but 39% of the pramipexole group withdrew versus 16% of the placebo group. Most common side effects were nausea and headache.

## Pramipexole Augmentation for Treatment-Resistant Unipolar Depression

> [!info] Source
> *The Lancet Psychiatry*, 2025 — double-blind, placebo-controlled

- Patient population: community-dwelling, ~40% male / ~60% female; all on an antidepressant (vast majority SSRI or SNRI)
- Intervention: placebo versus pramipexole with a target dose of 2.5mg daily
	- Mean achieved dose at 12 weeks: 2.3mg
- Efficacy measure: decrease in QIDS-SR16
	- Minimal clinically important difference for QIDS-SR16 is ~5
	- Mean baseline QIDS-SR16: 16 (severe depression)

> [!success] Result
> - Pramipexole group: QIDS-SR16 decreased by 6.4
> - Placebo group: QIDS-SR16 decreased by 2.4
> - Curves began to separate at 2–3 weeks, suggesting relatively rapid onset
> - Effect sustained through the 48-week observation period

> [!warning] Tolerability
> - 20% dropout in pramipexole group versus 5% in placebo
> - Nausea: 67% vs. 41%
> - Headache: 63% vs. 49%
> - Dizziness: 56% vs. 36%

## Thoughts and Takeaways

Treatment-resistant depression is among the more difficult problems in LTC medicine, and one where psychiatry consultation, when available, is invaluable. These two trials together make a reasonable case for pramipexole at different points in the clinical algorithm.

In **Parkinson's with comorbid depression**, the tolerability of SSRIs remains superior, but pramipexole offers the advantage of targeting both motor and affective symptoms simultaneously. In a patient with well-controlled motor symptoms, I would still start an SSRI. In a patient with inadequately controlled motor symptoms and significant depression, pramipexole is worth considering first.

For **treatment-resistant unipolar depression**, augmentation with atypical antipsychotics remains the best-studied approach, but in LTC this is often complicated: by dementia, by Parkinson's, and by the regulatory environment around antipsychotic use. Pramipexole is a reasonable alternative with a favorable safety profile in this population. One useful clinical heuristic from the 2025 trial: benefit typically emerges within 2–4 weeks; if there is no meaningful response by week 6, it is unlikely to be effective.

> [!note] My Approach in Brief
> 1. Start with an SSRI or SNRI
> 2. If inadequate response, add bupropion or mirtazapine depending on patient characteristics
> 3. If still refractory, consider augmentation: atypical antipsychotic, liothyronine, or pramipexole
> 4. In patients with very limited prognosis, methylphenidate deserves earlier consideration
> 5. Lithium is best reserved for those with subspecialty support

> [!tip] Bottom Line
> Pramipexole is not a first-line agent, but it earns a place in the LTC depression toolkit, particularly where antipsychotics carry outsized risk.
