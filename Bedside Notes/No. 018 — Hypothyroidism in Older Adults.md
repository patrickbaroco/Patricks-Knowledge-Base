## Don't Chase the Number

> [!summary]
> - The "normal" TSH range used in most labs (0.5–4.5 mIU/L) is derived from younger populations and does not apply to frail older adults
> - TSH naturally rises with age; a TSH of 5–7 mIU/L in an 80-year-old may be entirely appropriate
> - Over-treatment of hypothyroidism is common in LTC and carries real risks: atrial fibrillation, bone loss, falls, and cognitive decline
> - Before increasing levothyroxine, ask: is this patient symptomatic? Is the TSH actually high for their age?
> - In frail older adults, a reasonable TSH target is 2–6 mIU/L; in advanced frailty or limited prognosis, an even higher TSH may be acceptable
> - If TSH is over-suppressed (<1 mIU/L, or even higher in more frail patients), this should almost always prompt a dose reduction

## Why the Standard TSH Reference Range Doesn't Apply to LTC

> [!note]
> The TSH reference range printed on most lab reports — typically 0.5–4.5 mIU/L — is derived from studies of younger, healthy adults. TSH rises with age as a normal physiological change. Large population studies, including NHANES data, have shown that the median TSH in adults over 80 is closer to 2–3 mIU/L, and the upper limit of "normal" for this age group may be as high as 7–8 mIU/L.
>
> When we see a TSH of 5.5 in an 85-year-old and reflexively increase the levothyroxine dose, we may be treating a lab value rather than a patient. The more important questions are:
> - Is the patient symptomatic? (fatigue, cold intolerance, new or worsened constipation, weight gain, cognitive slowing)
> - Has the TSH been stable over time, or is it rising?
> - What is the clinical context? Has there been a recent illness, concern of non-adherence, or new medications that could affect levothyroxine absorption?

## The Risks of Over-Suppression

> [!warning] Atrial fibrillation
> Exogenous thyroid hormone excess — even subclinical hyperthyroidism from over-treatment — is an independent risk factor for atrial fibrillation. A suppressed TSH (<0.1 mIU/L) is associated with a 3-fold increased risk of a-fib in older adults. In a population already at elevated cardiovascular risk, this is not a trivial concern.

> [!warning] Bone loss and fracture risk
> Subclinical hyperthyroidism accelerates bone turnover and reduces bone mineral density, particularly in postmenopausal women. This translates into increased fracture risk, compounding the already elevated fall risk in LTC patients.

> [!warning] Falls and cardiovascular stress
> Excess thyroid hormone increases heart rate, reduces diastolic filling time, and can provoke or worsen heart failure. In frail patients, even subtle increases in resting heart rate contribute to exercise intolerance and fall risk.

> [!warning] Cognitive effects
> Both hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism impair cognition in older adults. Over-treatment can cause anxiety, insomnia, and agitation. These symptoms may be misattributed to dementia or behavioral disturbance, and inappropriately treated with additional medications.

## Reasonable TSH Targets by Functional Status

> [!success] Robust (community-dwelling, high-function AL)
> → **Target TSH: 1–4 mIU/L**
> → Standard dosing generally appropriate; monitor annually

> [!info] Intermediate (typical AL, mild–moderate frailty)
> → **Target TSH: 2–6 mIU/L**
> → Prioritize symptom assessment over the lab value; avoid dose escalation for asymptomatic mild TSH elevation

> [!caution] Frail (LTC, ADL-dependent)
> → **Target TSH: 3–7 mIU/L**
> → A mildly elevated TSH in an asymptomatic frail patient does not require treatment escalation

> [!danger] Advanced frailty / limited prognosis
> → **Target TSH: Accept up to 8–10 mIU/L if asymptomatic**
> → Consider whether continuing levothyroxine aligns with goals of care; symptomatic management takes priority

## When TSH Is Suppressed: Act

> [!tip] A suppressed TSH (based on goals for age) should almost always prompt dose reduction
> Suppressed TSH in a patient on levothyroxine means they are being over-treated, and it should prompt a reduction in dose.
>
> **Practical approach:**
> - If on a round dose (e.g., 100mcg daily), reduce by 12.5–25mcg and recheck TSH in 6–8 weeks
> - If dosing is already low, consider alternate-day dosing or switching to a lower available tablet strength
> - Do not wait for the next annual lab — a suppressed TSH in a frail older adult is a reason to deprescribe or de-escalate in the current visit
>
> Remember: in older adults, the margin between therapeutic and toxic is narrower, and the consequences of over-treatment are serious.

## A Note on Initiating or Continuing Levothyroxine

> [!note] Starting levothyroxine in older adults
> In younger patients, hypothyroidism is almost always treated. In frail older adults, the calculus is more nuanced. Subclinical hypothyroidism (elevated TSH with normal free T4, no symptoms) in adults over 80 has not been shown to benefit from treatment in randomized trials. The TRUST trial found no improvement in symptoms, quality of life, or cognition with levothyroxine versus placebo in older adults with subclinical hypothyroidism.
>
> Before initiating levothyroxine for a mildly elevated TSH in an older patient, ask:
> - Is the patient symptomatic?
> - Is the free T4 normal or low?
> - Is this a new finding, or has TSH been mildly elevated for years without progression?
>
> If the answers suggest watchful waiting, that is a defensible and evidence-based choice.

> [!note] Continuing levothyroxine in advanced dementia or limited prognosis
> For patients with advanced dementia or limited life expectancy, continuing levothyroxine warrants the same scrutiny as any other medication. If the original indication was subclinical hypothyroidism, or if the patient has been stable for years on a low dose, a trial of discontinuation may be appropriate, particularly if pill administration is difficult or goals of care have shifted toward comfort.

> [!tip] Once-weekly dosing
> Levothyroxine has a half-life of approximately 7 days, which makes it pharmacokinetically suitable for once-weekly dosing. Several small trials have shown comparable TSH control with a weekly dose equal to 7× the usual daily dose. The evidence base is modest and this is not a mainstream guideline recommendation, but it is a legitimate option in select situations. I have found it helpful for patients with adherence challenges or high pill burden where eliminating a daily medication has meaningful value.
>
> In LTC and AL settings where medications are administered by staff, daily dosing is usually straightforward and weekly dosing offers less advantage. But it is worth knowing this option exists.

## Conclusion

> [!summary]
> Hypothyroidism is common in older adults, and levothyroxine is one of the most frequently prescribed medications in LTC. But frequency of use does not mean it is always used well. Doses that were appropriate at 65 continue unchanged at 85, in a patient who is now frail, losing weight, and at risk for a-fib.
>
> Check the TSH. If it is suppressed based on goals for that individual patient, reduce the dose. And if the patient has advanced dementia and limited prognosis, ask whether continuing the medication at all is consistent with their goals of care.
