> [!tip] If you don't need to taper, don't taper…
> Therapeutic inertia is one of the biggest barriers to deprescribing. And when we wean or taper a medication, we often end up doubling or tripling the energy needed to overcome that inertia by decreasing a dose, which requires that we come back later to then decrease further or eventually to discontinue. So when a taper is not needed, we should fight the inertia just once and discontinue the medication abruptly or at least rapidly taper in a single EMR order (e.g., halve the dose for a week and then discontinue).

## Specific Medications

> [!note] PPIs
> PPIs are among the most overprescribed drugs in LTC. I see patients all the time who are on PPIs and they don't even know why. There is a misperception that these medications need to be tapered slowly to avoid rebound acid hypersecretion. Rebound hypersecretion is real with discontinuation of PPIs, but tends to be brief and manageable with PRN antacids. The [AGA guidelines on PPI deprescribing](http://bit.ly/4tDRqZ5) recommend either tapering or abrupt discontinuation, and the single published trial comparing a taper versus abrupt discontinuation did not demonstrate superiority of a taper.
>
> My personal recommendation is to discontinue omeprazole 20mg abruptly; anything more potent than that I de-escalate to omeprazole 20mg daily for 2 weeks and then discontinue, and offer a 2-week course of a PRN H2 blocker after discontinuation.

> [!note] Donepezil and other cholinesterase inhibitors
> I was always taught these needed to be tapered before discontinuation. In fact, it was the revelation that they do not need tapering that inspired this newsletter topic. The reason to consider a taper is that about 1/3 of the time patients will show functional or cognitive decline after discontinuation, and so a wean lets us "ease into" things and be watchful for clinical change. But if goals of care support discontinuation, or the dementia is very advanced, the medication can be simply stopped.
>
> If a taper is preferred, it can be done over a week: discontinue donepezil 10mg, and write an order for 1 week of donepezil 5mg and monitor for any changes.

> [!note] Memantine
> There is no pharmacologic reason to wean memantine, and the evidence basis for use of memantine is weak to begin with. In my opinion, memantine can always be discontinued abruptly without any taper.

> [!note] Antipsychotics for BPSD
> Antipsychotics prescribed for behavioral and psychiatric symptoms of dementia (BPSD) *at usual doses* can be discontinued abruptly without any taper. A number of studies have shown safety in abrupt discontinuation ([1](https://bit.ly/4ckAvUm), [2](https://bit.ly/4c4hnev), [3](https://bit.ly/3OfZ9gI)). Risk of relapse after discontinuation seems to be more related to severity of baseline symptoms and degree of initial response to the antipsychotic, not so much related to the discontinuation method. In the DART-AD study, survival was higher in those whose antipsychotics were discontinued than in those where they were continued.
>
> In most of these studies, usual-dose antipsychotics were discontinued abruptly, and those on higher doses had their dose halved for a week and then discontinued.
>
> **Doses generally considered "usual":**
> - Quetiapine ≤100mg
> - Risperidone ≤1mg
> - Olanzapine ≤10mg
> - Brexpiprazole ≤2mg
>
> If they are on higher doses, or you are worried about response, it is perfectly fine to halve the dose for a week or two, monitor, and discontinue. But I recommend writing the reduced dose with a stop date so inertia does not win the day. If the patient has re-emergent symptoms during the time on the decreased dose, you can increase back to the prior dose.

> [!note] Statins
> There is no reason to taper statins. When they are not indicated, just discontinue. There is no evidence of mortality benefit for statins in use for primary prevention in adults over 75, and I discontinue them. I also stop them when life expectancy is limited to a few years, as it takes up to 2.5 years of use for benefit even in secondary prevention.
>
> There were some observational studies that suggested possible harm of abrupt discontinuation, but the [largest trial to investigate this directly](https://bit.ly/3O5E1db) found no difference in mortality between patients continued on statins and those whose statin was discontinued. Additionally, there was a small improvement in quality of life for those whose statin was discontinued.

> [!note] Hydralazine
> Though hydralazine should rarely be used for hypertension, we are sometimes forced to use it when other options are not available due to renal failure. But if the blood pressure is below goal and we want to de-escalate hypertension medications, hydralazine is one of the first to target. Unlike clonidine and beta-blockers, there is no "rebound" with hydralazine.
>
> At doses of 50mg or less, you can just discontinue it and recheck the blood pressure. It may be prudent to decrease hydralazine in a stepwise manner if they are on high doses (100mg TID, for example), but only to be able to assess blood pressure as you come down on it. You can do this over a couple of days: halve the dose for a day or two, recheck blood pressure, and then discontinue if able.

## Conclusion

> [!summary]
> It feels "safer" to slowly taper a medication. But when there is no pharmacological reason to taper, we often end up causing harm by continuing medications longer than needed and exposing patients to side effects without reasonable expectation of benefit. If there is no reason to continue the medication, and no reason to taper it, then just discontinue it. At the very least, if you want to reduce the dose before discontinuation, provide a stop date for the lower dose. And then, of course, see the patient in follow-up to evaluate for any re-emergence of symptoms.
