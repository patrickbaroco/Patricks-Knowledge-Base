# Essentials of Fly Casting

**Instructor:** Simon Gawesworth  
**Source:** Far Bank Fly Fishing School  
**Length:** 37:30

## Summary

A basic fly cast consists of a backcast and forward cast. The fly line—not the fly—provides the weight that bends the rod and carries the fly.

Good casting depends on four fundamentals:

- Smooth acceleration: **start slow, finish fast**
- Translation followed by rotation
- A relatively straight rod-tip path
- Correct timing between the backcast and forward cast

The best learning progression is:

1. Pickup-and-laydown cast
2. False casting
3. Shooting line
4. Lengthening line during false casts
5. Changing direction gradually

## Key Takeaways

- Wear eye protection and a hat whenever casting.
- Practice with yarn rather than a fly when possible.
- Hold the rod lightly and use a comfortable grip and stance.
- Start each stroke slowly and accelerate into the stop.
- Use translation before rotation.
- Keep the rod tip moving along a relatively straight path to form narrow loops.
- Begin the next stroke when the line has almost completely straightened.
- Lengthen both the casting stroke and pause as more line is carried.
- Learn each stage separately before adding the next.
- Practice enough to develop correct muscle memory rather than reinforcing poor mechanics.

---

## Safety

- Wear sunglasses or other eye protection.
- Wear a hat to protect the head from a wayward fly.
- Use a short piece of brightly colored yarn instead of a hook while practicing.
- Consider flattening the barb when fishing.
- Leave at least 30–40 feet between casters.
- Tell another angler before walking behind them.

### Flattening a Barb

Use forceps or hemostats to press the barb flat against the hook shank.

Rather than squeezing from only one direction, rotate the forceps and compress the barb from several angles. The goal is to flatten it rather than break it off.

## How Fly Casting Works

In conventional spin fishing, the weight of the lure, sinker, or float bends the rod and carries the cast.

In fly fishing, the fly is generally too light to do this. The **fly line provides the casting weight**.

A fly-casting system consists of:

- Fly rod
- Fly line
- Leader
- Fly

The rod accelerates the weighted fly line, and the line carries the leader and fly toward the target.

---

## Grip and Stance

There is no single mandatory grip or stance for beginning casters.

### Grip Options

Common grips include:

- Thumb on top
- Index finger on top
- Handshake grip, with the thumb and index finger on opposite sides of the handle

Experiment and use the grip that allows the smoothest, most comfortable stroke.

Hold the rod near the top of the cork grip, close to its balance point.

Use a light grip. You should not be squeezing hard enough to create tension in the hand, forearm, shoulder, or neck.

### Stance Options

A right-handed caster can stand:

- Square to the target
- With the left foot forward
- With the right foot forward

For beginning practice, use whichever stance feels balanced and comfortable.

Later:

- A slightly open stance may help with distance.
- A square stance may improve accuracy and alignment.

---

## The Casting Stroke

Each backcast and forward cast consists of two components:

1. **Translation**
2. **Rotation**

### Translation

The rod moves through space with relatively little change in angle.

Translation creates stroke length and allows the rod to accelerate smoothly.

### Rotation

The rod rotates near the end of the stroke.

This final rotation accelerates the rod tip and forms the loop.

A useful sequence is:

> **Translate, then rotate**

Avoid rotating the rod too early. Excessive early rotation creates a wide, inefficient arc.

---

## Start Slow, Finish Fast

The central casting principle is:

> **Start slow, finish fast**

The rod should accelerate smoothly throughout the stroke and finish with a crisp rotation and stop.

This applies to both the backcast and forward cast.

The phrase does not mean making an abrupt or violent movement. The acceleration should be progressive rather than snatched.

---

## Casting Stroke Length

The casting stroke should change with the amount of line being carried.

### Short Line

Use:

- Short translation
- Compact casting stroke
- Short pause

### Long Line

Use:

- Longer translation
- Longer casting stroke
- Longer pause

The more line outside the rod tip, the more time it takes to straighten and the more room the rod needs to accelerate it smoothly.

---

## Loop Formation

A loop is the U-shaped section of line that travels away from the caster after each stroke.

### Narrow Loop

A narrow loop has a small distance between its upper and lower legs.

It generally produces:

- Less air resistance
- Better turnover
- Greater distance
- Better wind penetration
- More accurate line placement

### Wide Loop

A wide loop has a large gap between its upper and lower legs.

It creates more air resistance and usually loses speed more quickly.

### How to Form a Narrow Loop

Move the rod tip along a relatively straight path.

The more the rod sweeps through a rounded arc, the wider the loop tends to become.

A narrow loop does not come from using more force. It comes from:

- Smooth acceleration
- Correct stroke length
- Controlled rotation
- A straight rod-tip path
- A firm stop

---

## Tailing Loops

A tailing loop occurs when the lower leg of the loop crosses the upper leg.

This often creates knots in the leader, sometimes called wind knots.

A common cause is an abrupt, uneven application of force.

### Prevention

- Begin the stroke slowly.
- Accelerate smoothly.
- Avoid sudden power spikes.
- Finish with a controlled rotation and stop.
- Use a casting stroke long enough for the amount of line being carried.

---

## Timing

Begin the next stroke when the line has almost completely straightened.

### Starting Too Late

If you wait too long:

- The line begins to fall.
- The backcast may strike the grass or water.
- Slack develops before the next stroke.

### Starting Too Early

If you begin too soon:

- The line is still unrolling.
- The opposing forces create a sharp crack.
- The fly may snap off.
- The cast loses efficiency.

A useful practice method is to cast without a fly. A mistimed stroke often creates an audible crack.

### Adjusting Timing for Line Length

Short line straightens quickly, so the pause is brief.

Long line takes more time to straighten, so the pause must be longer.

Do not use one fixed rhythm for every line length.

---

## Pickup-and-Laydown Cast

The pickup-and-laydown cast is the first basic cast to learn.

### Setup

- Face downstream when practicing in moving water.
- Allow the current to straighten the line.
- Begin with the rod tip low and close to the water.
- Remove as much slack as possible.

### Backcast

1. Begin with a slow, smooth lift.
2. Accelerate into the backcast.
3. Rotate and stop the rod.
4. Allow the line to straighten behind you.

The line should leave the water quietly.

A loud spray usually means the rod was lifted too quickly.

### Forward Cast

1. Begin slowly.
2. Accelerate forward.
3. Rotate and stop.
4. Lower the rod only after the line has unrolled.

The backcast and forward cast should be mirror images.

---

## False Casting

False casting means keeping the line in the air for several backcasts and forward casts before laying it down.

### Uses

- Develop casting rhythm
- Dry a floating fly
- Change direction
- Lengthen line
- Reposition the cast
- Judge distance

### Basic Rod Positions

Use the clock-face analogy only as a rough reference:

- Stop near 1 o’clock on the backcast.
- Stop near 11 o’clock on the forward cast.

These high stops help keep the line airborne.

For the final delivery, stop slightly lower and then allow the rod to follow the line down.

### Practice Advice

Practice over water when possible. If the rod travels too far backward or forward, the line will strike the surface and provide immediate feedback.

If the cast begins to deteriorate, stop, reset the line, and begin again.

---

## Shooting Line

Shooting line allows slack line held in the non-casting hand to travel through the guides and extend the cast.

### Basic Drill

1. Pull in enough line to leave approximately 2–3 feet hanging from the rod hand.
2. Hold the line with the non-casting hand.
3. Keep the line hand close to the rod hand.
4. Begin false casting.
5. Establish a controlled rhythm.
6. On the final forward cast, release the line as the rod stops.

### Release Timing

Release the line **as the rod stops**.

#### Releasing Too Early

If the line is released while the rod is still moving:

- The rod unloads too early.
- Slack line rushes through the guides.
- The loop may collapse.
- The cast may fall short.

#### Releasing Too Late

If the line is released after the loop has already traveled forward:

- The line has lost the energy needed to pull out the slack.
- Little or no additional line shoots.

---

## Lengthening Line During False Casting

Once a single line shoot is comfortable, begin releasing a small amount of line during each forward cast.

### Sequence

1. Establish a controlled false-casting rhythm.
2. Release a small amount of line on the forward cast.
3. Catch the line before beginning the backcast.
4. Make the backcast.
5. Repeat the forward release and catch.
6. Continue until only 2–3 feet of loose line remain.
7. Release the remaining line on the final delivery.

As the line length increases:

- Lengthen the casting stroke.
- Lengthen the pause.
- Maintain smooth acceleration.
- Preserve the stop.
- Avoid forcing the cast.

---

## Changing Direction

The backcast should travel in the direction opposite the intended forward cast.

A cast behaves much like a bow and arrow: the forward delivery follows the direction established by the backcast.

Do not attempt a large change in direction in one stroke.

### Gradual Direction Change

1. Begin false casting.
2. Rotate the casting plane slightly with each stroke.
3. Continue changing direction in small increments.
4. Once the backcast is aligned opposite the target, make the final forward cast.

This method keeps the rod, line, and target properly aligned.

---

## Common Mistakes

### Gripping Too Tightly

Excessive grip pressure creates tension and prevents a fluid stroke.

**Correction:** Hold the rod securely but lightly.

### Starting Too Fast

A fast initial movement wastes the available stroke and may produce a tailing loop.

**Correction:** Start slowly and accelerate progressively.

### Rotating Too Early

Early rotation produces a wide rod-tip arc and wide loops.

**Correction:** Translate first and rotate near the end.

### Excessive Rod Arc

A large semicircular stroke creates open loops and poor turnover.

**Correction:** Match the rod-tip path and casting arc to the amount of line being carried.

### Starting the Next Stroke Too Soon

The line cracks or the fly breaks off.

**Correction:** Wait until the line has almost straightened.

### Waiting Too Long

The line drops and catches the water or ground.

**Correction:** Begin the stroke as the line finishes unrolling.

### Lifting the Line Too Quickly

The line tears noisily from the water and creates spray.

**Correction:** Begin with a slow lift, then accelerate.

### Shooting Line at the Wrong Time

The cast collapses or the slack remains unused.

**Correction:** Release the line as the rod stops.

### Adding Too Many Skills at Once

The caster loses rhythm while trying to manage the rod, line hand, timing, and line length simultaneously.

**Correction:** Master each stage before progressing.

---

## Practice Progression

1. Practice safe casting with yarn.
2. Find a comfortable grip and stance.
3. Practice translation followed by rotation.
4. Develop a smooth start-slow, finish-fast stroke.
5. Practice the pickup-and-laydown cast.
6. Learn to form controlled loops on both backcast and forward cast.
7. Improve timing by watching the line straighten.
8. Add false casting.
9. Practice shooting line on the final cast.
10. Release and catch small amounts of line during false casting.
11. Adjust stroke length and pause as the line lengthens.
12. Practice changing direction in small increments.

---

## Indoor Practice

### Indoor Casting Rod

A short indoor practice rod with a yarn line allows you to work on:

- Loop shape
- Timing
- Stroke length
- Translation and rotation
- Smooth acceleration
- Muscle memory

Watch the line unroll and begin the next stroke as it straightens.

### Practice Rod Acceleration by Sound

Remove the line and make the casting stroke with a short practice rod.

A good stroke produces most of its audible “whistle” near the end because the rod accelerates progressively.

If the sound begins immediately, the stroke likely started too fast.

### Rod-Butt Drill

When an indoor casting rod is unavailable:

1. Use the butt section of a fly rod.
2. Attach the reel.
3. Practice translation followed by rotation.
4. Listen for the reel to move or click at the end of the stroke.

The reel’s movement provides feedback that the acceleration occurred late rather than at the beginning.
