Some of the best fish hold in faster water. If you can achieve good drifts, you give yourself a very good chance. You also open up a lot of water that many anglers skip because they do not know how to get those drifts.

Dry flies in fast water. Here we go.

### This Water Is Underfished

**0:39** · Most people do not fish this kind of water with dry flies. I have even heard people say that fish will not eat dry flies in faster water. That is absolutely untrue. They will eat them, but you still need good drifts.

### Does the Dead Drift Matter?

**1:05** · Does a precision drift—a genuinely good dead drift—still matter?

Absolutely. Trout may be less forgiving in faster water because they have to make faster decisions, but a good drift is still critical. The fly needs to travel at the same speed as the current seam it occupies.

Do not simply accept that the fly is going to drag because it is in faster water. It does not have to drag.

A lot of people also say that faster water requires bushier dry flies. That is not necessarily true. When I fish dries, I am almost always using a fly that I expect the fish to eat. If they are eating size 18 olives in the flat water, I expect them to eat size 18 olives here as well. I do not want to switch to a size 12 Wulff simply because it is large and bushy.

### Do You Need Bigger Flies?

**2:02** · We tend to think a bushier fly will ride better and resist becoming swamped because it has more hackle and buoyant wings. In reality, what sinks a dry fly is often the tippet dragging on the water.

I could take a size 18 olive and place it in some of the fastest water here, and it would not become swamped very quickly on its own. What swamps the fly is the attached tippet, especially when that tippet begins to drag.

As with any other dry-fly presentation, the goal is to keep tension and drag off the fly. If you do that, you can fish almost any appropriate fly in this type of water.

### Fast-Water Tips Are Universal

**2:48** · This is not the fastest water we will encounter, but everything we discuss applies even to the fastest water you can find. The principles hold no matter how complex the current seams become.

I chose this location because it is approachable and allows us to see the techniques clearly.

To me, the most enjoyable part of fishing dry flies is that you can see success. I can watch the dry fly and determine whether it is moving with the current seam. There is no guessing. When fishing beneath the surface, there is much more uncertainty.

### The Most Fun Part

**3:22** · With a dry fly, I can see what the drift is supposed to look like and compare that with what is actually happening. When it is not right, I can make an adjustment.

Fishing faster water requires more work, which may be one reason many anglers skip it.

### Constant Motion

**3:36** · There is almost constant motion. You will see frequent [[mending]] throughout the drift.

I am not expecting long drifts. I am aiming for short, effective drifts. It may be more work, but to me it is also more fun because I am continually moving and making adjustments.

When fishing a flat, I may make longer casts and achieve much longer drifts. I might cast once, make a mend in the softer water, and let the fly travel. The entire pace is different.

Here, we are going to see what it takes to get good dry-fly drifts in fast water.

I am fishing a fly that I expect the trout to eat. It is summertime, which is ant season for me, so I am using a size 14 parachute ant. On most mornings, they will eat it.

### Build Slack Into the Cast

**4:38** · The first essential skill is building slack into the cast.

I use what I call a [[lagging curve]]. To accentuate that lagging curve, I also use a [[crash cast]]. The [[leader]] is designed to help produce this slack.

It is not only about where the fly lands. More importantly, it is about how the tippet lands. I do not want a straight line extending across the currents because it will drag immediately. Multiple currents cut across the tippet and create drag.

You need purposeful slack. That is what the lagging curve provides.

Look at how the leader lays out. The fly lands in one place while the tippet lands in a curve upstream. It is not only the tippet section. Much of the middle section, and even part of the butt section, may be included in the curve. The leader does not extend in a straight line.

To create the lagging curve, instead of making a standard vertical casting motion perpendicular to the water, I move my arm laterally. I call it a lagging curve rather than a power curve because I am not driving the fly all the way around.

**6:01** · I am intentionally preventing the leader from extending completely. I am not seeking full turnover.

We often talk about the importance of turnover, but I do not want complete turnover here. I want the lagging curve.

In faster water, I also need to force that curve down more quickly. The lagging curve works in many types of water, but here I cannot wait for everything to settle gradually.

With a normal cast, the fly line lands first, then the leader, and finally the dry fly, because the line and leader are heavier than the fly.

### The Crash Cast

**6:41** · Normally, the line lands, then the leader, and then the fly.

The crash cast changes that sequence. By the time a dry fly lands after a normal cast, the tippet and leader may already be pulling downstream. That is the central problem.

Instead of allowing the cast to settle gently over the water, I force it down onto the surface. I use a sidearm angle, keep the back cast higher, and drive the curved leader downward so that it hits the water.

I call it a crash cast because I am taking the curve in the leader and deliberately crashing it onto the water. In this type of broken water, it is unlikely to spook fish.

The leader ripples onto the surface, and then the fly lands. I am putting everything down on my terms.

The curved leader lands and unfolds with the slack positioned where I want it: upstream of the dry fly. The leader and dry fly reach the water in very quick succession.

**7:53** · That allows me to stack slack where I want it and achieve good drifts, even in faster water.

The central concept is simple: take the lagging curve and force it down onto the water. You do not have time for the fly line to land, followed by the tippet and then the fly. You need everything to reach the water nearly at once.

### Get Short, Effective Drifts

**9:21** · Another important principle in fast water is that you are not trying to achieve long drifts. You are trying to achieve short, effective drifts.

As soon as the fly begins dragging, I pick it up. I am not expecting a 20- or 30-foot drift in fast water. I may get five feet of good drift, and that is perfectly adequate.

I am also fishing closer. I am not casting 50 feet of line, or even 30 feet. The farthest cast I have made here is probably about 25 feet from my position to where the fly lands.

In the fastest water, get as close as possible. Sometimes the fly will be directly beneath the rod tip.

### Leader Choice

**10:03** · I am using a slack leader design: a [[George Harvey dry-fly leader]].

The leader must be designed properly. It needs to be capable of building slack in the way I have described. The Harvey leader places that slack in the appropriate sections.

### Grease the Leader

**10:37** · When I fish fast water, I [[grease the tippet]]. You can use Mucilin, Payette Paste, or an ordinary gel floatant.

I almost always grease the leader because I do not want the tippet section to sink beneath the water. I do this even in slower water.

In slower water, I may begin greasing about a foot from the fly. In this faster water, I will grease the leader to within a few inches of the dry fly. You do not need much.

Greasing helps prevent the leader from being buried by the currents. The leader will not remain on the surface unless you help it by mending, holding line off the water, preventing it from becoming suctioned into the surface currents, and applying floatant.

### Lay It Down, Pick It Up

**11:36** · I have moved into one of the faster pockets.

If I stand farther back and lay a long section of line across five or six different current speeds, even a very good crash cast will not produce as good a drift as a closer approach.

Instead, I lay the line down and then pick up the excess. I make the crash cast at close range and immediately lift the extra line.

I place the fly into the pocket, lay the leader down, and pick up the excess. I can almost tight-line the presentation.

By “tight-line,” I do not mean that I am actually tight to the dry fly. I probably have two to four feet of tippet resting on the water. I simply keep the rest of the system elevated.

I have to be close to make this work. I force the leader down onto the water with the crash cast and then pick up the excess.

Line resting on the water creates drag, so pick it up.

**12:29** · When tight-line nymphing, we often use a tuck cast. The rod stops, the weighted nymph continues forward until it cannot travel any farther, and then it tucks downward.

You can create a similar motion with a dry fly if the leader is designed for it, but this leader is not designed for short, forceful turnover. It is designed to create slack, which is more useful for this presentation.

I do not want to punch the dry fly downward with a hard tuck cast. Instead, I force the curved leader onto the water and then lift the excess line.

**13:11** · I do not have a clever name for this. I simply call it “lay it down, pick it up.”

Lay the line down on your terms, and then pick up the excess. I am not picking up the entire leader, only the unnecessary line.

This is a short-range presentation. It happens quickly: put it down, pick it up.

Anytime I can keep line off or out of the water, I do so. That comes naturally to me because I spend so much time tight-line nymphing.

It is also a central tenkara principle: keep unnecessary line off the water whenever possible.

Even in this complex water, I can come underneath the tree limbs, lay the leader down, and pick up the excess.

**14:00** · The fish may be only 10 feet away, but I have not spooked them because I am positioned behind them. In broken, complex currents, they often do not know you are there.

The concept is simple, but you must be committed to using it consistently.

### The Hop Mend

**14:42** · The third and final principle is frequent mending, particularly short, quick mends that we call [[hop mends]].

I do not want large reach mends or slow mending motions. Hop mends are short and quick.

I am not mending simply because I made a cast and think I am supposed to mend. I mend for a specific reason.

Sometimes I do not need to mend. At other times, I mend frequently simply to lift or “aerialize” the line. This returns to the principle of keeping as much of the system off the water as possible.

Anything that is not resting on the water cannot create drag.

I may make one hop mend after another, preventing the line from becoming suctioned into the surface. That can produce a perfect dead drift through the entire pocket.

I am also not always mending upstream. Sometimes I mend in another direction to keep myself positioned for the hook set.

### Mend for the Hook Set

**16:06** · If I have too much line to pick up before coming tight to the dry fly, I may mend toward the fly to improve my position.

The goal is to remain ready for the strike.

You are building slack, but not so much slack that you cannot set the hook. You want the system positioned so that you can respond immediately and come tight very quickly.

### Set Fast

**16:27** · In faster water, I set the hook almost as quickly as I can.

A trout rises, takes the fly, and returns downward rapidly. It does not rise and eat lazily. If the fish eats quickly on the surface, I set quickly.

I am not waiting several seconds before setting the hook.

### Everything Fits Together in a System

**16:48** · These techniques apply whenever the currents become fast or complex.

Remember the three main principles:

First, use the lagging curve and crash cast to build and position slack.

Second, lay the leader down and then pick up the excess, keeping unnecessary line off the water.

Third, use frequent hop mends: short, purposeful mends that prevent drag and keep you prepared for the hook set.

There are excellent opportunities to catch trout on dry flies in fast water. Learn how to fish it, develop confidence, and enjoy the challenge.
