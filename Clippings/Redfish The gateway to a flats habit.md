#fishing 

Redfish are simultaneously a second-to-none fly rod target and a great first-step species for flats fishing beginners

![redfish fly fishing](https://www.hatchmag.com/sites/default/files/styles/full-page/public/field/image/553A2240_0.jpg?itok=7oiJIyIu&timestamp=1784401056 "Photo: Chris Hunt.")

Photo: Chris Hunt.

When we were kids, my generation was dutifully warned about the evils of substance abuse. We were warned of complete strangers wandering oh-so-purposely into our paths with the sole intent of giving out free dope so as to perpetuate life-altering addiction among our crop. Far be it from me to discourage those who discourage drug and alcohol abuse, but I’ll also be the first to chime in and say, “Hey, Mr. Middle School Principal from 1982, I saw my first joint passed around at a party with a bunch of friends I knew and trusted, and I never had to deal with tall, sinister stranger in a dirty black Raiders hoody (because, as you know, all Raiders fans are sinister) foisting a fatty on me, evil grin and all.”

And I guess I got lucky. I never really developed the taste for drugs, even after flirting with the “gateway substances” that, for the most part, are now legal in most states. I get it. Others aren’t so lucky, and I’ve witnessed crippling addiction up close. My empathy cup runneth over. It’s a despicable disease that gnaws at the souls of its victims, even during their brightest hours. Conquering it takes real constitution, and I’m duly impressed with those who can and do stay sober.

So it’s with no small amount of caution that I approach you, neophyte fly angler, with a proposition — a “gateway” fish, if you will — that has the potential to inspire a lifelong habit. Let’s say you’ve been wandering the hills of Appalachia for wild trout for a few years now, or perhaps the tall country of the Rockies, following blue lines on the map into the backcountry in search of native cutthroats. “What’s next?” you might ask. “Where do I go from here?”

I’m tall, my hoody is likely something my significant other adorned with some kitschy phrase, like “Ho’s away! I have an owner!” I’m not sinister. Not even a little. And I think Mark Davis’ haircut is perhaps the best thing about the Raiders. So, no, I’m not here on a random mission to turn an otherwise manageable fly-fishing habit into something that negatively alters your existence.

But, sure. Consider me the buddy at the party passing the bottle around.

“Here,” I might say. “Try this. It’s a redfish.”

#### The next step starts here

Most fly fishers — not all, mind you — continue to evolve once they pick up the long rod. Most thrive off the challenge that comes with fooling fish with flies. In time, every dirtbag trout angler will pick up an article on the wonders of the Caribbean flats, and the untold challenges and hardships that come with wading across a watery desert in the hopes of encountering an elusive bonefish or a 100-pound tarpon, or (clutch your pearls, Mom) achieving the oft-established pinnacle of the craft and catching both of those fish *plus* an angry permit, all in the same damn day, for the elusive “grand slam” of saltwater fly fishing. In time, most of us will at least give the salt a try.

Unfortunately, most will try to skip a grade, and go right from the canyons of the Colorado high country or the rugged forests of the Alleghenys to the marls of Abaco, the turtlegrass flats of Belize, or the mangrove hammocks of the Keys.

First, let’s face it. You’re ill-prepared. Your double haul is non-existent. A light breeze turns your cast into a soup sandwich. Your fish-eye? What fish-eye, Jethro? You’re. Not. Ready.

How do I know this? Because years ago, I brazenly flew from the trout country of eastern Idaho to the white-sand flats of Long Island in the southern Bahamas, thinking I would just check that bonefish off the list and that would be that. After five days of fighting the first real wind I’ve ever had to deal with, of battling sideways rain, gray, angry skies, and continuous refusals from what I then considered to be the biggest assholes on the flats, I slunk back to the Rockies. Fishless.

Bonefish? I hated bonefish. Tarpon? To hell with tarpon. Permit? I was 99 percent certain that permit were like the sasquatch or the Skunk Ape. Everybody talks about them. But they’re not real.

And then I had a moment. A few years earlier I ventured into the marshes of south Louisiana while on a trip to visit my dad, who, at the time, lived in Baton Rouge. I booked a trip with a marsh guide who took me out in his skiff, and, in one day, I’d managed to catch not one, but *five* beefy redfish. On a fly. I cast flies at tailing fish. Cruising fish. I put flies in front of v-shaped wakes, and generally had an absolute blast, all while upping my game a bit.

“Redfish,” I thought to myself. “I need more redfish.”

![flats fishing texas laguna madre](https://www.hatchmag.com/sites/default/files/styles/full-page/public/field/image/ptsc_0041.jpg?itok=OBnxlb13 "Photo: Chris Hunt.")  

Photo: Chris Hunt.

So, for the next couple of years, I planned a few DIY adventures to the wadable flats of the south Texas coast. In time, I graduated to renting kayaks and paddling the lower Laguna Madre, going from crystal-clear flat to crystal-clear flat. I worked on my casting. I discovered my (somewhat) lazy fish-eye, and, in time, the redfish of the Texas sand didn’t stand a chance. But, I told myself, I’m still not ready for The Show. I need more seasoning down here on the Triple A flats before I take my game to the next level. One afternoon, after hooking a couple of nice reds in an honest-to-God gale, I managed to make what was then the cast of my lifetime — I put a fly on the nose of a feeding sheepshead at 80 feet, and managed to hook and land “the prison permit.”

“I think you’re ready,” a fishing buddy of mine told me after watching my angling gymnastics routine and absolutely sticking the landing.

#### The best way to become a flats aficionado

Redfish are among my favorites, even today, years and years after that first visit to the dark marshes of Louisiana. Sure, it’s possible to have a tough day on the redfish flats, just as it's possible to have an off day chasing brookies in the backcountry. But, on the whole, redfish are among the most forgiving of the inshore flats fish. And that’s completely on them. On perfect redfish days, they’ll chase and eat damn near anything that resembles food. On off days, they’ll be perplexingly challenging, but you’ll likely, at the very least, earn a look or a follow until you stumble upon what it is they really want.

But more than just being a sporting flats target, redfish are excellent teachers, even if they have no idea they occupy an esteemed perch in the fly-fishing lecture halls. Redfish offer anglers every opportunity to become better anglers. They provide solid moving targets for developing double-haulers. They follow stripped flies and aggressively gobble them down, only to fight like hell and come loose, just as any bonefish worth its salt will do. They also offer more consistent success — rewards for well-cast flies, tight loops into a stiff breeze, and, when it all comes together, the “eat.”

#### A fish worth chasing

I’ve characterized redfish as a warm up fish, a stepping stone to Big League fly fishing, and, while they do occupy an important niche in any would-be saltwater fly caster’s journey, that’s not exactly fair, and it’s not entirely accurate. Don’t mistake the redfish as anything less than a worthy fly rod target. The redfish guides of the Carolina Low Country, the Louisiana marsh or the Texas Gulf Coast are every bit as instructive as the fish themselves tend to be. Just this past winter, while staying at the gorgeous seaside retreat, [Bay Flats Lodge](https://hatch.travel/trips/texas/bay-flats-lodge) on Texas’ San Antonio Bay, I “rediscovered” the absolute joy that comes with fly casting to redfish.

My first day started predictably slow — it was a chilly December day that just needed to warm up a bit. After skipping across the oyster beds not 20 minutes from the lodge for a couple of hours, we had some serious looks, and one missed hookup. But, my guide and I both determined, we were kinetic. Things were moving, and, in time, we’d figure it out. And, as redfish seem to do, they came to life like those slow-glowing stadium lights that take their time but eventually pump the lumens onto the gridiron. By the end of the day, we were in a groove and I honestly lost count of the fish we pulled from the water that, in some instances, was just a few inches deep.

Over the next two days, the reds of San Antonio Bay and Matagorda Island, offered up yet another refresher. They’ll do what bonefish generally won’t — they’ll eat on top. We cast big foam Gurglers at these denizens of the flats, and the reds earned a hell of a lot more esteem in my book. Watching these big predatorial drum lock in on lightly twitched Gurgler reminded me why I never should have taken a breather from these majestic shallow-water eaters. Redfish are worth chasing. Yes, they’ll help you hone your game for fish generally considered more challenging, but, under the right circumstances, big, angry reds provide a challenge themselves.

#### Final word

It’s fine to consider the redfish the “gateway fish” to flats fishing. And, frankly, I think it’s absolutely true. But there are lots of reasons to chase redfish that have nothing to do with eventually “graduating” to another quarry.

First, for most of us, they’re a hell of a lot closer and easier to reach than the permit on the flats of San Pedro. Second, it’s less expensive to book a flight to Houston or Charleston, generally speaking, than a flight to Tulum or Coxen Hole. Reds aren’t peppered around the Caribbean or other tropical flats paradises — they’re home-grown, American fish.

So take it from this tall, pudgy, bearded, older stranger in the “I like big bugs and I cannot lie” hoody. Try the redfish. It might be habit-forming. And in this instance, that’s perfectly OK.

Before the time of electronic navigation tools—when the hallowed hideaways were discoverable only by map and compass or through careful direction of someone who trusted you—this was an enchanted place.

There were no man-made attractions here, so there was no profit to be made from anything, so there was nothing to advertise either in grotesque flyers dispersed at the local businesses (all more than 20 miles distant) or buzzing neon signs pointing the way. There were no catchy commercial jingles or newspaper advertisements. A lot of people from not that far away never even knew the place existed.

It was just a wide spot in a little creek carving through a deep, secluded hollow and crossing under a one-lane bridge on the farthest of backroads. For those not intimately familiar with it, those who had never bounced over the miles of truck-rattling ruts to rapture in its cool currents on a blistering July day or stood on the bedrock under those currents and felt the wildness of the ancient hills pulsing through a bent fishing pole, it was just a hillbilly swimming hole.

In those days, which were not so long ago, people in the know (mostly hillbillies) did not speak of the rough sort of beauty peculiar to these secret places in the company of unvetted ears, and folks not familiar with the backroads did not drive them for the pure hell of it. So no one came here uninvited, in a manner of speaking. Few outside of locals, or kin to, came deliberately to places like this because few outside of the culture could imagine such places. And if they could imagine them, they couldn’t imagine why anyone would go there.

Or, for that matter, cared to know.

There was always a government-designated campground at the wide spot, at least as far back as I can remember. But it was barely maintained and had no amenities. The switchback road into the hollow, too formidable and unforgiving for RVs, made it a tent-only affair. No public restrooms made it a shovel-first affair. And that type of camping did not, for many reasons, hold the broad appeal it seems to now. Like the wide spot, few knew about the campground. I don’t recall ever seeing anyone camp there that my father did not know and never more than one camp at a time. On many visits, we never saw another human. It was this way for the entirety of my childhood.

But sometime during my third decade, technology, wanderlust, and a revisiting of simpler ideas pervaded the population at large and threatened the wide spot with an infestation of weekend visitors. I think the mountains themselves seemed to sense this, as if the humble secrets hidden by steep timbered ridges and the circumspect culture of a people rooted in their thin soils were in peril.

And some places just don’t abide defilement.

The official cause was too much rain for too long, turning waterlogged soil to cascading slurry. This was, for sure, the mechanics behind it. But I believe the parade of trucks, Jeeps, and ORVs grunting through the hollow tested the hills’ patience, the vibrations of too many internal combustion engines splintered the underpinning. Until one day the old stony bones of the Boston Mountains let loose and the hills settled their bulk on the road with a landslide.

The mass of soil, rock, and vegetation covered only one of the roads leading to the wide spot—the one most known and most hospitable and therefore most heavily traveled—and that was enough.

I don’t remember for how long the road was impassable, but I recall a few summers of sublime solitude akin to the days of my boyhood. During that time we saw a grand total of one other vehicle at the wide spot. It drove over the bridge as we splashed below then quickly backed up into the washed out campground driveway, turned around and left. I think they were lost.

Those sacred summers were a return to the Eden I knew as a boy that I could share with my daughters and nephew. It was a place they could sniff the perfume of wild hydrangeas clinging to creek-side bluffs so steep and deep in the dark heart of the hollow that sunbeams never gilded their lacy flowers. It was a place they could sit in riffles burbling with cold spring water, where they could find a rare peace and sense of belonging alongside the ever-searching mink, the fierce little brown bass, the wise and patient watersnake.

But all good things must end.

Eventually, the dozers came and shoved the fallen shoulders of the mountain aside. The road was smoothed, and the government campground enhanced and renovated. It was still nigh on impossible to get an RV into the place, but now there were lantern poles and restrooms. Even more precise and foolproof electronic mapping technology combined with a burning fervor to reconnect with the untamed (or an interpretation thereof), and an unquenchable desire to tell every damn person within earshot (a range now amplified through social media) about “this amazing place I found” brought the crowds back.

Then came pandemic when we relearned the hard way that dense populations of animals (even, despite our anthro-arrogance, humans) in close quarters is exactly how and why viruses evolved and thrive. People remembered that open, changing air between us is a good thing, that infinite shades of green paired with haunting wood thrush song can soothe a lot of what ails body, mind, and spirit.

Suddenly, everyone knew about the wide spot. Suddenly, everyone was going there.

Caravans of off-roaders (dirt-roaders is a more apt label) planned excursions and plastered digital invites across the interweb complete with step-by-step directions. Images of both vehicles and people lined up at the wide spot—something I could never imagine in a million years—became commonplace.

With this combination of discoverability and the unquestionable romantic appeal, the wide spot became a social media star. The wide spot became a hashtag.

Photo: Johnny Carrol Sain.

We didn’t visit the wide spot for a few years after those blessed summers of solitude, mostly because I knew full well the attention it was getting, and places with lots of people are places I don’t want to be. Doubly so for those with which I have an intimate history. But nostalgic yearnings welled when my granddaughter requested a camping trip for her ninth birthday. Urged by a longing to share a place so special to me as a nine-year-old, I suggested the wide spot.

So we pitched tents in the refurbished campground not far enough away from other campers, made s’mores around a government-built fire pit, and descended a cement staircase to the creek’s sparkling holiness where at least fifteen other folks—strangers to me in these waters I knew so well—waded and swam in the cool Ozarkian aquamarine.

I waded and swam a while, too. I tried to meditate on the infinite green, but all I saw was people. I listened hard for the ghostly warbles of the wood thrush.

But all I heard was a radio.

As shadows grew, I hiked upstream away from the designated swimming area with a 4-weight fly rod searching for something I worried was lost forever. And in the still-hidden pockets where slick creek rocks and the specter of timber rattlers long as your leg make the travel more painstaking, I still found wild hydrangeas, fierce little brown bass, and wise watersnakes—remnants of the creek I once knew.

I also found a searing heartache in knowing that it would never be that creek again.

I understand that change is the only constant, that we are only here until someone else comes along—cultures come and go, one displaced by another and every one with different ideas about what is sacred and what is not. I understand that untamed places are ultimately domesticated, the numinous eventually rendered into the ordinary. It is the way of people. It is the way of the world.

I wrestle with this reality every day of my life, struggle to accept that I can’t be a gatekeeper for something utterly unknowable.

And I struggle, too, for words to convey my mourning for what is lost forever. Am I feeling a blue sadness or white-hot rage when the whip-poor-will’s slender melancholy notes are blotted out by the throbbing beats of a subwoofer? Was it a righteous red-faced anger that engulfed me when my granddaughters told me of the piles of dead sunfish they found on the creek bank? Is it a blackened resentment singeing my soul over the irreverent appropriation of my backwoods heritage?

How can I feel anything but bitterness about the degradation of a transcendent place by too many people seeking their own definition of what it means to experience nature? How is it that their definition requires so much of what I come here to escape?

But if I want to preserve this intimate relationship with the wide spot through my middle years and beyond, somehow I’ve got to reckon this new reality of sharing it with people who don’t know it like I do, who may not and may never know it as anything more than “an amazing place they found.” I’ve got to coexist with some folks who will barter the spirit of the mountains for nothing more than a few digital “likes.”

I pray for enlightenment to flow from the mountains and through me like creek water, for potent words that pour from my lips and drip from my fingers. I long for clear sentiments to help them understand the fragility of this jewel.

But the truth of the matter—a truth that’s sadly been proven all through history—is that more accessibility always means more people. More people, even those with the best of intentions, always kill the wild magic. And when the wild magic is gone, enchanted places die.
