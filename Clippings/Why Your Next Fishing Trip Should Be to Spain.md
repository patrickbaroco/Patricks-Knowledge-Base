## Why Spain is a Fly Fisherman's Dream

The Spanish Pyrenees, with their glacial mountain lakes and teeming spring-fed creeks, are one of the sport’s best-kept secrets.

![This image may contain Animal Fish Trout Human and Person](https://media.cntraveler.com/photos/5703e09fa8c6df1e2f77d688/1:1/w_960,c_limit/pyrenees-hranek-3.jpg)

Matt Hranek

I first read about trout fishing in Spain in *The Sun Also Rises* when I was 18 years old, already a passionate if ham-handed trout fisherman. In the decades since, I have been fortunate (and self-indulgent) enough to have angled for trout in more than a dozen countries. Yet those few pages from Hemingway—in which Jake Barnes and his wise-ass friend Bill hike to the remote Irati River, where they chill their wine bottles in a spring and talk about bullfighting and women while hooking countless fish—remain one of the most vivid evocations of trout fishing I know of, and the only mention I’ve ever come across of it being practiced in [Spain](https://www.cntraveler.com/galleries/2016-03-29/the-most-beautiful-beaches-in-spain-and-portugal).

Until, that is, a few years ago, when the angling booking agent Richard French and I were floating on the Collón Curá River in Argentina, a gold standard for trout fishing. “You have got to try Spain,” Richard told me. “The fishing is as good as it is here, yet nobody knows about it.”

Always eager to discover new water, I signed up on the spot for a week with him the following June. So splendid was that experience—some of the best fishing I’ve had, with rugged countryside, wildly flavorful food, and plenty of dry-land activities to spare non-fishers, such as my wife, Patricia, from sitting all day on a riverbank—that we immediately made plans to return, this past September, with a group of 14 friends. Ten of us went to fish; six never wet a line; none of us will ever forget the trip.

We began in the ancient village of Arén, lodged in the Pyrenees foothills between Aragon and [Catalonia](https://www.cntraveler.com/stories/2014-12-18/things-to-do-in-barcelona-weekend-guide). The four-hour drive from Barcelona carried us through a russet and sage-green flatland cultivated in corn and fruit, then into steeper countryside, past flocks of sheep and table-topped hills with saw-toothed peaks rearing beyond. In Arén, population 300, you’ll find a Baroque cathedral and a museum of dinosaurs whose footprints were discovered nearby. But its most improbable feature may be Casa Domenc, a 250-year-old inn and restaurant on the village square, out of whose tiny kitchen is served some of the finest food in Spain.

> The fishing is as good as it is \[in Argentina\], yet nobody knows about it.

We were met in Arén by Iván Tarín, our hyperactive Spanish guide. From March through November, his company, [Salvelinus](http://www.salvelinus.com/) (the Latin name for brook trout), puts clients with fly rods onto more than 50 mountain lakes, 14 rivers, and 60 tributaries in the Spanish Pyrenees—most of which they will have all to themselves. On Monday, you might find yourself fishing bouldery freestone rivers for shy zebra trout; on Tuesday, chucking streamers for hog rainbows in a reservoir; and on Wednesday, casting nonstop through a day of lights-out fishing on an *ibón* —one of some 2,500 high-mountain glacial lakes in the Pyrenees.

Found at altitudes over 6,000 feet, these *ibóns* are accessible only by long hikes, or by helicopter if the weather is right. We were fortunate to have clear skies and crisp fall temperatures, and after being deposited by the helicopter on the shore of a frigid sapphire lake, I stood for a full 20 minutes taking in the hush before finally reminding myself to rig a rod. In the preternaturally clear water, we could see the trout we cast to appearing to flit among the reflected peaks. It felt, in Iván’s perfect phrase, like “casting into the sky itself.”

Each night, anglers and non-anglers—who spent the days touring monasteries, cathedrals, and wineries—gathered at a long table in the wine cellar at Domenc, run by the owner, Juan Antonio Pascual Anmella, for an eight-course dinner: carpaccio of tuna with pomegranate vinaigrette; a timbale of potato, black trumpet mushrooms, and quail eggs; crispy black sausage with tomato marmalade; perhaps trotters with porcini, all matched with excellent Spanish wines. Exhausted and overserved, we’d retire to the down comforters of Anmella’s La Ribagorza Lodge, only to repeat the exercise the next morning.

By the fourth day, we had worked our way east to south-central Catalonia. Outside Peramola, my fishing pal Nana Lampton and I mulled over one of the puzzles that can raise trout fishing from a mere sport to a single-minded fascination with what is difficult.

From a bridge over a tailwater river, Nana and I and our guide, Serge Vazeat, looked down at a clear slow-water pool. A half dozen rainbow and brown trout, each 25 to 30 inches long, were gulping insects in slow surges that brought their great dark backs and dorsal fins out of the slack current, beneath which waved a jungle of weeds. The conundrum: It would be all but impossible to wade within casting distance without spooking them; and if you did manage to hook one, there would be no way to land it through the weeds on the ultra-fine leader tippet and minuscule fly you would have to use to tempt the fish to eat.

> I will remember those two trout long after I have forgotten all the others I caught in Spain.

One is always well advised to listen to one’s guide. But sometimes the sharpest joy of fishing resides in the attempt to solve unsolvable problems, so Nana and I ignored Serge’s reasonable advice to go upstream and catch some catchable fish, and instead spent the most riveting hour of the trip trying to catch these.

After wading carefully to within casting reach of the gulpers, I stood motionless for ten minutes, until the fish had gone back to feeding. Twice I managed to drop a midge-cluster fly the size of a peppercorn in the right place, and twice I hooked one of the enormous trout—only to have it promptly break off in the weeds. I will remember those two trout long after I have forgotten all the others I caught in Spain.

We spent our final three days at the lushly landscaped [Hotel Can Boix de Peramola](http://www.canboix.cat/?lang=EN) in Catalonia’s Alt Urgell region, where shepherds tend their flocks with crooks and women sell wild mushrooms out of their aprons. Hannibal crossed the Alt Urgell with his elephants en route to Italy, and it is one of Europe’s most important areas for Romanesque art and architecture, to which the non-anglers bent their attention while the anglers fished the Segre.

Flowing through the Alt Urgell for nearly 30 miles, the Segre is Spain’s greatest trophy-trout river, and among the greatest anywhere. The year before, I had released in one short afternoon four rainbows and a brown, all over 20 inches long. This year, due to heavy rains, the river was the color of *café con leche*, and the fishing was slow, even during a blizzard-like evening hatch of white mayflies.

If the Segre trout were off their feed, our group was anything but on the trip’s penultimate night over dinner at El Molí d’Alòs. This is one of the very few restaurants I would happily crawl naked through a field of nettles to eat in. Set in a century-old stone building by the riverbank, it consists of a bar and dining room separated by shelves of local wines and grappa, house-made manchegos, olive oil, smoked meats, and jars of quail eggs and tomato relish. All the food is raised by the owners: Lidia Moya; her chef husband, Siso Cuartero; and her father, Santiago. Under the dour gaze of wild boar heads, we sat at a 20-foot table until well after midnight, feasting on Iberian ham and hard cheeses, olives, almonds, tomato-and-mint salads, potato-and-onion omelets, and lamb and boar sausages. We toasted the trip, one another, Lidia and Siso and Santiago, and anything else we could think of with cava and house vino tinto, poured straight into our mouths from long-spouted *porrons.*

As we staggered out, I had a brief exchange with a guy at the bar: a pig-tailed, scar-faced, broken-nosed bacán with a sheepdog on a leash. He told me he was a painter and pointed out some of his canvases hanging behind the bar, all of which featured undressed females.

“I can see you like women,” I said to him.

“All,” he said, grinning hugely. “Women, art, wine, food, fishing, the mountains. All with the others very strong!”

I shook the man’s hand and called him brother.

## ... Now Go Fish

**When to Go** Fly-fishing season in the Pyrenees runs from March through November. If you really want to get your Hemingway on, come in July and combine angling with a visit to Pamplona for San Fermin.

**Getting There** Fly into [Barcelona](https://www.cntraveler.com/destinations/barcelona); from there, it’s a four-and-a-half-hour drive to Arén, in the Pyrenees foothills, or two hours to the Alt Urgell region.

**How to Do It** You could strike out on your own and fish several of the spots mentioned here, but we’d leave the planning to the pros, who can get you to the most remote ibóns. Richard French, owner of [Toronto](https://www.cntraveler.com/galleries/2014-09-22/what-to-do-in-toronto-the-coveteur-s-guide) -based Slipstream Angling Worldwide, has fished the Pyrenees for years, working with Spanish outfitter Salvelinus; they can design trips for novices and pros alike. French recommends a minimum of four nights for three days of angling, or more to build in fish-free activities like helicopter hiking, vineyard visits, and architectural tours (slipstreamangling.com).
