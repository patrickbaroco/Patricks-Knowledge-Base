# Double Haul Fly Casting

**Instructor:** Simon Gawesworth  
**Source:** Far Bank Fly Fishing School  
**Length:** 25:34

## Summary

The double haul increases rod load and line speed by pulling on the fly line with the non-casting hand during both the backcast and forward cast. Done correctly, it produces greater distance, better wind penetration, improved line control, and less casting fatigue.

The key is not strength but coordination and timing. Learn the movements separately, eliminate creep, match the speed of the feed to the speed of the unrolling line, and only then combine the movements into continuous false casting.

## Key Takeaways

- Build a sound basic cast before learning to double haul.
- The hauling hand performs four actions during each complete casting cycle: haul, feed, haul, feed.
- Begin by stopping between each action rather than trying to cast continuously.
- Bring the hauling hand fully back to the rod before beginning the next casting stroke.
- Delay each haul until the casting stroke is underway.
- Match the feed speed to the speed of the unrolling line.
- Begin with an easy-to-cast line system, then progress toward normal fishing lines.
- Once the haul becomes automatic, shift attention back to maintaining a compact, controlled casting stroke.

## Practical Advice

### What a Double Haul Does

A **haul** is a pull on the fly line with the non-casting hand. A **double haul** uses one haul during the backcast and another during the forward cast.

The pull temporarily increases rod bend and therefore increases line speed. This allows the caster to generate more energy with less effort from the casting arm.

A single haul during the backcast may increase backcast speed, but it does little to accelerate the forward cast. For most distance and wind applications, the double haul is substantially more useful.

### Prerequisites

Do not use the double haul to compensate for a poor basic cast.

Before adding a haul, you should be able to:

- Form consistent loops on the backcast and forward cast.
- Make firm, positive stops.
- Maintain control of the fly line.
- Allow the line to straighten before beginning the next stroke.
- Adjust the casting arc appropriately for the amount of line being carried.

If the underlying cast is unstable, adding the hauling movement usually makes the faults more difficult to diagnose.

## The Four Movements

The casting hand makes two principal movements:

1. Backcast
    
2. Forward cast
    

The hauling hand makes four:

1. Haul on the backcast.
    
2. Feed the line hand back toward the rod.
    
3. Haul on the forward cast.
    
4. Feed the line hand back toward the rod.
    

A useful verbal rhythm is:

> **Haul — feed — haul — feed**

The hauling hand should begin near the casting hand before each stroke. During the stroke, the hands separate. After the stroke, the hauling hand returns toward the rod as the loop unrolls.

---

## The Army-Style Learning Progression

### Stage 1: Four Separate Movements

This is the simplest way to learn the coordination.

#### 1. Backcast and Haul

Make the backcast while pulling the line hand away from the rod hand.

Then stop. Let the line fall if necessary.

#### 2. Feed

Keep the rod hand completely still.

Slide the line hand all the way back until it meets the rod hand.

Then stop again.

#### 3. Forward Cast and Haul

Begin the forward casting stroke.

As the rod accelerates and rotates, pull the line hand away from the rod hand.

Then stop.

#### 4. Feed

Keep the rod still and return the line hand all the way to the rod hand.

This returns both hands to the starting position.

Practice the four movements with deliberate pauses until each action is distinct and controlled.

### Stage 2: Combine the Movements into Pairs

Once the four movements are reliable, combine them:

- Backcast haul immediately followed by the backcast feed.
- Forward-cast haul immediately followed by the forward-cast feed.

Think of the hauling hand as a tennis ball bouncing at a steady tempo:

> **Down and up — down and up**

The feed should not linger at the bottom, nor should it snap upward faster than the line.

### Stage 3: One Complete Cast

Remove the pauses between the four hand movements, but pause after completing the full backcast-and-forward-cast cycle.

The sequence becomes:

> **One — two — three — four — pause**

This helps preserve coordination while beginning to develop fluidity.

### Stage 4: Continuous False Casting

Once the complete cycle is reliable, remove the final pause and maintain a continuous rhythm:

> **One — two — three — four — one — two — three — four**

At this stage, the hauling movement should gradually become automatic.

## Timing the Haul

The haul should not begin before the rod begins its casting stroke.

Start both hands moving together. As the casting stroke develops, separate the hands by pulling with the line hand.

### Backcast

- Move both hands into the backcast.
- Delay the haul slightly.
- Pull the line hand away as the rod accelerates and stops.

### Forward Cast

- Begin moving both hands forward.
- Delay the haul until the forward stroke develops.
- Pull the line hand away as the rod accelerates and rotates.  

An early haul often finishes before the rod reaches its most useful acceleration. A properly delayed haul adds line speed at the part of the stroke where it has the greatest effect.

---

## Feeding the Line

After each haul, the line hand must return toward the rod hand.

The speed of this feed should match the speed at which the fly line is unrolling.

- If the line is moving slowly, feed slowly.
- If the line is moving rapidly, feed rapidly.
- Let the unrolling line help pull the line hand back toward the rod.

Feeding too fast creates slack and disrupts control. Feeding too slowly may prevent the line hand from returning to position before the next haul.

Maintain light sensitivity in the fingers rather than gripping the line rigidly.

---

## Avoiding Creep

**Creep** occurs when the rod hand begins moving into the next casting stroke while the line hand is still returning from the previous haul.

Instead of the line hand returning fully to the stationary rod hand, both hands drift toward one another.

This shortens the available casting stroke and weakens the next haul.

To prevent creep:

- Finish the haul.
- Keep the rod hand completely still.
- Bring the line hand all the way back to the rod hand.
- Begin the next casting stroke only after the hands have reunited.

If creep appears while combining movements, return to the four-step drill with distinct pauses.

---

## Restoring the Basic Cast

When first learning to haul, the caster may become so focused on the line hand that the rod hand develops a wide, uncontrolled casting arc.

Once the haul rhythm becomes reliable, redirect attention to the rod hand:

- Maintain appropriate acceleration.
- Preserve firm stops.
- Keep the casting arc no wider than necessary.
- Form controlled loops.
- Avoid overpowering the rod.

The shooting-head setup may allow the haul to function even with poor rod mechanics, but that does not mean the resulting cast is efficient.

The final objective is a good basic cast with a coordinated haul added to it.

---

## Recommended Learning Progression

The instructor recommends learning with lines that provide strong feedback and are easy to shoot.

### 1. Separate Shooting Head

A shooting-head system consists of:

- A relatively short, heavy head.
- A very thin running or shooting line.

The heavy head creates a noticeable pull and makes the timing easier to feel. The thin running line produces little friction.

This is best treated as a training tool rather than a normal fishing setup.

### 2. Integrated Shooting Head

The head and running line are manufactured as one continuous line.

It retains much of the easy shooting and strong feedback of a separate shooting head while feeling more like a conventional fly line.

### 3. Weight-Forward Line

Progress to the regular weight-forward line used for fishing.

This is more difficult because:

- The running line is thicker.
- Friction increases.
- The head may provide less pull and feedback.

This is the most important practical transition because it transfers the skill to the line you will actually fish.

### 4. Double-Taper Line

A double-taper line is presented as an advanced training tool.

It is more difficult to haul because:

- Much of the line is relatively thick.
- Friction is greater.
- The front of the line provides less pulling force.
- Feeding line cleanly is harder.

Practice with a double taper only after the basic technique is well established.

## Shooting Line During the Cast

Initial practice should use a fixed amount of line.

Once the haul is coordinated, begin allowing a small amount of line to slip through the fingers during each feed:

1. Haul on the backcast.
    
2. Feed and allow a small amount of line to slip backward.
    
3. Catch and control the line.
    
4. Haul on the forward cast.
    
5. Feed and allow a small amount of line to slip forward.
    

Continue lengthening the line until the desired amount is carried.

Then make a final forward haul and release the line to shoot the cast.

Do not introduce line shooting until the haul-and-feed sequence is reliable with a fixed line length.

---

## Advanced Friction Reduction

During a forward cast, the rod bends in a way that can cause the fly line to contact both the guides and the rod blank. This may increase friction, especially with:

- Light fly lines.
    
- Double-taper lines.
    
- Thick running lines.
    
- Long feeds.
    

When friction prevents the line from feeding cleanly, two methods are suggested.

### Option 1: Rotate the Entire Rod

Rotate the rod approximately 90 degrees so that the guides are oriented to the side.

This can keep the line riding primarily through the guides rather than contacting the blank.

The disadvantage is that the reel may tend to rotate downward, requiring a firmer grip.

### Option 2: Rotate the Upper Rod Sections

Keep the handle and reel in the normal position, but rotate the upper rod sections so that the guides are oriented approximately 90 degrees to the side.

This may reduce line-to-blank contact without changing the normal position of the reel.

This is an advanced refinement rather than a prerequisite for learning the double haul.

## Common Mistakes

### Hauling Too Early

The line hand pulls before the casting stroke has properly developed.

**Correction:** Begin the casting stroke first, then separate the hands as the rod accelerates.

### Leaving the Line Hand Down

The caster finishes the haul but does not return the line hand to the rod.

**Correction:** Complete the feed after every haul.

### Creep

The rod hand moves toward the line hand during the feed.

**Correction:** Freeze the rod hand and return the line hand fully to it.

### Feeding Too Fast

The line hand returns faster than the line is unrolling.

**Correction:** Match the feed to the line speed and allow the line to pull the hand upward.

### Excessively Wide Casting Arc

The caster focuses on the haul and loses control of the rod.

**Correction:** Once the haul becomes automatic, return attention to the basic casting stroke.

### Progressing Too Quickly

The caster attempts continuous false casting before mastering the separate actions.

**Correction:** Return to the four-step drill and rebuild the movement sequence.

---

## Practice Sequence

1. Establish a controlled basic overhead cast.
    
2. Use a fixed length of line.
    
3. Practice the four army-style movements with pauses.
    
4. Eliminate creep.
    
5. Combine the movements into two pairs.
    
6. Complete one uninterrupted four-action cycle.
    
7. Add continuous false casting.
    
8. Restore a compact, controlled rod stroke.
    
9. Begin shooting small amounts of line during the feeds.
    
10. Progress from a shooting head to an integrated head and then to a normal weight-forward line.
    
11. Use a double-taper line only as advanced practice.
    
12. Experiment with guide orientation only if line friction is impairing the feed.
