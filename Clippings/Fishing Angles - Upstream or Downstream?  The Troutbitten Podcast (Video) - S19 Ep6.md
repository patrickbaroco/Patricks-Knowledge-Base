# Fishing Angles
## River Reference

### Default

- **Wade upstream** for the best combination of stealth, positioning, and drift control.
- Treat **wading direction** and **casting direction** as separate decisions.
- Begin below the target water and fish the nearest lies before advancing.

See [[#Separate Wading Direction from Casting Direction|Wading vs. casting direction]] and [[#The Default Approach Wade Upstream|The upstream approach]].

### Choose the presentation by tactic

- **Nymphs:** Cast upstream or up and across. Fish close, control the entry, and lead the flies without pulling them.
- **Dry flies:** Begin up and across. Use curves and slack to protect the drift.
- **Streamers:** Cast upstream when you want to strip, jig, pause, or change the fly’s direction. Cast down and across when you want a steady swing or glide.
- **Wet flies and sink tips:** Fish across or down and across, allowing the current to swing the fly through the target water.
- **Spey presentations:** Best suited to broad rivers, long casts, and downstream swings.

See [[#Nymph Presentation Angles|Nymphs]], [[#Dry-Fly Presentation Angles|Dry flies]], and [[#Streamer Presentation Angles|Streamers]].

### Change the angle when:

- A fish repeatedly refuses a good upstream presentation.
- Conflicting currents create drag.
- An obstruction prevents the preferred cast.
- The fish is holding in smooth water or a glassy tailout.
- The river is large enough to reposition without spooking the fish.
- Sunlight, shade, or access favors a different route.

### Nymphing checks

- Fish closer whenever possible.
- Maintain controlled contact as the flies enter.
- Match tippet length to the depth and speed of the water.
- Shorten the system in fast, shallow riffles.
- Use small mends or rod-tip flips to extend an indicator drift.

See [[#Nymphing Fundamentals That Affect Your Angles|Nymphing fundamentals]].

### When several fish are rising

- In low, clear water, begin with the downstream fish and work upward.
- In heavier or broken water, consider targeting the best fish first.

### After the hookup

Keep the fish upstream or beside you whenever possible. Move your feet, use side pressure, and follow a large fish rather than fighting it from a fixed position.

See [[#Fighting Fish Maintain the Upstream Angle|Fighting fish]].

> [!tip] Core rule  
> **Begin upstream. Change the angle only when the water, tactic, or fish gives you a specific reason to do so.**

## Choosing Your Wading Direction, Casting Direction, and Presentation Angle

### Summary

Wading direction and casting direction are separate decisions. In most situations, the best default is to **wade upstream**, because this improves stealth, positioning, and the ability to create a dead drift. Your casting angle should then be selected according to the tactic, current structure, and location of the fish.

For nymphs and dry flies, an upstream or up-and-across presentation is usually the most versatile starting point. Downstream presentations become useful when swinging wet flies or streamers, fishing glassy tailouts, reaching inaccessible lies, working from a boat, or adapting to complex seams. The correct angle is therefore not a fixed rule. It is the angle that produces the required presentation while allowing you to approach, hook, and fight the fish effectively.

# 1. Separate Wading Direction from Casting Direction

**28:08–29:33**

Begin by distinguishing between two questions:

1. Are you moving upstream or downstream through the river?
    
2. Are you presenting the fly upstream, across, or downstream?
    

These directions do not always have to match.

For example, an angler may wade downstream while fishing streamers but still cast upstream and across. Conversely, an angler working upstream may occasionally present a dry fly downstream to a selective fish.

Treat wading direction as a question of **approach and positioning**. Treat casting direction as a question of **presentation**.

---

# 2. The Default Approach: Wade Upstream

**29:34–32:51**

For most walk-and-wade trout fishing, work upstream. The anglers in this discussion estimated that they wade upstream approximately 90–95 percent of the time.

## Why upstream wading usually works best

### Approach fish from behind

Trout normally face into the current. Approaching from downstream keeps you behind the fish and outside much of its forward field of view.

### Reduce the effect of pressure waves

Wading creates waves and underwater disturbance. When you move downstream, those disturbances travel with the current toward the fish. When you move upstream, the current works against the waves and helps dissipate them before they reach the water ahead.

**55:58–56:21**

This advantage is most important in shallow, clear, or relatively smooth water. In rough or high-velocity water, the river may conceal more of your disturbance.

### Enter each section from the correct position

Working upstream naturally places you below a run, riffle, or pool before you begin fishing it. This allows you to start at the downstream edge and gradually work higher.

Entering from above can place you too high in the pool. That shortens your available drift, limits casting angles, and may force you into an awkward presentation.

**30:26–31:24**

### Build the river methodically

Fish each section from the bottom upward. Avoid walking directly into the best water before you have covered its lower edge.

A useful sequence is:

1. Stop below the target water.
    
2. Observe the current and likely holding lies.
    
3. Fish the closest downstream water first.
    
4. Advance only after covering the available lanes.
    
5. Continue upstream into the next position.

# 3. Exceptions to Upstream Wading

Upstream movement is the default, not an absolute rule.

## Wade downstream when an obstacle requires it

**29:42–30:11**

A deep hole, steep bank, fallen tree, or other obstruction may prevent you from continuing upstream. In that case, leave the water, move above the obstruction, and work back downstream as necessary.

## Wade downstream at night when appropriate

**30:11–30:24**

Some anglers intentionally work downstream while night fishing. Visibility is limited, fish may be less sensitive to approach, and downstream movement may better suit the chosen presentation.

## Fish downstream while returning to the access point

**54:58–55:58**

When walking back to the vehicle, you may encounter rising fish or productive water. A downstream presentation can be used without walking far below every fish and repositioning.

This is less methodical than a planned upstream approach, and it may spook more fish, but it allows you to fish water that would otherwise be passed over.

## Plan around sunlight

**31:24–32:51**

The best route is not always a continuous upstream progression. Sun angle may determine which water should be fished first.

For example:

1. Fish a shaded section before direct sunlight reaches it.
    
2. Leave the river and walk downstream.
    
3. Reenter below another shaded section.
    
4. Work upstream through that water.
    
5. Use bends, banks, and terrain to remain in favorable light.
    

Think about the river as a series of sections rather than a single uninterrupted route.

# 4. Nymphing Fundamentals That Affect Your Angles

**5:18–15:27**

Before selecting a casting angle, make sure the nymphing system itself allows you to control the flies.

## Fish closer whenever possible

**6:31–7:55**

A common problem is fishing too far away. Excessive distance makes it harder to:

- Maintain contact.
    
- Control depth.
    
- Detect strikes.
    
- Keep the flies in one current lane.
    
- Prevent drag.
    
- Set the hook quickly.
    

Large water does not automatically require long casts. Wade into a better position whenever it is safe to do so.

## Establish contact as the flies enter

**8:21–8:49**

Fish may take a nymph immediately after it lands. If the system is completely slack at entry, the fish may eat and reject the fly before the angler detects anything.

Aim for controlled contact at entry. The system does not need to be pulled tight, but it should be organized enough that you can detect an immediate take.

## Match tippet length to the water

**8:49–14:44**

The distance from the tippet ring or end of the sighter to the anchor fly should reflect the depth and speed of the water.

If the water is five feet deep and only three feet of tippet is available below the sighter, the flies may not reach the required depth without submerging the sighter.

The opposite problem occurs in shallow water. Six feet of tippet can be fished in one foot of water, but only if the angler maintains a shallow rod angle and keeps most of the tippet above the surface. Otherwise, the extra tippet produces slack, drag, and delayed strike detection.

### Practical guideline

Use enough tippet to reach the deepest water you reasonably expect to fish, but shorten the system when you will spend significant time in fast, shallow water.

A shorter system improves:

- Contact.
    
- Entry control.
    
- Strike detection.
    
- Hook-setting speed.
    
- Accuracy.
    

A longer system provides:

- More depth.
    
- Greater versatility.
    
- Less need to rebuild the leader at every pool.
    

## Keep the sighter above the water when possible

**9:18–9:46**

Keep the lower end of the sighter or backing barrel just above the surface when conditions allow. Watch for a slight bow rather than pulling the entire system perfectly straight.

A small amount of controlled slack allows the nymphs to drift naturally. A completely rigid system may drag the flies.

## Adjust more aggressively for shallow, fast riffles

**11:27–14:44**

Fast, shallow riffles can produce extremely quick takes. Trout may intercept the fly almost immediately after entry and spit it out within a fraction of a second.

These situations often benefit from:

- Shorter tippet.
    
- A compact casting range.
    
- Immediate control at entry.
    
- Close attention to the sighter.
    
- A fast but controlled hook set.
    

## Carry interchangeable rigs

**14:45–15:27**

Pre-rigged tippet sections can be stored on small foam wheels and changed at the tippet ring.

Useful options include:

- A short, light rig for shallow water.
    
- A longer or heavier rig for deeper water.
    
- Separate streamer and nymphing systems.
    
- Different tippet diameters for rapid comparison.
    

This allows you to adapt to a new water type without rebuilding the entire leader.

# 5. Nymph Presentation Angles

**43:28–49:13**

## Use upstream or up-and-across as the baseline

A true dead drift is easiest to produce when the nymphs begin upstream of the angler and drift back toward or past the angler’s position.

The standard sequence is:

1. Cast upstream or up and across.
    
2. Allow the flies to enter with controlled contact.
    
3. Lead or track the flies without pulling them.
    
4. Maintain the flies in one current lane.
    
5. Follow the drift downstream.
    
6. Set immediately when the system hesitates, jumps, or changes direction.
    

Starting the cast downstream makes it difficult to achieve a genuine dead drift because tension immediately acts against the current.

## Use downstream nymphing when access is limited

**45:47–47:51**

A downstream presentation may be necessary when:

- The river is too deep to cross or wade below the fish.
    
- A fallen tree prevents an upstream cast.
    
- The target lies beneath or beside an obstruction.
    
- A bank, ledge, or current prevents a better position.
    
- The fish is rising or holding in a distant downstream bucket.
    

This may not be the ideal angle, but an imperfect presentation is better than abandoning a productive lie without trying.

## Extend a suspension drift downstream

When fishing beneath an indicator, begin the cast upstream or across whenever possible. As the rig approaches the end of its natural drift, use small flips or mends to feed additional line downstream.

This can extend the drift into a distant bucket while minimizing drag.

The sequence is:

1. Begin above or across from the target.
    
2. Establish a natural drift.
    
3. Feed line with small rod-tip flips.
    
4. Mend only enough to prevent tension.
    
5. Allow the indicator and flies to continue downstream.
    

Avoid beginning with the rig directly downstream unless no other approach is available.

## Prepare for the fight before making a difficult cast

A fish hooked downstream beside wood or other structure immediately has a positional advantage.

Before casting:

- Use tippet strong enough for the cover.
    
- Decide where you can move.
    
- Identify the direction in which the fish must be pulled.
    
- Be prepared to apply pressure immediately.

# 6. Streamer Presentation Angles

**33:23–43:28**

Streamers can be presented effectively from multiple directions. The best angle depends on the desired movement, water type, equipment, and position of the fish.

## Down-and-across presentation

**34:31–35:30**

A downstream or down-and-across cast is especially useful with:

- Sink-tip lines.
    
- Traditional streamer swings.
    
- Wet flies.
    
- Broad rivers.
    
- Steelhead or lake-run fish.
    
- Water that is difficult to cover from close range.
    

A common presentation is:

1. Cast approximately 45 degrees downstream.
    
2. Establish immediate contact.
    
3. Allow the line and fly to sink.
    
4. Let the current carry the fly across the river.
    
5. Control the speed of the swing with rod position and line tension.
    
6. Allow the fly to hang briefly near the end of the swing when appropriate.
    

This approach maintains contact and allows a sink tip to work as intended.

## Upstream-quartering swing

**35:30–36:07**

Another option is to quarter the cast upstream, allow the fly to sink, and then let it begin fishing as it comes across and starts to swing.

This provides additional sink time before the line comes under full tension.

## Spey and two-handed presentations

**36:07–39:12**

Spey fishing is fundamentally built around downstream presentations. It is useful for:

- Covering large rivers.
    
- Delivering flies long distances.
    
- Swinging streamers or wet flies.
    
- Fishing water that cannot be reached by wading.
    
- Maintaining an efficient rhythm while covering broad runs.
    

It may not be the most productive trout tactic in every situation. Use it when the water and presentation justify the tool, or when you specifically want to fish that style.

## Advantages of casting streamers upstream

**40:43–41:40**

An upstream or up-and-across streamer cast provides more control over the fly’s movement.

From an upstream position, you can:

- Strip the fly.
    
- Jerk-strip it.
    
- Jig it.
    
- Pause and allow it to fall.
    
- Change the head angle.
    
- Allow the fly to move downstream naturally.
    
- Alternate between tension and slack.
    

Once the fly is directly downstream, the current keeps it under constant tension and limits these options.

An upstream presentation may also orient the fly’s head downstream or across the current, which some anglers find produces more confident strikes and better hookups.

## When the downstream glide is effective

**41:40–42:47**

A down-and-across presentation can be particularly effective in:

- Late winter.
    
- Early spring.
    
- Post-spawn periods.
    
- Pool tailouts.
    
- Slow, steady glides.
    
- Broad water that cannot be reached with a short-line system.
    

In these situations, the fly can glide and hang under steady tension. This movement may be more effective than an active upstream retrieve.

The key is to select the angle according to the intended motion. Do not choose the direction merely because it is traditional.

---

# 7. Wet-Fly Angles

**32:53–36:07**

Traditional wet-fly fishing is closely associated with working downstream.

## Classic wet-fly sequence

1. Position yourself upstream or slightly across from the target water.
    
2. Cast across or down and across.
    
3. Allow the flies to sink.
    
4. Maintain controlled contact.
    
5. Let the current swing the flies through the target lane.
    
6. Follow the swing with the rod.
    
7. Allow the flies to hang briefly at the end.
    
8. Step downstream and repeat.
    

This method is particularly effective in caddis water and where trout are willing to chase or intercept moving soft hackles.

Upstream wading is not required when the tactic itself is built around a downstream swing.

# 8. Dry-Fly Presentation Angles

**49:13–1:00:03**

The dry-fly discussion produces the greatest disagreement because both upstream and downstream presentations can create excellent dead drifts.

## Upstream and up-and-across presentation

This is the preferred default for most riffles, runs, and broken water.

Advantages include:

- A stealthy approach from behind the fish.
    
- Longer available drifts.
    
- Easier progression through the river.
    
- More opportunities to fish several current lanes.
    
- Better control over placement.
    
- Less disturbance of upstream fish.
    
- Familiar hook-setting mechanics.
    

The cast does not need to be directly upstream. An up-and-across angle of approximately 45 degrees is often more useful.

## Use a lagging curve

**50:50–52:01**

When presenting up and across, arrange the leader so the fly reaches the fish before the tippet and fly line create drag.

This is sometimes described as a [[lagging curve]] or **candy-cane cast**.

The layout resembles a hook or candy cane:

- The fly lands at the leading end.
    
- The tippet curves upstream or to the side.
    
- The leader and line remain outside the fly’s immediate lane.
    
- The current gradually removes the curve during the drift.
    

This allows the fly to remain free while the heavier line is positioned in a different current.

## Account for slack during the hook set

**52:01–53:45**

The curve that protects the drift also introduces slack. As the fly approaches, recover part of that slack without pulling the fly.

When the fish eats:

1. Allow the fish to close its mouth.
    
2. Lift smoothly rather than striking violently.
    
3. Set to the side when the angle allows.
    
4. Let the remaining curve provide a small delay.
    

For slow-rising fish, deliberately pause before setting. A verbal cue—such as quietly saying the fish’s name or another short phrase—can prevent an excessively fast reaction.

## Downstream dry-fly presentation

**49:31–50:50**

A downstream presentation becomes valuable in:

- Glassy pools.
    
- Smooth tailouts.
    
- Difficult spring-creek water.
    
- Rivers with selective fish.
    
- Situations where the fish may see the leader before the fly.
    
- Water with multiple conflicting current seams.
    
- Large rivers where lateral positioning is possible.
    

The fly reaches the fish first, with the leader trailing behind it. This can reduce the chance that the fish sees the tippet before the fly.

A downstream presentation may also allow the angler to use heavier tippet because the fly is presented first and the fish is hooked below the angler.

## Hook-setting from downstream

**52:01–52:56**

When the fly is below and across from the angler, set the hook to the side rather than directly upstream.

A lateral set can move the hook into the corner of the fish’s mouth and reduce the chance of pulling the fly directly away.

The angler must still wait long enough for the fish to take the fly. Downstream dry-fly fishing often requires more patience than an upstream presentation.

## Drift length

**54:05–54:58**

An accurate upstream or up-and-across cast can often produce a longer dead drift than a downstream presentation, particularly when the angler can place effective curves in the leader.

A downstream drift can be extended by feeding line and mending, but that requires careful control and may be less efficient in complex water.

## Current seams may favor downstream presentation

**58:06–58:42**

When several currents of different speeds lie between the angler and the fish, an upstream cast may place too much fly line across conflicting seams. Those currents quickly pull the line and create drag.

A downstream 45-degree presentation may place less line across the conflicting currents and preserve the drift.

Evaluate the entire path between you and the fish, not only the current directly around the fish.

# 9. Choosing Which Rising Fish to Target First

**58:42–1:00:03**

When several fish are rising, the usual strategy is to begin with the fish farthest downstream and work upward. This minimizes disturbance because the upstream fish do not see or feel the fight occurring behind them.

However, this rule can change.

## Begin downstream when:

- The water is low and clear.
    
- Fish are easily spooked.
    
- The group is tightly concentrated.
    
- Hooking one fish is likely to disturb the others.
    
- You want to maximize the total number of opportunities.
    

## Target the best fish first when:

- The water is high or broken.
    
- Disturbance is less likely to affect the other fish.
    
- One fish appears substantially larger.
    
- The primary goal is the best fish rather than the greatest number of fish.
    
- The larger fish occupies a position that may become inaccessible after another fish is hooked.
    

Observe for several minutes before casting. Compare rise forms, feeding lanes, displacement, and the amount of water each fish moves.

---

# 10. Test Both Directions on a Selective Fish

**57:14–58:08**

When a fish refuses several good upstream presentations, change the angle rather than repeatedly making the same cast.

A practical sequence is:

1. Approach from downstream.
    
2. Make five or six accurate upstream or up-and-across presentations.
    
3. If the fish refuses, stops rising, or ignores the fly, move upstream without disturbing it.
    
4. Reposition above and to the side.
    
5. Present the fly downstream.
    
6. Compare the fish’s response.
    

Some selective fish respond better when the fly arrives before the tippet and line. Testing both directions can reveal whether the problem is the fly, the drift, or the approach angle.

---

# 11. Match the Angle to River Size

**56:21–58:42**

River size determines which presentations are practical.

## Small streams

On a stream approximately 15 feet wide, a downstream presentation may require the angler to stand too close to the fish. The approach itself can ruin the opportunity.

Favor:

- Upstream wading.
    
- Short casts.
    
- Close control.
    
- Compact leader systems.
    
- Fishing each lie before advancing.
    

## Large rivers

On a river that is dozens of yards wide, the angler may have enough lateral space to position upstream, downstream, or beside the fish without approaching closely.

This creates more opportunities for:

- Downstream dry-fly presentations.
    
- Spey casts.
    
- Sink-tip swings.
    
- Long across-current casts.
    
- Deliberate repositioning around complex seams.
    

The larger river does not automatically require longer casts. It simply provides more positional options.

# 12. Fishing from a Boat

**1:00:03–1:01:35**

Casting angles from a drifting boat depend on seat position and boat control.

## Front angler

The front angler often casts ahead of the boat. The boat then drifts toward the fly and the target water.

This requires discipline. Casting too far behind the boat shortens the drift and allows the boat to overtake the presentation.

## Rear angler

The rear angler may cast:

- Across to the opposite bank.
    
- Slightly downstream.
    
- Into water that the front angler cannot reach.
    
- Behind the forward angler’s lane.
    

When both anglers fish the same bank, their available angles become more limited.

## The rower controls the drift

The boat’s speed and position influence the presentation as much as the current does.

A skilled rower can:

- Slow the boat.
    
- Hold the boat beside a feeding lane.
    
- Match the speed of the fly.
    
- Extend a drift.
    
- Reposition the anglers for another cast.
    
- Prevent the boat from overtaking the fly.
    

Communicate with the rower. The best casting angle may fail if the boat is moving at the wrong speed.

---

# 13. Fighting Fish: Maintain the Upstream Angle

**1:02:30–1:04:56**

Once a fish is hooked, the preferred position is generally to keep the fish upstream of you.

A fish directly downstream can use the current, and the combined force may make it difficult to control or recover line.

## Establish a strong position

For a large fish:

1. Adopt a balanced, athletic stance.
    
2. Keep the rod bent and maintain pressure.
    
3. Move your feet rather than fighting entirely from one position.
    
4. Work downstream or laterally until the fish is upstream or across from you.
    
5. Keep the fish away from logs, ledges, and heavy current.
    
6. Use side pressure to turn its head.
    
7. Recover line whenever the fish allows it.
    
8. Be willing to follow the fish.
    

## Change places with the fish when necessary

If the fish runs downstream, move with it. Your goal is to regain a position beside or below the fish.

Do not remain stationary while a large fish repeatedly gains distance downstream. Each run increases the amount of line and current pressure between you and the fish.

## Apply more pressure than you think

Many anglers lose control because they are afraid of breaking the tippet and apply too little pressure.

Use the rod angle and side pressure deliberately. Strong, controlled pressure is often safer than allowing the fish to enter heavy current or structure.

The correct amount depends on:

- Tippet strength.
    
- Hook size.
    
- Rod action.
    
- Current speed.
    
- Fish size.
    
- Nearby cover.
    
- The angle between the rod, line, and fish.

# 14. Practical Decision Guide

## Start with these defaults

- **Wading direction:** Upstream.
    
- **Nymphs:** Upstream or up and across.
    
- **Dry flies:** Up and across.
    
- **Streamers:** Up and across when active manipulation is desired.
    
- **Wet flies:** Across or down and across.
    
- **Sink tips:** Across or down and across.
    
- **Fighting fish:** Keep the fish upstream or beside you.
    

## Consider a downstream presentation when:

- The fish is in a smooth tailout.
    
- The fish repeatedly refuses upstream presentations.
    
- Conflicting seams drag an upstream cast.
    
- You want the fly to arrive before the leader.
    
- You are swinging wet flies or streamers.
    
- You are using a sink tip or Spey system.
    
- The target cannot be reached from below.
    
- An obstruction prevents an upstream cast.
    
- You are returning to the access point.
    
- Boat position requires it.
    

## Consider changing the wading route when:

- Direct sunlight will soon reach the water.
    
- An obstacle prevents upstream travel.
    
- The water is too deep to continue.
    
- Another angler occupies the intended section.
    
- A better approach is available from the bank.
    
- The target fish requires a downstream presentation.
    

---

# Final Principle

**42:47–43:28**

Angle selection is situational. Most of the time, an upstream approach and upstream presentation provide the best combination of stealth, control, and natural drift. Experience teaches when to break that pattern.

Do not fish upstream or downstream merely because that is how you have always done it. Decide what presentation the fish requires, identify the safest and quietest position from which to create that presentation, and choose your wading and casting angles accordingly.
