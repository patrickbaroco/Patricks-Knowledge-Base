Aphasia is an acquired disorder of **language** — the cognitive processes of formulating, understanding, and producing meaningful speech — as distinct from [[Dysarthria]], a disorder of the *motor execution* of speech. Aphasia is, with rare exceptions, a disorder of the **dominant cerebral hemisphere** (the left hemisphere in essentially all right-handed individuals and roughly 90%+ of left-handed individuals), while dysarthria can localize anywhere from the cortex down through the brainstem, cranial nerves, neuromuscular junction, or muscles themselves.

## The three-axis classification

Nearly every aphasia subtype can be classified along three independent bedside-testable axes:

- **Fluency** — is spontaneous speech effortful, halting, and reduced (non-fluent) or is it produced easily and at a normal or increased rate (fluent)?
- **Comprehension** — can the patient understand spoken and/or written language (test with commands: "show me two fingers," "close your eyes")?
- **Repetition** — can the patient repeat a spoken phrase, even one that is semantically meaningless to them?

| Subtype | Fluency | Comprehension | Repetition | Classic localization |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Broca's (motor / expressive / non-fluent) | Non-fluent | Intact | Impaired | Left inferior frontal gyrus (Brodmann 44/45) |
| Wernicke's (sensory / receptive / fluent) | Fluent | Impaired | Impaired | Left posterior superior temporal gyrus (Brodmann 22) |
| Global | Non-fluent | Impaired | Impaired | Large dominant MCA territory, both Broca's and Wernicke's areas |
| Conduction | Fluent | Intact | Impaired (disproportionately) | Arcuate fasciculus (the white-matter tract connecting Broca's and Wernicke's areas) |
| Transcortical motor | Non-fluent | Intact | Preserved (disproportionately good) | Anterior watershed / peri-Broca's, sparing Broca's area itself |
| Transcortical sensory | Fluent | Impaired | Preserved (disproportionately good) | Posterior watershed / peri-Wernicke's, sparing Wernicke's area itself |
| Mixed transcortical | Non-fluent | Impaired | Preserved (disproportionately good) | Large watershed territory around both language areas, sparing the perisylvian core |
| Anomic | Fluent | Intact | Intact | Least localizing — word-finding difficulty alone, can occur with lesions almost anywhere in the language network, or non-focally |

The transcortical aphasias are the least intuitive category: it can seem paradoxical that a patient with impaired spontaneous speech or comprehension could still repeat fluently, but the explanation is anatomic — repetition relies on a direct perisylvian loop (Wernicke's → arcuate fasciculus → Broca's) that can remain intact even when the surrounding cortex responsible for *spontaneous, self-generated* language is damaged by a watershed (border-zone) infarct.

## Bedside pearls

- **Frustration as a clue.** A patient with Broca's aphasia typically knows exactly what they want to say and appears visibly frustrated by their inability to say it — watched through a window without sound, the struggle is often obvious. A patient with Wernicke's aphasia, by contrast, speaks fluently and is often unaware of, and unbothered by, the fact that what they are saying does not make sense — through the same window, the conversation can look entirely normal. See [[CPS 193 - Impaired Speech and Broca's Aphasia from Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis|CPS 193]] and [[CPS 237 - Slurred Speech and Leptomeningeal Carcinomatosis from Gastric Adenocarcinoma|CPS 237]] for this pearl applied at the bedside.
- **Wernicke's aphasia is comparatively uncommon in practice** relative to how often it is taught, largely a function of MCA vascular territory anatomy — Broca's-pattern presentations are seen considerably more often clinically.
- **Repetition is the single most useful discriminating test** once fluency and comprehension have been assessed, since it is what separates the "core" perisylvian aphasias (Broca's, Wernicke's, global, conduction) from the transcortical aphasias, which share the same fluency/comprehension pattern but spare the repetition loop.
- **Word-finding pauses on low-frequency words** (a patient readily naming "glove" or "ball" but struggling with "cactus" or "hammock") can be an early, subtle sign of a mild non-fluent aphasia before it becomes clinically obvious as halting speech.

## Related but distinct: alexia without agraphia

A patient who cannot read (alexia) but *can* write (no agraphia) has a specific disconnection syndrome, classically from a lesion involving the left visual cortex plus the splenium of the corpus callosum (or, in a less well-known variant, the visual word form area in the left inferior medial temporal lobe) — the written word can no longer reach language cortex from either hemisphere's visual input, while the ability to *generate* written language, which does not depend on that visual pathway, is preserved. This is a genuinely distinct, more posterior localization from Broca's aphasia, and should be distinguished from a Broca's aphasia patient's inability to *read aloud* — which reflects the same expressive/output deficit responsible for their inability to speak spontaneously, not a separate visual-language disconnection. In practice, if a patient cannot vocalize written text but can still follow a written command silently (e.g., a written "close your eyes"), the reading pathway itself is intact and the deficit is output-related (Broca's), not a true alexia. This distinction was worked through directly in [[CPS 193 - Impaired Speech and Broca's Aphasia from Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis|CPS 193]].

## Chronic-tempo aphasias: primary progressive aphasia

Aphasia is not exclusively an acute, stroke-associated phenomenon. **Primary progressive aphasia (PPA)** describes a chronic, insidious language decline from neurodegenerative disease, with three recognized variants that mirror the acute syndromes above in some respects: a **non-fluent/agrammatic** variant (grammar-predominant, most often frontotemporal lobar degeneration pathology), a **semantic** variant (word-meaning-predominant, also most often frontotemporal pathology), and a **logopenic** variant (word-finding-pause-predominant, notable as the PPA variant most often associated with underlying Alzheimer's pathology rather than frontotemporal degeneration).
