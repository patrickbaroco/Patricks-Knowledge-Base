## Definition

Neurophobia is the fear and avoidance of clinical neurology among learners. Ralph Josefowicz named it in *Archives of Neurology* in 1994, and located its cause in teaching rather than in the specialty: when basic neuroscience is taught disconnected from the bedside, learners experience neurology as tract memorization with no clinical payoff, conclude the field is uniquely hard, and avoid the one thing that would have fixed it, the neurologic exam.

The diagnosis matters because the remedy follows from it. If neurology were genuinely harder than other specialties, nothing could be done. If the difficulty is an artifact of how it was taught, then a better teaching engine dissolves it. The CP Solvers Neurology Virtual Morning Report series exists as that intervention, and [[Localization × Time Course]] is the engine it runs on.

## How it operates

- **Avoidance is self-reinforcing.** The exam feels opaque, so it gets skipped; skipping it keeps it opaque. Each deferral to a consultant confirms the learner's belief that neurology is somebody else's job.
- **Unfamiliarity gets mistaken for difficulty.** A learner who would cheerfully work through a complex acid-base problem stalls at a cranial nerve deficit, not because the reasoning is harder but because the vocabulary is unpracticed.
- **The localizing power is the antidote.** Berkowitz's framing is that neurology lets you place a lesion to within centimeters at the bedside, before any imaging. Once *where* is settled, *what* collapses to a short list that time course narrows further. The specialty's reputation for difficulty inverts once this is felt in a real case.
- **It is a teaching failure, so it is fixable by teaching.** Josefowicz's original claim was diagnostic, and every episode in this series is a test of it.

## Examples from CP Solvers episodes

- **The founding statement, on air.** Berkowitz introduces himself to the Clinical Problem Solvers community and frames the mission to end neurophobia, built around his "E=mc² of neurology" schema. This is the earliest Neurology VMR recording in the vault and the fullest version of the framing; later episodes reference it rather than repeat it. ([[CPS 146 - Right-Sided Weakness and Numbness and Medial Medullary Syndrome from a Vertebral Artery Dissection|CPS 146]])
- **The antidote demonstrated on a hard case.** A 68-year-old with weeks of intermittent confusion. Fixing the *where* (brain) and the *tempo* (subacute, intermittent) collapses an enormous altered-mental-status differential into a short testable list before a single result returns. The episode names Josefowicz's 1994 coinage explicitly. ([[CPS 153 - Intermittent Altered Mental Status and Non-Lesional Autoimmune Epilepsy|CPS 153]])
- **The framing as a standing ritual.** The same end-neurophobia introduction opens episode after episode across the run, which is itself the point: the claim is repeated until it is believed. ([[CPS 160 - Hiccups and Vertigo from a PICA-Territory Stroke via Patent Foramen Ovale|CPS 160]], [[CPS 167 - Unilateral Sensory Changes and Multiple Sclerosis with a Brown-Séquard Pattern|CPS 167]])
- **Where the standard engine needs a different first move.** Movement disorders resist localization-first reasoning, so Berkowitz teaches phenomenology first: describe before you name. The engine is adapted rather than abandoned, which is what keeps the method credible. ([[CPS 186 - Left-Sided Involuntary Movements and Hemichorea from Non-Ketotic Hyperglycemia|CPS 186]])
