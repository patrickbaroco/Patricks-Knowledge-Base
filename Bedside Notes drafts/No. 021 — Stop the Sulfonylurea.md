## The Best A1c on the Med List

The admission medication list says glipizide 5 mg twice daily, and the A1c is 6.9%. It reads like a success. The drug is working, the number is at goal, and nobody wants to be the clinician who makes it worse.

The question worth asking is how the 6.9% was reached. A low A1c on metformin and an SGLT2 inhibitor is a fine result. The same A1c on a sulfonylurea, in an older resident with CKD, is a record of how much insulin her pancreas has been made to release whether she needed it or not. That is the finding that should worry us.

> [!summary] TL;DR
> - Sulfonylureas are generally unsafe in long-term care and should be stopped in nearly all residents. Don't wait for a hypoglycemic event to justify it.
> - **CAROLINA** (6,033 patients, median 6.3 years): linagliptin and glimepiride produced the same cardiovascular outcomes (11.8% vs 12.0%), but hypoglycemia occurred in **10.6% vs 37.7%**.
> - **Linagliptin-LTC** (140 residents): linagliptin 5 mg and glargine (mean 10.6 units a day) produced similar mean daily glucose (146 vs 157 mg/dL), but glucose below 70 occurred in **3% vs 37%**.
> - Fluoroquinolones, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, clarithromycin, metronidazole and fluconazole all raise the effective sulfonylurea dose. Two of those are standard UTI treatment.
> - Replace it: a DPP-4 inhibitor (generic sitagliptin, dosed by eGFR) for most residents, or repaglinide if there is a real reason to keep a secretagogue.

## Why the Low Comes at Night

A beta cell releases insulin when glucose rises. Glucose enters the cell, its metabolism closes a potassium channel, and the cell secretes. That channel is how the beta cell knows the glucose is high.

A sulfonylurea closes the channel directly. It is a thermostat with the wire cut: the furnace runs whether the house is cold or not. Insulin keeps coming however low the glucose has already fallen. The effect is prolonged in older people and in renal impairment, which describes most of our residents, and so the low tends to arrive overnight, hours after the last meal, when no one is eating and no one is checking.

Glyburide is the worst of the class and is Beers-listed. Glimepiride and extended-release glipizide are not meaningfully safer in an older resident with CKD.

## What the Trials Show

> [!info] CAROLINA (Rosenstock, *JAMA* 2019)
> - Randomized, double-blind: linagliptin 5 mg vs glimepiride 1 to 4 mg, added to usual care, 6,033 adults with type 2 diabetes and elevated cardiovascular risk, followed for a median of 6.3 years.
> - Major cardiovascular events: 11.8% vs 12.0% (HR 0.98). Noninferior, not superior.
> - At least one hypoglycemic event: 10.6% vs 37.7% (HR 0.23).
> - Glycemic control was similar between the arms, and weight favored linagliptin by 1.5 kg.

CAROLINA enrolled people with a mean age of 64 and relatively early diabetes. Our residents are older, frailer and more often living with CKD, all of which prolong a sulfonylurea's effect, so the hypoglycemia gap is unlikely to be smaller in our population.

> [!info] Linagliptin-LTC (Umpierrez, *JAMDA* 2018)
> - Open-label, three Atlanta facilities, 140 residents with type 2 diabetes and an A1c near 7.9%.
> - Linagliptin 5 mg daily vs glargine started at 0.1 units/kg/day and titrated (mean dose 10.6 units a day).
> - Mean daily glucose 146 vs 157 mg/dL, meeting the noninferiority margin.
> - Glucose below 70 mg/dL in 3% vs 37% of residents (3 events vs 136).

Linagliptin-LTC is not a sulfonylurea trial, but it answers the next question. When the sulfonylurea comes off, insulin is not the automatic replacement. Ten units of glargine a day bought no better control than a pill, with twelve times the hypoglycemia. The limits are real: 84% of the residents were post-acute admissions, the mean stay was 49 days, so exposure was about seven weeks, residents with an eGFR below 45 were excluded, and both arms carried a correction scale.

## The Interaction Problem

Fluoroquinolones, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, clarithromycin, metronidazole and fluconazole each raise the effective dose of a sulfonylurea enough to cause hypoglycemia, so the sulfonylurea has to be reduced or held whenever one of them is started. Two of those are the standard treatment for a urinary tract infection, in residents who are treated for infection several times a year.

A drug that needs holding every time an infection is treated is a drug being managed around rather than with.

## The Argument That Doesn't Apply Here

The strongest outpatient case for keeping a sulfonylurea is practical: nobody at home can inject insulin. In a facility a nurse gives injections every day, so that argument does not exist. And as Linagliptin-LTC shows, most residents coming off a sulfonylurea do not need insulin either.

## What to Use Instead

Never remove without replacing. The choice follows the hierarchy in the [[Diabetes in LTC]] chapter: a GLP-1 receptor agonist, an SGLT2 inhibitor, metformin or a DPP-4 inhibitor first, depending on what else the resident carries.

- **DPP-4 inhibitor, for most residents.** Generic sitagliptin is preferred on cost: 100 mg at an eGFR of 45 or above, 50 mg at 30 to 44, and 25 mg below 30. Expect about 0.5% off the A1c. Linagliptin needs no renal adjustment at any eGFR but costs roughly ten times as much; it is worth a prior authorization when the eGFR is low, fluctuating or unreliably measured, and the CAROLINA hypoglycemia figures are the script for that request. Avoid saxagliptin and alogliptin (heart failure hospitalization signals in SAVOR-TIMI 53 and EXAMINE).
- **Repaglinide, if a secretagogue is truly needed.** It works through the same channel with a much shorter duration of action. Start 0.5 mg within 30 minutes before each meal when the A1c is under 8%, or 1 to 2 mg when it is 8% or higher, and double no more often than weekly to a ceiling of 4 mg per meal and 16 mg a day. It is held when the meal is skipped; if it is not held, it carries the same risk as the drug it replaced. No adjustment is needed down to a creatinine clearance of 40; between 20 and 40, start at 0.5 mg and titrate slowly. Clopidogrel limits repaglinide to 0.5 mg per meal and 4 mg a day.

> [!example] A switch
> A resident with an A1c of 7.4% is on glipizide 5 mg twice daily. She eats breakfast and dinner reliably and usually refuses lunch. Stop the glipizide and start repaglinide 0.5 mg at the start of breakfast and dinner, held if she refuses the meal. The order matches how she actually eats.

## After the Switch

Check a fasting glucose daily for the first week to catch symptomatic rebound, then an A1c at three months. A modest rise in the A1c is the intended result.

## Talking with Families

The family has often been told for years that a lower number is better, and a 6.3% looks like the best result in a long time. From the chapter:

> *"I know a 6.3 looks like the best result he has had in years. For years everyone told you high blood sugar damages the body, and that is true. But low blood sugar damages it too. Each low chips away at memory and thinking, and it is a very common reason someone his age on this medication ends up falling. We are going to take that drug away, let the number drift up a little, and protect the things you are most afraid of losing."*

## Thoughts and Takeaways

Sulfonylureas are cheap, familiar and effective at lowering a number, which is why they persist on admission lists. Every one of those virtues belongs to the outpatient world. In a facility the injection barrier is gone, the antibiotic interactions are constant, and the resident who drops to 50 at 3 a.m. is the resident most likely to fall on the way to the bathroom.

Waiting for a documented low before stopping one means waiting for the event we are trying to prevent. Nocturnal lows in a resident with a reassuring A1c are found only if someone looks, and often no one does.

> [!important] Bottom Line
> Stop the sulfonylurea at admission or at the next visit, in nearly every resident, without waiting for a low. Replace it with a DPP-4 inhibitor or another first-tier agent, or with repaglinide matched to the meals the resident actually eats. Check fasting glucose for a week, recheck the A1c at three months, and expect it to rise a little.
