## What the Scale Actually Gave

Open the chart of any resident with a correction scale and the order is easy to find: four tiers, rapid-acting insulin, before meals and at bedtime. Then open the MAR and add up what was actually given over the past month. That number is rarely in the note, and it is the one that tells you what to do next.

Correction insulin feels safe because it is given only when needed. Many residents arrive with it from the hospital, where it sits inside a system of frequent checks and meal trays timed to the dose. In a facility those conditions are not reliably present, and the scale tends to outlive the illness that started it.

> [!summary] TL;DR
> - A sliding scale without basal insulin is almost never appropriate in long-term care. A correction scale on top of basal insulin should be rare, short and simple.
> - The risk is asymmetric. A week of asymptomatic glucose in the 200s causes no meaningful harm; one correction dose given to a resident who then doesn't eat can cause a low, and every low in this population is a fall, a delirium or a cardiac event.
> - Read the MAR. What was given over the past month decides the next step: stop the scale, add a missing dose, or titrate the basal.
> - A correction needed every day is a dose adjustment waiting to be made. Make it, then stop the scale.
> - Document what was actually given, over what period, and what you intend to do about it.

## Three Different Orders

The terms get used interchangeably, and they shouldn't be.

- **Sliding scale:** rapid-acting insulin dosed by the current glucose with no basal insulin. It is reactive and poorly timed, and it produces alternating lows and highs without addressing the pattern underneath. The 2023 AGS Beers Criteria list it as a medication to avoid.
- **Correction insulin:** a supplemental rapid-acting dose layered on a scheduled basal regimen to trim a pre-meal excursion.
- **Prandial insulin:** a scheduled dose of rapid-acting insulin given with a specific meal, usually the largest one.

Most of what follows is about the second, because it is the order that persists quietly for months.

## Why Precision Is the Problem

Rapid-acting insulin has an onset of about 15 minutes, a peak near one hour and a duration of about four hours. That precision is exactly why it covers a meal well when the dose and the tray arrive together. It is also why it is dangerous when they don't. In a facility the medication pass and the meal tray do not always arrive together, and a dose given before a meal that goes uneaten has nothing to work on but the resident's glucose.

Two more problems follow. A second correction given inside four hours of the first stacks on top of it. And the scale hides the pattern it was meant to bridge: once a value has been corrected, it can no longer be read to adjust the basal or prandial dose.

## The Asymmetry

The argument for stopping a correction scale rests on comparing what can go wrong in each direction. A week of asymptomatic values in the 200s while a basal dose is titrated causes no meaningful harm. A single correction dose given to a resident who then does not eat can cause hypoglycemia. Short-term hyperglycemia is recoverable. Hypoglycemia is not reliably so.

## Read the MAR

> [!tip] What the last month shows, and what to do
> - **Rarely given (a few units a week):** stop the scale. It adds fingersticks and risk without changing the glucose.
> - **Given most days around the same meal:** a prandial dose is missing. In a resident who qualifies, a GLP-1 agonist is the first addition. Otherwise give one dose of rapid-acting insulin after the largest meal: 4 units or 10% of the basal dose, given after the meal once intake is known, held if less than half was eaten, and titrated by 1 to 2 units every three days against the glucose before the next meal. Then stop the scale.
> - **Given most mornings before breakfast:** the basal dose is too low. Check the overnight drop first; if the fasting value is 50 mg/dL or more below the bedtime value, meals are the problem and more basal insulin is the wrong answer. Otherwise titrate every third day against the mean fasting glucose. Then stop the scale.
> - **Large doses at most meals on a small basal dose:** this is a sliding scale in all but name. Add up the total daily dose and rebuild the regimen.
> - **Resident not eating reliably:** stop the scale today.

The pre-meal values tell you which meal is responsible. The pre-lunch glucose reflects breakfast, the pre-dinner glucose reflects lunch, and the bedtime glucose reflects dinner.

## The Hospital Return

A resident returning from the hospital on insulin gets a regimen review within 72 hours, rebuilt from the total daily dose rather than transcribed. Hospital regimens are built around acute illness and around correction scales, and the need usually falls once the illness resolves.

A sliding scale is discontinued on admission. A correction scale is kept only for a resident with type 1 diabetes or insulin deficiency, or for a transient driver such as a steroid course or a resolving infection. If it is kept, it runs with meals only, never at bedtime, in two tiers, and stops automatically at seven days unless renewed with a documented reason. A scale that fires twice a day for three days is telling you to adjust a dose.

## If a Scale Stays

> [!info] The rules
> - Rapid-acting insulin only, with meals only, never at bedtime, and never within four hours of the previous dose.
> - One rule the medication pass can execute: 1 unit for every 50 mg/dL above 200, to a maximum of 4 units, in a resident whose total daily dose is under 40 units.
> - No correction scale for a resident who is not eating reliably.
> - A correction needed daily means a basal or prandial dose needs adjusting. Once it is adjusted, the scale is discontinued.

> [!example] A case from the chapter
> A 68 kg woman is admitted on glargine 40 units twice daily and a four-tier correction scale, with an A1c of 6.9%. Her transfer log shows fasting values of 85 to 110 mg/dL, bedtime values of 200 to 230 and one value of 62. Her total daily dose is over 1 unit/kg, the log contains a low, and the overnight decline exceeds 100 mg/dL.
>
> On admission the correction scale is stopped and glargine is restarted at 20 units each morning (0.3 units/kg). On day seven her fasting values are 110 to 135 while bedtime values stay near 210, so dinner is uncovered. Aspart 4 units is added after dinner, held if she eats less than half. Two weeks later she is on glargine 24 units and aspart 4 units, with no correction scale, no hypoglycemia and half the fingersticks.

## Documenting What Was Given

An order says only what is available to give. The amount administered shows whether the regimen is working, and it should drive any change to the basal dose. A note that addresses the order without saying what was given leaves the important question unanswered.

Two examples that answer it:

> "Receiving approximately 4 units every other day over the past month. Continuing, because [reason]."

> "Receiving approximately 6 units with each meal. Increasing basal insulin; will monitor correction insulin given over the next two weeks and reassess."

Two reasons that don't hold up on their own. "Simplifying the regimen" does not describe a correction-only regimen, which means more injections than a single daily basal dose and more hypoglycemia risk. And when a resident or family declines scheduled insulin, the note should show that the alternative was explained and specifically declined.

## Thoughts and Takeaways

The correction scale survives because each renewal is a small, defensible decision. Nobody chose to give a resident six units with every meal for three months; it accumulated, one renewed order at a time, while the basal dose that should have absorbed it stayed where it was.

Adding up the MAR is the whole intervention. A clinician who knows the number almost always knows what to do with it: stop a scale that is never used, convert a scale that is always used into the dose it is standing in for, and stop any scale in a resident who isn't eating.

> [!important] Bottom Line
> Before renewing a correction scale, count what it actually delivered over the past month and write that number in the note. Then stop it, convert it into the basal or prandial dose it has been covering for, or keep it with a stated reason and a date to look again.
