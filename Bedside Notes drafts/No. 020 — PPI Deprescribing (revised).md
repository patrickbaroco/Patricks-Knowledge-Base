## Tell the Patient

Pull the med list on almost any resident who's been in a facility more than a year and you'll find a PPI. Ask why, and the honest answer is usually "it was on the list when she got here." It was started after a GI bleed a decade ago, or during a hospitalization for something unrelated, or because a bedbound patient on prednisone and an NSAID seemed to need one at the time. Whatever the reason was, it hasn't been revisited since.

We've known for years that this is a problem worth fixing. A new trial out of France tells us which fix actually works, and it isn't the one most of us have been relying on.

> [!summary] TL;DR
> - A cluster-randomized trial of over 34,000 patients on long-term PPIs found that giving **patients** an educational brochure, on top of the usual guidance to their prescriber, roughly **doubled** successful deprescribing compared with targeting the prescriber alone (15% vs. 7-8%).
> - GERD symptom control was **no worse** in the group that cut back the most. Fear of rebound symptoms shouldn't be the reason we leave PPIs alone.
> - Deprescribing efforts aimed only at the prescriber's inbox underperform ones that also inform the patient (in our setting, the resident and family). That lesson reaches well beyond PPIs.
> - PPIs are one of the few classes where a taper genuinely matters, unlike the [[No. 017 — When Deprescribing Doesn’t Need a Taper|drugs we covered in issue 17]]. Stopping cold after months of use can cause rebound acid hypersecretion.

## Why This Matters

I'm pretty ruthless with PPIs, and they're near the top of every deprescribing list for good reason. Most patients started on one never had a clear, ongoing indication in the first place, and long-term use carries real if individually modest risks: hip fracture, *C. difficile* infection, hypomagnesemia, B12 deficiency, and community-acquired pneumonia all have reasonably solid signal behind them. Associations with chronic kidney disease and dementia are weaker and more likely confounded, and I hold those loosely. But the overall picture is a drug we hand out easily and revisit rarely, in a population where unreviewed medications drive falls, fractures, and infections.

The hard part has always been getting the deprescribing to happen. Chart reviews flag them, pharmacy consults recommend stopping them, and the recommendation dies quietly at the next visit because nobody wants to be the one who reintroduces a resident's heartburn.

## The Trial

A cluster-randomized trial published in *JAMA Internal Medicine* (April 2026) tested this directly. It enrolled 34,409 patients who had been on a PPI for at least a year, cared for by nearly 1,500 GPs in 683 practices in western France. Practices were randomized to usual care, to a deprescribing algorithm sent to the GP alone, or to the same algorithm plus a brochure for the patient explaining why the medication might no longer be needed and what to expect if it's reduced.

> [!info] Results
> - Usual care: 7.0% achieved a ≥50% reduction in PPI use over the study period.
> - GP-only guidance: 7.7%. Essentially no better than doing nothing.
> - Combined patient + GP intervention: 14.9%, roughly double either comparator.
> - Reflux symptom scores were statistically comparable across all three arms. The group that deprescribed the most did not report worse control of their symptoms.

The GP-only arm barely moved the needle: 7.7% against 7.0% for doing nothing at all. We tend to assume that if we get the right information in front of the prescriber, behavior will follow. It didn't. Giving the patient a reason to ask the question themselves doubled the rate.

## Clinical Perspective

The friction in deprescribing lives in the visit. A recommendation that exists only in a chart or a pharmacy note asks the prescriber to spend a few minutes of a busy visit starting a conversation nobody requested, defending a change against a habit, and taking on (in their mind, at least) the risk of a symptom flare. A patient who's already been told "this may not still be helping you, and here's what to watch for" walks into that same visit as a collaborator.

That makes deprescribing a communication problem as much as a pharmacology one. In [[No. 015 — Communication and Understanding|Communication and Understanding]] I wrote that understanding is built between the speaker and the listener. This trial is a rare case where someone measured what happens when the listener is left out.

This is one well-conducted trial in French primary care outpatients with a mean age of 68.6. Our residents are older and frailer, and effect sizes like this don't always survive replication. But direct patient involvement has outperformed prescriber-only nudges often enough elsewhere in the deprescribing literature that I'm willing to act on it now rather than wait for a confirmatory trial in our population.

## Practical Implications

> [!tip] What this looks like in LTC and AL
> - **Route deprescribing conversations through the resident or family, not just the chart.** A pharmacy consult that only reaches the physician is starting from the weaker arm of this trial. A short conversation, or even a one-page explanation, aimed at the resident or their POA changes the odds.
> - **Taper, don't stop.** PPIs are an exception to the "just stop it" approach. After more than a few weeks of use, stopping abruptly can cause rebound acid hypersecretion that's worse than the original symptoms, and that's the outcome that scares everyone off trying in the first place. A step-down (once daily to every other day, or a switch to an H2 blocker for a few weeks) avoids that trap.
> - **Set expectations explicitly.** Tell the resident or family that some transient reflux during the taper is possible and expected, and doesn't mean the medication needs to restart. That expectation-setting is likely a good chunk of why the brochure arm worked.
> - **Don't let "no clear indication" be the whole conversation.** Naming a concrete reason (fracture risk, infection risk, one less pill) tends to land better than a general "we're trying to reduce medications."

> [!important] Bottom Line
> If your deprescribing efforts stop at a note in the chart or a message to the prescriber, this trial suggests you're leaving most of the benefit on the table. Bring the resident and family into the conversation directly, taper rather than stop, and don't let fear of rebound symptoms be the reason a medication with no remaining indication stays on the list indefinitely.
