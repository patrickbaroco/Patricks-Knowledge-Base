## Deprescribing Is a Conversation, Not a Chart Review

Pull the med list on almost any resident who's been in a facility more than a year and you'll find a PPI. Ask why, and the honest answer is usually "it was on the list when she got here." Started after a GI bleed a decade ago, or during a hospitalization for something unrelated, or just because a bedbound patient on prednisone and an NSAID seemed to need one at the time. Nobody remembers, and nobody has revisited it since.

We've known for years that this is a problem worth fixing. What a new trial adds is a much more useful piece of information: which fix actually works.

> [!summary] TL;DR
> - A cluster-randomized trial of over 34,000 patients on long-term PPIs found that giving **patients** an educational brochure, on top of the usual guidance to their prescriber, roughly **doubled** successful deprescribing compared with targeting the prescriber alone (15% vs. 7-8%).
> - GERD symptom control was **no worse** in the group that cut back the most. Fear of rebound symptoms shouldn't be the reason we leave PPIs alone.
> - The lesson isn't really about PPIs. It's that deprescribing efforts aimed only at the physician's inbox routinely underperform ones that also inform the patient (or in our setting, the resident and family).
> - PPIs are one of the few classes where a taper genuinely matters, unlike the [[No. 017 — When Deprescribing Doesn't Need a Taper|drugs we covered in issue 17]]. Stopping cold after months of use can cause rebound acid hypersecretion.

## Why This Matters

PPIs are near the top of every deprescribing list for good reason. Most patients started on one never had a clear, ongoing indication in the first place, and long-term use carries real if individually modest risks: hip fracture, *C. difficile* infection, hypomagnesemia, B12 deficiency, and community-acquired pneumonia all have reasonably solid signal behind them. Associations with chronic kidney disease and dementia are weaker and more likely confounded, and I'd hold those loosely, but the overall picture is a drug we hand out easily and revisit rarely, in a population where the accumulated risk of "rarely revisited" medications is exactly what drives falls, fractures, and infections.

The problem has never really been *whether* to deprescribe PPIs. It's been getting it to actually happen. Chart reviews flag them, pharmacy consults recommend stopping them, and the recommendation dies quietly at the next visit because nobody wants to be the one who reintroduces a resident's heartburn.

## What Changed

A cluster-randomized trial published in *JAMA Internal Medicine* (April 2026) tested this directly. Roughly 1,500 general practices in western France, more than 34,000 patients on a PPI for at least a year, three arms: usual care, a deprescribing algorithm sent to the GP alone, and the same algorithm plus a patient-facing brochure explaining why the medication might no longer be needed and what to expect if it's reduced.

> [!info] Results
> - Usual care: 7.0% achieved a ≥50% reduction in PPI use over the study period.
> - GP-only guidance: 7.7%. Essentially no better than doing nothing.
> - Combined patient + GP intervention: 15.0%, roughly double either comparator.
> - Reflux symptom scores were statistically comparable across all three arms. The group that deprescribed the most did not report worse control of their symptoms.

The GP-only arm is the finding I keep coming back to. We tend to assume that if we just get the right information in front of the prescriber, behavior will follow. Here, it barely moved the needle. What moved it was giving the patient a reason to ask the question themselves.

## Clinical Perspective

None of this is really about acid suppression. It's about where the friction in deprescribing actually lives. A recommendation that only exists in a chart or a pharmacy note asks the prescriber to spend a few minutes of a busy visit initiating a conversation nobody requested, defending a change against a habit, and taking on (in their mind, at least) the risk of a symptom flare. A patient who's already been told "this may not still be helping you, and here's what to watch for" walks into that same visit as a collaborator instead of a passive recipient of a change being done to them.

That reframes deprescribing less as a pharmacology problem and more as a communication one. [[No. 015 — Communication and Understanding|We've talked before]] about how much of what we call "noncompliance" is really a failure to actually explain ourselves. This trial is a rare case where someone measured it.

I'd also flag the humility point: this is one well-conducted trial in French primary care outpatients, not nursing home residents, and effect sizes like this don't always survive replication. But the mechanism, that direct patient involvement outperforms prescriber-only nudges, has shown up often enough elsewhere in the deprescribing literature that I think it's worth acting on now rather than waiting for a confirmatory trial in our specific population.

## Practical Implications

> [!tip] What this looks like in LTC and AL
> - **Route deprescribing conversations through the resident or family, not just the chart.** A pharmacy consult that only reaches the physician is starting from the weaker arm of this trial. A short conversation, or even a one-page explanation, aimed at the resident or their POA changes the odds.
> - **Taper, don't stop.** PPIs are an exception to the "just stop it" approach. After more than a few weeks of use, abrupt discontinuation can cause rebound acid hypersecretion, worse than the original symptoms, which is exactly the outcome that scares everyone off trying in the first place. A step-down (once daily to every other day, or a switch to an H2 blocker for a few weeks) avoids that trap.
> - **Set expectations explicitly.** Tell the resident or family that some transient reflux during the taper is possible and expected, and doesn't mean the medication needs to restart. That expectation-setting is likely a good chunk of why the brochure arm worked.
> - **Don't let "no clear indication" be the whole conversation.** Naming a concrete reason, fracture risk, infection risk, one less pill, tends to land better than a general "we're trying to reduce medications."

## Bottom Line

> [!important] Bottom Line
> If your deprescribing efforts stop at a note in the chart or a message to the prescriber, this trial suggests you're leaving most of the benefit on the table. Bring the resident and family into the conversation directly, taper rather than stop, and don't let fear of rebound symptoms be the reason a medication with no remaining indication stays on the list indefinitely.
