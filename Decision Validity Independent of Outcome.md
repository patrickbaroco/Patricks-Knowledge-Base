## Definition

A decision is judged by the information available when it was made, not by what happened afterward. A test that returns positive does not retroactively make ordering it good reasoning, and a diagnosis caught late does not prove the earlier reasoning was faulty.

The bias runs in both directions and the upward direction is the one that goes unnoticed. Teams routinely audit decisions that preceded bad outcomes and almost never audit the ones that preceded good outcomes, so poor reasoning that happened to land well is quietly absorbed as competence.

## How it operates

- **Retrospective coherence is manufactured.** Once the answer is known, the path to it looks obvious and the wrong turns look avoidable. The reasoning has not changed; only the reader's information has.
- **The upward error is the invisible one.** Crediting a decision because of how it turned out teaches the wrong lesson to whoever made it, and it feels generous rather than sloppy, which is why it survives.
- **Debriefing needs two separate questions.** Was the reasoning sound given what we knew? And separately, did we get it right? Collapsing these produces either false guilt or false reassurance.
- **Self-scrutiny should be proportional to error, not to outcome severity.** A minor oversight and a consequential error both deserve reflection; only one deserves the weight of a bad outcome attached to it.
- **A rigorous differential keeps its value when the answer turns out simple.** The breadth was the right response to the uncertainty that existed at the time.

## Examples from RLR episodes

- **The lesson delivered twice in one episode.** Reza's intern Paul pushed for a CT that Reza and his senior resident Emma declined; palliative care later independently recommended the same scan, which revealed a care-changing abnormality. Reza publicly credited Paul, and Emma pointed out that crediting the decision *because of how it turned out* was itself the error. The same point returns in Robbie's closing reflection: the CT was ordered while both hosts were fixated on empyema recurrence and then *C. difficile*, not on the opiate-induced constipation that was the answer. The result did not validate the reasoning that produced it. ([[RLR 152 - Fixated on Empyema and C. diff- Opiate-Induced Constipation and Stercoral Colitis Hiding in Plain Sight|RLR 152]])
- **Coaching a team through misplaced guilt.** After a delayed stroke diagnosis, an intern recalls the patient reaching for his phone and not finding it days earlier, and a senior resident wishes she had done a more thorough neurologic exam. Reza works through the debrief without endorsing the premise that the outcome proved the earlier reasoning wrong. ([[RLR 160 - No Fat to Inflame- Recurrent Gallstone Pancreatitis in a Lean Patient, and the Physical Therapist Who Caught a Silent Stroke|RLR 160]])
- **The differential that turned out unnecessary.** An extensive structural-vulnerability workup for cryptogenic bacteremia, when the real explanation was a New Orleans seafood trip and *Vibrio vulnificus*. The episode closes on the point directly: a rigorous differential is valuable even when the correct answer did not need it. ([[RLR 165 - Draw and Hold- Rigors, Bacteremia, and a Vibrio Vulnificus Diagnosis from a New Orleans Seafood Trip|RLR 165]])
- **Calibrating how much a miss should weigh.** A missed fever, an intern's intense self-blame, and an infection that resolved without intervention. The episode argues for anchoring self-scrutiny to the harm actually caused rather than to a colleague's reaction, and for distinguishing diligent-but-imperfect care from genuine error. ([[RLR 142 - A Missed Fever, a Blamed Intern, and a UTI That Almost Fixed Itself- A Case Study in Feedback, Not Diagnosis|RLR 142]])
