A schema for abnormal bleeding: localize the defect to the blood, the vessel wall, or the bridge (von Willebrand factor), then quadrant the blood problem.

## The Big Picture: Blood, Wall, or Bridge
Effective hemostasis requires intact blood components, an intact vessel wall, and an intact **bridge** that tethers the forming clot to the wall. Abnormal bleeding therefore localizes to one of three buckets ([[RLR 064 - Hematuria and Post-TAVR Perivalvular Leak|RLR 064]]):

- **Blood** — a problem in the platelets or the coagulation factors.
- **Vessel wall** — a structural or angiogenic lesion (tumor, ulcer, angiodysplasia, varix). The lesion itself provides the source.
- **Bridge** — **von Willebrand factor (vWF)**, which anchors platelets to exposed subendothelium and carries factor VIII. Defects here (congenital or [[Acquired von Willebrand Syndrome|acquired]]) impair primary hemostasis despite normal platelet counts and normal PT/PTT.

**Proportionality drives the search.** When a structural wall lesion is present but the patient bleeds **out of proportion** to it, a coexisting problem in the blood or the bridge must be interrogated rather than accepting the anatomic lesion as sufficient ([[RLR 064 - Hematuria and Post-TAVR Perivalvular Leak|RLR 064]]; see [[Law of Proportionality]]).

## Quadranting the Blood Problem
Once the "blood" bucket is in play, cross two axes — **quantitative vs qualitative** and **platelet vs coagulation** — to produce four checkable categories ([[RLR 064 - Hematuria and Post-TAVR Perivalvular Leak|RLR 064]]):

- **Quantitative platelet** — [[Thrombocytopenia|thrombocytopenia]]. Counts **>50k** are usually hemostatically sufficient in the absence of another defect, so a modestly low count (e.g., 60k) is at most a minor contributor to significant bleeding ([[RLR 064 - Hematuria and Post-TAVR Perivalvular Leak|RLR 064]]).
- **Quantitative coagulation** — factor deficiency/consumption, reflected in a prolonged **PT/PTT**. A normal PT/PTT excludes most quantitative coagulopathies.
- **Qualitative platelet** — normal count, dysfunctional platelets. Three broad causes: **medications** (aspirin, clopidogrel, NSAIDs), **[[Uremic Platelet Dysfunction|uremia]]**, and **genetic** disorders (Glanzmann, Bernard-Soulier — implausible when new in an elderly patient) ([[RLR 064 - Hematuria and Post-TAVR Perivalvular Leak|RLR 064]]).
- **Qualitative coagulation** — factors present but working poorly, or a defect the screen misses: **hypothermia** (enzymatic factors slow when cold), **hyperviscosity**, and **factor XII deficiency** (so proximal in the intrinsic cascade that it is invisible on routine PT/PTT — and paradoxically pro-thrombotic, but a classic "screen-invisible" teaching point) ([[RLR 064 - Hematuria and Post-TAVR Perivalvular Leak|RLR 064]]).

## Working the Bridge
When platelets and PT/PTT are unrevealing, pivot to vWF. **Acquired von Willebrand syndrome** should be suspected in patients with **vascular shear** (stenotic/prosthetic valves, LVADs, AV fistulas), **anti-vWF antibodies**, or **chelation by malignancy** (myeloproliferative neoplasms) ([[RLR 064 - Hematuria and Post-TAVR Perivalvular Leak|RLR 064]]). See [[Acquired von Willebrand Syndrome]].

## Using the Framework at the Bedside
- **Confirm the bleeding is real and localized.** For mucocutaneous bleeding (petechiae, epistaxis, menorrhagia) suspect a primary hemostasis / platelet / vWF defect; for deep bleeding into joints and muscles suspect a coagulation-factor defect.
- **Know your lane.** Thresholds for DDAVP, platelet transfusion, and vWF assays are frequently a hematology decision; the internist's job is to localize the defect and consult appropriately ([[RLR 064 - Hematuria and Post-TAVR Perivalvular Leak|RLR 064]]).
