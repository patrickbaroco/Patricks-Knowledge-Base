# CGSC — Antipsychotic Use in Dementia, 2026-09-28

3:30–4pm, Teams. The first CGSC I set the agenda for. Jennie Byrne did not attend, and Dr. Williams (BH) covered psychiatry. Will Sampson, Jen Rucci and Dr. Williams did the talking; most of the invite list never responded. Source: meeting transcript, about 31 minutes. The transcript's speakers are unlabeled and are identified here from context.

**Bottom line: the framing landed and nobody challenged the method.** Discussion went two places: who belongs in the denominator, and why assisted living runs so much higher than skilled nursing. Rucci closed with the year's verdict: *"we've made no progress, and it's almost October."* The measure comes back for 2027 in some form, and her ask is that we not make the same mistakes twice.

## What I presented

Rules and rates, the frame from my prior job: rates at the enterprise level, rules at the individual level. These are rates. Restricting to residents with dementia, as Byrne suggested by email, is the better measure. It removes Kentucky's primary psychiatric work in younger patients without dementia, and the number is still bad: about 24% of residents 65+ with dementia are on an antipsychotic.

Two findings. The gap is concentrated: 50 buildings, about 4% of the 1,202, hold about a quarter of the excess over a 10% target, which is both astonishing and a reason for optimism. And the rate climbs with dementia density. Buildings with more residents with dementia put a larger share of them on an antipsychotic, and that comes down largely to memory care. Durham Ridge 40 of 97; Willow Ridge 31 of 40.

Both problems are real at once. Several hundred buildings would have to reach goal for the enterprise to reach goal, which is widespread overuse, and a few dozen buildings hold a double-digit share of it, several with 60% of their residents with dementia on an antipsychotic. In the Virginia outliers I opened, residents had no other qualifying diagnosis and the notes almost all read *failed prior GDR attempts*. That leads to the rule: what individual-level standard defines performance for a resident who stays on one.

## The denominator

**Rucci confirmed Durham Ridge's denominator is residents with dementia.** Will: the building began as a facility for people with serious and persistent mental illness and is now essentially memory care, with many residents who aged in from the earlier population. Comorbid primary psychotic disorders are likely far more common there than in a typical building. (Rucci, after the meeting, went further: most of its dementia diagnoses are wrong. See [[Jen Rucci — Clinical Quality Planning, 2026-09-28]].)

**Rucci's distinction: excluding residents with psychotic disorders and restricting to residents with dementia are different cuts.** A dementia-only denominator that keeps residents with comorbid schizophrenia counts people who should stay on their antipsychotic.

Several ADCOs I sent the graphs to said the same about particular buildings: high primary psychiatric populations that should fall out. My hesitation about excluding psychotic diagnoses wholesale is Virginia's large inherited stock of schizophrenia and schizoaffective disorder first diagnosed in patients' 70s and 80s. Those inaccurate, poorly documented diagnoses are exactly what we should be catching, and they put facilities at real risk. Dropping whole buildings with high primary psychiatric rates is defensible. Dropping the diagnosis at the resident level would hide the problem; that belongs in an individual drill-down.

**Diagnostic accuracy.** Rucci, from supervising our psychiatric providers: diagnoses are poor across the board, and she finds dementia wrong more often than psychotic disorders. Will split it in two: whether a resident has a dementing illness is probably accurate, and which syndrome (Alzheimer disease, vascular, Lewy body) is not. Rucci agreed that was her meaning. In skilled care I see the first level as reliable, since the BIMS gives objective data on everyone. The error I see runs the other way: hospital discharges arrive with schizophrenia listed to explain risperidone. Rucci calls it *retrospective diagnosing* from the medication list, and required indication fields make it worse. Syndrome classification is the most requested of my NP talks (the 4×4 dementia grid), helped along by unspecified dementia not being billable.

## Assisted living versus skilled nursing

**Dr. Williams asked about the ALF/SNF split, and why we measure assisted living at all when CMS does not, unless the operators want it.** The rate is higher in ALF by quite a bit. I see it inside single CCRCs with the same provider and much the same population. Bridgewater is the best building in Jacob's data, 1 of 47 on the SNF side, and not as good on the AL side.

My argument is that CMS is right about this measure. Antipsychotics in patients with dementia are lethal and overused, including as chemical restraint. Skilled rates are lower because they are measured, star-rated and pushed down. Assisted living has no regulatory counterweight, and facilities often want residents sedated and easy to manage. Mortality risk does not depend on which side of the door the resident lives on. Residents also migrate: I admitted two people from assisted living today who ran out of money, one of them on quetiapine without need. Virginia is discussing antipsychotic use in ALF as a state-level measure, and the state is already collecting the data.

**Rucci on operator pressure:** some, not most. ALG and Brookdale, our largest ALF partners, have started paying attention. The company's SNF rate is about half its ALF rate in populations generic enough to compare, which means regulation is producing different care, and she objects on ethical grounds: care should be insurance-agnostic and address-agnostic. Ohio leads the states partly because it is almost all SNF. Ohio and Indiana have also built strong partnerships between our psychiatric providers and SNF leadership in GDR meetings.

**Williams's counterpoint:** populations and resources differ, ALFs have not invested in managing disinhibited behavior, and a single lens flattens that. My pushback, from this morning: Virginia ALFs are private-pay and relatively well resourced, and the one I rounded at still ran two weekend shifts with one nurse and one CNA for about 80 residents and missed an entire med pass.

## Positive outliers

Rucci's point about Ohio and Indiana is the lever I want. Study the low outliers as well as the high, learn what they do, and at minimum move the buildings above the average line toward average. **Rucci: talk to Laura**, who has investigated the Ohio and Indiana process and credits the ADCOs there for building it. Rucci expects Ohio's advantage to hold on SNFs alone, and says the only thing different about our model there is leadership, specifically the ADCO's relationship with the building.
