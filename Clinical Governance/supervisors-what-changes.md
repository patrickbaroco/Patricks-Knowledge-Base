# What Changes for Supervisors

*Draft, for discussion. Nothing here is in place yet.*

---

## The short version

One lane is becoming two.

**Administrative and billing compliance** — coding, signatures, timeliness, attendance, documentation delinquency — works exactly as it does today. Supervisors are copied, nothing changes, and nothing in the new charters touches it.

**Findings about an individual clinician's care** run on different rules. Those don't get copied to anyone with authority over that clinician's job.

---

## Why the second lane exists

Federal and state law protect a peer review process — the committee, the people who serve on it, the people who bring concerns to it, and the company. That protection isn't automatic. It's conditional on the process actually being what it says it is.

**A peer review finding that routinely goes to the people who control someone's employment isn't a peer review finding.** It's a management record with different letterhead, and it will be treated as one.

So the exclusion isn't a matter of taste. Copy a supervisor on individual findings and three things fail at once: the rule keeping employment authority out of the decision becomes decorative, the separation between employment and peer review records collapses, and the privilege claim becomes very hard to make.

---

## The part that's easy to miss

**Being outside this protects supervisors. It isn't a demotion.**

A supervisor copied on a peer review finding can be deposed about it — what they knew, when they knew it, what they did about it. Their knowledge becomes the company's knowledge.

Under a copy-everyone culture, every supervisor is a potential witness to everything. Under this structure, they aren't. That's the trade, and it's a good one.

---

## What supervisors keep

- **Everything administrative**, exactly as now.
- **Notice of every final action**, and what it means operationally — who can do what, starting when.
- **Systems findings.** When an audit turns up a broken protocol, an unusable template, or a workflow that manufactures a problem, it goes to the person who can fix it. Without names. This is the most useful thing the audit produces, and supervisors get all of it.
- **The ordinary supervisory relationship**, expressly unaffected. A quality intervention doesn't suspend it, replace it, or transfer it to a committee — that's written down specifically so nobody assumes otherwise and steps back.
- **The ability to refer anything, at any time.** No threshold, no waiting period, no form. A supervisor with a concern about a clinician's care takes it straight to peer review, and it doesn't need an audit to have found it first.

---

## What supervisors lose

Visibility into individual clinical findings that don't result in a final action.

That's real and worth stating plainly rather than dressing up. It's also the category where visibility costs the most and buys the least — a documentation gap on four charts, corrected and closed, isn't information a supervisor needs, and is information the company would rather not have in circulation.

---

## The boundary that will get blurry

Documentation delinquency starts in the administrative lane. It can cross.

Where it recurs on a pattern, or where a link to patient welfare appears, it becomes a matter for peer review. **In post-acute that link is shorter than the hospital version**, because the note is how a clinician communicates with the facility — a note that isn't there means a facility operating without information it needs.

So a documentation problem can start as something a supervisor handles and become something they don't see. That isn't arbitrary, but it will feel arbitrary if nobody says it in advance.

---

## The practical risk

The most likely way this goes wrong isn't a decision. It's a distribution list.

If the same analytics function, the same tooling, and the same report templates serve coding audits and clinical audits, **the copy happens automatically and nobody ever chooses it.** The separation has to be built into the systems and the lists, not just written into a charter.

That belongs on the implementation checklist, not in a policy binder.
