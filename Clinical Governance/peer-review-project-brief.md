## 1. Purpose

Eventus has no chartered peer review structure. Review of clinician performance happens today, informally, without a charter, defined committee, due process, privilege protection, or immunity for participants. The goal is to build a formal clinical governance architecture that:

- Qualifies Eventus as a "health care entity" under HCQIA
- Provides immunity to committee members and reporting clinicians
- Protects the record under state peer review privilege and, where appropriate, federal PSQIA privilege
- Gives clinical quality findings a path to consequence that does not run through employment
- Survives leadership turnover

**Framing for leadership:** this is not a new practice. Professional review actions (e.g., recommending a period of concurrent review with defined exit criteria) have already occurred. The construct protects a practice that is already happening without protection.

**Framing for the board specifically — added 2026-08-26.** Lead with **exit diligence**, not with patient safety. At exit, clinical quality diligence on a multi-state employed-clinician group asks what the peer review process is, what credentialing defines, and what professional review actions have been taken. Today those answers are none, nothing restrictable, and some, undocumented. Diligence findings do not get explained away; they get priced, held back, or excluded from reps and warranties. That is the only argument in this program denominated in something a financial sponsor already tracks, and it is worth more than every safety argument combined. Full objection-by-objection treatment in `board-objections-and-rebuttals.md`.

## 2. Organizational facts

**Ownership and board.** Private equity — General Atlantic. GA board, GA partner as CEO. No clinical members; no regulatory duty for quality of care. **The board is not a realistic recipient of case-level clinical governance authority.** Ask once, don't build contingent on it.

**Leadership.** COO (Cary Trainor). Interim CMO — a structural risk; design so the CMO role holds notice, not authority, which is both better design and interim-proof.

**Clinical leadership.** Three chairs: primary care, psych, quality (Patrick). Four regional CMOs, each overseeing 10–20 physicians administratively. RDCOEs — regional directors of clinical operations and excellence, the NP/PA equivalents of regional CMOs.

**Standing clinical committees.** Primary care committee and psych committee. Both feed the CQC's standards and education work. Neither participates in peer review, receives audit findings at individual attribution, nor is chartered as a peer review body — decided 2026-08-25.

**Clinician population.** Physicians, NPs, PAs, psych NPs. Psychotherapists potentially in scope but no cause to date.

**Footprint.** Virginia, North Carolina, Ohio, Indiana, Kentucky. Roughly 50/50 LTC and ALF.

**Corporate structure.** Single entity across all five states — no state-level professional corporations. Confirmed 2026-08-26. The charter is one entity's, the board resolution is adopted once, and the NC and Indiana entity-qualification questions are asked once rather than per state.

**Patrick's role.** Chair of Clinical Excellence. Chairs both the Clinical Quality Committee and the Peer Review Council. This dual chair is permanent — it is the job description, not a temporary condition. Design around it rather than planning to unwind it. **Both chairmanships are ex officio and untermed**, held by virtue of the Chair of Clinical Excellence position for as long as that position is held; committee term limits therefore apply to members, not to the chair. This does not extend to the CGC chair, which remains elected and remains a different person — see §3.

**Credentialing** is owned by a separate department, not yet consulted. **Existing peer review** is run by a colleague as a small part of a larger role; meeting scheduled.

## 3. Design decisions made

### Structure

**Two co-equal committees.** CQC (quality standards, education, audit) and PRC (peer review, sealed, individual accountability). Do not nest one under the other — with a single chair, a reporting line between them is an accountability layer accountable to no one. Both charter to the CGC. **Both sit outside the PSES** — see the CQC subsection below and §4.

**Clinical Governance Council (CGC).** New body. Built from the three clinical chairs, four regional CMOs, and the RDCOEs — roughly ten members. Receives PRC recommendations with authority to accept, reject, modify, or remand. This is the separation from management, and it is achievable without asking the GA board to assume a clinical duty.

**"Council," not "Committee."** Council distinguishes the governing body from the two committees reporting to it; a committee receiving recommendations from committees reads as peer-level, which is the confusion the structure exists to prevent. The privilege statutes define committees by function rather than name, so the naming is safe — but the charter states expressly that the Council is a committee within the meaning of each cited statute, listing them, so the point is never available to argue.

**CGC chair.** Not Patrick. Not the CMO. Not the chairs of primary care or psych (they would recuse on their own service lines, i.e., most cases). Therefore: **elected from among practicing clinicians on the council, with board ratification.** Elected rather than appointed matters — an appointed chair in a PE-owned group is a chair management selected. Defined term, vice-chair who presides on recusal, and the chair may not concurrently hold employment authority over clinicians. **The CGC chair does not sit on the PRC.**

**CGC composition — resolved 2026-08-26 during charter drafting.** Three linked decisions, all in `cgc-charter-draft.md` Articles III and VII.

*The defect.* As composed in the brief, every member holds either a committee chairmanship or employment authority, so nobody was eligible to be elected chair under the chair eligibility rule. Worse, eight of fourteen would have held employment authority over the clinicians the Council reviews — a standing composition that undercuts the separation-from-management claim regardless of how recusal handles the individual case. Not a legal defect; HCQIA does not bar supervisors from peer review bodies. A defect in the specific claim this design is making.

*At-large members: six.* Elected by and from practicing clinicians holding no employment authority, at least two physicians and two APCs, twelve-month eligibility, staggered terms, elections administered by the Secretary. Six rather than three because they carry the action vote and the primary care and psych chairs recuse on their own service lines in most cases; five is the floor at which the vote still functions after recusals.

*Two-class voting.* All seventeen decide charters, framework, and policy. Only members holding no employment authority vote on professional review actions — nine of seventeen. Members with employment authority may present and provide information, then withdraw before deliberation. **This is the same rule already adopted for the CQC**, applied here for the same reason, which is what keeps it reading as a principle rather than a maneuver. It also decouples independence from the composition ratio: administrators can be added without ever touching the action vote.

*Seat everyone now; let growth fire the fix.* All four regional CMOs and all four RDCOEs sit ex officio. At nineteen voting members the ex officio seats convert automatically to four and four elected delegates. The conversion is self-executing — no amendment, no resolution, nobody has to propose taking a seat away from a colleague. Chosen over trimming seats at adoption because the political cost of the trim lands in year one, when the program has the least credibility to spend.

**Hearing panel.** Constituted separately by the CGC when a recommendation is adverse. Drawn from clinicians on neither standing committee. Discipline-matched. This provision absorbs the dual-chair problem — HCQIA's safe harbor fails if the person who chaired the initial review also leads the body reviewing it.

**Management (CEO, COO, CMO, regional CMOs).** Receives notice of outcomes. No authority in the peer review chain. Employment consequences run on a separate track, on a separate record.

**Board.** One-time delegation by resolution; annual aggregate report back. Not in the case flow.

**Three additions from drafting the resolution, 2026-08-26.**

*Indemnification (Resolution § 7) — not previously discussed anywhere in this project, and the most practical determinant of whether anyone will serve.* HCQIA immunity is a rebuttable presumption against damages. It does not prevent being sued, does not reach injunctive or declaratory relief, and expressly excludes civil rights claims. Clinicians do not join peer review bodies without knowing the company stands behind them. If the program cannot recruit members, none of the rest of the architecture matters. Do not trade this away.

**Insurance is not part of the ask — corrected 2026-08-26.** Participants are covered and defended under the company's existing umbrella policy. Indemnity and defense coverage are separate instruments and the resolution now requests only the first. One item for counsel, framed as confirmation rather than a request: that the umbrella in fact reaches service on a peer review body. It is worth having that in writing before anyone is recruited to serve.

*No appeal to the board (Resolution § 3).* Serves two purposes at once: it keeps the board out of the clinical chain, which is what makes adoption easy to say yes to, and it makes the Council's action genuinely final, which matters for reporting timing and for HCQIA. **The framing to use with the board: they are better off after adoption than before.** Professional review actions are being taken today with no chartered body, no process, and no immunity, and the board owns that exposure already without any of the corresponding protection.

*Council access to counsel (Resolution § 9).* The Corporation's counsel reports to management and the Council's function is to be independent of management. Drafted as separate counsel funded on the Chair's written certification of divergent interests. Most likely provision to be struck, and survivable if it is; fallback is that the Chair may raise a conflict directly with the board.

**Two things to resolve before the resolution goes anywhere.** Whether the disclaimer of clinical duty in § 3 does any work — a board may not be able to disclaim a duty it would otherwise owe simply by resolving that it does not, and the substantive protection is the delegation to a competent body, not the sentence. And whether § 10's ratification of prior action conflicts with the effective-date boundary written into all three charters: one ratifies professional review actions already taken without process, the other closes the old record and opens a new one. **They may be inconsistent. Counsel decides which treatment is wanted, and only one of them survives.**

### Reporting lines

- Board → CGC: charter/delegation (solid)
- PRC → CGC: recommendations, never deliberations (solid)
- CGC → hearing panel: constitution when adverse (solid)
- CGC → NPDB / state boards: obligation attaches to the final action, so the line comes off the deciding body (solid)
- CQC → PRC: threshold-triggered referral — findings, data, and disposition history only, never CQC deliberations (solid)
- PRC → CQC: de-identified aggregate patterns, and remand where review concludes the issue was systems rather than individual (solid)
- Primary care / psych committees → CQC: standards and education input only (dashed)
- CGC → management: notice only (dashed)

The CQC → PSO line is removed. See §4 — the patient safety track no longer has a body attached to it.

### CQC scope, and the audit-to-peer-review referral

**Decided 2026-08-25.**

**Scope.** Design quality standards, drive education agendas, conduct quality audits. Chart audits are structured-question based and AI-assisted within the EMR. The primary care and psych committees feed standards and education; they do not participate in peer review and do not receive findings at individual attribution. Because they touch only the open half of the CQC's output, they need no charter of their own — but that boundary has to be written, since privilege breaks at the edge of an unchartered body.

**Position.** Outside the PSES. Standards-setting and audit are prospective and normative; a PSES holds incident capture and retrospective analysis assembled for PSO reporting. Protection therefore comes from state peer review privilege plus HCQIA, which means the five-state analysis done for the PRC has to be run for the CQC as well.

**Two records, from the start.** Standards and education circulate — you cannot privilege a document distributed to every clinician in the group, and should not try. Audit findings and committee deliberations are the protected record. Ohio's document-by-document proof requirement makes a mixed file dangerous: published standards sitting alongside case-level findings invites the argument that the whole file is operational.

**Composition.** A numeric clinician floor in the charter, not “mostly clinicians” — ≥50% licensed clinicians is the Indiana and NC statutory floor, and the committee as described clears it easily, but membership drifts and an adjective will not survive a composition challenge. Physicians, APCs, one or two psychotherapists.

**Psychotherapists.** Useful on the CQC for standards and education. Before any audit touches a therapy chart, establish whether the group's therapists maintain psychotherapy notes separated from the rest of the record — HIPAA's heightened protection attaches only to those, and quality audit is not obviously within the health-care-operations exception.

#### Referral design

**The problem is not the threshold, it is the dual chair.** Any referral rule that ends in a judgment call puts Patrick in the position of deciding who goes to a committee he also chairs — the weakest point in the construct under HCQIA's fact-gathering and reasonableness prongs.

**Solution: asymmetric discretion.** The CQC may refer above threshold on severity. It may not decline to refer once a threshold is met. Escalation is discretionary; suppression is not available. The referral becomes a rule being administered rather than a decision being made — the same move that solves the note delinquency problem.

**Every finding gets a disposition.** An undispositioned flag is an unanswered question in a plaintiff's exhibit. An AI audit running across every chart creates a timestamped, enterprise-wide record of notice; once the system knows at scale, “we did not know” is gone permanently. That cuts toward building the pathway properly, and it means the PRC has to be sized for the volume the audit actually generates.

Three dispositions:

1. **Systems** — attributable to process, tooling, or workflow. Stays in CQC, feeds standards and education, individual attribution not carried forward.
2. **Individual, below threshold** — documented, fed back to the clinician, counted toward threshold. The feedback is not optional; it is what puts the clinician on notice, and notice is what makes a later pattern referral fair.
3. **Referral to PRC** — triggered three ways:
   - **Severity.** A single finding meeting defined criteria, regardless of count.
   - **Pattern.** N findings of a defined type in a rolling window, compared against peer group rather than raw count. Raw counts import case mix directly into the reasonable-belief prong — the clinician covering the highest-acuity SNF fails structured questions more often.
   - **Non-response.** Failure to engage with tier-two feedback. The cleanest of the three: behavioral, objective, and it does not require anyone to relitigate the clinical merits.

**Referral packet:** findings, underlying data, disposition history. Not CQC deliberations. Same rule as PRC → CGC.

**Remand.** The PRC can return a matter to the CQC where its review concludes the issue was systems rather than individual. Without it the PRC has only two outcomes, action and no action; remand is how the two committees stay honest with each other about where a problem actually lives.

**Notice and effective dates.** The CQC writes the standard, audits against it, and refers on an audit finding of failing it. Normal, but it needs a guardrail: no *audit* referral on a standard the clinician was not on notice of. (This bounds the audit route only — see the two-route statement in §4. A concern raised directly to the PRC carries no standard, no notice lag, and no threshold.) Standards get effective dates, documented dissemination, and a defined lag before findings against a new standard count toward a pattern threshold. Without it, the first audit cycle after any new standard generates a wave of referrals from clinicians who never saw it — unfair on its face and a direct attack on the reasonable-belief prong.

**Custody of the count.** Threshold tracking is an administrative function, not a chair's judgment. Concrete duty for the committee coordinator role.

#### Setting thresholds without a baseline

Volume is unknown — the program is new. That is not a blocker, because the number is not a charter input.

**The charter defines the mechanism; a schedule holds the numbers.** Disposition tiers, trigger types, asymmetric discretion, and who sets thresholds by what process go in the charter. The thresholds themselves live in a schedule the CQC adopts and reviews annually. Charter now, calibrate later, no amendment required.

**Calibration period.** One to two quarters in which every finding still receives a disposition and tier-two feedback still occurs, but the pattern trigger does not fire. At the end the distribution is known and the threshold is set from it. Two conditions make this a calibration rather than a soft launch: a written end date in the charter, and the severity and non-response triggers live from day one. Neither of those needs a denominator, so nothing serious is being observed and ignored — only the pattern arithmetic is suspended, and the reason is stated on the record.

**Thresholds are relative and absolute together.** Rate materially above peer group *and* a minimum absolute count, both required. The relative component absorbs case mix and self-normalizes as volume changes, so the number never has to be guessed in advance. The absolute floor prevents a pure percentile from mechanically referring someone every cycle even when the whole cohort is performing well.

**Systems circuit breaker.** If a large fraction of clinicians would cross a threshold, that is a systems finding by definition — the standard is wrong, the audit question is badly specified, or the problem is real and organizational. It routes to standards revision, not to mass referral. Without this line, the pressure when volume surprises you runs toward quietly raising the bar until the number is manageable, which is the suppression that asymmetric discretion exists to prevent.

**PRC sizing follows the same logic.** Constitute review panels case-by-case from a pool rather than as a fixed standing membership conducting every review. The hearing panel provision already works this way; extending it to review panels makes volume elasticity structural rather than a staffing problem.

#### Superseded in part, 2026-08-26 — the CQC stays lean

Most of the apparatus above came out of the CQC charter and into the referral schedule, or was dropped. Two reasons, both decided in conversation.

*Anyone can refer to the PRC.* Once that is true, the CQC's referral is one source among several rather than a privileged gateway, and PRC screening plus Council decision already absorb the dual-chair concern the threshold machinery was built to solve. The elaborate version was solving a problem that a simpler structure does not have.

*Acceptance.* People will tolerate procedural weight in the PRC because everyone understands what peer review is for. They will not tolerate it in the quality committee, whose participation the whole program depends on. A quality committee that reads like a disciplinary apparatus does not get used.

**What survives, and why each earns its place:** audit questions must map to an adopted standard in effect (closes the notice loop); every finding is dispositioned as systems, individual, or no action; comparison against peer group rather than raw count; revenue-linked measures may never refer an individual; and a criterion that would refer a large fraction is a systems finding by definition. Five sentences.

**What made it lean is one provision, not one hundred:** the CQC has no authority over any individual at any point. It refers and stops. Everything else could be short because of that.

#### Standards and measurement — decided 2026-08-26

*Two sources of standard.* Accepted standard of care where an applicable guideline exists; internal written standards informed by best available evidence where none does. The argument for the second, which belongs in the charter itself: LTC and ALF residents are underrepresented in or excluded from the trials most society guidelines rest on, so an applicable standard frequently does not exist to be adopted. Where the committee authors one it states the evidence and the reasoning that carried it to this population. Adoption is the committee's act with an effective date regardless of who drafted it — the Clinical Handbook can write them, the committee adopts them.

*How standards are written — added 2026-08-26, CQC § 4.7.* Four drafting requirements, each earning its place. **Decisions documented, never treatments prescribed** — our residents are frail enough that the right call is often to withhold what a guideline indicates, and a standard written as a treatment requirement pressures clinicians to treat against the resident's interest. **Element by element, not a global attestation** — the HFrEF minimum is satisfied by naming each of the four classes with drug and dose or an absence with a class-specific reason, and is not satisfied by "GDMT reviewed, not a candidate." **Reasons of a verifiable kind** — a lab value, an event, an intolerance, a documented conversation, a goal of care; not a conclusion. **Answerable from the record**, or it cannot be audited and is not adopted.

*The anti-macro controls — CQC § 5.10.* A documentation standard can be satisfied by documentation, and a stored phrase passes forever. Two standing controls: the audit cross-checks any asserted fact against where it is independently recorded (a named drug and dose against the active orders, a stated lab reason against the labs), and the committee samples charts that **passed** at random each quarter. Auditing only the failures is how a standard becomes a macro. The cross-check is the stronger of the two — a stale phrase drifts out of agreement with the medication list on its own.

*No pairing of minimum and reach goal — clarified 2026-08-26.* The designation attaches to a standard, not to a clinical topic. Most subjects carry a minimum and nothing else. And most of what would informally be called a reach goal is a **rate** — a share of a population on a therapy — which is an organizational measure belonging to analytics and the systems lane, not a standard any individual meets or fails. CQC § 4.4(b) is reserved for the narrow case of an individual-level standard sitting above the floor, such as documented titration toward target dose within a defined period.

*Three classes of measure.* Process and decision measures, which are the substance. Treatment-gap prevalence measures — a resident with Afib and no anticoagulation and no documented reason — which look like outcomes but are a documented decision state, directly attributable, unambiguous, and the strongest thing the program can audit. True outcomes, used to find signal and never to attribute it. The third mirrors PRC § 8.1 deliberately: two committees with different theories of quality would be a problem.

*Economic credentialing — rule rewritten 2026-08-26, CQC § 5.8.* The original barred any measure with financial linkage to the entity from ever referring an individual. That is wrong and would have collapsed on contact: under an ACO, an ISNP, capitation, or bundles, essentially every clinical measure has financial linkage, and the rule would have excluded avoidable hospitalization — the most clinically important measure in this population and the most financially consequential one.

Replaced with three principles. Every standard states its clinical basis at adoption without reference to any payment arrangement. **Volume is never itself the criterion for an individual finding** — the audit selects on the events it can find, but the finding rests on whether the documented decision was appropriate, so a clinician whose decisions were right generates nothing whatever the count. And a measure drawn from a payer or rating program is adopted by its clinical content, never by the program's threshold.

*A longer version was drafted and discarded, and the reason generalizes.* It would have required at least one audit measure aimed at under-response, with the findable events named. Dropped because the protection depends on the process having been followed, so **every added specific is another way to breach our own charter in complete good faith.** A rule that has to be broken is worse than no rule. The under-response measures remain the right design intent; they live in the referral schedule and the committee's judgment rather than as a charter duty.

*Selection is not a finding — added 2026-08-26, CQC § 5.6(a).* Stress-testing an antipsychotic-in-dementia scenario exposed a gap: the charter defined audit questions, findings, dispositions, and referral criteria, but never said how a clinician or a chart gets chosen for examination. Rate-based selection was permitted only by omission.

The working pattern is two thresholds doing different jobs — an organizational target (say 12%) that is a rate and therefore not a standard at all under § 4.4, and a higher screening threshold (say 24%) that says *go look*. Selection carries no consequence, records nothing about the care, and needs no comparison group, no risk adjustment, and no notice period.

**A crude selection threshold is defensible precisely because the finding is made from the chart.** The case-mix question — the clinician whose facility carries many residents with primary psychiatric illness — is answered by examining the chart, and that is a better place to answer it than a risk-adjusted denominator, because a chart shows *why* a rate is what it is and a denominator only shows that it is. This is the answer to "your threshold is not adjusted for anything," and it is stronger than building a model.

*Payer denominators are selection artifacts too — § 5.8(d).* Where a measure is adopted from a rating program, that program's denominator and exclusions are not clinical criteria. An antipsychotic prescribed for well-supported bipolar disorder with the rationale documented is appropriate care whether or not the CMS denominator excludes that diagnosis. Adopt the clinical content; never inherit the payer's judgment about what counts.

*The asymmetry is real and does not go away.* Over-response leaves an event; under-response leaves an absence. What makes it tolerable is that the audit is not the only route — under-response reaches the PRC from a colleague, a facility, or a family, with no threshold and no standard requirement. Which means it is only tolerable if that route is genuinely usable and people know about it. That is a communications problem, and it belongs in the clinician-facing explainer (queue item 13).

*The standard of care is not displaced — corrected 2026-08-26.* The rule that audit questions must map to an adopted standard was overbroad as first drafted. It conflated two things: the **audit instrument**, which scores every clinician against a stated criterion and must therefore state it, and **what is reviewable**, which is the standard of care — external, professional, and largely unwritten. Hospitals discipline for departures from standard of care without a written policy on the scenario, because that is what peer review is.

The worse failure was the inference the drafting invited: that whatever the standards program does not address is permissible. A written standards program hands that argument to the other side unless it is expressly disclaimed. Now disclaimed at CQC § 6.7(b) and PRC § 8.1.1(a).

Three provisions carry the fix. CQC § 6.7 — departures from the standard of care are reviewable whether or not any adopted standard addresses them; absence of a standard is not a defense; anyone may refer directly as a person, in which case the audit rules do not apply; and compliance with an internal standard does not establish that care met the standard of care. PRC § 8.1.1 — adopted standards are evidence of what was expected, not the measure, and do not exhaust it. And the audit-mapping rule is now expressly bounded to the instrument.

**Stated affirmatively, because a savings clause alone reads as an exception — swept through every document 2026-08-26.** There are two referral routes, not one with a carve-out.

1. **The audit route.** Structured questions scoring every clinician, measured against standards Eventus has adopted, disseminated, and put into effect. This route carries the mapping rule, the notice lag, and the thresholds, because a computer scoring everyone must be scoring against something stated.
2. **Every other route.** A colleague, a facility, a manager, an adverse event, an external event, self-referral. Measured against the generally accepted standard of care. **No adopted standard required, no threshold, no notice lag.**

The second route is the larger one and the one peer review has always run on. Written standards exist so the audit has something to measure; they are not a list of the only ways a clinician can fall short. Any document that implies otherwise is wrong and should be corrected on sight.

*Minimum expectation versus improvement target — added 2026-08-26.* On adoption every standard is designated one or the other. Only departures from a minimum expectation can refer; departures from an improvement target generate systems data and never refer. A quality committee will naturally set ambitious standards, and without this the ambition becomes a disciplinary trap — someone referred to peer review, with NPDB consequences attached, for missing a stretch goal that exceeded what the standard of care required. Designation happens at adoption, on the record, rather than being argued about at the point of referral.

**By express vote, no default** — resolved 2026-08-26. A standard adopted without a recorded designation is in effect for education and measurement but cannot support a referral until designated. Defaulting to minimum expectation would let an aspirational standard become disciplinary through inattention; defaulting to improvement target would leave gaps. The committee decides what a standard is for at the moment it writes it.

*No facility QAPI overlap.* Organizational endeavor only. Stated expressly in the charter because F865 QAPI documentation is independently required and therefore unprotected — taking it into the committee record would contaminate the record.

#### Cadence — decided 2026-08-26

The quarterly CGC did not merely lag, it broke the precautionary mechanism: a body meeting quarterly cannot receive a recommendation, convene, give notice, seat a hearing panel, and act inside the 45-day hard stop, and imminent danger does not wait for the next quarterly meeting.

- **CQC — monthly.**
- **PRC — monthly**, with screening running continuously between meetings rather than waiting for one.
- **CGC — quarterly for governance business**, plus an obligation to convene within 15 days of an adverse recommendation and within 5 business days on 24 hours' notice to ratify a precautionary restriction, at a reduced quorum for that purpose only.
- **Precautionary restriction moved to the PRC.** Chair plus two members impose immediately; **it lapses automatically at 5 business days unless the Council ratifies.** That pairing is the design — immediate enough to be useful, impossible to sustain without the deciding body. The 30-day reportability clock and the 45-day hard stop both run from imposition, not ratification.

### Recusal

Automatic, not discretionary. Triggers to write out:
- Prior involvement in the patient's care
- Supervisory relationship with the clinician under review
- Participation in an employment decision about that clinician
- Collaborating physician reviewing their own NP

**Recusal means exclusion from materials and deliberation, not just abstention from the vote.** Recused members leave the room; minutes record that they left. With ~10 members and typically ~2 recusals, quorum is not a practical concern.

**Recusal reaches the CQC's individual-disposition work, not only the PRC.** The supervisory-relationship and employment-decision triggers apply wherever individually attributable findings are discussed. Simpler alternative if membership makes case-by-case recusal unwieldy: CQC members holding employment authority participate in standards and education but not in individual dispositions.

**Committee chairs recuse at the CGC on their own committee's matters — added 2026-08-26.** Not addressed previously. Without it, Patrick chairs the PRC, signs its recommendation, and then votes on that recommendation at the CGC. Rule: present the matter, answer questions, withdraw before deliberation. Automatic, no finding required. Costs one vote out of fourteen and closes the last place the dual chair leaks.

### Discipline-matched review

Panel composition requirements by discipline, written into the charter rather than decided case by case. NP care reviewed with meaningful NP representation; PA by PA/NP equivalents; psych NP by psych-trained clinicians.

**Psychotherapists: do not fold in by default.** Different licensure boards, different standards, and psychotherapy notes carry heightened HIPAA protection that standard case review is not built to handle. Write the charter so the CGC can constitute a discipline-appropriate panel when needed; leave detail until there is a real case.

### NP/collaborating physician oversight

**Decision:** an NP review does *not* automatically trigger a peer review of the collaborating physician.

Rationale: auto-trigger converts every NP case into a physician case, and the consequences are asymmetric (HCQIA mandatory NPDB reporting attaches to physicians, not NPs). In a group where NPs carry most visit volume, that doubles case count and attaches the higher-consequence track to the multiplier. Predictable result: physicians decline to collaborate, or collaborate and stop reporting.

**Instead:** oversight adequacy is a *defined element of every NP review* — examined every time, documented every time. Systems findings route to the CQC. A physician review opens only if the oversight assessment surfaces a specific concern about that physician, with its own notice and its own due process at the point it opens.

Write the oversight element jurisdiction-neutrally ("identify the supervisory or collaborative structure applicable in this jurisdiction and assess its adequacy") — Virginia has an autonomous practice pathway, other states differ, some have none.

### PRC design decisions — added 2026-08-26 during charter drafting

All in `prc-charter-draft.md`.

**Categorical exclusion of employment authority from the PRC and the Reviewer Pool.** Stronger than the brief's case-by-case recusal. A regional CMO from one region reviewing another region's clinician still presents as supervisors judging clinicians; the reviewer pool is large enough that exclusion costs nothing operationally; and what those people know reaches the committee through referral and interview, where § 11111(a)(2) protects them. Consistent with the two-class rule at the CGC and the same rule at the CQC. **Narrows the pool — confirm before adoption.**

**Standing committee plus a Reviewer Pool.** Per-case review panels drawn from both, discipline-matched by rule rather than by chair's discretion. This is where volume elasticity actually lives.

**Screening as a distinct step that determines route, not merits.** Not previously specified. Most referrals end here. The rules that make it safe: every referral gets a recorded disposition, and no adverse finding or intervention issues at screening — otherwise screening becomes an unwritten review conducted by three people.

**Anti-drift clock on Tier One.** A quality intervention extended twice, or running past six months, returns to the committee to be closed or converted with full process. Without it Tier One accumulates into a de facto restriction that never received process and was never reported — the same failure mode as the administrative hold, in a different costume.

**Precautionary restriction with an automatic hard stop at forty-five days**, Secretary notification at day twenty, and the reportability determination expressly reserved to the CGC with counsel. Makes the thirty-day cliff someone's tracked duty rather than something a person has to remember.

**Write as though the subject will read it.** The Indiana access provision converted from a caution into a binding rule on the record: findings stated, evidence identified, characterizations avoided.

### External review of the CQC charter — changes made 2026-08-26

Eleven objections, nine accepted outright, one modified, and one defect found that was worse than anything raised.

**The terminology collision, and the fix.** Observation, rate, and individual finding were all being called a finding, which put CQC § 5.5 and § 5.6 in direct conflict. Now: an **observation** is an unvalidated chart-level apparent departure; a **rate** is aggregate performance across a population and never describes an episode; an **individual finding** is a validated observation the committee determines indicates a departure from a minimum expectation, and only a finding can be referred. Dispositions attach to observations, which means "no action" describes something that never became a finding at all. And the charter now says expressly that a CQC individual finding is not a PRC finding — the CQC decides only whether its audit was accurate about its own standard.

**The correction step — the genuinely new item.** § 5.4 validated the instrument; nothing validated the observation, and an instrument accurate in 95% of charts is wrong about a given chart one time in twenty. New CQC § 5.5(a): no observation may be dispositioned Individual until the practitioner has had ten business days to identify factual error, missing documentation, attribution error, or record context. Deliberately narrow — a record-correction step, not an appeal. **No argument on the clinical merits, because that would rebuild peer review inside the quality committee and destroy the separation § 1.3 and § 6.5 exist to create.**

**The § 6.7(c) bypass.** As drafted, anyone could relabel an audit failure as a personal referral and the notice period evaporated. Fixed, but not as the reviewer proposed: requiring facts "apart from" the standard failure would block legitimate referrals, because the same chart routinely fails a standard *and* independently departs from the standard of care. **What must be independent is the basis, not the facts.** Enforced at PRC screening (new PRC § 4.7) rather than by prohibition alone — a rule nobody can police is weak.

**The principle that came out of it and generalizes:** a provision that *constrains a power* is safer to add than one that *imposes a duty.* You breach a duty by inattention; you breach a constraint only by acting. That is why § 5.8 stays short and why § 6.7(c) can afford a paragraph.

**Non-retaliation was broken in all three charters, not one.** CQC § 10.2 barred adverse action "for being the subject of an audit finding"; PRC § 5.6 and CGC § 12.3 were worse, barring it for being the subject of a review — which read literally forbade the Council from acting on its own review, and would have been quoted by the first practitioner facing an action. All three rewritten with explicit causation (**because** of participation, reporting, selection, or status) and a savings clause preserving action on conduct established through any lawful process and ordinary operational action unrelated to the review.

**Six smaller repairs.** § 4.2 no longer says the committee "adopts" a published guideline — it derives from it and states any adaptation, because adopting an ACC/AHA guideline entire would import hundreds of recommendations as expectations and would imply that a society recommendation and the standard of care are the same thing. § 4.3 now records the education and implementation plan at adoption, which states the improvement loop without creating a separate duty that can go unmet. § 5.2(b) no longer calls treatment-gap measures "unambiguous" — anticoagulation managed elsewhere, incomplete med rec, rationale in an unseen document. § 5.8(c) pointed at § 4.4 for rate targets when § 4.4 says they are not governed by Article IV; now points at § 2.6. § 5.9 gained a severity carve-out, because forty percent of clinicians doing something dangerous is a systems condition and still does not make any particular instance acceptable. § 10.1 softened to "to the fullest extent provided by applicable law," matching CGC § 12.4 rather than asserting five states' outcomes.

**And the records problem the reviewer half-found.** Draft standards were orphaned — neither open nor protected. The obvious fix, protecting all drafts, collapses § 7.1, whose whole point is that the primary care and psych committees need no charter because they touch only open material. Resolution: **a draft becomes open when it is circulated**, and the internal drafting record — uncirculated drafts, evidence tables, members' comments — stays protected. Which means a circulated draft must not carry candid assessment of performance or of any practitioner. That is now written into § 8.1 rather than left to drafting discipline.

### Fair hearing procedure — drafted 2026-08-26

Three decisions in it are not drafting choices and should be made on purpose.

**The burden sits on the Council, not the practitioner (§ 5.5).** The conventional hospital model puts an initial burden of coming forward on the review body and then shifts the burden of persuasion to the practitioner to show the recommendation lacks a factual basis. This draft rejects that. The Council's representative bears it throughout, by a preponderance.

The reasoning is situational rather than legal. This is a new program in a PE-owned group asking clinicians to accept that a committee can restrict how they practice, and **the allocation of the burden is the single most visible signal of whether the process is real.** It also costs little — a recommendation that cannot be supported by a preponderance is a recommendation that should not have been made. Counsel may prefer the conventional allocation. That is a legitimate view and it should be chosen rather than inherited.

**The Council's review of the panel is deliberately limited (§ 7.2).** In a hospital the medical executive committee recommends and a separate board decides after the hearing. Here the Council both proposes and finally decides, because the board is deliberately out of the case flow — which means the hearing panel is the *only* independent step in the chain. A Council free to substitute its judgment would make that panel advisory in fact. So it may reject or modify only on stated grounds: unsupported by the record, material procedural defect, or error as to authority or standard. **This is the provision most likely to be softened in review, and softening it removes the independence the hearing exists to supply.**

**The precautionary arithmetic did not work, and Article VIII fixes it.** The 45-day hard stop versus 30 days to request a hearing plus 30 days' notice — the minimums exceed the stop before anyone sits down. Without a rule, either the restriction lapses in every contested case or the hard stop is breached in every contested case. Answer: the restriction continues where the practitioner requests a hearing, with Council review every 30 days and reportability assessed at 30 days regardless. **PRC § 9.5 amended to match**, with the anti-drift purpose preserved — the only thing that extends a precautionary restriction is a process the practitioner asked for.

**A tool we do not have and might want.** 42 U.S.C. § 11112(c)(1)(B) allows a restriction of not longer than fourteen days while an investigation determines whether a professional review action is needed, without the subsection (b) procedures. It does not require imminent danger and it sits inside the thirty-day reporting threshold — a genuinely useful middle instrument between doing nothing and invoking imminent danger. Flagged for counsel rather than built.

**Practical note for anyone who asks how long this takes:** a contested matter on the ordinary schedule runs roughly 120 days from notice of proposed action to final decision, and that is the floor. Only 60 of those days are statutory — thirty to request a hearing, thirty from notice to hearing — and they can be shortened only by the practitioner's waiver.

**An expedited schedule is therefore offered as a matter of course, not on request — added 2026-08-26, § 2.5.** Ten days to request, ten days' notice, ten days for the panel's recommendation, ten for the council's decision: roughly 45 days rather than 120. Two provisions keep the offer honest. **Declining carries no inference** and is never recorded as delay or considered by the panel or the council on any question. And the offer is repeated at every review where a precautionary restriction is running, because that is exactly when a practitioner's view of the ordinary schedule changes.

The reasoning: the statutory periods run for the practitioner's benefit, and someone who believes a proposed action is unwarranted usually wants it resolved rather than extended. **A process that makes them wait four months for a vindication they expect is a process that punishes by delay**, and it falls hardest on the people who are eventually cleared.

**It also answers the strongest operational objection to the hearing apparatus.** If formal process takes four months and a Tier One intervention takes a conversation, pressure builds to keep matters in Tier One that do not belong there — the same drift the anti-drift clock at PRC § 8.3 exists to prevent, arriving through the front door instead. A 45-day path for anyone who wants one relieves most of that pressure.

**Still open:** two compressions in the ordinary schedule that cost nothing — constituting the panel in parallel with the notice period rather than before it, and fifteen days for the panel's written recommendation rather than thirty. Together they take the ordinary schedule from ~120 days to ~95. Not yet made.

### Third review pass — changes made 2026-08-26

Four substantive, all accepted, plus cleanup. No architectural problems found.

**"Under Article VI" was wrong in eight places, not the two identified.** Article VI holds both routes — the audit referral at §§ 6.1–6.5 and the direct standard-of-care referral at §§ 6.6–6.7 — so every audit-route provision saying "under Article VI" was silently restricting § 6.7 as well. §§ 1.3, 3.1(d), 4.4(a), 4.4(b), 4.4 designation paragraph, 4.5, 5.1, and 5.5(b) all now cite §§ 6.1–6.5.

**The correction test would have gutted § 4.7(b).** "The substance was there, not where it was filed" is right for a treatment-gap observation and wrong for a documentation standard: a consultant's note establishes that a resident is anticoagulated, and establishes nothing about whether the treating practitioner made the element-by-element entry the standard requires. Now the test is **whether the required substance existed in a form sufficient to satisfy the applicable standard**, with a sentence saying outright that § 5.5 corrects the audit and does not relax the standard.

**Clinical appropriateness had crept back into § 5.5.** Having removed it from § 5.8, I reintroduced it a section earlier with "a drug that should not have been ordered." Replaced: a decision made and not documented is a different departure from a documented decision that did not satisfy the standard. Same distinction, no clinical judgment by this committee.

**§ 8.3 asserted a privilege it also disclaimed.** It said the drafting record "is protected on the ground that it is the Committee's own deliberative material" and then said whether prospective functions attract protection is an open question. Both cannot be true. Now separated: **classification is not privilege.** The charter says how it files documents; it does not claim that filing them that way makes them protected. A charter asserting privilege it may not have is worse than one that stays silent.

**Cleanup.** An observation "carries no conclusion and no consequence" rather than "produces nothing," since it now produces notice and a response window. § 10.1 no longer says PRC protection is "not in doubt" — a lapse, since we have been disciplined about never overstating immunity, and "the statutory fit is clearer" is what is true. And the obsolete open-items bullet is gone.

**One redirect rather than a straight acceptance.** The reviewer wanted "a record altered after notice of an audit is a graver problem than any finding the audit could have produced" deleted as rhetorical. Their §8.4 argument is a stretch — that section governs how an individual's care is described, and this characterizes a category of conduct. But their replacement does more work: **improper alteration of an existing entry may independently warrant referral under § 6.7**, which says something the charter otherwise did not, namely that falsifying a record is itself reviewable. Operational rule in the charter; the deterrent sentence belongs in the clinician-facing explainer, queue item 13.

**Architecture is closed.** What remains in all three charters is bracketed defaults and the counsel list.

### Second review pass — changes made 2026-08-26

Ten further points, all accepted, two extended, plus one error of my own found in the cross-check.

**The worst of them: § 5.8 had quietly given the CQC the clinical-merits role.** It said an individual finding "rests on whether the documented decision was appropriate to the clinical situation" — which is exactly the judgment § 5.1 and § 5.5 assign to the PRC. Rewritten in standard vocabulary: a finding rests on whether the decision **satisfied the applicable minimum expectation.** That is not only consistent, it is tighter — the clinical basis now runs through the standard rather than through an ad hoc appropriateness call. It also relocates a burden correctly. If a standard cannot distinguish appropriate antipsychotic use in well-documented bipolar disorder from inappropriate use in dementia, that is a § 4.7 drafting failure, not something to smooth over at the finding stage.

**The notice period ran against the wrong event.** As drafted, a standard disseminated 1 January could reach care furnished 10 January so long as the audit ran in April — ninety days had passed by the time of the *finding*, which was all the text required. Now: **only care furnished on or after ninety days following dissemination may support a finding that counts toward a referral criterion.** The period runs against the care. This is a fairness protection, not wording.

**Records: nothing moves.** § 8.1(b) said the adopted text "moves to the open record" two lines above "documents are not moved between them." Fixed by making it true — on adoption a clean final version is *entered* into the open record and the drafting record stays protected permanently. Nothing migrates, nothing is in both. And § 8.3's "retrospective posture" no longer characterizes the whole protected record, because the drafting record is prospective standards work and saying otherwise is an internal inconsistency in a document Ohio would read page by page. It now describes the audit record only, and says plainly that protection for prospective functions is an open counsel question rather than an assertion.

**Retrospective chart repair — the answer is amend freely, and it does not change the finding.** The first instinct, barring amendment, was wrong: the chart is the communication instrument to the facility, so completing an incomplete record is owed to the resident regardless of any audit. The rules that make it safe are in § 5.5(a). The observation records **the date the record was examined**, and that state governs. What corrects an observation is information that existed before notification — **the test is whether the substance was there, not where it was filed**, which admits the misfiled consult and the discharge summary under another encounter and excludes reasoning written afterward. An amendment must be a proper addendum, never an alteration; **a record altered after notice of an audit is a graver problem than any finding the audit could have produced.** Amendments made after notification are disclosed and recorded, which protects the practitioner more than anyone. And amending satisfies the standard in later cycles, which is the sentence that makes people willing to do it.

**Findings now state their kind.** Decision, documentation, or both. A drug correctly ordered and not documented is a real departure from a § 4.7 standard and a different one from a drug that should not have been ordered. The PRC needs that distinction at screening, and the CQC is the body positioned to record it.

**§ 11111(a)(2) may not reach the CQC at all.** The federal provision protects information given to a *professional review body*, and § 1.3 says in terms that this Committee is not one. So the reassurance we have been giving — anyone who reports in good faith is federally protected — is solid for the PRC and genuinely uncertain for the CQC. § 10.1 now conditions the statement and adds the routing answer: **a concern about an identified practitioner goes to the PRC**, where the protection is not in doubt. Better practice anyway, and it means the question never has to be resolved in a case that matters.

**My own error, found by cross-checking the anti-bypass rule.** PRC § 4.7 disposed a premature audit referral under § 4.4(b) or (c) — Systems or Administrative. Neither describes it. A referral that cannot proceed on the basis offered is § 4.4(a), no action, reason recorded, returned to the CQC to run its course. Corrected.

**Four smaller ones:** § 6.4 retitled "a referral is not a *PRC* finding," since § 6.1 now says what the Committee refers *is* a finding. § 6.3 now carries the practitioner's correction material to the PRC, so mitigating context does not have to be reconstructed. § 5.2(b) dropped "directly attributable," which sat two clauses before the explanation of why it sometimes isn't. § 10.1's first sentence had been softened to "the fullest extent provided by applicable law" while the second still asserted immunity flatly; both are conditioned now.

### The records custodian is a chartered position — decided 2026-08-26

Both charters named an abstract "Secretary." Replaced throughout with the **Executive Coordinator, Clinical Excellence**, matching `coordinator-role-proposal.md`. Not cosmetic, and it cuts two ways.

**The exposure it creates.** A committee officer is a creature of the committee. An employed person reports to Management — so absent express provision, Management holds a standing channel into the peer review record through the person who physically holds it. Nobody has to do anything improper for this to be a defect; it is a defect in the reporting structure itself. Four provisions close it (Council Charter § 3.7.1–3.7.5, incorporated by the PRC at § 2.7):

- the duty in these functions runs to the committee and **expressly overrides ordinary employment reporting obligations, including to her own supervisor**;
- case content is barred as an input to her evaluation, compensation, or any employment decision;
- adverse action by reason of these functions is prohibited and is itself reviewable by the CGC and reportable to the board;
- the confidentiality undertaking survives termination of employment.

**The dual-role problem** the resourcing case flagged is answered at PRC § 2.9 rather than left undefined: she handles every matter under a no-discussion rule, with an alternate custodian designated at the chair's determination where her working relationship with the practitioner is close enough that custody could reasonably be questioned. Recorded, matter-specific, and expressly not a finding about her.

**Why this is also the strongest version of the resourcing argument.** The role is now named in two charters with written duties attached to legal consequences — minutes to the Ohio standard, the calibration count, the thirty-day NPDB clock, the day-twenty precautionary notification, the at-large election, the case register. That is a chartered position, not a job description. It is far easier to fund a position the governance documents require than one a manager has requested.

**Unresolved and material:** whether state peer review privilege in each of the five states reaches a non-clinician employee acting as custodian. HCQIA § 11111(a)(1) covers staff and persons assisting; the state statutes were written around clinicians and this has never been analyzed. The entire records architecture rests on it.

### Privileges construct

**Decision: pursue it, contingent on resourcing and a board resolution.** Do not win the principle and then implement alone.

**Why it matters:** without a restrictable authorization, the only lever is employed/not employed. Most findings warrant proctoring, focused review, or a scope restriction — none of which exist without something to restrict. A committee that can only do nothing or end a career will do nothing. Worse, every serious finding routes to HR by default, collapsing the firewall.

**Answered 2026-08-26: no privileges exist today, and the executive team supports creating them.** Three consequences.

*The good one.* Because nothing restrictable exists, no professional review action affecting privileges can have occurred, so there is no unrecognized NPDB reporting obligation sitting in the past. That latent-exposure worry is closed. Building from zero is also easier than retrofitting — grandfathering is clean, since equivalent unrestricted privileges as of an effective date is the only sensible starting position and no one has a prior scope to be compared against.

*The one to watch.* "We can create privilege levels" is agreement about an artifact, not about authority. The construct only works if privilege actions run through the CGC rather than management. Executive comfort with the tool existing is entirely compatible with an unexamined assumption that HR or the CMO will operate it — and that assumption collapses the firewall the whole design exists to build. Surface it early, in the language of *who decides*, not *what exists*.

*Obligation created, not discovered.* Once privileges exist, reportable actions become possible going forward. Eventus must register with the NPDB in order to report; reporting is mandatory for organizations that follow a formal peer review process, an Initial Action Report is due within 30 days of the action, and organizations attest every two years that all reportable actions have been submitted. Querying is permitted but not required for group practices — worth exercising at hire and at reappointment anyway. The biennial attestation is an ongoing duty needing a named owner, not a setup task, and it is a second concrete argument for the coordinator role.

**Naming, and why the legal term is unavoidable. Clarified 2026-08-26.** What the executive team agreed to is autonomy tiers as a matter of practice — unrestricted, focused review, mandatory consultation — not a hospital-style credentialing apparatus. That is the right instrument and it is axis 1 below. But autonomy tiers *are* clinical privileges under § 11151(3) regardless of what they are called: the definition reaches "other circumstances pertaining to the furnishing of medical care under which a physician or other licensed practitioner is permitted to furnish such care by a health care entity."

This is not a technicality to be drafted around, because the immunity and the reporting duty come from the same hook. HCQIA immunity is available for a professional review action affecting privileges; a construct deliberately built so as not to be privileges sheds the immunity along with the duty. You cannot have the lever without the obligation — they are the same fact.

**Therefore:** name the tiers however lands best internally — *clinical authorization* reads less like hospital medical staff apparatus than *privileges* — and anchor them to § 11151(3) in one sentence of the charter. Same move as Council-not-Committee.

**Where the drafting care actually goes:** the line inside the tiers. Retrospective review of completed care, with the practitioner's authority unchanged, is a quality intervention and ordinarily non-reportable. Anything requiring consultation or approval *before* acting restricts authority and is capable of being reportable at >30 days. That line is where the two-tier remediation model lives, and it is a counsel question, not a drafting preference.

**The privilege axes that actually matter in LTC/ALF** (scope is not a procedure list; the lever is conditions *added*, not services removed):

1. **Autonomy tier** — the primary one. Unrestricted / concurrent review / mandatory consultation / proctoring. Applies to every clinician regardless of setting. NPDB examples expressly contemplate mandatory consultation periods.
2. **Collaborating physician authority** — separable from a clinician's own patient care, requires different competence.
3. **Controlled substance prescribing** — concrete, auditable, independently restrictable.
4. **Setting/acuity tier** — SNF post-acute vs. ALF. Imperfect (close to a scheduling decision) but defensible when the finding is acuity-specific.

**Medical director designation is NOT the flagship privilege.** Three reasons: (a) under F841 the *facility* designates, so Eventus can decline to nominate or withdraw a nomination but cannot revoke; (b) pay is attached, and a peer review body reallocating stipends invites the economic-credentialing attack on HCQIA's "reasonable belief the action furthers quality care" prong; (c) it is *role* performance, not patient care. Handle administratively, informed by quality input, decided on an independently documented record. Non-renewal at term is the cleanest instrument. Stark/AKS fair-market-value analysis applies to stipend reallocation — contracting must be in the room.

**Two-tier remediation.** Coaching, education, and focused review as non-reportable quality interventions carrying most of the volume. Formal privilege restriction as a distinct, rarer category with full HCQIA process. The second exists so the first has somewhere to escalate.

**Implementation sequence:** charter and board resolution → privilege taxonomy → credentialing policy and forms → employment contract amendment → grandfather the existing workforce (equivalent privileges as of an effective date, no individual review) → new hires through full process → reappointment cycle begins year two. Twelve to eighteen months. Credentialing operations needs an owner who is not Patrick.

**Do not** build a hospital medical staff with bylaws, officers, and general meetings. **Do not** ever let privileges become a compensation input.

### Economic consequences

Privileges have economic consequences *automatically and downstream* — a restricted clinician sees a different panel and bills differently. That is fine; the restriction was a clinical judgment and the financial effect is mechanical.

What defeats immunity is the committee reaching for money as the instrument. The firewall constrains *who decides and on what evidence*, not whether economic consequences may follow. Management may restructure a role or decline to renew a directorship — on its own record, on its own grounds, at its own time.

If the program's efficacy depends on hitting people financially, that describes performance management, which Eventus already has.

### Note delinquency (worked policy example)

Default to an administrative performance matter under a written policy. Escalates to peer review on pattern, or where the patient-welfare nexus is clear — **and in LTC that nexus is shorter than the hospital analogy suggests, because the note is the communication instrument to the facility.** Say so explicitly in the policy.

Delinquency is often a signal (impairment, burnout, cognitive change, overload). Build a pathway that asks before it sanctions.

**Pulling a clinician from duties:** build as an automatic consequence of a uniformly applied written policy with a defined threshold, not a discretionary case-by-case decision. Per NPDB guidance, automatic/administrative suspensions are not reportable — but the label does not control; reportability turns on whether the action meets the criteria. Three traps: (1) the >30-day cliff, where a professional review action becomes reportable even absent actual harm if the entity determines the conduct could adversely affect patient welfare; (2) resignation during a hold, which can be characterized as surrender while under investigation; (3) drift, where an administrative hold quietly converts into an unchartered professional review action. Put a hard clock in the policy: at day X it escalates formally, with process.

### Medical director underperformance (worked case example)

Diagnose before responding. Three distinguishable problems, often tangled: attending performance (peer review), medical director role performance under F841 (administrative), and capacity/overload (neither — a caseload problem misread as performance).

**Sequence is the exposure.** Pull the directorship first and it looks like the review was cover. Complete peer review then pull it and it looks like economic credentialing. The directorship decision must rest on independently documented role-performance grounds, recorded contemporaneously and separately from the PRC record. If the only evidence is the PRC's clinical finding, there is no director-performance case.

Have a successor ready before moving — Eventus is contractually obligated to supply a director.

**Watch for:** facilities wanting a director removed for non-quality reasons (holding the line on stewardship, resisting pressure on hospitalization rates, documenting things administrators would rather not have documented). A facility complaint is a signal to look, not a finding.

## 4. Legal framework

### HCQIA

**Entity status.** § 11151(4)(A)(ii) covers an entity — group medical practice named explicitly — that provides health care services *and follows a formal peer review process for the purpose of furthering quality health care*. The formal process is not evidence of qualification; it is the qualifying condition. No charter, no entity status.

**Immunity.** Damages immunity for the professional review body, its members, staff, and anyone assisting, when the four standards are met: reasonable belief the action furthers quality care; reasonable effort to obtain the facts; adequate notice and hearing; reasonable belief the action is warranted by the facts.

**A separate provision immunizes anyone who provides information** to a professional review body, failing only if the information was false and known to be false. This is the protection reporting clinicians actually need — lead with it when recruiting participation.

**Limits.** Rebuttable presumption, not a shield — available, not automatic, and it turns on whether the process was actually followed. Damages only; injunctive and declaratory relief are outside. Civil rights claims expressly excluded. Attaches to actions affecting membership or privileges based on competence or conduct — **not to employment decisions**, and not to statements made outside the process. Hallway and email repetition of findings is the more likely defamation vector.

**Employment termination standing alone is likely not a professional review action.** That is the firewall working, and it is why CGC acts on privileges while management acts on employment, as separate decisions on separate records. It also means "terminate if bad enough" is the path with the *least* protection, not the most — the fact pattern that produces defamation and tortious interference claims with no HCQIA immunity for the people who recommended it.

**Discipline coverage.** The professional review action definition is written around physicians. For NPs, PAs, and psych NPs the federal immunity is thinner and state immunity provisions carry more weight — argues for running identical procedure for everyone rather than tiering it.

### PSQIA / PSO

**Correction to a common misconception: PSWP is privileged against you, too.** It may not be used in medical staff disciplinary proceedings, and PSQIA prohibits adverse employment action against someone for good-faith reporting. Model PSES policies state outright that peer review activities are not conducted within the PSES.

**Superseded 2026-08-25 — the CQC sits outside the PSES.** Its work is standards, education, and audit: prospective and normative, not incident capture assembled for PSO reporting. Both committees therefore rely on state peer review privilege plus HCQIA, and the audit-to-peer-review referral works without contortion. This is also the ordinary architecture — QA committee audits, deficiencies refer to peer review, both under the state statute — and PSQIA is voluntary, so for most groups doing this the question never arises.

**Consequence: the patient safety track has no home.** Event reporting, aggregate systems learning, RTH root cause work, and facility-level pattern analysis were assigned to a CQC-inside-the-PSES track that no longer exists. Either a separate designated patient safety function owns them, or the PSO question waits until there is a body to own it. Waiting is defensible — the PSO is the only piece of the program that is not load-bearing for the rest.

**Note the direction model PSES policies actually use.** Peer review and QA are conducted *outside* the PSES, and information copied from them may be incorporated *in*. One-way, outside to inside. Whatever patient safety function eventually exists, that is the flow — and the corollary is that PSWP is not the vehicle for anything that has to survive contact with a disciplinary process.

**PSES design is the whole ballgame.** Courts do not take a provider's word that material is PSWP. First step is an inventory of every PI, QA, peer review, and patient safety activity, and which committees, departments, and staff categories conduct each. Ambiguity is the failure mode — a committee that sometimes operates inside and sometimes outside produces a record no court will honor.

**Anything independently required stays outside.** *Southern Baptist Hosp. of Fla. v. Charles* (Fla. 2017): documents required under state law are not PSWP regardless of PSES designation. This is the single most common failure mode.

**Cost and effort.** No published pricing; quoted per organization. Flat rates common for non-hospital groups; some association PSOs free to members. No federal fees. Front-loaded effort — the inventory and policy, roughly 20–40 hours plus counsel review over ~2 months. Ongoing is light; cadence and volume are provider-set. Nursing homes, home health, hospice, and medical offices are all eligible provider types. **Select for post-acute/LTC domain depth** — comparative feedback is only useful if the denominator resembles the population.

### State privilege — five-state summary

Full analysis in the companion document. Headlines:

**Virginia — best fit, and not close.** § 8.01-581.17 extends privilege to committees established under guidelines adopted by a state professional association. The Medical Society of Virginia has published guidelines whose scope expressly covers committees established by an entity that *employs or contracts with physicians for the provision of professional medical services*. **Adopt MSV guidelines by reference in the charter.**

**Ohio — definitionally generous, evidentially demanding.** "Health care entity" expressly includes for-profit corporations, LLCs, professional corporations, ACOs, and combinations. Committee definition is function-based and expressly includes any attendant hearing process. But courts require document-by-document proof of scope; generalities lose the privilege. Rewards meticulous segregation and precise minutes.

**North Carolina — workable, with an entity-coverage gap.** § 90-21.22A defines a medical review committee by composition and purpose, with no hospital-organizer requirement. But the discovery bar runs to a defined list of defendants (licensed Chapter 90 providers, licensed PSOs, ASCs, hospitals) that does not obviously include an employed-physician management company. Individual clinicians protected; corporate protection is an open question. Nursing homes fall under a separate statute (§ 131E-107).

**Indiana — hardest fit.** Protection requires organization in one of nine enumerated ways; whether Eventus qualifies as a "professional health care organization" is a threshold yes/no question. ≥50% clinician composition floor (easily cleared). **Critically: I.C. 34-30-15-6(b) confines statutory hearing and appeal rights to independent practitioners with privileges, and expressly excludes providers working solely as employees.** Build to HCQIA regardless; treat Indiana as a floor already exceeded. One useful feature: disclosure of final action to state licensure boards is permitted without waiver. One constraint: a provider under investigation may see accumulated records pertaining to their own practice — assume the subject reads the file.

**Kentucky — historically worst, recently much improved.** *Tibbs v. Bunnell* (2015) held an incident report auto-transmitted to a PSO discoverable. KRS 311.377 amended effective 7/14/2018 to extend protection to any entity performing a designated professional review function. Then *Baptist Healthcare Systems v. Kitchen* (Ky. Dec. 18, 2025): root cause analysis **fully protected** as PSWP under a four-part test, and — significantly — factual information inside it is **not severable**, even where unavailable elsewhere. The contemporaneous incident report was **not** protected, having been prepared to satisfy state regulatory obligations.

**The *Kitchen* split generalizes:** retrospective analytic work developed for PSO reporting is protected; contemporaneous documentation created to satisfy an independent obligation is not. Same line as *Charles*, now confirmed in the least friendly state.

**Charter must satisfy the union of all five states' requirements, not the average:**

| Requirement | Driven by |
|---|---|
| Adopt recognized association guidelines by reference | Virginia |
| ≥50% licensed clinician membership | Indiana, NC |
| Function-based committee definition, hearing process expressly in scope | Ohio |
| Document-level segregation, precise minutes | Ohio |
| Retrospective analytic posture, not contemporaneous incident capture | Kentucky |
| Full HCQIA process regardless of state entitlement | Indiana |

**Universal:** underlying facts are never protected anywhere. Medical records, independent witnesses, and otherwise-available information remain discoverable in all five states. Say this plainly in the charter — clinicians will ask.
