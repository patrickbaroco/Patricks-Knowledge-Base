# Clinical Governance: Changes Since the September 29 Packet

*Changes made to the charters and supporting documents after the packet dated September 29. Newest entries at the bottom.*

---

## September 29: a flag for charts that pass but raise a concern

**Quality Committee Charter, new § 5.5(b)(1.1).**

The audit checks whether a decision was documented, not whether it was a good one, so a note can meet a standard while recording a decision no clinician would defend. An example: an antipsychotic dose reduction documented with its date and outcome, where the drug was restarted because the resident "began sneezing." That note meets a standard asking for the date and outcome of the last reduction.

The person examining a chart can now flag it for the Chair when it meets the standard but the care it records raises a concern. Flagged charts are brought up when the Chair asks the standing safety question, alongside the charts that failed. Before this change, that question reached only failed charts.

A flag is not a finding. The clinician is not notified, it counts toward nothing, and it cannot lead to a referral through the audit. What can follow is a committee member's own referral to peer review, as an individual, or nothing.

**Noted for the measure work, not yet changed.** When the antipsychotic standard is written, the "outcome" of a dose reduction should be defined by the documented target behavior: how that behavior responded, or another documented clinical reason for what was decided next. The sneezing note would then fail the standard itself.

## October 4: most audits become a report and an offer of help

**Quality Committee Charter, new §§ 5.4.1, 5.4.2, 5.5(e), 4.7(f); Audit Referral Policy and Peer Review Committee Charter conformed.**

**Every clinician whose charts are read gets a review report.** It lists each chart, what met the standard and what did not, any commendation, and an offer of help. It invites the clinician to say where the review got something wrong, with no deadline. Nothing in it is a finding, and the Committee decides nothing about the clinician. For most standards, this is the whole of the individual process.

**The formal check runs only where a referral could follow.** On a standard where a repeated gap can be referred, the Committee looks again at a small sample of the clinician's charts some months later. If the same gap appears, the clinician gets a formal notice with ten business days to correct the record, covering the first report as well, and only then can the written referral rules apply. The same check runs at once on the rare standard where a single gap can be referred. If more than a quarter of the clinicians reviewed miss a standard, nobody gets an individual follow-up: that is a teaching problem.

**A clinical comment.** The clinician reading a chart may add advice on a resident's management that the standard does not reach, such as an antipsychotic whose indication does not warrant it or a dose reduction given up early. A physician member writes it on request, and must when the comment concerns a physician's note. It counts toward nothing.

**The physician's periodic visit note is read too.** The regulatory visit in skilled nursing, and the periodic physician visit in assisted living, review the whole plan of care. Where a primary care standard applies, that note is read as the physician's own entry, and the physician receives a report of their own.

## October 5: the departments drawn as equals, a request for help, and a one-page flow

**The seat map is redrawn.** The primary care and psychiatry committees now sit beside the Quality Committee, with arrows running both ways, instead of beneath it. Each department works out the standards for its own discipline, the Quality Committee adopts them, teaches them and audits them, and a priority can start on either side. No charter changed for this; both already state each department's part.

**A clinician can ask for help without being audited. Quality Committee Charter, new § 7.7.** Any clinician may ask for help in a clinical area at any time, with no reason needed. The request goes to the education team, who decide how to answer it. It opens nothing: no charts are picked because of it, nothing is counted, and nobody keeps a record that it was asked. It also changes nothing else. The audit and every route to peer review run as they would have.

**A supervising psychiatrist's note is read the way a primary care physician's is. Quality Committee Charter, § 4.7(f).** The physician's periodic note is read as the physician's own entry wherever it is in the chart: the regulatory visit in skilled nursing, the periodic visit in assisted living, and a supervising psychiatrist's visit or review. A psychiatrist who rounds and makes supervisory visits is read exactly as a primary care physician is, and receives a report of their own. Notes that begin after a standard was adopted count only after the usual notice period.

**How a Review Unfolds is brought up to the October 4 flow.** It now shows the review report to every clinician whose charts were read, the look back only where a repeat could be referred, the formal check only where a referral could follow, the clinical comment, the physician's own note, and the request for help.

**New: the review process on one page**, sized for a slide. It shows where a matter starts (a clinician's request for help, an audit, or compliance or anyone with a patient-safety concern), each step it can take, and every place it can end.
