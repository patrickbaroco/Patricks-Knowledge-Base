## How to use this

Their side is steelmanned. Several of these objections are correct, and the ones that are correct need conceding out loud rather than argued past. A room that watches you concede a real point believes you on the points you refuse to concede.

**Every answer has a "Say it like this" block written in plain language — no statutes, no terms of art, no clinical shorthand.** Those are the sentences to actually speak. The material under "Behind it" is for your own confidence, not for the room. If you find yourself reaching for it out loud, you have lost the thread.

Three rules before any of it.

**Lead with what happens at exit, not with patient safety.** The safety argument is true, it is why you are doing this, and it will not move a financial sponsor. The exit argument moves them and is also true.

**Never say this removes risk.** It changes what kind of risk you have. Say that instead.

**The board is not the enemy here.** These are the objections of people being asked to sign something they cannot easily unsign.

# Part 1 — The five that can kill it

## 1.1 "You are asking us to permanently hand authority over our own workforce to a committee we do not appoint, whose chair is elected by employees, with no appeal to us."

**Their side.** Our duty runs to the company and its investors. In no other function would we give binding authority over the workforce to an elected body made up of that same workforce and then write ourselves out of the appeal. Sales does not elect the person who can take away a rep's territory. And this is effectively permanent, because unwinding it in the middle of a case would destroy the very protection you say we are buying.

**Why it is strong.** All of it is accurate, and the permanence point is the sharpest part. It usually gets waved away.

> **Say it like this.** You are giving up one thing and keeping everything else. The committee decides whether a clinician can practice without oversight. It cannot touch pay, hiring, firing, contracts, schedules, or assignments — those all stay with management, and the charter says so in plain terms. And yes, this is hard to reverse, deliberately. A review process that management can overrule whenever it wants is not a real review process, and the federal protection only exists if the process is real. The thing that limits us is the same thing that protects us. You cannot have one without the other.

**Behind it.** § 11151(4)(A)(ii) conditions health care entity status on actually following a formal peer review process. Retained board powers are material amendment and dissolution — and dissolution mid-program is a nuclear option, not a control. Say so rather than pretending otherwise.

**Where it lands.** Concede permanence. Then use it.

---

## 1.2 "You are creating a reporting obligation we do not have today."

**Their side.** Right now nothing is reportable, because we do not define what any clinician is permitted to do, so there is nothing to restrict. Your own analysis says so. You want us to create that, and with it a permanent federal record that follows our clinicians, in a market where we are fighting to recruit them. Every report is also a written admission by our own committee that one of our people gave substandard care, handed to the next plaintiff for free.

**Why it is strong.** Every clause is true. This is the most defensible objection in the set.

> **Say it like this.** There is no version of this where nothing happens. The alternative to limiting what someone can do is firing them — and firing someone gets us no legal protection at all. It is the option with the most exposure, not the least. Most of what this committee will actually do is education, coaching, and looking over someone's shoulder for a while, none of which goes on any record anywhere. Formal limits are rare on purpose. And the part people forget: a plaintiff already gets the chart. What we do not have today is anything showing we looked, thought about it, and did something. Right now they get the facts and we have nothing to put next to them.

**Behind it.** Termination standing alone is likely not a professional review action, so no HCQIA immunity attaches, and it is the fact pattern that generates defamation and tortious interference claims. Underlying facts are discoverable in all five states regardless. Add the recredentialing point if it helps: clinicians with known performance histories being reattested to payers on clean files is exposure that exists today and is harder to explain than a report.

**Where it lands.** Concede that reportability is genuinely new. Then make them price the alternative instead of comparing to zero.

## 1.3 "What does this cost, and what do we get?"

**Their side.** Show us the number. A dedicated coordinator. Ongoing legal fees. Three committees — one with nine people, two meeting monthly — full of clinicians whose time has a revenue value we can calculate. Outside reviewers. And the return is a lower chance of things that have not happened, which will never appear in any statement we produce. We are in a hold period. This is exactly the kind of spending that gets cut.

> **Say it like this.** When we sell this company, the buyer's clinical team will ask three questions. What is your process for reviewing clinician performance. What does your credentialing actually define. And what performance actions have you taken. Right now our answers are: we do not have one, nothing, and some, but we did not document them. That is a finding. Findings do not get explained away in diligence — they get priced, or they turn into money held back at closing. This is the cheapest time we will ever have to fix it. On cost: the coordinator role is mostly work someone is already doing. The lawyers are already engaged. The committees are a few hours a month for people who are already in clinical leadership meetings.

**Behind it.** This is the only argument in the program denominated in something the sponsor already tracks. Bring an actual range with stated assumptions — refusing to estimate is what makes a board assume the number is bad.

**Where it lands.** Strongest available frame. Lead with it.

## 1.4 "You have built something so detailed that our most likely failure is not following our own rules."

**Their side.** You keep saying the legal protection only applies if the process was followed exactly. Then you hand us three charters, five separate record systems, mandatory recusals, notice windows, twenty-day response periods, thirty-day clocks, forty-five-day stops, and an annual composition check. Every one of those is a way to breach our own charter, and every breach is a gift to the other side. A simpler program we could actually follow would protect us better than an elaborate one we cannot.

**Why it is strong.** This may be the best objection in the entire document, and it is the one least likely to be anticipated.

> **Say it like this. ** You are right, and it is the reason we are asking for the coordinator role. This does not work if it is administered by whoever has time that week. It works if one person owns the calendar, the minutes, the clocks, and the files, full stop. That is not administrative support — it is the thing that makes the protection real. And the alternative does not actually help: a vague charter is worse, because at least one of our states requires us to prove document by document what was covered, and generalities lose. We do not get to choose between complicated and simple. We choose between complicated and administered, or complicated and not.

**Behind it.** Ohio requires document-by-document proof of scope. The failure mode is drift, not complexity — an intervention that runs indefinitely, a hold that quietly becomes something else, a recusal not recorded. The anti-drift clocks exist because that failure is predictable.

**Where it lands.** Concede fully. This objection is the single best argument for funding the coordinator role, so treat it as help rather than attack.

## 1.5 "Our clinicians will leave, and we cannot replace them."

**Their side.** Recruiting into long-term care is brutal already. You are proposing to audit every chart with software, build files on people, and create a committee that can restrict how they practice and put it on a national record. Our competitors do not do this. The clinician who has three offers will take the one without the review apparatus, and the ones who stay will be the ones who cannot go elsewhere.

**Why it is strong.** The recruiting market is real, the asymmetry is real, and nobody at the table wants to be the one who caused an exodus.

> **Say it like this.** Ask what happens to the clinicians who are doing good work today. Right now they work alongside people whose problems nobody addresses, they cover for them, and they have no way to raise a concern that goes anywhere. That is what drives good clinicians out, not review. The people most likely to leave over this are the small number whose practice would not survive being looked at. And the protection runs the other way too: right now, a clinician who reports a colleague is exposed. Under this, they are protected by federal law for speaking up in good faith, whatever the outcome. That is a recruiting argument, not a liability.

**Behind it.** 42 U.S.C. § 11111(a)(2) protects anyone providing information unless it was false and they knew it. It does not depend on the outcome or on the committee getting anything right. This is the protection that matters most for participation and it should be in every recruiting and onboarding conversation.

**Where it lands.** Strong, but do not oversell. Some turnover is likely in year one, and saying so is better than being surprised by it.

# Part 2 — Board

## 2.1 "Why now? Nothing has gone wrong."

**Their side.** No lawsuit, no regulator, no payer requirement, no incident. A new chair arrived and is building his function. We see this pattern constantly.

> **Say it like this.** Two things. First, we are already doing this — we have already told a clinician to practice under review with conditions attached. We did it with no committee, no written process, and no protection for anyone involved. So the exposure exists now; the question is only whether it is protected. Second, we are about to turn on software that reviews every chart against quality questions. The day that runs at scale, we have a dated record of everything we knew. After that, "nobody flagged it" stops being available to us. The only sequence that works is building the process before the record exists.

**Where it lands.** Strong. The audit timing is specific and near-term and hard to dismiss.

## 2.2 "We are a management company, not a hospital. Does this law even apply to us?"

**Their side.** The statute names group practices. Whether a multi-state employed-clinician structure qualifies is genuinely unsettled — your own analysis flags it in three states. You are asking us to build an apparatus on a foundation you call an open question.

> **Say it like this.** It is an open question, and our counsel is working it. But we cannot answer it by waiting, because the law protects entities that actually run a formal process — running one is what qualifies you. There is no order of operations where we first prove we are covered and then build. And we have written the charter to satisfy the strictest requirement in each of our five states rather than the average, so that if one state falls short the whole thing does not collapse. Worst case, we are still far better off than we are now, which is no protection anywhere.

**Where it lands.** Partial. Concede the uncertainty; the answer is that waiting does not reduce it.

## 2.3 "The indemnification is open-ended."

**Their side.** You want us to stand behind, without limit, people we do not appoint — including outside reviewers who do not work here.

> **Say it like this.** This is narrower than it sounds. Our people are already defended under the company's umbrella coverage, so I am not asking for a policy. I am asking for the company to say in writing that it stands behind people who serve. No clinician sits on a committee that can end a colleague's career without knowing that. The federal protection helps you if you reach the end of a case; it does not stop anyone from suing you in the first place. If open-endedness is the worry, put a bad-faith clawback on it. Do not take out the promise.

**Behind it.** HCQIA immunity is a rebuttable presumption against damages. It does not reach injunctive or declaratory relief or civil rights claims. Indemnity and defense coverage are different instruments; the company has the second and is being asked for the first.

**Where it lands.** Defend this one. Offer structure, not weakening.

## 2.4 "An elected employee body with private meetings is a union in embryo."

**Their side.** Clinicians elect representatives. Those representatives hold power over what colleagues may do. Their discussions are confidential and management cannot see them. We spend real money avoiding structures that look like this, and you want us to create one ourselves.

**Why it is strong.** Financial sponsors are genuinely sensitive here and the resemblance is not imaginary.

> **Say it like this.** The things a union bargains over — pay, hours, schedules, assignments — are exactly the things this committee is forbidden to touch, by name, in the charter. It cannot see compensation and it cannot act on scheduling. What is left is clinical judgment about clinical practice. This is also how every hospital medical staff in the country is organized, and none of them has been treated as a labor organization for existing.

**Where it lands.** Put it to counsel before answering it definitively in a room. The charter is drafted so the answer should be no, but that conclusion is counsel's to give, not yours.

## 2.5 "This depends on one person, and our CMO seat is temporary."

**Their side.** He chairs two of the three bodies, owns the audit program, and wrote the whole architecture. Structures built around an individual do not survive that individual.

> **Say it like this.** The design assumes the CMO seat turns over — that is why the CMO seat sits for the council's governance business and is excluded, by seat, from every decision about a named clinician. Nothing about a new CMO changes anything here. On me: I am specifically not the chair of the governing council, I cannot be, I do not vote on it at all, and I step out of the room before it decides anything about a named clinician. Every role in the charter is defined by position rather than by name, so it survives any of us leaving.

**Where it lands.** Strong on the CMO. Partial on the chair — see 4.4.

# Part 3 — Executive team

## 3.1 "You are replacing a decision with a procedure."

**Their side.** Today, if a facility raises a concern, we can move. Now there is intake, screening, notice, a twenty-day response window, a panel, sixty days to findings, then a council meeting and hearing rights. Meanwhile the facility is unhappy, and an unhappy facility can terminate a contract worth real money.

**Why it is strong.** Operationally accurate. These timelines are slow relative to how facility relationships actually move.

> **Say it like this.** Nothing here slows down anything management does today. You can still act on employment, on your own grounds, on your own timeline — the charter says that explicitly and does not add a day to it. What gets a process is limiting how someone practices, which is a different decision. If someone is dangerous right now, three people can restrict them the same afternoon. And here is what we gain: today the only lever anyone has is keep them or fire them, which is why most concerns get neither. A committee that can only do nothing or end a career will do nothing every time.

**Where it lands.** Strong. Pair it with a facility-communication protocol so "we are handling it through our clinical governance process" is a real thing someone can say on the phone.

## 3.2 "Confidential means we cannot see it. How do we manage people we are not allowed to know about?"

**Their side.** We are accountable for clinical operations and you are telling us there is a body that knows things about our people we cannot access.

> **Say it like this.** You get told the outcome and the conditions — what was decided and what it means for how that person works. What you do not get is the discussion that got there, and that is the part that, if it circulated, would strip the protection from everyone including the company. You keep every management tool you have today, based on what you know yourself.

**Where it lands.** Strong. Outcome versus discussion is intuitive once said and rarely contested afterward.

## 3.3 "You are carving one of our employees out of our management chain." (coordinator role)

**Their side.** She may decline to tell her supervisor things. Part of her work cannot be evaluated by her manager. She has special protection against being disciplined. That is a precedent and precedents spread.

> **Say it like this.** It applies only to the committee work, not to the rest of her job. It is the same arrangement we already accept for compliance and internal audit, and for the same reason — the person holding the file cannot sit inside the reporting line the file is protected from. If we skip it, the weakest point in the whole records system is the person who physically holds the records.

**Where it lands.** Strong. The compliance comparison does most of the work.

## 3.4 "Star ratings and shared savings are how we make money, and you are excluding exactly those."

**Their side.** The measures with money attached are the ones you have declared off-limits for holding individuals accountable. That reads as a quality program built to avoid the quality that pays.

> **Say it like this.** They are excluded from referring an individual, not from the program. Standards, education, and fixing the system can all target them, and that is where the leverage actually is, because no single clinician controls a facility star rating. Here is why the line matters: if we ever discipline someone over a measure with money attached to it, the first thing their lawyer says is that we did it for the money, not for the patients. That argument writes itself, and one case like that would poison every decision the committee has ever made.

**Where it lands.** Strong once the reasoning is given. Never state the rule without it.

## 3.5 "Physicians will stop collaborating with nurse practitioners."

**Their side.** If reviewing an NP can pull in the collaborating physician, and physicians carry consequences NPs do not, physicians will refuse to collaborate — or will collaborate and stop raising concerns.

> **Say it like this.** Reviewing a nurse practitioner does not open a review of their physician. We look at whether the oversight arrangement was adequate, every time, and we write down what we found — but a physician only gets reviewed if we find a specific problem with what that physician did, and then it starts fresh with its own notice and its own process. We built it this way deliberately, because the alternative turns every NP case into a physician case and physicians stop signing up.

**Where it lands.** Strong. Raise it yourself before a physician on the board does.

# Part 4 — The committees themselves

## 4.1 "There is not a single regional leader on the council."

**Their side.** The regional CMOs and regional directors know these clinicians better than anyone in the company, and the body that decides about them seats none of them.

> **Say it like this.** An earlier draft seated every regional leader by office, which made the council seventeen people. The point was to hear from operations, and two enterprise seats do that better: the Chief Medical Officer and the Chief Operating Officer. Nine seats is a body that can meet and decide. The regional leaders have not lost their voice. They refer concerns, they are interviewed, and the council can invite them for any matter where what they know is needed.

**Where it lands.** Moderate. The real cost is that operations is heard through two people rather than eight. If the board wants more, the charter already lets the council invite a regional leader to any matter (CGC § 3.8).

## 4.2 "The CMO and COO sit on the council and are sent out of the room for the only decision that counts."

**Their side.** They sit on the council, they attend, and then they are told to leave the room before any decision about a clinician. These are the people accountable for the clinical enterprise. You have built a body that excludes its most senior members from its most important act.

**Why it is strong.** It is the sharpest objection to the two-class voting rule and it has a real point buried in it.

> **Say it like this.** They sit for everything else the council does: the charters, the framework, policy, whether the audit plan is looking at the right things. What they do not do is sit for a decision about a named clinician — because they also decide these people's jobs, and a decision about someone's practice made by their boss is not a peer review, it is a management decision wearing a different hat. That distinction is the entire reason the federal protection exists. If we blur it, we lose the protection, and then we are back to firing people as the only tool.

**Behind it.** Flagged for counsel in reverse: whether a clinician could argue the deciding body excluded the members with the most direct knowledge. The answer should be that they may attend the presentation and provide information, and only deliberation and the vote are closed to them, but it needs testing.

**Where it lands.** Strong, and this is the objection where the plain-language version is much better than the technical one.

## 4.3 "Nobody with management authority can even be in the reviewer pool. That is most of our clinical leadership."

**Their side.** You have excluded every regional CMO and every regional director from serving on the peer review committee or reviewing a single case. That is a large fraction of our most experienced clinical people, permanently barred from clinical review work.

> **Say it like this.** This one I will trade if you want. My reasoning is that a regional leader reviewing another region's clinician still looks like a supervisor judging a clinician, and we have plenty of other clinicians to draw on. But case-by-case recusal is a defensible alternative and I am not going to fight over it. What they can always do is raise a concern, and be interviewed, and be protected by law for doing both.

**Where it lands.** This is on the trading list. Concede it early — it looks generous and costs little.

## 4.4 "The chair has too much concentrated power."

**Their side, at its strongest.** He owns the audit that generates referrals. He chairs the committee that screens them. He chairs the committee that reviews them. He sits on the body that decides them, even without a vote. Stepping out of the room does not undo the fact that he shapes what gets looked at, how it is described, and what evidence goes with it.

**Why it is strong.** Because it is right, and no amount of recusal drafting fully answers it.

> **Say it like this.** Here is what is actually in place. I am not the chair of the governing council and I am not allowed to be. I have no vote on it, and I leave the room before it deliberates on anything about a named clinician. Screening takes two other people, not just me. My committee cannot impose a formal restriction on anyone — it can only recommend, and a different body decides. Hearing panels exclude everyone from both of my committees. And anyone in the company can refer a case directly without going through me at all. What is left is real: I still influence what gets looked at. So here is something I will offer — once a year, the council gets a report showing where every referral came from. If they are all coming from me, that will be visible to a body I do not chair.

**Where it lands.** Offer the referral-source report unprompted. Conceding before you are pushed buys credibility on the things you are refusing.

## 4.5 "Three people can restrict someone the same afternoon."

**Their side.** You just told us the chair and two members can immediately limit how a clinician practices, without a hearing, without the council. That is enormous power in very few hands.

> **Say it like this.** They can start it. They cannot keep it. It expires automatically after five business days unless the full council votes to continue it — nobody has to do anything for it to end, it just ends. That pairing is the whole design. It is fast enough to be useful when someone is actually in danger, and it is impossible for three people to sustain on their own.

**Where it lands.** Strong. The automatic expiry is the answer and it should be said in the same breath as the power.

## 4.6 "Sixty days to findings. A facility will not wait sixty days."

> **Say it like this.** Sixty days is the outside limit for a full review with the person's response and a panel of their peers — it is not how long a facility waits to hear from us. Screening happens within ten business days and we can tell the facility that day that it is being handled. If someone is dangerous, we restrict them immediately. What takes sixty days is being fair to someone whose career is on the line, and if we short-cut that, we lose the case and the protection.

**Where it lands.** Strong, but only if the facility-communication protocol actually exists. Build it.

## 4.7 "You are building files on people that they get to read."

**Their side.** You have written into the charter that we should assume the clinician will read the file. So we are creating written assessments of our own people, that they can obtain, that can be used against us in an employment claim.

> **Say it like this.** In one of our states, a clinician under review can get the records about their own practice. That is the law, not our choice. So we wrote the rule that we write everything as though they will read it — findings, evidence, no editorializing. That is not a weakness. A file written that way is a file that holds up, and a file that would embarrass us if read is a file that was going to be a problem anyway.

**Where it lands.** Strong. It converts a constraint into a discipline.

## 4.8 "Why can the quality committee not just fix things? Why does everything go to another committee?"

**Their side.** You have a quality committee that finds problems and then is forbidden from doing anything about them. That is a committee designed to generate work for a different committee.

> **Say it like this.** The quality committee fixes almost everything — standards, education, changing how the work is set up. What it cannot do is anything to an individual person, and that is on purpose. The moment a quality committee can also punish you, people stop telling it things, and then it stops finding anything. Keeping it clean of individual consequences is what keeps it useful. Individual concerns go to the review committee, and that is a small fraction of what it handles.

**Where it lands.** Strong. This is the sentence that makes the quality committee acceptable to clinicians.

## 4.9 "Who decides what the standard is? You?"

**Their side.** Your quality committee writes its own standards and then judges people against them. In a for-profit company, an internal standard is a business rule wearing a white coat.

**Why it is strong.** It is the objection a clinician will raise, and it is the fair version of a real risk.

> **Say it like this.** Where a professional society has published a guideline, we adopt theirs rather than writing our own. The problem is that our patients — very old, many conditions, limited life expectancy, living in a nursing home — are usually excluded from the studies those guidelines are built on. So for a lot of what we do, there is no outside standard to adopt. When we write one, we have to state in the standard itself what evidence it rests on and how we got from that evidence to these patients. And we label every standard as either a minimum expectation or something we are working toward — the audit can only refer you for falling below a minimum, never for missing a stretch goal. None of that is the limit of what gets reviewed. A serious concern about care goes to review whether or not we ever wrote a standard about it.

**Where it lands.** Strong. The exclusion-from-trials point is unanswerable and it is also just true.

## 4.9a "So if it is not in your written standards, it is allowed?"

**Their side.** You have built a standards program and tied referrals to it. The natural reading is that anything your standards do not cover is fine. That is not a position we want on the record, and it is the first thing a plaintiff will quote back at us.

**Why it is strong.** It is the failure mode of every written standards program, and it would be a real defect if the charters did not close it.

> **Say it like this.** No, and the charters say so in terms. The written standards are what the audit measures against — that is all they are for, because a computer scoring every chart has to be scoring against something stated. They are not a list of the only ways a clinician can fall short. Anyone can bring a concern about care that no written standard addresses, and it gets reviewed the same way, against what a reasonable clinician in that setting would have done. That is what peer review has always been. And having a written standard does not protect you either — meeting our standard does not establish that the care was appropriate.

**Behind it.** CQC § 6.7 and PRC § 8.1.1. Four parts each: reviewable regardless of any adopted standard; the absence of a standard is not a defense and implies nothing about acceptability; anyone may refer directly as a person, in which case the audit rules do not apply; and compliance with an adopted standard does not establish that care met the standard of care.

**Where it lands.** Strong, and worth raising before someone else does. A standards program that has not disclaimed this inference has handed it away.

## 4.10 "Software is going to audit every chart. This is surveillance."

**Their side.** You are running artificial intelligence over every note our clinicians write, scoring them, and feeding the results into a process that can restrict their practice. However you describe it, that is what it is, and people will experience it that way.

> **Say it like this.** The audit is already running — that is not something I am proposing, it is something that exists. What does not exist is any rule about what happens to what it finds. Right now those flags go nowhere and nobody has to do anything about any of them. What I am proposing is that every finding gets a decision written next to it: this is a system problem, this needs a conversation with the clinician, or this is nothing. Most will be the first two. And no question can even be asked unless it maps to a standard we adopted and told people about first. The alternative is not privacy. The alternative is a growing pile of flags nobody answered for.

**Where it lands.** Strong. Reframing from "we are starting surveillance" to "surveillance exists, we are adding rules" is the whole move.

## 4.11 "The audit will find problems everywhere. You cannot refer everyone."

> **Say it like this.** If a rule would send a large share of our clinicians to review, that is not a clinician problem, it is a bad rule or a real system failure — and the charter says so explicitly. It goes back to fixing the standard or the question, not to mass referral. We also compare people to peers doing similar work rather than counting raw numbers, so someone covering the sickest building is not penalized for the building. None of that limits the separate route: a serious concern about a single case goes straight to review without any threshold at all.

**Where it lands.** Strong, and it is worth volunteering rather than waiting to be asked.

## 4.12 "Minimum expectations versus improvement targets is bureaucracy."

> **Say it like this.** It is one vote when we adopt a standard, and it prevents a specific bad outcome. A quality committee naturally wants to set the bar high. If we do not label which standards are the floor and which are the goal, someone eventually gets sent to peer review for missing a goal we set higher than what the profession actually requires. We decide what a standard is for when we write it, not when someone is accused under it.

**Where it lands.** Strong. It is cheap and the failure it prevents is easy to picture.

## 4.13 "We have to run an election?"

**Their side.** Elected at-large members means ballots, eligibility rolls, campaigns, and disputes. We are a company, not a democracy.

> **Say it like this.** It is a handful of seats, once a year, run by one person. The reason it is an election rather than an appointment is that in a company owned by a financial sponsor, a committee whose members were all picked by management is a committee whose independence nobody believes — including a jury. The election is what makes it real rather than decorative. If the mechanics worry you, we can keep it simple: nominations, a ballot, a majority, done.

**Where it lands.** Moderate. If they push hard, appointment by the council itself from practicing clinicians is a fallback, though it costs some of the credibility.

## 4.14 "Our clinical leaders already cannot make meetings, and you have added three."

> **Say it like this.** The nine-person council meets four times a year for governance, and only comes together on a case when there is a case. The two committees meet monthly and are small. Peer review cases are reviewed by the committee's own members, two to a case, so there is no separate group of reviewers to recruit and schedule. For most people on the council this is a few hours a quarter. And a lot of it replaces conversations we are already having, less usefully, without a record.

**Where it lands.** Moderate. Be ready with an actual hours-per-person estimate.

# Part 5 — What clinicians will say, which executives will carry into the room

## 5.1 "Nobody will report a colleague if it can end their career."

> **Say it like this.** Federal law protects anyone who gives information to this kind of committee in good faith. That protection does not depend on whether the committee agrees with you, or on how the case comes out — the only way to lose it is to lie on purpose. That is stronger protection than anyone raising a concern has today, and we should be saying it constantly, not burying it in a charter.

**Where it lands.** Strong, and underused. This should be in onboarding, not just in the charter.

## 5.2 "This is the hospital bureaucracy I came here to get away from."

> **Say it like this.** We deliberately did not build a medical staff with bylaws, officers, and general meetings. There are three committees, most clinicians will never interact with two of them, and the most likely thing that ever happens to a good clinician is a note saying we looked at something and it was fine. What we did take from hospitals is the part that protects you: you get told, you get to respond, and people in your own field decide.

**Where it lands.** Moderate. Backed up mostly by how the first year actually feels.

## 5.3 "What if the committee is wrong?"

> **Say it like this.** You get written notice of what is being looked at and time to respond. If it goes toward a formal restriction, you get a hearing in front of people from your own field who were not part of the review and are not on either committee. A different body than the one that reviewed you makes the final decision. And nothing that only lasts a short time and involves education or oversight goes on any national record at all.

**Where it lands.** Strong, and it is the answer that gets a program accepted by the people inside it.

# Part 6 — The objection they should make and probably will not

## 6.1 "Your own program builds the map a plaintiff will use."

**Their side.** Every disposition you record is a document saying the organization knew something, with a date on it. Audit at scale and you produce a complete inventory of everything you were ever on notice of, next to a record of what you chose to do about each one. Every time you chose education rather than restriction, someone will hold up your file and ask why you did not do more.

**Why it matters.** This is the sharpest argument against the program and it is more sophisticated than what a board usually produces. Better to have met it in a meeting than in a deposition.

> **Say it like this.** The knowing comes from the audit, and the audit is already running. What we choose is whether the knowing comes with any evidence that we handled it. A file that shows we found it, thought about it, and did something proportionate is the best defense there is. A file that shows we found it and nothing else is indefensible — and that is exactly where we are today.

**Where it lands.** Partly conceded, openly. The program does create records that will sometimes be uncomfortable. The alternative record is worse.

---

# Part 7 — Trading positions

**Concede early and visibly.** Each costs little and buys credibility elsewhere.

- The council's access to separate counsel. Least defensible, most likely to be struck.
- The bar on regional leaders serving in the reviewer pool (4.3). Case-by-case recusal is a defensible substitute.
- The number of at-large members and their terms.
- Meeting cadence — anything monthly can start quarterly and tighten.
- The coordinator's title, and the timing of the resourcing ask.
- The annual referral-source report from 4.4 — offer this one unprompted.

**Do not trade these.**

- **Indemnification.** Without it there is no committee. Offer a bad-faith clawback before weakening the promise. Insurance is not in the ask — the company already defends its people.
- **The committee may not see compensation, productivity, or revenue.** This is the cleanest answer to any claim that a decision was about money: they cannot have weighed what they never saw.
- **Employment decisions and practice restrictions stay separate.** Collapse this and the protection disappears and every finding routes to HR by default.
- **No management vote on individual actions.** Presence and information yes, vote no.
- **No appeal to the board.** Giving this away puts them back in the clinical chain, which is against their interest as much as the program's. Tell them so.

---

# Part 8 — What not to say

**Do not lead with legal risk.** Boards hear risk arguments constantly and discount them by reflex. Lead with what a buyer finds at exit.

**Do not lead with patient safety as a moral claim.** It is true, and in that room it reads as naive and invites "we all care about patients."

**Do not lean on "this is what hospitals do."** The answer is that we are not a hospital, and it is fair. Use the comparison only where it does specific work.

**Do not overstate the protection.** Say it is a presumption that can be rebutted. Say it covers money damages and not orders to do or stop doing something. Say it is available, not automatic. If you describe it as a shield and it later fails, everything else you said loses its credibility.

**Do not present this as free.** It costs money, clinician time, and speed. Acknowledging all three survives scrutiny that an unqualified pitch will not.

**Do not threaten.** Any version of "if we do not do this we will get sued" turns a governance proposal into a prediction that will be remembered and tested.
