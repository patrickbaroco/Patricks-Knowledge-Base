# Patient Safety Organizations: A Primer

*Prepared for the Eventus peer review program build-out*

---

## Glossary

**AHRQ** — Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality. The federal agency that lists (certifies) PSOs and maintains the directory of them. AHRQ does not regulate what you do internally; it certifies the organization you report to.

**Common Formats** — AHRQ's standardized event reporting specifications. They exist so that data from different providers can be aggregated meaningfully. Versions exist for acute care, ambulatory surgery, and — relevant to you — nursing home settings.

**Component PSO** — a PSO that is a subsidiary or division of a larger entity (a health system, an association) rather than a freestanding organization. Component PSOs carry additional separation requirements. Building one is an option for large organizations; for most groups, joining an independent PSO is the cheaper path.

**Disclosure** — a defined term of art under the Patient Safety Rule, not a colloquialism. It means releasing PSWP to a legally separate entity or person. Permissible disclosures are enumerated and narrow. Impermissible disclosure can waive protection and carries civil money penalties.

**HHS OCR** — Office for Civil Rights, which enforces the confidentiality provisions of the Patient Safety Act. Same office that enforces HIPAA.

**NPSD** — Network of Patient Safety Databases. The national aggregated, non-identifiable dataset that PSOs contribute to. Participation feeds it; it is not a public reporting mechanism.

**Patient Safety Act / PSQIA** — the Patient Safety and Quality Improvement Act of 2005. The statute. Codified at 42 U.S.C. § 299b-21 et seq.

**Patient Safety Activities** — the defined set of activities that qualify for protection: efforts to improve safety and quality, collection and analysis of PSWP, development of improvement strategies, dissemination of findings, and related work. Activities outside this definition don't generate PSWP.

**Patient Safety Rule** — the implementing regulation, 42 C.F.R. Part 3. Where the operational detail lives.

**PSES** — Patient Safety Evaluation System. Your internal system for collecting, managing, and analyzing information intended for reporting to a PSO. This is the thing you build. It is defined by a written policy, not by software.

**PSO** — Patient Safety Organization. An external entity, listed by AHRQ, that receives and analyzes PSWP from providers and returns feedback.

**PSWP** — Patient Safety Work Product. The protected material: data, reports, analyses, and deliberations assembled within a PSES for reporting to a PSO, plus the deliberations and analyses of the PSES itself. Privileged and confidential under federal law.

**De-designation / dropping out** — removing information from the PSES before it is reported to the PSO, which strips its PSWP status. This is permitted and deliberate; the pathway must be written into your PSES policy.

**Functional reporting path** — the sequence by which information enters the PSES, is analyzed, and is transmitted to the PSO. Courts scrutinize whether this path was actually followed, not just whether a policy describes it.

---

## What a PSO is, and the problem it solves

Clinicians do not report candidly into systems they believe can be subpoenaed. That is the entire premise. Before 2005, quality improvement work in most settings relied on state peer review privilege statutes, which vary enormously in scope, are written around hospitals, and offer nothing to a multi-state organization trying to run one consistent program.

PSQIA created a federal privilege that does not depend on state law. Information developed for reporting to a PSO becomes patient safety work product: privileged, confidential, and not subject to discovery or admission in civil, criminal, administrative, or disciplinary proceedings. In exchange, providers contribute to a national learning system.

The PSO itself is external — an organization listed by AHRQ that receives your data, aggregates it across participants, and returns comparative feedback. The privilege attaches because of the reporting relationship, not because the PSO does anything protective on your behalf.

---

## The mechanism

Three components, in sequence.

**1. You establish a PSES.** This is a written policy defining what information is collected for patient safety purposes, who collects it, which committees and roles operate inside the system, how it moves, and how it is transmitted to the PSO. It is not a database. It is a documented boundary.

**2. Information inside the PSES becomes PSWP.** Protection attaches at the moment information is assembled or developed within the PSES for reporting — not when the PSO receives it. Deliberations and analyses conducted within the PSES are protected immediately and cannot subsequently be de-designated.

**3. You report to the PSO.** Cadence and volume are largely yours to set; participation agreements typically leave the type and number of reported events to the provider.

The critical implication: the protection is a function of *where information lives*, not what it contains. A root cause analysis of a hospital transfer is protected if it was developed inside the PSES for PSO reporting. The same analysis, conducted by the same people, outside the PSES, is discoverable.

## What the privilege does not cover

This is where most organizations get hurt, and where I would spend your reading time.

**The underlying facts remain fully available.** Medical records, billing records, discharge data, and the observations of anyone who was present are not protected and never were. A plaintiff can obtain the chart, retain an expert, and depose every clinician involved. What they cannot get is your analysis of what went wrong. The Illinois Appellate Court in *Daley v. Teruel* made this point directly in rejecting the argument that PSQIA obstructs legitimate claims.

**Anything you are independently required to create or maintain falls outside.** This is the holding of *Southern Baptist Hospital of Florida v. Charles* (Fla. 2017), which reversed a provider-favorable appellate decision and confirmed that documents required under state law are not PSWP regardless of whether they were also placed in the PSES. The operational lesson: **state and federal reporting obligations must be completed outside the PSES.** If you satisfy a mandatory reporting duty using a document you also designated as PSWP, you likely have neither protection nor a defensible position.

**Peer review that leads to action is a poor fit.** PSWP is privileged in disciplinary proceedings — including yours. Model PSES policies routinely state that medical staff peer review activities are not conducted within the PSES. PSQIA also prohibits adverse employment action against an individual for good-faith reporting into the system. Route your PRC deliberations into the PSES and you may find you have protected the record so thoroughly that you cannot use it to support the action or defend it at hearing.

**State courts disagree with each other.** *Charles* (Florida) and *Daley* (Illinois) reach different conclusions on the scope of PSQIA preemption, with the Illinois court expressly disagreeing with Florida's reading. The Eleventh Circuit in *In re Baycare Medical Group* (2024) rejected the "sole purpose" test that several courts and most plaintiffs' counsel had relied on to defeat the privilege — a meaningful development in the provider's favor, but one that underscores how unsettled the terrain remains. For a national organization, assume the least protective interpretation in your least protective state.

## Why PSES design is the whole ballgame

Courts do not take a provider's word that material is PSWP. They examine whether the organization actually had a system, whether the policy defined it clearly, and whether the information in dispute genuinely traveled the described path.

The recommended first step is an inventory: catalogue every performance improvement, quality assurance, peer review, and patient safety activity in the organization, and identify which committees, departments, and categories of staff conduct each one. That inventory determines what you can later claim, and it is the document that establishes you had a considered system rather than a retroactive theory.

The inventory also forces a decision you would otherwise defer: for each activity, is it inside the PSES or outside it? Ambiguity here is the failure mode. A committee that sometimes operates inside and sometimes outside, depending on the topic, produces a record no court will honor.

Anyone with PSWP access must be trained on the privilege and confidentiality limits. Access itself should be defined and documented.

## Joining: cost and effort

**Fees.** There is no published market price; PSOs quote per organization. Hospital pricing is typically volume- or bed-based. For non-hospital groups, flat rates are common. Some association-affiliated PSOs charge members nothing — the AOA PSO is free to AOA members, for instance. Providers may participate in more than one PSO. There are no federal fees; AHRQ charges nothing to list a PSO and nothing to participate in one.

**Effort, front-loaded.** The inventory and PSES policy are the real work: expect a meaningful block of your own time over roughly two months, plus counsel review. This is not delegable in its substance, because the inventory requires knowing what the organization actually does rather than what the org chart says.

**Effort, ongoing.** Light. Reporting cadence and volume are yours to set. The recurring obligations are maintaining access controls, training new staff with PSWP access, and periodically confirming that practice still matches policy.

**Selection.** Eligibility is not a constraint — nursing homes, home health, hospice, and medical offices are all explicitly eligible provider types. The differentiator worth paying for is domain depth. Comparative feedback is only useful if the denominator resembles your population, which argues strongly for a PSO with post-acute and long-term care experience over a hospital-oriented one.

## Application to Eventus

The clean architecture is two tracks with two different protections.

**Inside the PSES: the Clinical Quality Council track.** Event reporting, aggregate analysis, systems learning, return-to-hospital root cause work, facility-level pattern analysis. This is exactly what PSQIA was built for, and it is work Eventus already does without protection.

**Outside the PSES: the Peer Review Council track.** Individual clinician review that can lead to a professional review action, its deliberations, its recommendations, and any hearing. This track relies on state peer review privilege and HCQIA's safeguards.

That split is not a compromise. It reinforces the firewall the peer review program needs anyway: the body doing systems learning and the body doing individual accountability should not share a record.

Two Eventus-specific complications worth naming now:

**Facility-generated documents.** Your clinicians work in buildings Eventus does not own, each with federal QAPI obligations and state reporting duties. Documents created to satisfy those obligations sit outside your PSES by definition. Any transmission of PSWP to a facility is a disclosure to a legally separate entity and needs an enumerated permission.

**Multi-state variability.** The privilege that PSQIA offers is uniform; the state-law backdrop it interacts with is not. Design the PSES to the strictest jurisdiction in your footprint rather than to Virginia.

3. What is the permissible-disclosure analysis for transmitting anything to a contracted facility?
4. Does routing CQC work into a PSES create any risk of contaminating the PRC track, given that I chair both bodies?
