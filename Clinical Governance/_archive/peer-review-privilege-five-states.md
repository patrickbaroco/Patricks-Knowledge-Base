# Peer Review Privilege Across the Eventus Footprint

*Virginia · North Carolina · Ohio · Indiana · Kentucky*
*Companion to the PSO primer. Not legal advice — this is a map for the conversation with counsel.*

---

## The short version

The five states divide into three groups.

**Favorable and well-fitted to an employed group practice:** Virginia, Ohio.
**Workable but with a coverage gap that needs analysis:** North Carolina.
**Poorly fitted to an employed model:** Indiana.
**Historically hostile, recently improved:** Kentucky.

The design implication is straightforward: the PRC charter must be written so that a committee organized under it qualifies in *all five* states. That means satisfying the union of the requirements, not the average.

---

## Virginia — Va. Code § 8.01-581.17

**Best case in the footprint, and it is not close.**

Virginia originally protected only hospital-based committees. The statute was amended to extend privilege to non-hospital quality assurance and peer review committees, and the pathway is unusually explicit: privilege attaches to a committee **established pursuant to guidelines approved or adopted by** a national or state peer review entity, accreditation entity, national professional association of health care providers or its Virginia chapter, or a statewide association representing licensed providers.

The Medical Society of Virginia has published guidelines under § 8.01-581.17 for exactly this purpose. Their stated scope covers committees established by an entity that **employs or contracts with physicians for the provision of professional medical services**, or any affiliate of such an entity — which is a direct description of Eventus.

**Practical consequence:** Virginia gives you a checklist. Adopt the MSV guidelines by reference in the PRC charter and the privilege question in Virginia is largely answered.

**Limits:**
- A witness with knowledge of the facts is not shielded merely because they gave information to the committee, and cannot be insulated from deposition by virtue of committee involvement.
- No privilege attaches to medical records kept in the ordinary course of business.
- Disclosure can be compelled by a circuit court for good cause arising from extraordinary circumstances.
- Incident reports are increasingly treated as medical-record-like and therefore discoverable even when routed to a qualified committee.

**Note:** Virginia's statute separately defines "patient safety data" to include reports made to patient safety organizations along with root cause analyses and quality improvement products, and addresses exchange of that data among entities. Worth having counsel read § 8.01-581.17(F) alongside the PSES design.

## Ohio — O.R.C. §§ 2305.25, 2305.252

**Second best, and structurally the most generous definition.**

Ohio protects proceedings and records within the scope of a **peer review committee of a health care entity**, in any civil action against a health care entity or health care provider arising out of the matters reviewed.

Two definitional features matter to you:

**"Health care entity" is broad.** The definition expressly includes for-profit corporations, limited liability companies, partnerships, professional corporations, accountable care organizations, other health care organizations, and combinations of the foregoing. Legislation in 2014 (HB 123) added ACOs and multi-entity combinations specifically to make system-level peer review protectable.

**"Peer review committee" is function-based, not setting-based.** It covers a utilization review, quality assessment, performance improvement, credentialing, or **other** committee that conducts credentialing or quality review activities involving the competence, professional conduct, or quality of care of health care providers — and, importantly, **any attendant hearing process initiated as a result of the committee's recommendations or actions.** Your hearing panel is expressly within the privilege.

The statute also names **a board or committee of a long-term care facility or other health care entity** when reviewing professional qualifications or activities of providers.

**The catch is evidentiary, not definitional.** Ohio courts have been clear that the privilege is not a general cloak. The party asserting it must establish that a committee meeting the statutory definition exists, and must then show **document by document** that each withheld record is within the scope of that committee. Generalities about the types of documents a peer review committee usually holds are insufficient; the Cleveland Clinic learned this the hard way on appeal. Ambiguity in a supporting affidavit is enough to lose the privilege even where the underlying process was legitimate.

**Practical consequence:** Ohio rewards meticulous record segregation and precise minute-keeping. It punishes commingling.

Note also O.R.C. § 2305.253, which addresses incident and risk management reports separately — another argument for keeping incident reporting outside the PRC record.

## North Carolina — N.C. Gen. Stat. § 90-21.22A

**Workable, with a real question about entity-level coverage.**

North Carolina defines a "medical review committee" by its **composition and purpose**: a committee composed of health care providers licensed under Chapter 90, formed to evaluate the quality, cost, or necessity of health care services, including provider credentialing. There is no requirement that a hospital organize it, which is favorable.

Protection is substantial: proceedings, the records and materials the committee produces, and the materials it considers are confidential and not subject to discovery or introduction into evidence. Committee members carry civil immunity absent malice or fraud. A member may testify in a civil action but cannot be asked about their committee testimony or opinions formed as a result of committee proceedings.

**The gap.** The discovery bar in § 90-21.22A(c) runs to civil actions against a defined list: a provider of health care services who directly provides services and is licensed under Chapter 90, a licensed provider-sponsored organization, a licensed ambulatory surgical facility, or a licensed hospital. An employed-physician management company is not obviously on that list. Your individual clinicians are protected; whether Eventus as a corporate defendant is protected is a question worth putting to counsel directly.

**Also:** nursing homes are covered by a separate statute (§ 131E-107), not this one. Facility-level review and your clinician-level review sit under different provisions.

**Standard caveats apply:** information otherwise available is not immune merely because it was presented to the committee.

**Procedurally:** whether the privilege applies is a question of law, in camera review is proper, and an order compelling production is immediately appealable as affecting a substantial right — useful to know if you ever need to fight one.

## Indiana — I.C. 34-30-15; definition at I.C. 34-6-2-99

**The hardest fit, for two independent reasons.**

**First, the organizer requirement.** Indiana does not protect any committee doing peer review work. It protects committees organized in one of nine enumerated ways. The plausible routes for Eventus are organization by the professional staff of a "professional health care organization," or by the governing board of one. Whether Eventus qualifies as a professional health care organization under the cross-referenced definitions is a threshold legal question with a yes-or-no answer, and everything else depends on it.

There is also a composition floor: **at least 50% of committee members must be individual professional health care providers**, or the committee must be organized as an interdisciplinary committee conducting evaluation of patient care services. Your proposed structure clears this comfortably.

**Second — and this is the finding that most affects your design — Indiana's statutory hearing and appeal rights do not extend to employees.** I.C. 34-30-15-6(b) confines the right to a hearing and appeal to professional health care providers who have been granted or have applied for privileges as independent practitioners, and states expressly that the right is **not applicable to any professional health care provider who works in the hospital or health care facility solely as an employee** or as the employee of another practitioner.

This cuts in an unexpected direction. It does not mean you should provide less process in Indiana — HCQIA's safe harbor requires adequate notice and hearing regardless of what state law confers, and providing less would forfeit federal immunity. It means Indiana's statutory scheme was not written with your model in mind, and you should not assume the state framework will supply either the protections or the procedures. Build to HCQIA and treat Indiana state law as a floor you have already exceeded.

**One useful feature:** the statute permits disclosure of committee records to the appropriate state board of registration and licensure where the committee considers it necessary for recommended disciplinary action, and permits disclosure of *final action* without waiving privilege. That is a cleaner reporting channel than several other states provide.

**One constraint to note:** a provider under investigation is permitted at any time to see any records accumulated by the committee pertaining to their own practice. Build the charter assuming the subject will read the file.

## Kentucky — KRS 311.377

**Historically the worst jurisdiction in the country for this. Substantially better now.**

**The history.** In *Tibbs v. Bunnell* (2015), the Kentucky Supreme Court held that an incident report entered directly into a hospital's patient safety evaluation system and automatically transmitted to its PSO was **not** patient safety work product and was discoverable — reasoning that because it contained information normally found in reports maintained for state regulatory agencies, it fell outside the protection. The U.S. Supreme Court denied certiorari over the objection of the AHA and the Joint Commission, with the Solicitor General opposing. For a decade, Kentucky was the case everyone cited for the proposition that the PSQIA privilege was unreliable.

**Two things have changed.**

**Statutory.** KRS 311.377 was amended effective July 14, 2018 to extend privilege and confidentiality protections to any entity performing a designated professional review function — a meaningful broadening beyond the hospital-centric prior scheme.

**Judicial.** On December 18, 2025, in *Baptist Healthcare Systems v. Kitchen*, the Kentucky Supreme Court took up PSQIA and KRS 311.377 together in a case involving a hospital fall. The holdings are consequential:

- The **root cause analysis was fully protected** as PSWP. The court applied a four-part test: it was an analysis, prepared for the purpose of reporting to a PSO, in fact reported to that PSO, and contained detailed analysis of care processes with operational improvement suggestions and key takeaways.
- The court **rejected the argument that factual information within the RCA was severable and discoverable.** A plaintiff cannot reach facts inside protected PSWP even where those facts are unavailable elsewhere. This is a strong holding and a meaningful retreat from *Tibbs*.
- The **incident report was not protected** — not under PSQIA, because it was prepared contemporaneously and pursuant to hospital policy to satisfy state regulatory reporting obligations; and not under the state peer review privilege, because it was an immediate post-incident record rather than a substantive retrospective review and evaluation.

**Practical consequence, and it generalizes beyond Kentucky.** The *Kitchen* split maps precisely onto the architecture already proposed: retrospective analytic work developed for PSO reporting is protected; contemporaneous operational documentation created to satisfy an independent obligation is not. Kentucky is now an argument *for* the PSO route rather than against it — but only if the PSES is built so that the reporting-obligation documents never enter it.

## Cross-cutting design implications

**1. Write the charter to the union of requirements, not the average.**

| Requirement | Driven by |
|---|---|
| Adopt recognized association guidelines by reference | Virginia |
| ≥50% licensed clinician membership | Indiana (floor), NC (composition-based definition) |
| Committee defined by function, with hearing process expressly included in scope | Ohio |
| Document-level segregation and precise minutes | Ohio (evidentiary burden) |
| Retrospective analytic posture, not contemporaneous incident capture | Kentucky |
| Full HCQIA process regardless of state entitlement | Indiana (no employee hearing right) |

**2. The facts are never protected anywhere.** All five states preserve discovery of the medical record, of witnesses with independent knowledge, and of information otherwise available. Nothing in this program shields the underlying care from scrutiny, and the charter should say so plainly — clinicians will ask.

**3. Mandatory reporting documents stay outside.** Virginia, Ohio, Indiana, and Kentucky all contain provisions treating regulatory or incident reporting separately. *Kitchen* and *Charles* both turned on this. It is the single most common failure mode.

**4. Entity-level coverage is the open question in NC, and the threshold question in Indiana.** Both deserve specific attention from counsel. In both states, individual clinician protection is more secure than corporate protection.

**5. The PSO adds a federal layer that does not vary.** Across five states with five different schemes, PSQIA is the only uniform protection available — and after *Kitchen*, it is a stronger one than it looked five years ago. That argues for the two-track design: CQC work inside the PSES for federal protection, PRC work outside it relying on state privilege plus HCQIA.

## Questions for counsel, by state

**Virginia** — Confirm that adopting the MSV guidelines by reference satisfies § 8.01-581.17(B)(iii), and review § 8.01-581.17(F) on patient safety data exchange against the proposed PSES design.

**Ohio** — Confirm Eventus meets the "health care entity" definition. Design the affidavit template now that will be used to establish scope if privilege is ever challenged.

**North Carolina** — Does the § 90-21.22A(c) discovery bar protect Eventus as a corporate defendant, or only the individual licensees? If only the latter, what follows for corporate exposure?

**Indiana** — Does Eventus qualify as a "professional health care organization" under I.C. 34-6-2-99(a)(2)(A)? If not, is there another organizer route?

**Kentucky** — Does the 2018 amendment to KRS 311.377 reach an employed multi-state group practice performing a designated professional review function? Review *Baptist Healthcare Systems v. Kitchen* (Ky. Dec. 18, 2025) directly.

**All five** — Which clinician disciplines are covered? These statutes were written around physicians. NP, PA, psych NP, and psychotherapist coverage varies and has been analyzed here only at the margins.
