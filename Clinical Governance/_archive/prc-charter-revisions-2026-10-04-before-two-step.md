# PRC Charter — Revision Record, 2026-08-30

*Three rounds. Round 1 begins below; **Round 2** at "Second review round"; **Round 3** at "Third review round" at the end. Later rounds supersede earlier ones wherever they differ.*

*Companion to `prc-charter-draft.md`. Records what an external governance review raised, what was accepted, what was adjusted, what was found beyond it, and what remains open. All changes described here have been applied to the charter draft.*

**Scope of the review.** Governance and internal coherence, not statutory verification. Every point below was checked against the charter text before disposition.

**Summary.** Eleven numbered points and two cleanup items were raised. Nine were accepted as stated, two were accepted with a different remedy, and one was found to be only half right. Three further defects were identified in the course of verification, one of which — the § 4.7(b) exposure now addressed at § 8.1.2(d) — carries more live risk than anything on the original list.

---

## 1. Panel or Committee — who is the factfinder

**Raised:** the draft made the Panel the factfinder at § 6.7, § 8.1 and § 8.5, and the Committee the factfinder at § 3.3 and § 10.1.

**Confirmed, and sharper than raised.** The operative consequence was § 8.5: a matter closed on the Panel's finding with no Committee vote at all. Under § 6.7, panelists who are not members do not vote — so a disposition issued from a body that partly could not vote on it. Beyond the incoherence there is a statutory reason to fix it: 42 U.S.C. § 11112(a) conditions immunity on the professional review body having acted in the reasonable belief that its action furthered quality health care, after a reasonable effort to obtain the facts. A distinction that floats between two entities hands a plaintiff the question of which one held the belief.

**Resolution — accepted as proposed.** Panel is investigative and advisory and *proposes*; Committee adopts, modifies, or rejects by vote, and the findings of the Committee are the findings it adopts. Applied at § 6.7, § 8.1, § 8.5, and § 14.4 ("Panel findings" → "Panel proposal"). Section 6.7 now states the § 11112(a) reason in the text.

**Related — § 2.8.** Confirmed: the section gave quorum and the Adverse Recommendation supermajority and never stated how ordinary action is approved. Added: *"Except as this Charter otherwise provides, the Committee acts by majority of members present and voting at a meeting at which a quorum is present."*

**Added beyond the review.** Once the Committee is the decisionmaker, a three-member Panel drawn from a six-member Committee could disable the quorum that must vote on its own proposal. Section 2.8 now states that Panel service is not a ground of recusal, that a member who served votes as a member, and that the proposal does not bind its author.

## 2. § 4.7 — the audit-bypass hole

**Raised:** the independent-basis test in the first paragraph had consequences attached only where the CQC notice period had not run.

**Confirmed.** The conjunction made the notice period a necessary condition. A clinician executing the audit could personally refer "Dr. X failed the CQC standard," with no independent basis, once ninety days had passed — or on a finding the CQC's own ratified criteria would not refer.

**Resolution — accepted as proposed.** The consequence now attaches to the test itself, and applies regardless of the notice period, regardless of whether the CQC made an individual finding, and regardless of whether an Audit Referral Policy criterion was met. The notice period is retained as the clearest illustration.

**Added — and overstated; see Round 2, item 1.** The sentence as first drafted read: *noncompliance with a CQC standard, unaccompanied by an independent concern about the standard of care or about patient safety, does not reach this Committee by any route.* That is the design. The section now says so, and says expressly that it obstructs no one with a safety concern — it requires only that they say what the concern is.

---

## 3. § 4.3 — what "mandatory referral" mandates

**Raised:** the section did not establish whether screening may still choose No action, Systems, or Administrative. Two remedies were offered: rename it "mandatory opening of review" and require § 4.4(d), or accept that it adds almost nothing.

**Confirmed that the section was empty. Neither proposed remedy adopted.** The provision conflated two duties falling on different people — an obligation on anyone holding the information to bring these forward, and a constraint on what screening may then do. And a flat "must open" is too rigid in two recurring cases: a licensure action on a purely administrative ground, and a referral naming a Practitioner who was not involved.

**Resolution — split into three subsections.**

- **(a) The duty to refer** — falls on any person holding the information, is discharged by referring, and is expressly *not* discharged by forming a view that the matter would not survive review.
- **(b) The duty to open** — a referral under (a) is dispositioned under § 4.4(d). **Systems and Administrative are withdrawn for these matters.** A mandatory-referral matter may well reflect a systems condition; the Committee finds that *in* the review and refers it under Article XI, rather than in place of reviewing.
- **(c) One exception, named** *(wording superseded — see Round 2, item 2)* — No action is available on a single ground: the Practitioner named was not involved, or the matter does not concern the practice of medicine. Recorded with reasons and reported to the Council. The section states expressly that a judgment the care was probably acceptable is not a ground under it.

## 4. § 1.7 cross-reference, and § 6.5 psychotherapist procedure

**Cross-reference confirmed and fixed.** § 1.7 cited § 7.5 (Systems conditions, in the oversight Article). The psychotherapist provision is § 6.5.

**Standing procedure — accepted.** "Procedures the Council adopts at the time" meant procedure written once the person and the matter were known, which is procedure written about them. Section 6.5 now requires a **standing discipline-specific addendum adopted in advance with counsel review**, and no review of a psychotherapist opens until it is in effect.

**Added — the fallback the proposal needed.** A precondition on opening a review is a shield if nothing is carved out of it. Section 6.5 now states that screening under § 4.4 and the precautionary authority under Article IX are unaffected, and that where a matter cannot open for want of an addendum the Chair notifies the Council, which shall adopt it or record why it will not.

**Consequence to track: this creates an adoption dependency.** Until the Council adopts the addendum, the Charter cannot open a review of a psychotherapist. That belongs on the implementation checklist.

## 5. § 8.1.2 — what a CQC standard proves

*The premise of this item was itself mistaken. See Round 2, item 4, and the CQC § 4.7(e) note.*

**Raised:** calling an adopted standard evidence "of what the profession accepts" is inconsistent with a CQC architecture that permits internally authored standards, LTC-specific adaptations, and improvement targets — and with § 8.1.2(c) two lines below.

**Confirmed, and this is the most consequential item in the round.** The reformulation was accepted. But the live exposure is narrower and worse than the framing suggested, and required a further provision.

CQC § 4.7(b) permits a standard to dictate the **form and authorship** of documentation — that an element be entered by the treating Practitioner rather than established by the record as a whole, and element by element rather than by global attestation. No professional body imposes that. It is an auditability convention. On the correction-step work of the same week, it is also the requirement practitioners will most often fail.

If the Charter says an adopted CQC standard is evidence of professional acceptance, then in a malpractice action a plaintiff quotes the Entity's own governance document to argue that a Practitioner's failure to make her own entry is evidence that **her care** fell below the standard of care. That converts a documentation convention into a liability benchmark, in a document the Entity wrote about itself.

**Resolution.** The opening paragraph now distinguishes by source: evidence of the Entity's clinical expectation always; relevant evidence of professional acceptance only where the standard adopts or restates a published guideline or established consensus; not so where internally authored, adapted, or designated an improvement target; conclusive in no case. And a new **§ 8.1.2(d)** states that a § 4.7(b) form-or-authorship requirement is a documentation convention of the Entity, is not evidence of the standard of care, and that noncompliance with it is not evidence that care fell below standard.

**Counsel question added:** whether such a disclaimer is effective against a third party or binds only the Entity's own bodies. If it does not travel, the exposure has to be managed in how CQC § 4.7(b) standards are drafted rather than here.

## 6. § 3.2(a) and Article IX

**Raised:** § 3.2(a) forbids Professional Review Actions; Article IX lets three people restrict Clinical Authorization.

**Confirmed, and worse than raised** — the contradiction is on the face of the two lists in the same Article, since § 3.1(f) grants the precautionary authority that § 3.2(b)–(a) appears to withhold.

**Resolution — accepted as proposed, with one drafting constraint.** Section 3.2(a) now states that Article IX is the sole exception, that a § 9.1 restriction is temporary, lapses under § 9.2, terminates under § 9.5, and is not a final Professional Review Action **under this Charter**. It expressly does not decide, and does not depend on, whether the restriction is a professional review action at the moment of imposition for § 11112(c)(2) purposes — which remains an open counsel question and should not be pre-empted by a governance clause.

## 7. § 11.3 out of sync with CQC § 6.3

**Confirmed and fixed.** Section 11.3 listed the individual finding, the underlying data, and the disposition history. CQC § 6.3 also transmits what the Practitioner submitted in the correction step and what the CQC did with it.

**Mirrored — and to the redrafted § 5.5(a), not the prior text.** The correction step as redrafted also records *which grounds were sustained*, and PRC § 4.7 screening needs that. Section 11.3 now carries all of it, with a sentence on why: a referral arriving with only the adverse half of the CQC record invites this Committee to begin where the CQC ended, which is the opposite of § 11.4. The provision is marked to be kept aligned with CQC § 6.3.

---

## 8. § 13.9 effective-date boundary

**Raised:** read literally, care furnished before the effective date becomes unreviewable.

**Confirmed, with a more decisive example than the one given.** The reading does not merely exclude a late-reported event — it kills § 4.1(f) permanently. A malpractice claim, a settlement, and a licensure board action concern care furnished years earlier by their nature. As drafted, that entire referral source could never produce a reviewable matter.

**Resolution — accepted.** The boundary is now drawn around records: pre-effective-date peer review records are not imported; a matter pending on the effective date has its prior record closed and a new one opened. An express sentence states that nothing in the section limits review of care furnished before the effective date.

---

## 9. § 13.6 disclosure

**Confirmed, and contradicted by four provisions** on a naive reading — § 3.2(e) counsel-analyzed disclosure, § 10.1 transmittal to the Council, Article XI referrals to the CQC, and § 14.1 referral of administrative matters to Management.

**Resolution — accepted.** Reframed on authorization rather than medium or audience, with the in-process disclosures listed. The warning keeps its force and gains a boundary: *the test is authorization, not medium — an email to counsel is within the process and a conversation with a facility administrator is not.*

---

## 10. § 12.1 permanent recusal

**Raised:** "hold or held a supervisory relationship" has no time limit, permanently excluding anyone who supervised the Practitioner years ago.

**Confirmed. Accepted with one correction to the proposed remedy.** The suggestion relied on "the general reasonable-person impartiality clause" carrying the older cases. That clause does not exist in the form assumed: § 12.1's catch-all reaches only *personal, familial, or financial* relationships and would not have caught an old supervisory one. Time-bounding alone would therefore have opened a gap rather than softened a rule.

**Resolution.** The supervisory trigger is bounded at [five] years **and** a general residual clause is added covering any other relationship or circumstance — including an older supervisory one — that a reasonable person would regard as bearing on impartiality. The section now records why: § 2.3 already excludes every holder of Employment Authority from Committee and Pool, and an unbounded lookback compounds that exclusion until the discipline-matched panels required by § 6.3 cannot be constituted.

## 11. § 15.2 compliance trap

**Confirmed and accepted.** "It shall be stated in every communication soliciting information" is a freestanding administrative duty of exactly the kind removed from the CQC charter for the reason recorded there — a charter obligation that can be breached by ordinary inattention is worse than no obligation. Moved to the operating procedure, with a sentence making clear that an outdated template is a process error rather than a breach of the Charter.

## Cleanup items

**Bracketed defaults — confirmed.** The forty-five-day precautionary stop was listed twice. Removed.

**Open item 1 — half right.** The note said the categorical exclusion in § 2.3 is "consistent with the two-class voting rule at the Council and the same rule at the CQC." *Consistent with* is not a claim of identity, so the note was not wrong. But it invites the misreading, and the accurate statement is that § 2.3 **goes further**: the Council and the CQC seat holders of Employment Authority and exclude them from individually attributed decisions, while this Committee does not seat them at all. Rewritten to say so, with the reason — the CQC has prospective and organizational work for such a member to do, and this Committee has no other work.

## Defects found in verification, beyond the review

**A. Article IX cannot be completed within its own hard stop.** Section 9.3 said the review proceeds "on an expedited basis" and defined nothing, so § 14.4 governed by default: sixty days to Panel proposal plus fifteen to the Committee's recommendation. Seventy-five days, against the forty-five-day termination in § 9.5. Every precautionary restriction would have lapsed before the Committee could act, or survived only through the hearing exception in § 9.5 — which is to say, only in contested matters, which is precisely backwards.

**Fixed at § 9.3.** Expedited now expressly displaces § 14.4. The Chair sets and records the schedule at imposition, so that the Committee's recommendation reaches the Council in time to act before the forty-five days expire. A floor is placed under the Practitioner's response period ([ten] calendar days, not shortenable) and extensions are available only at the Practitioner's request — so that expedition is not taken entirely out of the Practitioner's side.

**B. § 8.5 cited the wrong Article for records retention** — Article XII is Recusal; records are Article XIII. Fixed.

**C. § 12.3 did not cover screening.** Chair recusal was addressed for constituting and presiding over a Panel, but § 4.4 gives screening to "the Chair and [two] members" and nothing said who screens when the Chair is recused. Section 12.3 now gives the Vice-Chair the Chair's functions in the matter, screening included.

# Second review round — 2026-08-30

*A further external review of the revised charter. Seven changes, all applied. One of them corrects an error introduced in Round 1; one corrects an error that had been sitting in both charters, undetected, since the CQC draft was written.*

## 1. § 4.7 — "by any route" contradicted § 4.1(a)

**Raised, and correct.** Round 1 added a sentence stating what the anti-bypass rule commits the Entity to, and overstated it: *noncompliance ... does not reach this Committee by any route.*

That is not the architecture. The CQC's audit-referral route under § 4.1(a) delivers an individual finding against a minimum expectation, on criteria the Council has ratified, **without the CQC having formed a standard-of-care view** — and § 11.4 exists precisely because it has not. The overstatement would have read § 4.1(a) out of the Charter.

**Resolution.** Narrowed to the direct-referral route under § 4.1(b), which is the only route § 4.7 governs, with the § 4.1(a) route stated expressly as the pathway that remains open. A sentence was added making clear that this section polices the boundary between two routes rather than closing one, and that the CQC need not form a standard-of-care view before referring under its own criteria.

## 2. § 4.3(c) had become physician-specific

**Raised, and correct.** "Does not concern the practice of medicine" is a term of art that in most jurisdictions does not describe nurse practitioner or physician assistant practice, in a Charter that applies uniformly under § 1.7.

**Resolution — accepted, with a narrower test than either suggested.** The alternatives offered were "clinical or professional practice" and "clinical practice." The first would swallow the lapsed-continuing-education case the exception exists for; the second would let an impairment-related or conduct-related board action out through an escape hatch built for paperwork. Adopted: **the matter is administrative in nature and does not concern the Practitioner's clinical practice, competence, or fitness to practise** — with an express sentence that a matter raising a question of impairment or fitness is not within the exception whatever its origin.

## 3. § 6.5 contradicted itself

**Raised, and correct.** "No provision of this Charter applies to a psychotherapist by default" sat four lines above the Round 1 carve-out for screening and Article IX.

**Resolution — accepted, with the carve-out widened.** Scoped to provisions *governing the conduct of an opened review*. The carve-out now covers the duty to refer under § 4.3(a), screening under § 4.4, **Article XIII**, and Article IX. Article XIII was the gap in the proposed fix: a matter that has been referred and screened has to sit in a file governed by some records and confidentiality rule, and screening-plus-Article-IX alone left it governed by none.

## 4. § 8.1.2's negatives were categorical, and its premise was wrong

Two separate problems, one small and one not.

**The wording — accepted as proposed.** Saying an internally authored standard "is not" evidence of professional acceptance, and a documentation requirement "is not" evidence of the standard of care, purports to control what external evidence may establish. A charter cannot do that and a court will disregard the attempt. Both restated as what the Entity's **adoption** does not by itself establish. That is sufficient: the object was never to bar evidence, only to stop this Charter from supplying the plaintiff's premise about itself. It also brings (a)–(d) into line with (c), which was already correctly hedged.

**The premise — and this is the more serious finding.** The review noted that § 8.1.2(d) described CQC § 4.7(b) as permitting a treating-practitioner authorship rule, and that the CQC language did not clearly say so. **On checking, it does not say so at all.** CQC § 4.7(b) governs granularity — element by element rather than global attestation — and nothing in CQC Article IV confers an authorship requirement of any kind.

That assumption was load-bearing in four places: the qualification paragraph in CQC § 5.5(a); grounds (iv) and (vi), the § (a)(4)(i) exclusion and § (a)(8)(iii) in the § 5.5(a) redraft; the practitioner notice, which promised the enclosure would say which standards required the Practitioner's own entry; and PRC § 8.1.2(d). The PRC Charter was describing a CQC rule the CQC Charter did not contain.

**Resolution — and the rule that replaced it is better, not merely repaired.** The design intent, once stated, was never authorship: it is that **the treating Practitioner's note must account for each element**, and that an attribution to another clinician satisfies it. New **CQC § 4.7(e)** provides for it as an opt-in designation. Four things satisfy such a standard — the action taken; the absence with a verifiable reason; the absence with the decision attributed to an identified clinician or service; the absence with a defined pending step. What fails is silence.

The difference matters beyond drafting. An authorship rule — write it yourself although the consultant already did — is a documentation tax with no clinical content, and is indefensible in precisely the forum where it would be attacked. An accounting rule has real content: in a resident carrying several guideline-relevant therapies across several clinicians, a note silent on an element is indistinguishable from a note written by someone who did not know it was there, and only the treating Practitioner's own entry can tell those apart. **PRC § 8.1.2(d) is correspondingly easier to defend**, and now carries the sentence that matters most — that whether a treating Practitioner should have known and recorded the status of a guideline-relevant therapy is a question for the Panel on the facts, answerable either way, and that this subsection does not assert the underlying expectation is nothing.

Two failure modes were identified and provided for in § 4.7(e): a pending step satisfies the standard for the cycle in which it is entered and not indefinitely, and an attribution is tested against the record under § 5.10(a).

## 5. § 8.5.1 — adverse finding without intervention

**Raised, and correct.** Article VIII provided for care that met standard, care that did not and warrants Tier One, and care warranting Tier Two. It had nowhere to put a minor isolated departure meriting a recorded finding and nothing else — a case that will be common.

**Resolution — accepted, as its own provision rather than a clause in § 8.5**, and with three things the proposal did not state: the closure is not a Quality Intervention, it is not reportable, and the Practitioner is notified in terms distinguishable from the § 8.5 no-finding letter. Without it, § 8.2(a) written feedback becomes the floor by default, every trivial finding acquires a duration, exit criteria and a named monitor under § 8.6, and the file comes to look like a disciplinary record for matters that never warranted one.

## 6. Two more freestanding administrative duties

**§ 14.2 — accepted.** "The applicable policies shall say so" made this Charter's operation depend on another document having been updated. The documentation-to-patient-welfare nexus is now stated substantively in § 14.2, with an express sentence that the section does not depend on the conforming policy revisions having been made. Those revisions move to the implementation checklist.

**§ 13.7 — accepted in substance, resolved differently.** The point that "shall say so plainly to any Practitioner who asks" is a duty of the same shape as the § 15.2 template rule is well taken, but the risk profiles differ: a template omission is inadvertent, while telling a Practitioner their records are protected when they are not is a misrepresentation with consequences. Restated as a prohibition — **the Committee shall not represent that a fact is protected from disclosure by reason of this Charter where it is not** — which can be breached only by commission. This is the same form used at CQC § 8.1(a) for the same problem.

## 7. § 4.1 temporal ambiguity

**Accepted.** "Every other route is measured against the standard of care under § 8.1" now reads "**if opened for review, is evaluated against**," with an added clause stating that nothing in the section authorises a determination on the merits at screening and pointing to § 4.6.

# Third review round — 2026-08-30

*A combined read of both Charters, the first since they were revised in parallel. Four substantive issues, three cleanups, one counsel question. All applied. Two of the four were created by the previous round's fixes, which is the characteristic cost of patching two documents that reference each other.*

## 1. CQC § 4.7(e) conflicted with § 4.7(b) and (c)

**Raised, and correct.** Subsection (b) requires an absent element to be accompanied by a reason specific to that element; (c) defines what counts as a reason. Subsection (e) then permits an attribution or a pending disposition **instead** of a reason, and says expressly that the treating Practitioner need not repeat another clinician's reasoning. Read strictly, (e)'s alternatives did not satisfy (b).

**Resolution — accepted, and stated on both sides** so that neither subsection reads incorrectly alone. Subsection (b) now opens *"except as subsection (e) provides for a standard designated under it"*; subsection (e) states that its alternatives satisfy (b) notwithstanding the reason requirement.

**Subsection (c) also gained the distinction that explains why the two can coexist.** *"Not a candidate"* is a reason without a referent — which is what (c) was written to catch. *"Per cardiology"* is a referent without a reason, which (c) never contemplated. An attribution is now expressly not a reason under (c), and a standard may be designated to require both — which the Committee ordinarily should where an untreated condition carries a well-defined risk.

## 2. "Minimum expectation" asserted what the Committee cannot determine

**Raised, and the more substantive of the two drafting points.** CQC § 4.4(a) defined a minimum expectation as *a level below which care may indicate a departure from the standard reasonably expected of a Practitioner* — while PRC § 8.1.2(d) now says that the Entity's adoption of a documentation requirement does not by itself establish the standard of care. Not irreconcilable, but two documents pulling against each other on the same point, which is how a plaintiff comes to pick one.

**Resolution — fixed on the CQC side rather than left to the PRC to disclaim.** A minimum expectation is now defined functionally: an individual-level requirement the Entity expects Practitioners to meet, a departure from which may support an audit referral. An express sentence states that the designation establishes nothing about the legal or professional standard of care.

The tension only became unavoidable with § 4.7(e), which admits a standard that is a documentation and communication convention rather than a clinical rule. Note that the redefinition also **sharpens the two designations rather than blurring them**: the operative difference between (a) and (b) is now cleanly that one may support a referral and the other may never. What keeps a minimum expectation clinically grounded is § 5.8(a), which requires every standard to state its clinical basis at adoption — not the wording of the designation.

## 3. The PRC took impairment and fitness matters and had no way to decide them

**Raised, and the largest issue in this round.** Round 2 added a sentence to § 4.3(c) preventing an impairment matter from being closed as merely administrative. Right in intent, wrong in consequence: § 1.2 makes the Committee's subject *the care furnished*, and Article VIII supplies exactly one decisional standard, about care. A licensure action for substance-use impairment, or a cognitive-fitness concern with no identified episode of care, would have been mandatorily opened into a review with nothing for Article VIII to answer — and Article VII would have required an oversight assessment in a matter containing no care.

Two architectures were available: keep the PRC a clinical-care body and route fitness elsewhere, or widen § 1.2 and give Article VIII a second decisional standard.

**Resolution — clinical-care body.** New § 4.3(d) and a new § 4.4(e) screening disposition route health, impairment and fitness to a Practitioner Health pathway. **The Committee's involvement ends at referral** — it retains nothing, monitors nothing, imposes nothing, receives no outcome. That is the same construction as CQC § 6.5, and for the same reason. Where the matter also presents a question about care furnished, the Committee reviews **the care and not the condition**; a health condition may be context, and the Article VIII finding is about the care. Article IX is expressly unaffected, since imminent danger does not wait on a pathway.

**The governing concern was that peer review must not become an HR function.** In a health or fitness matter the employment side dominates — fitness-for-duty evaluation, accommodation, leave, and the employment consequence — and none of that is professional review. Section 3.2(b) already forbids this Committee from making, recommending or advising on any of it. Section 4.3(d) now adds the two sentences that were missing: **a matter does not become professional review by having been screened here**, and the Committee's file closes when the referral is made.

**Added after the first draft of § 4.3(d)**, on the point that the duty to act on knowledge of an impaired practitioner attaches to knowing and does not depend on what the receiving policy is like:

- **The referral is made without exception and without delay.** The same construction as § 4.3(a), pointed the other way, so that no view formed here about what will follow becomes a reason to retain a matter this Committee cannot decide — which would be exactly the investigate-and-manage posture the subsection forbids.
- **The fact of the referral, without content, goes to the Council.** A duty terminating in a referral into a void is not discharged in substance. This Committee cannot follow up without resuming a monitoring role; the Council can see that referrals go in and nothing comes out, and can act on it.
- **Referral is not a report.** A reporting obligation concerning an impaired or unfit practitioner stands independently of the internal referral, is not this Committee's to file under § 3.2(d), and is the Council's to determine under Article XI of its Charter. The Coordinator notifies the Chair of the Council on any § 4.4(e) disposition so the question is asked at the time. Drafted without reference to any jurisdiction's rule — the Charter commits to compliance and a counsel question establishes what compliance requires.

**The destination is designated by Council resolution rather than named in the Charter**, for the reason recorded at § 14.2: a charter that cites another document by name stops working the moment that document is renamed or replaced. This also means adoption need not wait on the receiving policy being settled.

**What is deliberately not decided.** The design of the pathway is a question for the Entity's people and compliance functions with counsel, not one a peer review charter settles. The Charter names a destination designated by the Council rather than a particular instrument, so that adoption does not wait on that question and the answer to it does not require an amendment here. **This project should identify the item, not absorb it.** Two counsel questions were added accordingly.

## 4. The anti-bypass rule was label-based, not origin-based

**Raised, and correct.** Section 4.7 policed audit-derived referrals arriving under § 4.1(b) — Practitioner or employee — and left § 4.1(c) Management and § 4.1(e) facility untouched. Since § 2.3 excludes holders of Employment Authority from the Committee and the Pool **but not from referring**, the person most likely to be looking at audit output across a region could make the identical referral through a route the section did not reach. That is exactly the route-label problem the section exists to eliminate.

**Resolution — accepted.** The test is now the **origin of the information and the basis asserted**, applying to any referral other than the CQC's own under § 4.1(a), whoever makes it and by whatever route. *The protections attaching to audit referrals cannot be avoided by choosing which box to tick.*

## Smaller corrections

- **PRC § 8.1.2(d)** carried a remnant of the Panel-as-factfinder architecture — "a question for the Panel." Now the Panel assesses and the Committee determines under § 6.7. Worth correcting precisely because Round 1 spent effort making the formal factfinder unambiguous.
- **CQC § 4.7(e)'s closing rule** read *"absent designation, an entry by any clinician in the record satisfies the standard"* — grammatically capable of meaning that any entry satisfies the whole standard. Narrowed to *"documentation by any clinician in the record may satisfy the applicable documentation requirement."*
- **CQC drafting notes** still said "findings are dispositioned," language the operative charter had already corrected to observations. Fixed.

## Counsel question added rather than drafted around

**PRC § 4.3(a) against § 1.5.** The duty to refer falls on "any person holding the information"; § 1.5 categorically bars the Committee from receiving patient safety work product. Read literally, the duty could direct the transfer of material the Committee may not receive.

The suggestion was to raise it with counsel rather than burden the charter. **A short qualifier was added anyway** — the duty is subject to applicable law and does not require or permit the transfer of patient safety work product or other legally protected material — on the view that a charter should not be left directing an act that may be unlawful, even pending advice. The question still goes to counsel, together with whether the Practitioner Health pathway should itself be chartered as a protected committee in any of the five states.

## Also applied, in the CQC Charter

Carried over from the working exchange that preceded this review, and consistent with it:

- **§ 4.7(e) settled and pending dispositions.** *"Anticoagulation on hold per cardiology"* is not a weak attribution but a defective pending entry: *on hold* asserts that something happens later, and the entry says nothing about what. The branches are now named, and a pending disposition must carry its resolving condition — an event, a date, a clinician, or a threshold. Failing that entry is not a harsher standard; the fix is four words, and the indefinite hold nobody revisits is how a resident with atrial fibrillation spends eighteen months off anticoagulation. The ignorance case is provided for expressly, so the rule cannot become a demand to reconstruct a specialist's reasoning.
- **§ 5.10(c) thin entries.** For the case the settled branch legitimately admits — *"discontinued by cardiology"*, no reason, satisfies the letter, teaches nothing. Recorded as thin, **always dispositioned Systems, never attributed, never referred.** Failing it would rebuild the authorship rule through the audit; ignoring it would make *"per cardiology"* the universal safe harbour within two cycles. Where thin entries on a measure carry real risk, the remedy is to redesignate the standard under § 4.6 to require both an attribution and a reason — not to refer anyone.

## Where this leaves the project

The reviewer's assessment that further drafting review returns less than counsel's answers held for Round 2 and holds more strongly now. What remains is not drafting:

- **The Audit Referral Policy schedule**, still undrafted, which § 4.7, § 4.3 and the CQC close-out all depend on.
- **The Practitioner Health pathway**, now a named dependency of PRC § 4.3(d).
- **The psychotherapist addendum**, a precondition to opening any such review.
- **The counsel questions**, now including the § 8.1.2(d) third-party question spanning both Charters, the § 4.3(a) / § 1.5 interaction, and the HCQIA treatment of health-related restrictions.
- **Confirm** "Adverse Recommendation" is defined in Council Charter Article II.

# Members review, 2026-09-18

*Applied to `prc-charter-draft.md`: §§ 2.4, 2.5, 6.1 and 6.6, recorded as item 33 in the Charter's open items.*

**Context.** Patrick spoke with a representative of the peer review committee at his local hospital. That committee has no separate body of reviewers. Members of the committee review the cases, two members to each case, and then decide as a committee. The Charter permitted that arrangement but presented a different one: a Reviewer Pool the Council "maintains," three-member panels drawn from membership and Pool alike, and a stated purpose of scaling capacity to volume. Read cold it described a second group to recruit before any case could be opened.

## 1. The standing membership is the reviewing body

§ 6.1 now says so. Panels are drawn from the standing membership, and from the Pool only where discipline matching under § 6.3 or setting experience under § 6.4 cannot otherwise be met. § 6.6 places external reviewers behind both. Nothing about § 6.7 changes: members who review vote with the Committee, which is the hospital's practice and was already the rule here.

## 2. The Pool is permissive and may be empty

§ 2.5 rewritten. The Council may maintain a Pool; membership carries no obligation to serve on a particular matter; and the Pool may be empty. Its stated reason is discipline matching as membership and case mix change, with volume scaling as the secondary case. Nobody has to be recruited into it before launch, and a psychiatric matter arriving before there is a second psychiatric-trained reviewer is handled under § 6.6 as it always was. Council § 4.2(j) continues to read "maintain the Reviewer Pool," which is still correct for a body the Council may keep at zero.

## 3. Two-member panels

[Three] to [two], with a third at the Chair's discretion and the reason recorded. HCQIA sets no panel size; the reasonable-effort standard at § 11112(a) is met by the Committee's process, not by headcount. Per-case load is the recruiting cost that matters once seats are settled, and this removes a third of it.

## 4. Physician floor from two to three

The one place two-member panels cost something. § 6.3(a) requires two physicians for a physician matter, and a § 2.4 floor of two physicians meant a single recusal emptied the panel and sent a routine physician case to an external reviewer. The floor rises to three, with the reason stated in § 2.4. The nurse practitioner and psychiatric matching rules are unchanged; the psychiatric case could never be paneled from a single psychiatric-trained member under any panel size, and the Pool and § 6.6 remain the answer there.

## What this does not touch

The hospital committee reviews physicians drawn from a medical staff organized by department. This Committee reviews physicians, nurse practitioners, physician assistants and psychiatric nurse practitioners across five states, and § 6.3 exists because of that. The hospital model transfers as a default about who reviews; it does not transfer as a reason to loosen matching.

# Revision of 2026-09-29: screening, APCs, and the agreed resolution

Decided with Patrick while reading *How a Review Unfolds*. Recorded as items 44–46 in the Charter's Open items.

## What changed

- **Screening (§§ 2.5.1, 4.4, 4.4.1–4.4.3, 4.5.1, 5.7.1, 12.1).** The Chair screens with the Department Chair for the Practitioner's service line, in place of two members. Department Chairs act for the Committee at screening, do not vote, are persons assisting under 42 U.S.C. § 11111(a)(1), sign the § 13.5 undertaking, and are subject to Article XII. Nothing learned at screening goes to the department. A member of the Practitioner's discipline substitutes where the Department Chair took part in the CQC's determination on an audit referral, or is recused, holds Employment Authority, or is unavailable. The Chair may add the other Department Chair where care crosses service lines. A member of the discipline who took no other part decides where the two screeners disagree. The agreed resolution is still proposed by those who screened.
- **APCs (§§ 1.6(a), (i)–(k), 1.7, 2.4, 6.3, 7.1, 7.3, 10.3).** Nurse practitioners and physician assistants are defined together as Advanced Practice Clinicians and stated to be peers. Panels: physician, two physicians; APC, two APCs; APC practicing in psychiatry, two panelists with psychiatric training, one of them an APC practicing in psychiatry. Service line and Department Chair defined.
- **Agreed resolution (§§ 5.7.3, 5.7.5, 5.7.6, 5.7.8).** The matter still closes when the plan is completed. It is then followed by two examinations of the Practitioner's care in the plan's clinical area, one for each of the two full quarters after completion, against the standard of care, disclosed in the notice before the Practitioner decides. A departure found on an examination is a new matter under § 4.1, and § 5.7.7(e) bars resolving it by agreement. What was learned is still not assessed. New § 5.7.6; former §§ 5.7.6–5.7.7 renumbered 5.7.7–5.7.8. Keeping the review open through both quarters was drafted first and rejected the same day: it would have lengthened the period in which a resignation is a resignation during review (§§ 9.6, 15.9) and blurred the agreed resolution into focused review without a finding. Counsel question added on whether the examinations are an investigation for Data Bank purposes.

## Why

Screening by the Department Chair keeps every member clear of a matter until a review opens and puts discipline knowledge at the first decision. The substitution for audit referrals keeps one person from deciding at the PRC's door what they decided at the CQC. APCs are treated as peers because their clinical responsibilities are identical and nurse practitioners far outnumber physician assistants. The examinations exist because a gap serious enough to reach peer review leaves the Entity obliged to be able to say the care now meets the standard; completion of a plan shows only that the plan was done. They follow a closed matter so that no review stays open against someone about whom no finding was made.
