# Assessing What Eventus Currently Has

A structured discovery process for a new Chair. Part 1 is a threshold decision that must be made before you ask anyone anything. Parts 2–4 are the interviews, documents, and questions. Part 5 is how to interpret what you find. Part 6 is how to write it up without creating a liability.

---

## Part 1 — Before you ask a single question

### 1.1 Run this under attorney-client privilege

This is the most important paragraph in the document.

You are about to conduct a systematic inquiry into whether your organization has been meeting legal obligations. That inquiry will generate documents. If Eventus has a reporting gap, a privilege gap, or a history of informal actions, **your assessment memo will be the cleanest possible summary of it, written by the person with the most authority to characterize it, and it will be discoverable.**

A finding like "we have conducted peer review since 2019 and have never registered with the NPDB" is a devastating exhibit if it sits in a shared drive with your name on it. The same finding, developed at the direction of counsel for the purpose of providing legal advice, is protected.

Two doctrines to know:

**Attorney-client privilege** protects confidential communications between a client and its lawyer made for the purpose of seeking or providing legal advice. In a corporate setting it covers communications with employees when they are gathering information at counsel's direction so counsel can advise the organization.

**Work product doctrine** protects material prepared in anticipation of litigation. Broader in some ways, narrower in others, and more easily overcome than attorney-client privilege.

**What this means practically:**

- Ask the general counsel (or outside health law counsel) to formally direct the assessment. A short engagement memo stating that counsel is conducting a legal compliance review and has asked you to gather facts for that purpose.
- Route findings **to counsel**, not to a distribution list.
- Mark documents appropriately: *Privileged and Confidential — Attorney-Client Communication — Prepared at the Direction of Counsel.*
- Tell interviewees the purpose. Privilege can fail if employees do not understand they are speaking with the organization's lawyers, through you, for legal advice.
- Understand that **the privilege belongs to Eventus, not to you.** The company can waive it. It is also not a shield for the underlying facts — the facts are always discoverable. It protects the *analysis and the communication*, which is exactly what you want protected.

Do not skip this because it feels bureaucratic. The gap between a privileged assessment and an unprivileged one is the difference between a fixable problem and an exhibit.

### 1.2 Decide what you are actually looking for

Four distinct questions, easily conflated:

1. **What exists on paper?** Policies, charters, committee descriptions.
2. **What actually happens?** The two are frequently unrelated.
3. **What legal obligations attach to what actually happens?** You may already be a "health care entity with formal peer review" whether or not anyone said so.
4. **What has already happened?** Historical actions, reports made and not made.

Question 4 is the one people avoid and the one that matters most. A forward-looking program built on an unexamined history is a program with a fault line running under it.

### 1.3 Frame it correctly to the organization

You are new in the role. If this reads as an audit, people will manage their answers.

The honest and effective frame: *I am building the national program and I need to know what already exists so I am not duplicating work or breaking something that functions.* That is true. It also gets far better information than "I am checking whether we have been compliant."

Do not misrepresent the legal dimension if asked directly. Say that counsel is involved because peer review carries reporting and confidentiality obligations you need to get right. That is also true and nobody finds it surprising.

## Part 2 — Who to talk to, in what order

Sequence matters. Talk to counsel first so the privilege structure is in place. Talk to the people closest to the actual work before you talk to the people who will describe it in the most favorable terms.

| Order | Who | What they know |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | General counsel / outside health law counsel | Entity structure, prior claims, privilege posture, whether anyone has thought about this |
| 2 | CMO | Intent, history, what he thinks exists, what he wants |
| 3 | Credentialing / medical staff services lead | The most underestimated interview. This person usually knows more about actual practice than anyone. |
| 4 | Quality / compliance lead | Incident reporting, QAPI, existing data, regulatory interface |
| 5 | Regional / state medical directors (3–4 of them) | What actually happens when a clinician has a problem |
| 6 | HR lead | Where clinical performance and employment action currently intersect |
| 7 | Risk management / claims | Claims history, malpractice reporting, what has already gone wrong |
| 8 | Contracting lead | Facility agreements and payer agreements, which hide obligations |
| 9 | IT / EMR lead | What data exists and whether it is extractable |
| 10 | 3–5 frontline clinicians (physicians and NPs) | What people believe the process is. Usually the most revealing. |

**On the regional medical directors:** interview them individually, not as a group, and ask for narratives rather than descriptions. "Tell me about the last time you had a serious concern about a clinician's care. Walk me through what happened." You will learn more from one story than from an hour of process description.

## Part 3 — Document request

Ask for everything by name. People do not volunteer documents they consider irrelevant, and their sense of relevance is not yours.

**Governance and policy**
- Any peer review policy, charter, or committee description, current and historical
- Corporate bylaws or governance documents referencing quality or peer review
- Quality Committee and Peer Review Council charters, if they exist
- Board or executive committee minutes referencing peer review or clinical quality oversight
- Organizational chart showing where quality, credentialing, compliance, and HR sit

**Credentialing**
- Credentialing policy and application packet
- Recredentialing schedule and criteria
- Delegated credentialing agreements with health plans
- Sample credentialing file (redacted)
- Primary source verification procedures
- NPDB query records and registration documentation

**Clinical performance**
- Any performance dashboard, scorecard, or metric set applied to clinicians
- Onboarding evaluation process for new clinicians (this is functionally an FPPE)
- Annual review templates used for clinicians
- Any existing case review forms or rubrics

**Incident and event**
- Incident reporting policy and system description
- Sample incident reports (redacted)
- RCA policy and any completed RCAs
- Complaint handling policy — patient, family, and facility

**Employment**
- Clinician employment agreement template
- Contractor agreement template
- Progressive discipline policy
- Termination policy and recent clinician termination records (with counsel)

**Contracts**
- Template facility agreement (LTC and ALF)
- Any facility agreement with unusual quality, reporting, or indemnification provisions
- Payer agreements, particularly Medicare Advantage
- Malpractice policy and carrier requirements

**Regulatory and claims**
- Any state medical board or nursing board correspondence regarding a clinician
- F-tag citations naming an Eventus clinician
- Claims history, open and closed
- Any prior litigation involving a clinician's competence

**Systems**
- EMR data dictionary or available report list
- Any existing quality data extracts

## Part 4 — The questions

Organized by domain. The bracketed notes tell you why the question matters and what a bad answer sounds like.

### 4.1 Threshold legal status

1. Is Eventus registered with the NPDB as a reporting entity? If so, since when, and who is the designated agent?
   *[If nobody knows the answer, that is the answer.]*
2. Has Eventus ever submitted a report to the NPDB? How many, and for what?
3. Has Eventus completed the biennial attestation? Who signed it, and on what basis?
   *[Attesting that all reportable actions have been submitted, without having analyzed whether any were reportable, is its own problem.]*
4. Does Eventus query the NPDB at credentialing and recredentialing? Is Continuous Query enrolled?
5. Has anyone ever obtained a legal opinion on whether Eventus is a "health care entity with formal peer review" under HCQIA?
6. What is the corporate structure? One entity or many? Which entity employs clinicians in each state?
   *[This determines which state's privilege statute applies, and whether the entity is the kind of entity a given statute covers.]*
7. Is Eventus licensed or otherwise regulated as a health care provider in each state where it operates, or does it operate purely as an employer of licensed individuals?
   *[This is the* Reginelli *question. It is the single most likely place your privilege fails.]*
8. Has anyone analyzed peer review privilege in the states where we operate?
9. Do we contract with a Patient Safety Organization? Has it ever been considered?
10. Has any peer review or quality material ever been produced in litigation? What happened?

### 4.2 What actually happens now

11. When a clinician has a concerning outcome, what happens? Walk me through the last three instances.
12. Who decides that a case warrants review?
13. Who does the reviewing? One person or a group? Same specialty or not?
14. Is there a written finding? Where does it live? Who can see it?
15. Is the clinician told? Before or after the conclusion? In writing?
16. Does the clinician have any opportunity to respond or provide context?
    *[This is HCQIA standard 2 — reasonable effort to obtain the facts. A review conducted without ever speaking to the clinician fails it.]*
17. What is the range of outcomes? What has actually happened to clinicians as a result of review?
18. Has a clinician ever been asked to stop seeing patients, reduce their panel, or step back temporarily while something was looked into?
    *[The* Poliner *question. Ask it directly and ask it more than once, in different words. This is the most likely unreported action and the most likely informal exposure. Ask specifically about arrangements described as "voluntary," "a break," "administrative leave," or "reassignment."]*
19. Has a clinician ever resigned while a concern was pending?
    *[Reportable if it occurred while under investigation. Get dates.]*
20. Has a clinician ever been terminated for clinical performance reasons? How many, and when?
21. How long does the process take from event to resolution?
22. Is anything tracked in aggregate? Has anyone ever asked what the review process has found across cases?

### 4.3 The documentary record

23. Where are peer review documents stored? Who has access?
24. Are peer review materials ever sent by email? To whom?
    *[Nearly always yes. Nearly always a waiver problem.]*
25. Do peer review documents carry a confidentiality legend? What does it say?
26. Are peer review files separate from credentialing files and HR files? Physically or only nominally?
27. What is the retention schedule? Has anything ever been destroyed?
    *[Ask carefully. Destruction after litigation is foreseeable is spoliation.]*
28. Do minutes exist? Do they record deliberation or only conclusions? May I see a sample?
29. Has any peer review finding ever been shared with a facility, a payer, or a third party?
    *[Facility sharing is common in this model and is a serious waiver risk.]*

### 4.4 Credentialing interface

30. What is the credentialing process at hire? What is verified?
31. Is there a formal evaluation period for new clinicians? What does it measure and who reviews it?
    *[If yes, you already have something FPPE-shaped, which is useful and also means obligations may already attach.]*
32. What triggers recredentialing? What information is reviewed?
33. Is there any ongoing performance monitoring between credentialing cycles?
    *[This is the OPPE-shaped question. Most groups have nothing here.]*
34. Have we ever declined to credential or recredential someone for competence reasons?
    *[Potentially reportable.]*
35. Do we hold delegated credentialing arrangements with any health plan? What does the plan require, and when were we last audited?
    *[NCQA delegated credentialing obligations are real, contractual, and audited. Groups frequently forget they signed up for them.]*

### 4.5 Employment interface

36. Who decides employment consequences for a clinical performance problem — the same people who assess the care, or different people?
37. Does the clinician employment agreement reference peer review, due process, or a hearing right?
    *[If it promises a process, failing to provide it is breach of contract regardless of HCQIA.]*
38. Does the agreement contain an arbitration clause? What does it cover?
39. Has a clinician ever sued or threatened to sue over a performance action?
40. Are performance concerns documented in the HR file, the peer review file, or both?
41. How do we handle a clinician who raises a safety or billing concern and subsequently has a performance issue identified?
    *[There should be a deliberate answer. There usually is not. This is the retaliation fact pattern.]*

### 4.6 Facility and payer obligations

42. Do our facility agreements require us to conduct peer review, or to report findings to the facility?
43. Do facilities have any contractual right of access to our clinical quality information?
    *[If yes, this may be a standing waiver. Flag immediately.]*
44. Can a facility demand removal of a clinician? What happens then, and is it documented as a clinical action or a business one?
    *[Facility-demanded removals are extremely common in this model and sit right on the reportability line. If it is characterized as a professional review action based on competence, it may be reportable. If it is a business decision, it is not. The characterization must be honest and must be made deliberately, not reverse-engineered.]*
45. Do payer agreements impose quality oversight, reporting, or credentialing obligations?
46. What does our malpractice carrier require regarding peer review, and does it offer any support or resources?

### 4.7 Data and systems

47. What clinical data can we actually extract by clinician? Visits, diagnoses, prescribing, transfers, deaths?
48. Do we have facility-level outcome data, and can it be attributed to individual clinicians?
49. Is there any existing dashboard? Who sees it? Is it used for anything?
50. Can we identify the population denominator for each clinician reliably?
    *[Without this, no rate-based measure is interpretable.]*
51. Does the EMR capture goals-of-care and advance care planning documentation in a retrievable way?
    *[Directly relevant to whether your reviewers will be able to assess cases fairly.]*

### 4.8 Culture

Ask these of frontline clinicians, not leadership.

52. If a colleague's care concerned you, what would you do?
53. Do you know whether your own care has ever been reviewed?
54. If your case were reviewed, what do you think would happen?
55. Has anyone ever asked you for input on a colleague's case?
56. Do you think the organization's response to clinical problems is fair?
57. Would you volunteer a case of your own for review?
    *[The answer to 57 tells you almost everything about whether a real program is possible in the next year.]*

## Part 5 — Interpreting what you find

### 5.1 A maturity scale

Score each domain. This is for your own orientation, not for a report.

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| 0 | Nothing exists. Concerns handled ad hoc by whoever hears about them. |
| 1 | Informal practice, no written policy. Individual medical directors handle problems personally. |
| 2 | Written policy exists but is not followed, or is followed inconsistently by region. |
| 3 | Consistent process with defined triggers, documentation, and outcomes. No measurement of the process itself. |
| 4 | Consistent, measured, calibrated, with aggregate learning fed back into education and system change. |

Domains: legal compliance (NPDB, privilege) · governance · case identification · review methodology · documentation discipline · escalation and response · credentialing interface · data infrastructure · culture.

**Realistic expectation:** most provider groups of this type score 1 in most domains, with credentialing at 2 or 3 because payer requirements forced it. Level 1 with active informal actions is the highest-risk state, because obligations attach to what you do, not to what you call it.

### 5.2 Red flags — findings that require immediate escalation to counsel

- Formal peer review has occurred and Eventus is not NPDB-registered
- Any clinician has been restricted, suspended, or has resigned during a pending concern, and no reportability analysis was done
- The biennial attestation has been signed without an underlying analysis
- Facility agreements grant access to clinical quality findings
- Peer review findings live in HR files, or the same people make both determinations
- Peer review material has circulated by ordinary email or to regional leadership
- No confidentiality legend on any peer review document
- A clinician has been reviewed without ever being interviewed
- Documents have been destroyed after a claim was made or threatened
- Any "voluntary" arrangement where a clinician stopped seeing patients under pressure

### 5.3 Findings that are good news and often overlooked

- An existing incident reporting system, even a poor one, gives you a case identification pipeline
- Credentialing infrastructure driven by payer requirements is often more mature than anything else and can be extended
- An onboarding evaluation process is a functioning FPPE analogue
- A quality committee with a charter gives you a governance parent rather than requiring you to build one
- Existing facility-level QAPI participation gives you a system-factor evidence base

## Part 6 — Writing it up

### 6.1 Produce two documents, not one

**Document A — the privileged assessment.** Addressed to counsel, marked privileged, prepared at counsel's direction. This is where the historical findings go: what has happened, what may not have been reported, where the exposure sits, what remediation is required. Narrow distribution. This document is not a policy document and should never be attached to one.

**Document B — the forward-looking program design.** Not privileged, not historical, no compliance findings. This is what you circulate: the charter, the structure, the triggers, the rubric, the implementation plan. It describes what the program will be, not what it was.

Keeping these separate is not gamesmanship. It is the difference between a design document that can be shared with the people who need to implement it and a document that cannot be shared with anyone.

### 6.2 What Document A should contain

1. Scope and method — who was interviewed, what was reviewed, over what period
2. Current state by domain, with the maturity score
3. Legal gap analysis: NPDB registration and reporting; privilege by state; contractual obligations
4. Historical actions requiring reportability analysis, listed by clinician and date
5. Immediate remediation items with a proposed sequence
6. Open questions requiring legal determination

### 6.3 What to do about a historical reporting gap

Do not decide this yourself and do not decide it quickly. The analysis involves whether each historical action met the reportability criteria, whether late reporting is appropriate, and what the exposure is either way. Late reporting has consequences; not reporting has larger ones. This is a decision for counsel with the CMO and probably the board.

What you can do is assemble the factual record cleanly, with dates, so that the decision is made on complete information.

## Part 7 — Sequencing

**Weeks 1–2** — Counsel engagement and privilege structure. CMO conversation. Document request issued.

**Weeks 3–5** — Interviews. Credentialing lead early. Regional medical directors individually. Frontline clinicians last, once you know what questions matter.

**Weeks 6–7** — Document review. Contract review with counsel. Multi-state privilege analysis initiated (this has the longest lead time; start it in week 2 if possible).

**Week 8** — Document A drafted and delivered to counsel.

**Weeks 9–12** — Remediation decisions with counsel and CMO. Document B drafted in parallel.

The multi-state privilege analysis and the NPDB historical review are the two long poles. Everything else can move faster than they can. Start both before you finish interviewing.

---

## Part 8 — Questions to hold in reserve

Some questions are better asked once you have credibility and a functioning program, because asking them early invites defensiveness and produces managed answers.

- Why was peer review not built before now?
- Who has resisted it, and on what grounds?
- Which regions would be hardest to bring into a national process?
- Are there clinicians everyone knows about?

That last one is worth naming to yourself even if you never ask it aloud. In every organization that has operated without formal peer review, there are one or two clinicians whose problems are widely known and have never been addressed. The informal system tolerated them; that is what informal systems do. Your program will surface them, probably within the first year, and the organization's response to the first one will determine whether anyone believes in the program afterward.

Think about that case before it arrives, not after.
