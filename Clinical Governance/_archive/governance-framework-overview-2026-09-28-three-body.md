# Clinical Governance Framework — Overview

*Draft, for discussion. Not adopted.*

---

The framework creates three connected clinical governance bodies: the Clinical Quality Committee, the Peer Review Committee, and the Clinical Governance Council. Together they define good care, measure whether it is being delivered, address individual clinical concerns fairly, and take formal action when necessary — while keeping quality improvement, peer review, employment decisions, and operational management separate.

## The three bodies

**Clinical Quality Committee — define, teach, measure.** The CQC sets evidence-based clinical standards suited to the long-term care and assisted living population, builds the education supporting them, and audits performance against them. It distinguishes minimum expectations from aspirational improvement targets, and **validates observations before any becomes a finding against an individual** — clinicians get an opportunity to show that a resident wasn't theirs, wasn't in the population the standard applies to, or that the note exists somewhere the search didn't look.

**Most findings are never referred anywhere.** A validated finding ordinarily produces a written close-out to the clinician, an offer of support that carries no obligation and is not recorded either way, and a further look at a sample of charts after a defined period. Where a predefined referral criterion is met, the matter goes to the PRC and the CQC's involvement ends. The CQC does not determine competence, discipline anyone, or decide whether care met the legal or professional standard of care.

**Peer Review Committee — review individual clinical care.** The PRC conducts confidential professional review where concern arises about the care furnished by an identified clinician. Matters reach it from the CQC, from clinicians, management, facilities, patients or families, from adverse events, external actions, or any other source. It determines independently whether care met the standard reasonably expected of a practitioner in that discipline and setting; **a CQC finding is evidence, not a PRC finding.**

Its dispositions are proportionate by design: close with no adverse finding; record a finding with no intervention; **agree an educational plan that closes the matter with no finding and nothing reportable**; provide feedback, education, mentoring or retrospective focused review; or recommend a restriction on Clinical Authorization to the Council. Members of the Committee review the cases, two to a case, and the Committee decides; no one holding employment authority sits on it.

**Clinical Governance Council — govern, decide, protect due process.** The Council is the body to which the Board delegates clinical governance authority, and **the Board may revoke that delegation.** It charters the two committees, ratifies the Audit Referral Policy, receives the CQC's annual audit plan and checks that it answers the enterprise's clinical priorities, maintains the Clinical Authorization framework, receives recommendations from the PRC, constitutes independent hearing panels, takes final Professional Review Actions, and determines reporting. Nine voting members: the two department chairs and five elected practising clinicians, who alone vote on any action concerning an identified clinician, and the Chief Medical Officer and Chief Operating Officer, who sit for governance business and withdraw from the rest. The Chair of Clinical Excellence, who chairs both committees, sits without a vote and presents what they send up.

## How it works

Define the standard → educate → audit → validate → fix the system, or refer an individual concern → independent peer review → proportionate response → formal Council action and a hearing only where clinical authority may be restricted.

**The audit is not the only route in, and it is not the most important one.** Care raising a patient-safety or standard-of-care concern can be referred directly, by anyone, with no threshold and no written standard required. The reverse also holds: failing an internal CQC standard does not establish substandard care. The PRC starts fresh and decides that question itself.

## What management keeps

**All employment authority is retained** — hiring, termination, discipline, compensation, contracting, scheduling, assignment, and medical director nomination are untouched, and the charters say so expressly. **The committees cannot see compensation, productivity, revenue or cost, and may not consider them**, which is the primary defense against any later claim that a clinical decision was financially motivated. Management receives notice of every final action and its operational effect.

## The reporting obligation

Defining clinical authorization creates a duty the organization does not have today: a restriction lasting longer than thirty days becomes reportable to the National Practitioner Data Bank.

**That duty and the legal protections arise from the same status and are not separable.** An organization that declines the obligation declines the protection with it, and goes on acting on clinician performance without either. Reports are also infrequent by design — the great majority of committee activity is education and oversight, which is not reportable.

## What the structure solves

It closes the quality loop rather than leaving quality to individual judgment exercised case by case.

It creates **intermediate responses** — feedback, education, mentoring, focused review, mandatory consultation, proctoring — between doing nothing and ending a clinician's relationship with the organization. Where those two extremes are the only options available, the first is the usual outcome, and the second is the action least protected by federal peer review immunity and most likely to be litigated.

It creates **procedural credibility.** The people who measure quality are not the people who judge competence. The people who conduct peer review do not make final restrictions. Clinicians holding employment authority do not decide adverse actions. Contested actions receive an independent hearing.

And it creates deliberate **information barriers** between quality improvement, peer review, Council decision-making, employment records, facility QAPI, and patient-safety work product. That separation is what keeps clinical quality work from becoming either an informal disciplinary process or an indiscriminate record of organizational problems.

## What is not yet settled

Two things are open by design and will be decided before adoption: the **Audit Referral Policy**, which sets the criteria by which an audit finding reaches peer review, and **indemnification** for those who serve on these bodies.

Matters raising a clinician's health, impairment or fitness to practise are **referred to HR**, which handles them under its own policies. Peer review does not investigate, adjudicate or manage such a matter, and neither receives nor requests any information about a Practitioner's health, evaluation, treatment or accommodation. What it keeps is the safety question: whether the Practitioner is still furnishing care while the matter is unresolved. The Council makes the designation by resolution rather than the Charter naming HR, so that a change to the receiving policy needs no amendment here.
