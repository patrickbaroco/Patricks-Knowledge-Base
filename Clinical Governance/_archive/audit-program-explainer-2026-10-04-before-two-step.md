# How a Quality Audit Would Work

*Draft, for discussion. None of this is in place yet — the committee that would run it hasn't been formed.*

---

## The short version

A committee checks charts against a small number of written standards. When something looks off, you hear about it, you get a chance to show the audit got it wrong, and most of the time that's where it ends.

**The committee running the audit can't do anything to anybody.** It can't discipline you, restrict your practice, or make you do anything. It writes standards, measures against them, fixes what’s broken, offers you education and looks at your charts again a few months later, and where a written rule is met it hands the matter to a different committee and stops.

---

## What a standard looks like

These four are illustrations. **The committee doesn't exist yet and will pick its own.** They're here to show the shape — every standard is tied to a specific problem and only applies to residents who have it. 

**Urine cultures.** If you order one, your note says what symptom prompted it. Confusion alone doesn't count. Cloudy or smelly urine alone doesn't count.

**Antipsychotics in dementia.** If a resident with dementia is on one, your note says what behavior you're treating, what was tried first, and whether lowering the dose has been considered.

**Sliding scale insulin.** If someone's still on sliding scale more than 30 days after they arrive, your note says why for that resident. **Inside 30 days the standard doesn't apply at all** — that's the window for figuring out their intake, and nobody needs to write anything about it.

**Anticoagulation in afib.** Your note accounts for it: they're on it, or they're not and here's the reason, or cardiology made the call, or it's on hold and here's what ends the hold.

That last one trips people up, so: *"held per cardiology"* doesn't meet the standard. *"Held per cardiology until after the epidural injection"* does. **You're not being asked to justify cardiology's decision or repeat their thinking** — just to show you knew where the issue stood, so the next person reading the chart does too.

## Following one through

### Nothing counts right away

The standard goes out with an example of a note that meets it. **Then 60 days pass before any of your care can be measured against it.** What you did in those 60 days doesn't count, even if the audit runs much later.

### How your charts got picked

**Usually there's no reason beyond the calendar.** Most audits are routine — the committee picks a measure for the cycle and reads charts across everyone it applies to. If you have residents with afib and afib is this quarter's measure, your charts get read. That's the whole story.

Sometimes something narrows it instead. A facility looks unusual on a measure, or a number stands out, and that decides where to start.

**Either way, being looked at says nothing about you.** It isn't a finding, it isn't a suspicion, and it never gets written down as though it were.

### Four charts get flagged

Four residents with afib, not on anticoagulation, nothing in your note about it.

**Nothing has happened yet.** No finding, no conclusion, nothing in a file anywhere.

### You get a letter and 10 business days

It lists each one — resident, date, what the standard wanted, what the audit found. Then it asks what the audit got wrong, and tells you up front what will and won't work.

**These clear a flag:**

- **It wasn't your patient.** You were covering, or cardiology manages it.
- **The resident doesn't have the condition.** Wrong code, an old echo, resolved after ablation. Or on the insulin standard — they got here three weeks ago.
- **It didn't happen.** You did order it, and there's an order to prove it.
- **You wrote it and the search missed it.** Your note says *"held per cardiology pending EGD 9/12"* and the tool was looking for a structured field. Say where to look. **This is the most common correction there is.**
- **The timing's wrong.** That care predates the date the standard started counting.

**These don't:**

- **A reason that's only in someone else's note.** If cardiology stopped it and your note doesn't mention it, the flag stands. But *"anticoagulation stopped by cardiology, prior GI bleed"* is all it takes — that's a sentence, not a justification.
- **Explaining it now.** If you thought about it and didn't write it down, the flag stands. Say so anyway — it gets recorded and it travels with the file.
- **Fixing the chart now.** Won't change this round. **But if you did the work and the chart doesn't show it, fix it** — you owe that to the resident either way. It counts from the next round on.

### Most of it goes away

Two of your four come off — one resident's afib resolved and was never re-coded, one is cardiology's patient outright.

This happens a lot. On a urine culture audit, many flags come back *"I didn't order that"* — nursing sent it under a standing protocol. **Which is the finding.** Two facilities have a protocol that manufactures the problem, that gets fixed, and nothing attaches to any clinician. That's worth more than any individual flag it produced.

### What's left goes one of three ways

- **Withdrawn.** The audit was wrong.
- **A systems problem.** The template has nowhere to put it, the standard's unclear, nobody was taught it properly. **Comes off your record completely** and goes to fixing the thing.
- **A confirmed gap.** The standard applied, your note didn't meet it.

**A confirmed documentation gap doesn't get referred to anyone.** Not to peer review, not to your manager, not to the facility, not into any employment file. It stays in the audit record, you get told about it, and what follows is the letter and the look described below.

It isn't nothing, though. If the follow-up finds the same gap again, **that second one is what the referral rules are actually built around** — which is why the letter tells you exactly what was wrong and gives you time before anything gets measured again.

### A second letter

What was found, what came off, what stood, and where to find the standard and an example.

**An offer of help.** Everyone who gets one of these gets the same offer — a conversation with someone who does this work, a short session, whatever's useful. Ask and it's set up. Don't and nothing happens. **Nobody records who takes it up or counts it anywhere.**

It also says plainly that the standard still applies and the items that stood still stand. The offer is help with it, not a pass on it.

**You can bring in your supervising physician, or not.** You can share the letter with them, or ask for help without sharing it. For that, the letter includes a separate page you can forward: a note asking for help with the clinical area ("I'd like to work on diabetes management"), with the review guide and none of your results. You decide; the committee sends nothing and never knows which you chose. And you can always ask your supervising physician for help, audit or no audit.

**And exactly when the next look is.** Nothing you do for the next six weeks is part of it — that's your time to use the offer and get the change in place. Then a sample of your charts covering the next three months.

### Three months later

Small sample, same standard. **Clear, and you're done** — back in the general pool, nobody looks again. Not clear, and you'll hear that too, and this time it matters that you were told and given time.

## Who sees this

**Not your supervisor.** Individual audit findings don't go to anyone with authority over your job — not your regional CMO, not operations, not into a personnel file. The committee itself can't see your compensation, productivity, or revenue, and isn't permitted to consider them.

That's a change from how coding audits work here, and it's deliberate. Those are billing and administrative compliance. They aren't privileged, supervisors should keep seeing them, and nothing here touches any of that.

Findings about your **care** run on different rules, because the legal protection this whole structure depends on comes apart if they circulate outside the committee.

**Your supervising physician** gets the same review guide the audit uses, and is expected to use it when reviewing your charts as part of ordinary supervision. That review is theirs, not the committee's: the committee doesn't choose which charts they read or see what they find. And if your supervising physician sits on the committee, they step out of any discussion about you.

**Results are reported as totals.** Each audit's results go to the CMO and COO for the whole company and for each RDCOE territory, physicians and APCs together, and they decide how to share them. No clinician can be picked out: a territory too small to hide anyone isn't reported.

**What supervisors do see:** everything administrative, exactly as now. Notice of any final action and what it means operationally. And systems findings — with no names on them — because a broken protocol is theirs to fix.

## The other way this can go

Everyone doing the review is a clinician. Sometimes what's on the page doesn't look like a documentation problem. It looks like residents aren't getting something they need.

**The chair asks that out loud, every time, before anything gets decided.** When the answer is yes, that person refers it themselves to the peer review committee, on patient safety grounds. The quality committee doesn't make that referral and takes no position on it.

**Anyone can do that, any time, about anything.** No audit, no threshold, no waiting. That route matters more than the audit does — an audit finds what it was pointed at, and a colleague finds what's actually there.

---

## If it does reach peer review

Screening happens in 10 business days, and plenty of things end right there.

If a review opens and it looks like a gap in one area with nothing else in question, the committee can offer a plan instead of going to a finding — a session, a look at the relevant charts with someone who does that work, done inside a set period.

**Take it and the matter closes with no finding, no action against you, nothing reportable.** Someone confirms you did it. **Nobody grades you.** 

Turn it down and you get the full review with every right that comes with it.

---

## Things that can't happen

- The audit committee restricting your practice. It has no such power, ever.
- Anything about your pay, schedule, assignment, or contract. It can't see that information and can't act on it.
- A finding built out of a rate or a number. Only a chart, read against a standard.
- Anything counting before you got the standard and had time.
- Getting looked at over and over. The follow-up runs once, then it's finished.
