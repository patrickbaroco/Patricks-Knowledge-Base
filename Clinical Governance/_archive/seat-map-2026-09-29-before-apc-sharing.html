Clinical governance framework · draft for discussion

Who sits where, and what each body does

Two chartered committees and two clinical departments. This page shows the seats on each, the work each one owns, and how the primary care and psychiatry committees feed the Quality Committee without ever touching peer review.

Seats are shown by role, not by name — nobody has been appointed or elected yet.

Not adopted. Charters drafted, under revision, prepared for counsel review.

How the four bodies connect

The CEO's written Authorization establishes both committees, and the CEO takes no part in any case. Quality and Peer Review are co-equal — neither reports to the other.

Body one

Clinical Quality Committee

Defines what good care looks like here, builds the education that teaches it, and measures whether it is being delivered.

Who sits on it

Chair of Clinical Excellence — chairs it ex officio, untermed
Chair of the Primary Care Committee
Chair of the Psychiatry Committee

Both sit as members by office.

Members appointed by the Chairopen

Physicians and APCs. Two-year staggered terms, renewable. No fixed number is set in the charter; four practising clinicians keeps the floor below met with margin. A licensed psychotherapist is consulted on any psychotherapy standard rather than seated, until the peer review addendum for that discipline exists.

At the table, not members

Analytics; learning & development; enterprise priorities — the ACO and clinical operations view, which the committee must answer in its annual audit plan; and the Executive Coordinator, who is custodian of the records and does not vote.

At least two-thirds of members must be licensed clinicians in active practice — the statutory floor in Indiana and North Carolina is half, and the charter says it may never be amended below that.
At most one member may hold employment authority. That member takes full part in standards, education and systems work — and withdraws before any finding attributed to a named clinician is discussed.
No finding is attributed to a named clinician with fewer than three eligible members present. Where fewer are in the room, it waits.

What it does

  1. Adopts the standards. Each one is designated at adoption as either a minimum expectation — which an individual can be held to — or an improvement target, which can never support a referral. The vote is express; there is no default.
  2. Sets the education agenda that teaches each standard, and what conforming documentation looks like, before anything is measured.
  3. Owns the audit. Every audit question maps to a standard in effect. The instrument is validated against clinician review before it can produce anything about an individual.
  4. Checks with the clinician first. An observation is not a finding until the clinician has had the chance to show the resident wasn't theirs, wasn't in the population, or that the note exists where the search didn't look.
  5. Closes most findings out. A written close-out, an offer of support that is never recorded or counted either way, and one further look at that clinician's charts about 135 days later.
  6. Routes systems conditions to whoever can act on them — and reports at the quarterly meeting with the CMO and COO whether they were acted on.
  7. Refers an individual concern to Peer Review only where a criterion in the Audit Referral Policy is met. Quality adopts the Policy, and it takes effect only on Peer Review's concurrence. Quality's involvement then ends completely.

What it cannot do

It is not a professional review body. It makes no finding about competence, does not decide whether care met the standard of care, and imposes no consequence of any kind on any individual, at any point.

Why the audit isn't a disciplinary instrument. Failing an internal standard does not establish substandard care. If a criterion would refer a large share of the clinicians it applies to, that is treated as a systems problem — the standard asks the wrong thing, the audit question doesn't measure it, or it was never properly taught — and it routes to revision or education, never to mass referral.

Body two

Peer Review Committee

Reviews the care an identified clinician furnished, decides whether it met the standard, and chooses a response proportionate to what it finds.

Who sits on it — 6 voting seats, and a non-voting Chair

Chair of Clinical Excellence — ex officio, untermed, presides without a vote

Not counted for quorum or any voting threshold. After each decision the Chair confirms in writing that the process was followed, and signs. Where the Chair cannot confirm it, the matter goes back to the Committee to be cured and decided again, and the Chair cannot substitute a result.

Members appointed by the Chair6

All licensed clinicians in active practice. Two-year staggered terms. A vice-chair is elected annually from among them and presides whenever the Chair is recused or absent, standing in the Chair's place without a vote in that matter.

Department chair for the clinician's service line — screens only, without a vote

Screens each referral with the Chair, acting for this committee and not a member of it. A member of the clinician's discipline screens instead where the department chair took part in the Quality Committee's decision on an audit referral. Nothing learned at screening goes back to the department, and every member comes to an opened review without having seen the referral.

Reviewer Pool — optional, and may be empty

Members review. Each case is reviewed by two members, three where the Chair says why. The pool is reached only where discipline matching cannot be met from the membership, and an outside reviewer sits behind both. Pool membership is not a committee seat.

Standing membership includes at least three physicians, two APCs, and one clinician with psychiatric training.
No one holding employment authority may serve — not on the committee, not in the pool. Supervisors may refer a matter and be interviewed; they do not review, deliberate, or vote. The exclusion is categorical, with no exception for a supervisor who happens to be the best-qualified reviewer available.
A vote to propose or take a professional review action needs a majority of all members not recused, and is not taken with fewer than four. Where recusals go deeper, the Chair appoints a Pool member or a Pool-eligible clinician for that case alone, and the clinician may object.

What it does

  1. Screens every referral within ten business days, the Chair with the department chair for the clinician's service line — deciding the route, never the merits. Matters arrive from the audit, from any clinician or employee, from management, facilities, patients and families, adverse events, or an external action.
  2. Opens a review and assigns two discipline-matched members to it. They investigate and propose; the committee decides.
  3. Closes with no finding where the care met standard, and notifies the clinician in writing that the review closed without an adverse finding.
  4. Agrees an educational resolution for a bounded knowledge gap: no finding, nothing reportable, and it may be refused with no consequence. What was learned is confirmed as done, never graded; the matter closes when the plan is completed, and the care that follows is looked at twice over the next two quarters.
  5. Imposes Tier One directly — written feedback, education, mentoring, or retrospective focused review. None of these restricts anyone's authority to practise and none is reportable. Each states its duration and its exit criteria in writing on the day it is imposed.
  6. Proposes Tier Two — mandatory consultation, proctoring, a scope restriction, non-renewal, or termination of clinical authorization. The clinician receives written notice, the material relied on, and the right to a hearing before any of these becomes final.
  7. Holds the hearing within peer review. A panel of at least three clinicians who are not members of the Committee, and took no part in the review, the audit, or the decision to propose, hears the matter and recommends. The Committee accepts the recommendation unless it is unsupported by the hearing record, the hearing had a material procedural defect, or it rests on an error about authority or the standard.
  8. Takes final action. Its signed decision is the company's action, and no officer, including the CEO, can review it. It determines reportability with counsel and causes reports to be filed with the NPDB and state licensing boards.
  9. Restricts precautionarily where there is imminent danger. The Chair and two members impose it, and it lapses at five business days unless members who did not join in imposing it ratify it.
  10. Restricts for up to 14 days while it investigates, for a concern short of imminent danger, on the same authority of the Chair and two members. The restriction ends on its own, is never extended or repeated on the same facts, and rests on no finding.
  11. Routes what isn't its subject — systems conditions to Quality, and any question of a clinician's health, impairment or fitness to the practitioner health pathway the CEO designates, which handles it under its own policies. Peer review keeps only the safety question: whether the clinician is still furnishing care while the matter is unresolved.

What it cannot do

It cannot make a restriction final without notice and the opportunity for a hearing. It cannot make, recommend, or advise on employment, compensation, contracting, scheduling, facility assignment or medical director designation. It may not receive or consider a clinician's productivity, revenue or cost. Management receives notice of a final action, stating what it is, when it takes effect and how long it lasts, and never the findings, the evidence, or the deliberations.

Most of what this committee does is not reportable. Nothing in Tier One restricts anyone's authority to practise, and the agreed educational resolution closes with no finding when the plan is completed, with the care looked at twice over the next two quarters. Most people's experience of the committee is designed to be: this doesn't have to be a big deal, do this education, we'll follow up, and we're done. What is different from the conversation that happens today is that there is now a but if you don't at the end of it.

The two departments

Primary Care and Psychiatry

Each owns the priorities and measures for its own discipline. Neither is chartered, and neither takes any part in peer review — which is what lets them stay unchartered.

Who sits on them, and where they connect

A chair for each — who also sits on the Quality Committee

This is the join. The department chairs are the same people in both rooms, which is how a discipline's priorities travel without any record travelling with them. Each chair also screens peer review referrals for their service line, in the Peer Review Committee's name; nothing from screening comes back to the department.

Practising clinicians of that discipline

The reason for a department layer with only two disciplines is participation: it brings the broader clinical community into the decisions instead of leaving them to a handful of senior roles.

They contribute to standards development and the education agenda, on material circulated to them for comment. That material is open — not privileged, and not treated as if it were.

What they never receive

The boundary CQC § 7.1
  • The Quality Committee's internal deliberative material — the protected half of its record.
  • Any observation or finding attributed to a named clinician. Not in aggregate with names attached, not informally, not at all.
  • Any part in a disposition — the departments are not in the room when a finding is closed out or referred.
  • Any part in peer review, in any form, at any stage. The chair's screening is done for the Peer Review Committee, and none of it reaches the department.

Why the boundary has to be written down. Privilege breaks at the edge of an unchartered body. Because the departments touch only the open, circulated half of Quality's output, they need no charter of their own — but that boundary has to be written down, which is what § 7.1 does.
