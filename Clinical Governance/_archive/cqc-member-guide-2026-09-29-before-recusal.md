# The Clinical Quality Committee — a guide for members

*A plain-language summary of the Committee's Charter, written for someone joining the Committee. It is a summary and nothing more: where it and the Charter differ, the Charter governs. Section numbers in parentheses show where to find the full text.*

---

## What the Committee is for, in one paragraph

The Clinical Quality Committee decides what good care looks like, writes it down, makes sure people are taught it, and then measures whether it is happening. Where the measurement turns up a problem in the care one clinician gave one resident, the Committee checks that with the clinician first, records what it found, offers education, and looks again at a sample of that clinician’s charts after a set period. Only where the finding meets a written referral rule does it pass the matter to the Peer Review Committee. Then it stops.

## The boundary

**The Committee has no authority over any individual, at any point** (§§ 1.3, 6.5). It does not decide whether a clinician is competent, does not decide whether care fell below the standard of care, and imposes nothing on anyone — no requirement, no monitoring, no remediation, no consequence of any kind. Those questions belong to the Peer Review Committee, which is a separate body with its own process, its own record, and its own charter.

Almost everything else in the Charter is short because of this. A committee that could sanction people would need appeal rights, evidentiary rules and hearings; this one refers and stops, so it does not.

Two related boundaries:

- **The Committee is not part of any facility's own quality program** (§ 1.6). Facilities run their own quality assurance and performance improvement programs under federal regulation. This Committee's work is organizational, sits outside those programs, and does not take their material into its files.
- **The Committee is not a patient-safety reporting operation** (§ 1.5). Setting standards and teaching them looks forward; the audit looks back at care already given. Neither is incident reporting.

## Who is on it

| | |
|---|---|
| **Chair** | The Chair of Clinical Excellence, by virtue of the office (§ 2.1). |
| **Members** | Appointed by the Chair, two-year terms, staggered and renewable. The chairs of the Primary Care Committee and the Psychiatry Committee sit as members (§ 2.2). |
| **Clinician floor** | At least two-thirds of members must be licensed clinicians in active practice (§ 2.3). |
| **Disciplines** | Physicians and APCs (nurse practitioners and physician assistants, who are peers). A licensed psychotherapist is consulted before any psychotherapy standard is adopted, and a seat for that discipline waits on the PRC's psychotherapist addendum (§ 2.4). |
| **Not members, but present** | Analytics staff, who track performance and report whether interventions moved the numbers (§ 2.6); learning-and-development staff, who deliver the education (§ 2.8); whoever the organization designates to bring its enterprise clinical priorities, including the ACO's and clinical operations' (§ 2.6.1); and the Executive Coordinator, Clinical Excellence, who keeps the records and sends the notices (§ 2.7). |

**One recusal rule.** At most one member may be someone who can hire, fire, discipline, set someone's pay, evaluate someone for pay, or direct their assignments. That member takes full part in standards, education and systems work — and takes no part in any decision about an identified clinician (§ 2.5). That member leaves before such a matter is discussed, and the minutes record when. The Peer Review Committee does not seat such a person at all.

---

## The four things the Committee does

1. **Adopts clinical standards** (Article IV).
2. **Sets the education agenda** that supports them (§ 3.1(b)).
3. **Runs the quality audit** against them (Article V).
4. **Refers individual findings to peer review** where a written rule is met (Article VI).

It also receives systems problems and de-identified summaries back from the Peer Review Committee (§ 7.2), and passes systems problems on to whoever can actually fix them (§ 7.5). It records commendations of individual charts and recognizes facilities and teams (§ 3.1(g)). The Chair reports its work quarterly to the Chief Medical Officer and the Chief Operating Officer, and the Chief Executive Officer receives the same report; no clinician is identifiable in it (§ 7.4).

## Standards: how they get written and adopted

**Where a standard comes from.** Either from published guidance by a recognized professional body, adapted where our population requires it, or — where no applicable guidance exists — from the best available evidence, with the reasoning written into the standard (§§ 4.1, 4.2).

The Committee does not adopt a society guideline wholesale. A guideline carries many recommendations, a good number of them untested in long-term care residents of advanced age with several conditions and limited life expectancy. Adopting one entire would import all of them as expectations of our clinicians.

**A standard becomes a standard only when the Committee votes it in** (§ 4.3), with an effective date, recorded in the standards register. The record of adoption also says what teaching will happen before anyone is measured against it.

### The two labels, and why the vote matters

Every standard is labelled, by express vote, as one of two things (§ 4.4):

- **A minimum expectation** — something the organization expects every clinician to meet. A departure from one of these can eventually support a referral to peer review.
- **An improvement target** — a level the organization is working toward, which may be better than what is strictly required. **A departure from one of these can never support a referral.** It produces information about systems, and nothing else.

There is no default. A standard adopted without a label is live for education and measurement, but cannot support a referral until the Committee labels it. A quality committee will naturally want to set ambitious standards; the label is what keeps ambition from becoming a disciplinary trap. It is decided on the record at adoption, rather than argued about later when someone's name is attached to it.

Note also that labelling something a minimum expectation is a statement of what *this organization expects*. It is not a statement about what the law or the profession requires. That question belongs to peer review, on its own record.

**Goals expressed as a percentage of a population are not standards at all** (§ 4.4) — a target share of residents on a given therapy, a completion rate. They belong to the analytics function and to systems work.

The reason is that a percentage is a property of a panel, not of a decision. Panels differ from one clinician to the next: different facilities, different acuity, different lengths of stay, different referral patterns, different numbers of residents. Two clinicians can each make the right call for every resident in front of them and still land on very different percentages, because they were looking at different residents. So a percentage tells the Committee where to look — it is a signal, and § 5.6 lets a rate decide whose charts get read. It cannot tell the Committee whether any particular decision was right, and no individual meets or fails a population rate. That question is answered by reading the chart.

### Four rules for how a standard is written (§ 4.7)

These are drafting requirements, not advice:

- **(a) Say what must be decided and documented — never what must be prescribed.** Many of our residents are frail and near the end of life, and for a good number of them the right decision is to withhold a therapy a guideline would otherwise indicate. A standard written as a treatment requirement pushes clinicians to treat against the resident's interest. A decision requirement does not.
- **(b) Where a standard covers several elements, each element is documented separately** — the action taken, or its absence with a reason specific to that element. A single line saying the whole subject was considered does not satisfy such a standard.

  The Charter's own illustration is guideline-directed therapy in heart failure with a reduced ejection fraction, which calls for four drug classes: a beta-blocker; an ACE inhibitor, angiotensin receptor blocker, or ARNI; a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist; and an SGLT2 inhibitor. An entry naming each of the four with what was given or why it was not — *"beta-blocker: carvedilol 3.125 mg twice daily; ACE/ARB/ARNI: none, recurrent hyperkalemia; MRA: none, recurrent hyperkalemia; SGLT2: dapagliflozin 10 mg daily"* — satisfies the standard. *"Guideline therapy reviewed, not a candidate"* does not, because it leaves the next clinician unable to tell which of the four were considered or why any of them is absent.
- **(c) A reason must point at something checkable** — a lab value, a documented event, an allergy or intolerance, a documented conversation, a stated goal of care. "Not a candidate" is a conclusion with nothing behind it. "Per cardiology" is the opposite problem: it records who decided, not why.
- **(d) A standard must be answerable from the chart.** If satisfying it needs information that exists nowhere in the record, it cannot be audited and is not adopted.

### One optional extra label (§ 4.7(e))

The Committee may designate a standard so that **the treating clinician's own note has to account for each element** — not justify it, account for it. Three things satisfy that:

- the action taken — *"apixaban 5 mg twice daily"*;
- a settled position — the element is not in place and that is the decision, with a checkable reason, an attribution, or both — *"SGLT2 inhibitor stopped by cardiology, recurrent mycotic infections"*;
- a pending position — not in place for now, **together with what resolves it** — *"anticoagulation on hold per cardiology until after epidural injection by pain management."*

"On hold per cardiology," with nothing about what ends the hold, does not satisfy it. The difference is four words, and they are the four words that separate a plan from a gap.

A clinician who does not know the reason satisfies the standard by saying so and saying what will establish it — *"reason not in the records available to me, will clarify at next visit."* This label is opt-in, requires a stated reason at adoption, and is not the default.

### Notice before any standard counts (§§ 4.5, 5.7)

Every adopted standard is sent to the clinicians it applies to, and the sending is documented. **Only care given sixty days or more after that can count toward a referral.** The clock runs against the care, not the audit: a standard sent on 1 January does not reach care given on 10 January just because the audit ran in April. The Committee can set a longer period, and normally should where the standard asks for a change in practice rather than a change in documentation.

Standards are reviewed at least every three years (§ 4.6).

## The audit: three words used precisely

The Charter uses three terms that are not interchangeable (§ 5.1). Getting these straight is most of understanding Article V:

- **Observation** — an apparent departure spotted in one chart, by the audit tool or by a person. It is unchecked. It says nothing about the quality of care and carries no consequence until the Committee dispositions it.
- **Rate** — aggregate performance across a population. A rate describes a group. It never describes what one clinician did for one resident.
- **Individual finding** — an observation the Committee has checked and confirmed as a departure from a minimum expectation. **Only an individual finding can be referred through the audit route.**

A rate can decide *whose charts get looked at*. It can never be a finding about anyone.

### How a cycle runs

1. **A measure is chosen**, and every audit question maps to an adopted standard in effect (§ 5.3). No standard, no question.
2. **The tool is checked against clinician reading** before its output can support any finding, and checked again whenever it materially changes (§ 5.4). See below.
3. **Charts are selected.** Selection can be crude — a rate, a threshold, an event — because selection is not a finding and carries no consequence (§ 5.6). Whether a clinician's patients are sicker than average is answered by reading the chart, not by building a denominator.
4. **Observations are produced.**
5. **The clinician is notified and has ten business days to correct the record** (§ 5.5(a)). This step is not optional and nothing is dispositioned as an individual matter until it has happened.
6. **Every observation is dispositioned** in one of three ways (§ 5.5(b)).
7. **Everyone notified gets a close-out letter** telling them what happened to every observation (§ 5.5(c)).
8. **A follow-up look happens once**, months later (§ 5.11).

### Step 2 in more detail: checking the tool

Most of the audit is run by software that searches charts for what a standard requires. Before anything that software produces can support a finding about a person, it has to be shown to find what a clinician reading the same chart would find.

In practice that means clinicians read a set of charts themselves, apply the standard by hand, and their answers are compared with the tool's, chart by chart — where they agree, and where they do not, and in which direction. The comparison is written down and kept. Whenever the tool changes in a way that could affect what it retrieves, it is done again.

**This establishes something about the tool, and nothing about any particular chart.** A tool can search correctly across a set of records and still miss what is in one of them — because a diagnosis was miscoded, because the entry sits in a part of the note the search does not read, because it came in as a scanned document, or because the resident's care was attributed to the wrong clinician. That is what step 5 is for, and it is why no observation becomes a finding until the clinician has been asked about it.

The two checks also feed each other. Every ground sustained at step 5 is recorded against the measure (§ 5.5(a)(8)); where attribution, population, or retrieval grounds are sustained often enough on a measure, the tool is checked again before that measure supports any further finding, and where the same ground keeps being sustained across several clinicians, the Charter says plainly that the measure or the tool is the problem rather than the people.

### Step 5 in more detail: the check with the clinician

This is quality control on the audit, not a proceeding. It asks one question — did the audit correctly identify a departure from the standard — and it does not ask whether the care was appropriate. Nobody argues clinical merits in front of this Committee, and the Committee does not listen to them.

**What corrects an observation** (§ 5.5(a)(1)):

- **Attribution** — it wasn't this clinician's patient, or wasn't their decision, or another clinician was managing that condition under a documented arrangement.
- **Population** — the resident doesn't have the condition. The diagnosis is absent, miscoded, or superseded by later testing. (That resident then comes out of the numerator *and* the denominator, and the rate is recomputed.)
- **It didn't happen** — the drug was ordered, the assessment was done, the message was sent, and there is a record showing it.
- **Retrieval** — the clinician's own qualifying entry is in the chart and the tool didn't reach it: scanned in, in a part of the note the tool doesn't read, or in a proper addendum made before notification.
- **Timing** — the care came before the standard could count, or the chart was examined before the entry was due.
- **Something else in the record satisfies the standard as written** — including another clinician's entry, except where the standard carries the "own note" label above.

**What does not correct it** (§ 5.5(a)(4)):

- Reasoning that existed but was never written down. The departure stands — but the explanation is recorded and travels with any referral.
- An addition to the chart made after being notified. (Making that addition is still right, and the Charter says so plainly: if care was given and the record doesn't show it, completing the record is owed to the resident and the facility. It must be a proper dated addendum, never an alteration.)
- An argument that the care was appropriate.

The notice has to state all of this up front, including the grounds that will not work, so nobody spends ten days assembling material that cannot help (§ 5.5(a)(5)).

### Step 6: the three dispositions (§ 5.5(b))

- **Systems** — the cause is process, staffing, tooling, workflow, or the standard itself. Stays with the Committee, feeds standards revision and teaching, and individual attribution is not carried forward.
- **Individual** — the observation is confirmed as an individual finding. What follows is decided by the referral rules, not by this Committee's mood on the day.
- **No action** — no departure, or the observation was corrected or withdrawn.

**Before any of these are settled, the Chair asks the safety question aloud** (§ 5.5(b)(1)): does any member hold a concern that what they are looking at reflects a departure from the standard of care, rather than from documentation? The minutes record that it was asked and answered.

The people running an audit are clinicians and will form clinical impressions. That is expected, and it is why the audit is worth running. What the Committee may not do is turn such an impression into its own finding — testing it needs a discipline-matched panel reading charts against the standard of care, which is peer review's job. A member who holds that kind of concern refers it **as a person**, under § 6.7(c).

**Why the Chair has to ask, rather than wait to be told.** That personal route only helps if someone uses it, and raising a concern about a colleague unprompted is a hard thing to do. It means speaking up out of turn, in a meeting called for another purpose, and being seen to single a named person out. Left to individual initiative, whether a concern ever gets voiced depends less on how serious it is than on how willing that particular member is to be the one who says it.

Asking the question every time changes what it costs. Answering a question the Chair has put to the whole room is an ordinary part of the meeting rather than an accusation someone chose to make, and every member has been asked whether or not they say anything. The referral that follows is still that member's own act — the Committee makes no referral on this basis and takes no position on whether one should be made.

### Step 7: the close-out letter (§ 5.5(c))

Everyone who was notified is told what became of every observation, which grounds were sustained, and where to find the standard and examples of good documentation. Someone whose observations were all withdrawn is told exactly that, and told that nothing further follows.

**The letter imposes nothing.** No obligation, no deadline, no monitor, no record of remediation. It reports an audit result and points at material open to everyone.

**It carries an offer of help** — a conversation with a clinician who works in that area, a teaching session, a case discussion (§ 5.5(c)(5)). The offer is fenced carefully:

- it goes to *everyone* who gets a close-out, whatever the outcome, so its presence says nothing about the person;
- nothing happens unless they ask, and there is no consequence for not asking;
- **whether it is taken up is never recorded, counted, or transmitted** — the moment uptake is counted, an offer has become a requirement;
- it is delivered by someone who took no part in the disposition and holds no authority over that person, and who does not tell their own management line who was offered or received it;
- it is never assigned or tracked in a learning system;
- it is not a peer review instrument, and it discharges nothing. It does not reduce the standard, the finding, or anyone's own obligation to close a gap once they know about it.

### Step 8: the follow-up look (§ 5.11)

**The year's measures are set in an annual audit plan** (§ 3.1(f)), which names each measure, the standard it measures against, and the Committee's answer to each enterprise priority brought to it. The Chair presents the plan to the Chief Medical Officer and the Chief Operating Officer at their quarterly meeting. They may say it is not measuring the right things; they do not say what a standard requires.

**The audit works one measure at a time.** A measure is chosen and swept across the whole organization; then the program moves to the next measure, and the one before it may not come round again for years. So a finding closed out this quarter would, without something deliberate, never be looked at again — and several parts of the Charter depend on somebody looking: the referral rules escalate on repetition after notice, the offer of help is only meaningful if anyone finds out whether it helped, and the Chair's quarterly report has to be able to say whether what the Committee does moves the numbers.

That is what this section is for. Where an individual finding is closed out without referral, the Committee looks again at a sample of that clinician's charts against the same standard, independently of whatever measure is being swept at the time.

- The window **opens forty-five days after close-out and runs ninety days.** The first forty-five days are time given to act, and no care in that period is examined. Ninety days is two to three chronic-care visits per resident — enough to ask whether practice changed, rather than whether a note got written.
- **It is disclosed in the close-out letter**: that it will happen, on what standard, roughly when.
- **It runs once.** It is not repeated and not extended. A repeating examination aimed at a named person is a monitoring status, and this Committee has no power to impose one.
- If the clinician has no eligible resident in the window, the matter closes. A finding is not held open waiting for a case.
- Systems dispositions get re-measured too — a program that intervenes and never measures again has no basis for saying it improves care.

## Commendation and recognition

**The audit also records good care** (§ 5.5(d)). Where the audit reads a chart and finds care that meets an improvement target, or documentation good enough to serve as an example for others, the Committee may record a commendation attributed to the clinician. The clinician is told in writing which chart, the date of service, and what the chart showed. Nothing is required in response.

- **It records what the chart shows.** It is not a disposition and decides nothing about the clinician's competence, favorably or otherwise.
- **It offsets nothing.** It plays no part in any disposition, any referral criterion, or any follow-up look, and it does not travel with a referral. A finding on one chart is not answered by excellence on another. The clinician may choose to give a commendation to the Peer Review Committee.
- **It stays in the protected record** and enters no employment or performance record. Because it concerns an identified clinician, the member who holds employment authority takes no part in it.
- **A commended chart can become a teaching example.** A clean version goes into the open record with the resident removed, and with the clinician's name removed unless they agree in writing to be named. The commendation itself is never published.

**Recognition is of facilities and teams, and it is public** (§ 7.6(a)). The Committee may recognize a facility, or the clinical team serving it, for performance on an organizational measure. Recognition draws only on the operational reports the organization produces in the ordinary course and never on the Committee's own records: no observation, no finding, no commendation, no comparison group.

**Where the Committee wants to learn how a facility achieves its results, the education staff go and ask** (§ 7.6(b)). They interview the people there and observe how the work is done; they do not audit charts. What they learn is written up for the open record as a description of shared practice. It names the facility only with its leadership's consent, identifies no resident, and names no other facility or clinician by way of comparison.

**Nothing public refers to the audit** (§ 7.6(c)). No recognition or practice description says or implies that the audit examined, selected, or found anything. The two tracks are kept apart because recognition that drew on the audit would move protected material into a public document and tell every reader where the audit had looked.

## Referral to peer review

**Only an individual finding can be referred through this route** (§ 6.1), and only where it meets a criterion in a written schedule called the **Audit Referral Policy** — adopted by this Committee, in effect only when the Peer Review Committee concurs, reviewed annually, and open to everyone (§ 6.2). Each revision takes effect the same way. The criteria are deliberately kept out of the Charter so they can be tuned against real findings without amending a governing document.

**What travels with a referral** (§ 6.3): the finding, the underlying data, whatever the clinician submitted and which grounds were sustained, what the Committee did with it, and the disposition history. **Not the Committee's deliberations. Nothing at all about the offer of support** — not whether it was offered, taken up, or declined.

**A referral is not a finding by peer review** (§ 6.4). It establishes that this Committee made an individual finding and that a criterion was met. Peer review screens every referral, works on its own record, and is not bound by anything this Committee thought.

**The Committee's involvement ends there** (§ 6.5). It takes no part in the review and hears nothing about the outcome beyond a de-identified summary.

### The separate route, which matters (§ 6.7)

Nothing in this Charter limits what peer review may look at.

- Care can fall below the standard of care whether or not any standard here addresses it. Most of clinical practice cannot be written down, and no organization writes a policy for every scenario.
- **The absence of a standard is not a defense.** Nothing in the standards program should be read to make acceptable whatever it does not cover.
- **Any person — any member, any auditor, any analyst — may refer a matter to peer review directly, as a person.** That route carries no threshold, no adopted standard, and no notice period.
- Where a matter came to someone's attention through the audit, a direct referral has to rest on a genuine standard-of-care or patient-safety concern, not on a dressed-up audit failure. The same facts can support both; what must be independent is the basis. Peer review screens for this.

## The guardrails a member should know by name

- **Widespread non-compliance is a systems problem, not a wave of referrals** (§ 5.9). Where a criterion would refer a large share of the clinicians it applies to, it routes to revising the standard, fixing the audit question, or teaching it properly — those being the three ordinary causes. A gap concentrated among people new to a clinical area is an onboarding condition, and the answer is a program, not something aimed at an individual.
- **The criterion is clinical, never economic** (§ 5.8). Every standard states its clinical basis at adoption, with no reference to any payment arrangement. A measure justified by revenue or cost avoidance is not adopted. Volume is never itself the basis of a finding. A measure borrowed from a payer or rating program is adopted for its clinical content, not for that program's threshold or denominator.
- **Controls against a standard that gets satisfied by a dot phrase** (§ 5.10). A standard that asks for documentation can be met by documentation. A macro or dot phrase dropped into every note will pass an audit indefinitely and mean nothing, and neither the tool nor a reviewer reading that note alone can tell the difference. Three standing requirements answer that: the audit cross-checks what a note asserts against the rest of the record, so a phrase that says a drug is on hold when the medication list shows otherwise becomes an observation in its own right; at least quarterly the Committee samples charts that *passed*, at random, to see whether the documentation reflects a decision anyone actually made; and entries that technically satisfy a standard while leaving the next reader no wiser are recorded as **thin**. A thin entry is a systems disposition, never an individual finding, and never counts toward anything. Thin entries are evidence about the standard and about the teaching.
- **Comparison groups** (§ 5.6). Built by analytics and adopted by the Committee, defined by things that plausibly affect the measure — discipline, setting, panel size, sometimes acuity or length of stay. **Where no adequate comparison can be built, no pattern-based criterion applies to that measure for that clinician.** Small disciplines and small panels produce numbers that cannot tell a real difference from noise.
- **Nothing generated before the Charter takes effect comes into the record or counts toward anything** (§ 8.7).
- **No retaliation** (§ 10.2) against anyone for giving information in good faith, taking part in the Committee's work, being selected for audit, or being the subject of a finding. That is protection for participation and status; it is not immunity from consequence on other grounds.

## Records: two files, kept apart (Article VIII)

**The open record** — adopted standards, education material, anything circulated, any draft sent outside the Committee for comment, and the Audit Referral Policy. This record is meant to circulate and **is not confidential**, and the Committee does not represent that it is. A document sent to every clinician in the organization cannot be privileged.

A practical consequence for members: **a draft becomes open the moment it is circulated.** So a circulated draft must not carry the Committee's candid assessment of current performance, of any facility, or of any clinician. That belongs in the other file and stays there.

**The protected record** — observations, individual findings, dispositions, referrals, minutes, deliberations, working drafts, evidence tables, members' comments, and the audit register (the map of questions to standards, the tool validation, selection thresholds, sample sizes).

No document moves between the two, and no document is ever in both.

A few placements worth remembering: the **referral policy is open**, because a clinician is entitled to know what can cause a referral. **Selection thresholds are not open**, because selection carries no consequence and publishing them would invite the audit to be managed rather than met. A comparison group's **definition** is open — a clinician is entitled to know the terms of a comparison drawn against them — while **which colleagues were in it and what their rates were** is protected, because that is a record of other people's care.

Also: **write everything as though the clinician will read it** (§ 8.4) — what was observed stated plainly, the evidence identified, characterization avoided. Every member signs a written confidentiality undertaking before receiving any part of the protected record (§ 8.6). Records are kept seven years. Nothing is altered or destroyed once litigation is filed or looks likely.

---

## Meetings (Article IX)

Monthly. Quorum is a majority of members, at least two-thirds of them practising clinicians. Decisions are by majority of those present and voting — and on anything concerning an identified clinician, only members not excluded by the recusal rule vote, and at least three of them must be present or the matter waits (§ 9.3). Minutes record what was considered, the documents reviewed, who was present, any withdrawal and its time, and what was done; deliberation is summarized, never transcribed. **Audit and referral business is done in executive session.**

## What is actually asked of you as a member

- Attend, and vote on standards — including the express label that decides whether a standard can ever support a referral.
- Bring the clinical judgment. Standards written for this population are the whole point, and most of the trials behind published guidelines did not include our residents.
- Withdraw from anything concerning an identified clinician if you hold hiring, firing, discipline, pay, evaluation, or assignment authority over anyone.
- When the Chair puts the safety question, answer it honestly. If you hold a genuine standard-of-care concern, that goes to peer review from you as a person, not from this Committee.
- Keep the two records apart in your own habits: candid assessments never go into anything that circulates.
- Sign the confidentiality undertaking, and treat everything in the protected file accordingly.
- Resist the pull to decide whether the care was good. That question belongs to peer review. The separation is what keeps this Committee's process short and what protects the people it measures.

## What is not settled yet

- **The Audit Referral Policy.** Its architecture is decided; the numbers in it cannot be chosen before there is a baseline to calibrate against.
- **Indemnification and insurance** for members and for people assisting with audits. The Chief Executive Officer's Authorization commits the organization to defend and indemnify them for good-faith service and to maintain insurance that covers it. The Authorization is not yet signed, and this is a fair thing to ask about before agreeing to serve.
- **Several bracketed defaults in the Charter** — the ten-business-day correction window, sixty-day notice period, forty-five-day and ninety-day follow-up parameters, term lengths, meeting cadence, retention period. All are drafting defaults, all are open to argument.

## Glossary

**Audit register** — the Committee's internal working record: which audit question maps to which standard, tool validation, selection thresholds, sample sizes. Protected.

**Commendation** — a protected record that one chart met an improvement target or could serve as model documentation. The clinician is told; it offsets nothing and enters no employment record.

**Comparison group** — the group of clinicians a rate is measured against. Its definition is open; who was in it in a given cycle is not.

**Disposition** — the Committee's decision about what an observation is: systems, individual, or no action.

**Improvement target** — a standard set above the floor of acceptable practice. Can never support a referral.

**Individual finding** — a checked observation confirmed as a departure from a minimum expectation. The only thing this Committee can refer through the audit route.

**Minimum expectation** — a standard the organization expects every clinician to meet. A departure from one can support a referral, if a written criterion is met.

**Observation** — an apparent departure in one chart, before anyone has checked it. Carries no conclusion and no consequence.

**Open record / protected record** — the two files the Committee keeps, one meant to circulate and expressly not confidential, one confidential and never mixed with the first.

**Rate** — aggregate performance across a population. Describes a group, never a person's care in a given episode.

**Recognition** — public acknowledgement of a facility or team, drawn only from operational reports outside the Committee's records.

**Standards register** — the record of every standard: its text, its effective date, its label, its comparison-group definition, and the teaching that preceded measurement. Open.

**Thin entry** — a note that technically satisfies a standard while leaving the next reader unable to tell where the matter stands. Recorded, treated as a systems issue, never referred.
