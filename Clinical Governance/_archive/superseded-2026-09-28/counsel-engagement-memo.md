## 1. The threshold question, framed

Eventus employs licensed clinicians and contracts with facilities. It is not itself a licensed health care facility in most or all of its markets. Nearly every question below reduces to a single issue: **do the peer review privilege statutes in our five states protect an entity of this type, or only the licensed individuals working within it?**

This is the *Reginelli* problem. In *Reginelli v. Boggs* (Pa. 2018), a physician staffing company was held not to be a "professional health care provider" under the state's peer review statute, so a performance file it maintained was discoverable. Pennsylvania is outside our footprint and the case is not controlling anywhere we operate, but the reasoning is the template for the entity-coverage question in at least two of our states, and it is the most likely single point of failure in the entire construct.

Specific instances:

- **North Carolina** — § 90-21.22A defines a medical review committee by composition and purpose with no hospital-organizer requirement, but the discovery bar runs to a defined list of defendants that does not obviously include an employed-physician management company. Individual clinicians appear protected; corporate protection is open. Nursing homes fall under a separate statute (§ 131E-107).
- **Indiana** — protection requires organization in one of nine enumerated ways. Does Eventus qualify as a "professional health care organization" under I.C. 34-6-2-99(a)(2)(A)? If not, is there another organizer route?
- **Kentucky** — does the 2018 KRS 311.377 amendment, extending protection to any entity performing a designated professional review function, reach an employed multi-state group practice?
- **Ohio** — the "health care entity" definition expressly includes for-profit corporations, LLCs, and professional corporations, which appears to resolve it favorably. Confirm.
- **Virginia** — § 8.01-581.17 extends privilege to committees established under guidelines adopted by a state professional association, and the Medical Society of Virginia's published guidelines expressly cover committees established by an entity that employs or contracts with physicians. Confirm that adopting MSV guidelines by reference in the charter satisfies § 8.01-581.17(B)(iii).

**Related structural question:** what is the corporate structure — one entity or many, and which entity employs clinicians in each state? This determines which statute applies and whether that entity is the kind of entity the statute covers.

## 3. Structural questions that gate charter drafting

The design places a new Clinical Governance Council between the Peer Review Council and management: the PRC recommends, the CGC accepts, rejects, modifies, or remands, and constitutes a hearing panel when a recommendation is adverse. Management receives notice only. The board delegates once by resolution and receives an annual aggregate report.

Two problems with that as drawn.

**3.1 There is no appellate layer, and the CGC both proposes and finally decides.**

The CGC accepts the adverse recommendation (which is the proposed action triggering notice), constitutes the hearing panel, and then takes final action on the panel's findings. It has formed and recorded a view before the hearing occurs. In the hospital model the board sits as the appellate layer; we have deliberately removed the board from the case flow and nothing replaced it.

HCQIA's safe harbor turns in part on adequate notice and hearing, and a self-reviewing body weakens that prong where we most need the presumption.

**Question:** can the board hold a narrow appellate role limited to *procedural* review — did the process the charter requires actually occur — without the board thereby assuming a clinical duty of care? If not, is a standing external appellate reviewer defensible, and does an external reviewer's participation affect privilege in any of the five states?

**3.2 Two officers with employment authority sit on the deciding body.**

The original design built the CGC from the three clinical chairs, four regional CMOs, and the regional directors of clinical operations and excellence, most of whom hold employment authority over the clinicians under review. The Charter as now drafted does not seat regional leadership by office (CGC § 3.9).

The Council is nine voting members in two classes. The clinical class is seven: the Chair of the Primary Care Committee, the Chair of the Psychiatry Committee, and five at-large practitioners elected by and from clinicians who hold no employment authority. The enterprise class is two: the Chief Medical Officer and the Chief Operating Officer.

The enterprise members hold employment authority by office. They take part in the Council's governance business, and they are excluded by seat from any determination concerning an identified clinician: they are not present for its deliberation and do not vote on it, whoever holds the office (CGC § 3.1). Only the clinical class votes on a professional review action, and an adverse action requires a majority of all eligible members not recused, whether or not present (CGC § 7.4). The Charter fixes two floors: at least two-thirds of voting members are clinicians in active practice, and members holding no employment authority are a majority (CGC § 3.2).

**Question:** does a deciding body that seats two officers with employment authority for governance business, and excludes them by seat from every determination concerning an identified clinician, satisfy HCQIA and the state statutes as the peer review decision-maker? Is an exclusion written into the Charter at the level of the seat sufficient, or does any of the five states require that such officers not be members at all? At nine voting members, confirm that the two-thirds clinician floor clears Ind. Code § 34-30-15 and N.C. Gen. Stat. § 90-21.22A.

**3.3 The dual-chair conflict — a design decision, and a request that you confirm it is sufficient**

The Chair of Clinical Excellence chairs both the Clinical Quality Committee and the Peer Review Committee, permanently and by job description. This is the role as defined, not a temporary condition to be unwound, so the design has to absorb it rather than plan around it.

Decisions taken, subject to your review:

- **The Chair of Clinical Excellence sits on the CGC ex officio and does not vote on anything** (CGC § 3.1.1). He participates fully in the council's governance work — charter, hearing panel constitution, board reporting, policy — and presents every matter arising from either committee. An interim draft gave the seat a vote and recused it from every matter arising from a committee he chairs, which reached every case and most of the governance business. The seat is now non-voting in terms.
- **On any determination concerning an identified clinician, he presents and withdraws.** He presents the recommendation and the record, takes questions, and leaves before deliberation and vote. Minutes record his presence, his presentation, and his departure, and record any recall and second departure if the council needs more from him. The intent is that the separation is visible in the document rather than asserted in the charter — non-voting presence during deliberation would not be distinguishable, in a later record, from participation.
- **He is not counted toward quorum, either composition floor, or any vote**, so that a non-voting member does not inflate the composition percentage that the Indiana and North Carolina statutes turn on.
- **The PRC vice-chair presides** whenever the chair is the referral source or has prior involvement in the patient's care or in the clinician's supervision. Because the Chair rounds clinically, this will arise with some regularity.
- **The chair does not sit on hearing panels**, which are drawn from clinicians on neither standing committee and constituted separately by the CGC.

**Question:** is that sufficient to keep the HCQIA safe harbor available where the same person chairs the recommending body and holds a non-voting seat on the deciding body, withdrawing before deliberation on any identified clinician, or does the safe harbor require full exclusion from CGC membership? If full exclusion is the safer answer, say so — the governance value of his participation in the CGC's non-case work is real but not worth the presumption.

## 4. Remaining questions, by domain

### Entity status and delegation
1. Will the board adopt a charter by resolution? Can a chartered clinical council hold delegated authority without the board itself assuming a clinical duty? Clinical leadership approval exists; board delegation does not, and I understand the second is what actually confers authority. Advise on how that resolution should be framed and what the board is and is not assuming by adopting it.
2. Does Eventus meet the HCQIA "health care entity" definition as currently structured — an entity providing health care services that follows a formal peer review process for the purpose of furthering quality health care? Note that the formal process is the qualifying condition, not evidence of qualification.

### Privileges and existing obligations
3. What do credentialing files actually say about scope? § 11151(3) defines clinical privileges to include other circumstances under which a practitioner is permitted to furnish care by a health care entity. If credentialing files already define restrictable scope, reportable actions may already be possible and a reporting duty may already exist, unrecognized.
4. Does a restrictable clinical authorization construct create exposure we do not have today, and does the two-tier design — non-reportable coaching and focused review, versus formal restriction with full HCQIA process — hold up? Specifically: a "focused review" that is imposed rather than accepted, conditions how a clinician may practice, and runs past 30 days appears reportable regardless of what the policy calls it. What written criteria distinguish the tiers by substance?
5. Stark and anti-kickback fair-market-value analysis on any reallocation of medical director stipends, and confirmation that the design's separation of directorship decisions from peer review findings is sufficient to avoid an economic credentialing characterization.

### NPDB
6. Is Eventus registered as a reporting entity? Since when, and who is the designated agent?
7. Has the biennial attestation been signed, by whom, and on what analytic basis? Attesting that all reportable actions have been submitted, without anyone having analyzed whether any were reportable, is a separate exposure from any underlying reporting gap.
8. Where should the authorized agent function sit? My view is compliance or the general counsel's office rather than the CMO, given that the attestation is a compliance instrument and the CMO role is currently interim.
9. Should Eventus voluntarily report non-physicians? Reporting is mandatory for physicians and permissive for others. A uniform written policy either way is defensible; ad hoc practice is not.

### Privilege mechanics
10. Does state peer review privilege extend to non-clinician staff supporting a committee — the coordinator who assembles packets, drafts minutes, and holds the record? HCQIA immunity expressly covers staff and those assisting. State coverage has not been analyzed.
11. Which clinician disciplines are covered by each state's statute? These were written around physicians; NP, PA, psych NP, and psychotherapist coverage varies and has not been analyzed.
12. Permissible-disclosure analysis for transmitting anything to a contracted facility. Facility agreements may grant access to clinical quality information, which would function as a standing waiver.
13. Review Va. § 8.01-581.17(F) on patient safety data exchange against the planned PSES design.

### PSO
14. Independent PSO versus component PSO given enterprise scale.
15. Confirmation of the two-track design: patient safety work product inside the PSES on the quality track, individual review outside it on the peer review track, with no committee operating in both.

### Existing records
16. Current peer review case records sit outside any privilege structure and are likely discoverable. They may contain candid assessments of named clinicians written under an assumption of confidentiality. I have not asked where they live and will not propose anything about them until you advise.

**Two specific questions.** Does my present awareness that these records exist and are unprotected trigger any preservation obligation? And when the new charter takes effect, I intend to close the old record and open a new one, on the theory that continuing the same file across the boundary undermines the argument that anything changed — confirm that is the right approach and that it creates no spoliation concern.

## 5. What I am not asking counsel to decide

The program design, committee structure, and clinical content are mine. What I need from you is the legal architecture the design has to fit inside — entity coverage, delegation, due process adequacy, reporting obligations, and the boundaries of privilege.

If the answer to section 1 is that corporate privilege is unavailable in one or more states, that changes the design rather than ending it, and I would rather know now than after the charters are drafted.

The approval I have is to build something robust. Building it correctly is a legal question before it is a clinical one, and I would rather spend the time now than rebuild later.
