# How a Quality Audit Would Work

*Draft, for discussion. None of this is in place yet — the committee that would run it hasn't been formed.*

---

## The short version

A committee checks charts against a small number of written standards. Everyone whose charts were read gets a report on them, with an offer of help and a chance to say where the review got something wrong. For most standards, that's where it ends.

**The committee running the audit can't do anything to anybody.** It can't discipline you, restrict your practice, or make you do anything. It writes standards, measures against them, sends you a report on your own charts, fixes what's broken and offers you help. On some standards it looks at your charts again a few months later, and where a written rule is met it hands the matter to a different committee and stops.

---

## What a standard looks like

These four are illustrations. **The committee doesn't exist yet and will pick its own.** They're here to show the shape — every standard is tied to a specific problem and only applies to residents who have it. 

**Urine cultures.** If you order one, your note says what symptom prompted it. Confusion alone doesn't count. Cloudy or smelly urine alone doesn't count.

**Antipsychotics in dementia.** If a resident with dementia is on one, your note says what behavior you're treating, what was tried first, and whether lowering the dose has been considered.

**Sliding scale insulin.** If someone's still on sliding scale more than 30 days after they arrive, your note says why for that resident. **Inside 30 days the standard doesn't apply at all** — that's the window for figuring out their intake, and nobody needs to write anything about it.

**Anticoagulation in afib.** Your note accounts for it: they're on it, or they're not and here's the reason, or cardiology made the call, or it's on hold and here's what ends the hold.

That last one trips people up, so: *"held per cardiology"* doesn't meet the standard. *"Held per cardiology until after the epidural injection"* does. **You're not being asked to justify cardiology's decision or repeat their thinking** — just to show you knew where the issue stood, so the next person reading the chart does too.

## Following one through

### Nothing counts right away

The standard goes out with an example of a note that meets it. **Then 60 days pass before any of your care can be measured against it.** What you did in those 60 days doesn't count, even if the audit runs much later.

### How your charts got picked

**Usually there's no reason beyond the calendar.** Most audits are routine — the committee picks a measure for the cycle and reads charts across everyone it applies to. If you have residents with afib and afib is this quarter's measure, your charts get read. That's the whole story.

Sometimes something narrows it instead. A facility looks unusual on a measure, or a number stands out, and that decides where to start.

**Either way, being looked at says nothing about you.** It isn't a finding, it isn't a suspicion, and it never gets written down as though it were.

### Four charts don't meet the standard

Four residents with afib, not on anticoagulation, nothing in your note about it. The other eight charts read meet the standard.

**Nothing has happened yet.** No finding, no conclusion, nothing in a file anywhere.

### You get a report

Everyone whose charts were read gets one, including clinicians whose charts all met the standard. It lists each chart: the resident, the date, whether it met the standard, and if not, what was missing and whether the gap looks like the decision or just the note. **Nothing in it is a finding.** Nobody has decided anything about your care.

If one of your notes is a good example of what the standard is for, the report says so, and asks whether a version with the resident's details removed can be used in teaching. Your name stays off it unless you say otherwise.

Sometimes the report includes a short note from a colleague about one resident's care: a reduction that might be worth another try, an indication that might not need the drug. It is advice, signed by the clinician who wrote it, and it counts toward nothing. **If it's about a physician's management, a physician writes it.**

### If the report got something wrong, tell us

There's no deadline. If you correct every item before a follow-up look, the look doesn't happen. Anything you correct comes off the report.

**These clear an item:**

- **It wasn't your patient.** You were covering, or cardiology manages it.
- **The resident doesn't have the condition.** Wrong code, an old echo, resolved after ablation. Or on the insulin standard: they got here three weeks ago.
- **It didn't happen.** You did order it, and there's an order to prove it.
- **You wrote it and the search missed it.** Your note says *"held per cardiology pending EGD 9/12"* and the search was looking for a structured field. Say where to look. **This is the most common correction there is.**
- **The timing's wrong.** That care predates the date the standard started counting.

**These don't:**

- **A reason that's only in someone else's note**, on a standard that asks your own note to account for it. *"Anticoagulation stopped by cardiology, prior GI bleed"* is all it takes: a sentence, not a justification.
- **Explaining it now.** If you thought about it and didn't write it down, the item stands. Say so anyway; it's recorded.
- **Fixing the chart now.** Won't change this report. **But if you did the work and the chart doesn't show it, add a dated addendum.** You owe that to the resident either way, and it counts from the next review on.

### Most of it goes away

This happens a lot. On a urine culture review, many items come back *"I didn't order that"*: nursing sent it under a standing protocol. **Which is the finding.** Two facilities have a protocol that manufactures the problem, that gets fixed, and nothing attaches to any clinician. That's worth more than any individual item it produced.

### An offer of help

**Everyone who gets a report gets the same offer**: a conversation with someone who does this work, a short session, a case discussion about one of your residents. Ask and it's set up. Don't and nothing happens. **Nobody records who takes it up or counts it anywhere.** It's help with the standard, not a pass on it.

**You can bring in your supervising physician, or not.** You can share the report with them, or ask for help without sharing it. For that, the report includes a separate page you can forward: a note asking for help with the clinical area ("I'd like to work on diabetes management"), with the review guide and none of your results. You decide; the committee sends nothing and never knows which you chose. (One narrow exception, for now, in psychiatry, where the supervising psychiatrist writes no visit note the review can read: if a colleague's comment on one resident's care comes up again at a follow-up, it can go to your supervising physician as a clinical question about that resident, and you'll be told.) And you can always ask your supervising physician for help, review or no review.

### What happens next depends on the standard

**For most standards, the report is the whole of it.** The committee uses the totals to fix the teaching, the tools and the standard, and measures again later across everyone.

**Some standards can lead to a referral if the same gap repeats after you've been told.** Which ones is written down when the standard is adopted, in a register anyone can read, and the report tells you. For those:

- **Nothing you do for the next six weeks is looked at.** That's your time to use the offer and get the change in place.
- **Then a small sample of your charts covering the following three months.** Clear, and you get a letter saying that's the end of it.
- **If the same gap shows up, you get a formal notice and 10 business days** to show where the review is wrong, including anything in the first report. Each item is withdrawn, put down to systems, or confirmed. If nothing from the first report is confirmed, the new items are treated as a first report of their own. If gaps are confirmed in both, the written referral rules decide whether it goes to peer review.

**A few standards work differently.** On a standard where a single gap is serious enough to warrant review, or where your figures on a measure stand well above those of comparable clinicians, the report says so and a formal notice comes with it.

**And if more than a quarter of the clinicians reviewed (and more than three of them) miss a standard in a cycle, nobody gets an individual follow-up.** That's a teaching problem, and the committee treats it as one.

### Physicians' visit notes

If you're the physician doing the regulatory visit in a skilled facility, or the periodic visit in assisted living, **your visit note is read against the same standard, as your own note.** The visit exists to review the whole plan, so the note should say where each element stands. Agreeing with the NP's plan is fine when the note says what you're agreeing with: *"quetiapine 25 mg at bedtime for distressing visual hallucinations; reduction in June followed by recurrence within a week; continue, reassess in August."* A bare "agree with plan" doesn't meet it. You get your own report, on your own note. Nothing about the NP's results comes to you this way.

## Who sees this

**Not your supervisor.** Individual audit findings don't go to anyone with authority over your job — not your regional CMO, not operations, not into a personnel file. The committee itself can't see your compensation, productivity, or revenue, and isn't permitted to consider them.

That's a change from how coding audits work here, and it's deliberate. Those are billing and administrative compliance. They aren't privileged, supervisors should keep seeing them, and nothing here touches any of that.

Findings about your **care** run on different rules, because the legal protection this whole structure depends on comes apart if they circulate outside the committee.

**Your supervising physician** gets the same review guide the audit uses, and is expected to use it when reviewing your charts as part of ordinary supervision. That review is theirs, not the committee's: the committee doesn't choose which charts they read or see what they find. And if your supervising physician sits on the committee, they step out of any discussion about you.

**Results are reported as totals.** Each audit's results go to the CMO and COO for the whole company and for each RDCOE territory, physicians and APCs together, and they decide how to share them. No clinician can be picked out: a territory too small to hide anyone isn't reported.

**What supervisors do see:** everything administrative, exactly as now. Notice of any final action and what it means operationally. And systems findings — with no names on them — because a broken protocol is theirs to fix.

## The other way this can go

Everyone doing the review is a clinician. Sometimes what's on the page doesn't look like a documentation problem. It looks like residents aren't getting something they need.

**The chair asks that out loud, every time, before anything gets decided.** When the answer is yes, that person refers it themselves to the peer review committee, on patient safety grounds. The quality committee doesn't make that referral and takes no position on it.

**Anyone can do that, any time, about anything.** No audit, no threshold, no waiting. That route matters more than the audit does — an audit finds what it was pointed at, and a colleague finds what's actually there.

## If it does reach peer review

Screening happens in 10 business days, and plenty of things end right there.

If a review opens and it looks like a gap in one area with nothing else in question, the committee can offer a plan instead of going to a finding — a session, a look at the relevant charts with someone who does that work, done inside a set period.

**Take it and the matter closes with no finding, no action against you, nothing reportable.** Someone confirms you did it. **Nobody grades you.** 

Turn it down and you get the full review with every right that comes with it.

---

## Things that can't happen

- The audit committee restricting your practice. It has no such power, ever.
- Anything about your pay, schedule, assignment, or contract. It can't see that information and can't act on it.
- A finding built out of a rate or a number. Only a chart, read against a standard.
- Anything counting before you got the standard and had time.
- Getting looked at over and over. The follow-up runs once for each report, then it's finished.
