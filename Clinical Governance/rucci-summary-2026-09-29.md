# Clinical Governance: What Changed on September 29

*A summary of the revisions in this packet. Each is written into the enclosed charters.*

---

## 1. Who screens peer review referrals

The Chair screens each referral with the department chair for the clinician's service line, primary care or psychiatry, in place of two committee members. Every Peer Review Committee member now comes to a case without having seen it, and the first decision is made with someone who knows how that service line practices. Department chairs screen on the Committee's behalf, have no vote, and take nothing back to their department.

- Where the department chair helped decide at the Quality Committee to send the case, a Peer Review Committee member of the same discipline screens it instead.
- Where care crosses service lines, the Chair may bring in both department chairs. A primary care clinician's antipsychotic prescribing is the usual example.
- Where the two screeners disagree, a committee member of that discipline decides.

## 2. Nurse practitioners and physician assistants

Both are defined as advanced practice clinicians (APCs) and treated as peers, since their clinical responsibilities are the same. An APC's case is reviewed by two APCs, with a physician added when the supervisory arrangement is in question. For an APC in psychiatry, the reviewers need psychiatric training.

## 3. The agreed education plan

When peer review finds a narrow knowledge gap, the clinician may accept an education plan instead of a full review. The case closes when the plan is completed, and the clinician's care in that clinical area is then checked twice, once in each of the next two quarters. What the clinician learned is never graded. A problem found on either check becomes a new case, and cannot be resolved with a second education plan. The clinician is told about the two checks before deciding whether to accept.

## 4. Recusal on the Quality Committee

A member steps out of any discussion of a clinician they supervise, a resident they treated, or anyone they have a close personal or financial tie to. When the Chair steps out, a member he has designated in advance presides.

## 5. Supervising physicians and the audit

Every APC is assigned to a supervising physician responsible for their practice and outcomes. Each audited standard now goes out with a public review guide: what the standard asks for, element by element, with examples of notes that meet it. The guide is shared with every supervising physician in the expectation that they will use it in routine reviews of their APCs' charts.

Those reviews are ordinary supervision and sit entirely outside the Quality Committee, including how charts are chosen, how completion is tracked, and any compensation for it. The Committee does not share its own findings on individual APCs; at our size, individual results sent to dozens of physicians would not stay confidential.

## 6. The APC decides what to share

Every close-out letter tells an APC they may share it with their supervising physician, or ask for help without sharing it. For the second, the letter includes a separate page the APC can forward: a request for help that names only the clinical area ("I'd like to work on diabetes management"), with the review guide and none of the results. The APC decides, and the Committee never knows which was chosen.

## 7. Reporting results

Each audit's results go to the CMO and COO for the whole company and for each RDCOE territory, one figure per standard with APCs and physicians together. No territory is labeled an outlier. A territory's result is withheld when too few charts or clinicians stand behind it, or when one clinician accounts for most of them, so that no one can be identified. The CMO and COO decide how the results are shared.

## Also updated

"What Happens to a Clinician" is now "How a Review Unfolds," written in the third person. The seat map and the member guides reflect the changes above.

## Questions added for counsel

- Whether the two follow-up checks after an education plan count as an "investigation" for Data Bank purposes.
- Whether state privilege protects a department chair who screens for the Peer Review Committee without being a member of it.
- Whether an APC's sharing of their own close-out affects the privilege on the Committee's records.
