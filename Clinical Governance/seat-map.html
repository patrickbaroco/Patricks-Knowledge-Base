Clinical governance framework · draft for discussion

Who sits where, and what each body does

Three chartered bodies and two clinical departments. This page shows the seats on each, the work each one owns, and how the primary care and psychiatry committees feed the Quality Committee without ever touching peer review.

Seats are shown by role, not by name — nobody has been appointed or elected yet.

Not adopted. Charters drafted, under revision, prepared for counsel review.

How the five bodies connect

Authority runs down from the board and decisions run back up. Quality and Peer Review are co-equal — neither reports to the other — and both are chartered by the Council.

Body one

Clinical Quality Committee

Defines what good care looks like here, builds the education that teaches it, and measures whether it is being delivered.

Who sits on it

Chair of Clinical Excellence — chairs it ex officio, untermed
Chair of the Primary Care Committee
Chair of the Psychiatry Committee

Both sit as members by office.

Members appointed by the Chairopen

Physicians, NPs and PAs. Two-year staggered terms, renewable. No fixed number is set in the charter; four practising clinicians keeps the floor below met with margin. A licensed psychotherapist is consulted on any psychotherapy standard rather than seated, until the peer review addendum for that discipline exists.

At the table, not members

Analytics; learning & development; enterprise priorities — the ACO and clinical operations view, which the committee must answer in its annual audit plan; and the Executive Coordinator, who is custodian of the records and does not vote.

At least two-thirds of members must be licensed clinicians in active practice — the statutory floor in Indiana and North Carolina is half, and the charter says it may never be amended below that.
At most one member may hold employment authority. That member takes full part in standards, education and systems work — and withdraws before any finding attributed to a named clinician is discussed.
No finding is attributed to a named clinician with fewer than three eligible members present. Where fewer are in the room, it waits.

What it does

  1. Adopts the standards. Each one is designated at adoption as either a minimum expectation — which an individual can be held to — or an improvement target, which can never support a referral. The vote is express; there is no default.
  2. Sets the education agenda that teaches each standard, and what conforming documentation looks like, before anything is measured.
  3. Owns the audit. Every audit question maps to a standard in effect. The instrument is validated against clinician review before it can produce anything about an individual.
  4. Checks with the clinician first. An observation is not a finding until the clinician has had the chance to show the resident wasn't theirs, wasn't in the population, or that the note exists where the search didn't look.
  5. Closes most findings out. A written close-out, an offer of support that is never recorded or counted either way, and one further look at that clinician's charts about 120 days later.
  6. Routes systems conditions to whoever can act on them — and reports to the Council when nothing was done.
  7. Refers an individual concern to Peer Review only where a criterion in the ratified Audit Referral Policy is met. Its involvement then ends completely.

What it cannot do

It is not a professional review body. It makes no finding about competence, does not decide whether care met the standard of care, and imposes no consequence of any kind on any individual, at any point.

Why the audit isn't a disciplinary instrument. Failing an internal standard does not establish substandard care. If a criterion would refer a large share of the clinicians it applies to, that is treated as a systems problem — the standard asks the wrong thing, the audit question doesn't measure it, or it was never properly taught — and it routes to revision or education, never to mass referral.

Body two

Peer Review Committee

Reviews the care an identified clinician furnished, decides whether it met the standard, and chooses a response proportionate to what it finds.

Who sits on it — 7 seats

Chair of Clinical Excellence — ex officio, untermed1
Members appointed by the Council on the Chair's recommendation6

All licensed clinicians in active practice. Two-year staggered terms. A vice-chair is elected annually from among them and presides whenever the Chair is recused.

Reviewer Pool — optional, and may be empty

Members review. Each case is reviewed by two members, three where the Chair says why. The pool is reached only where discipline matching cannot be met from the membership, and an outside reviewer sits behind both. Pool membership is not a committee seat.

Standing membership includes at least three physicians, two NPs or PAs, and one clinician with psychiatric training.
No one holding employment authority may serve — not on the committee, not in the pool. Supervisors may refer a matter and be interviewed; they do not review, deliberate, or vote. The exclusion is categorical, with no exception for a supervisor who happens to be the best-qualified reviewer available.
The Council's own chair does not sit here, so the body that reviews a recommendation is never the body that made it.

What it does

  1. Screens every referral within ten business days — deciding the route, never the merits. Matters arrive from the audit, from any clinician or employee, from management, facilities, patients and families, adverse events, or an external action.
  2. Opens a review and assigns two discipline-matched members to it. They investigate and propose; the committee decides.
  3. Closes with no finding where the care met standard, and notifies the clinician in writing that the review closed without an adverse finding.
  4. Agrees an educational resolution for a bounded knowledge gap: no finding, nothing reportable, sealed on completion, and it may be refused with no consequence. What was learned is confirmed as done, never graded.
  5. Imposes Tier One directly — written feedback, education, mentoring, or retrospective focused review. None of these restricts anyone's authority to practise and none is reportable. Each states its duration and its exit criteria in writing on the day it is imposed.
  6. Recommends Tier Two to the Council — mandatory consultation, proctoring, a scope restriction, non-renewal, or termination of clinical authorization. Each of these requires notice and a hearing before it becomes final.
  7. Restricts precautionarily where the risk is immediate — and that restriction lapses automatically unless the Council ratifies it.
  8. Routes what isn't its subject — systems conditions to Quality, and any question of a clinician's health, impairment or fitness to HR, which handles it under its own policies. Peer review keeps only the safety question: whether the clinician is still furnishing care while the matter is unresolved.

What it cannot do

It cannot take a final professional review action. It cannot make, recommend, or advise on employment, compensation, contracting, scheduling, facility assignment or medical director designation. It may not receive or consider a clinician's productivity, revenue or cost. And it files no report to the NPDB or any state board — that attaches to the Council's final action.

Most of what this committee does is not reportable. Nothing in Tier One restricts anyone's authority to practise, and the agreed educational resolution is sealed on completion. Most people's experience of the committee is designed to be: this doesn't have to be a big deal, do this education, we'll follow up, and we're done. What is different from the conversation that happens today is that there is now a but if you don't at the end of it.

Body three

Clinical Governance Council

The body the board delegates clinical governance to. Somebody has to approve what peer review recommends, and this is that body — plus the check that quality initiatives serve the mission rather than a few people's interests.

Who sits on it — 9 voting seats, and one non-voting chair

The two department chairs — Primary Care, Psychiatry2

Each recuses from any matter in their own service line.

At-large practitioners, elected by and from clinicians who hold no employment authority5

At least two physicians and two APCs; twelve months' service to be eligible; staggered terms. The council chair and vice-chair are elected from these seats only, and the chair's election is ratified by the board.

Enterprise members — the Chief Medical Officer and the Chief Operating Officer2

These two seats hold employment authority by office — shown faded, and excluded by seat from any decision about a named clinician, whoever holds the job.

Chair of Clinical Excellence — ex officio, non-voting

Chairs Quality and Peer Review, presents every matter that comes up from either, and does not vote on any of it. Not counted toward quorum or any floor.

Two classes of vote. All 9 vote on charters, framework and policy. Only the 7 clinical members vote on a professional review action; the two enterprise members may present information, then withdraw.
After the service-line recusal the design assumes, a typical action vote is six against a floor of four, and an adverse action needs a majority of all eligible members, not of those in the room.
The council chair may not sit on Quality or Peer Review, and may not hold employment authority over clinicians.
Regional leadership does not sit by office. Regional CMOs and RDCOEs refer, are interviewed and inform, on the same terms as anyone else with employment authority.

What it does

  1. Charters both committees, approves their charters, appoints the Peer Review members, and receives Quality's annual audit plan — checking that each enterprise priority is answered, without touching the standards.
  2. Ratifies the Audit Referral Policy — the criteria by which an audit finding is allowed to reach peer review — and reviews it annually.
  3. Maintains the clinical authorization framework and the autonomy tiers: what each clinician is authorized to do, which is the thing a restriction restricts.
  4. Accepts, rejects, modifies or remands what Peer Review recommends. It receives findings, evidence, the notice given and the clinician's response — never the committee's deliberations.
  5. Constitutes independent hearing panels when a recommendation is adverse, drawn from clinicians on neither standing committee.
  6. Takes every final professional review action. No other body can.
  7. Determines reportability and causes reports to be filed to the NPDB and state boards.
  8. Designates HR as the destination for health, impairment and fitness matters, by resolution, so peer review never adjudicates them.
  9. Reports clinical quality upward to the board annually, in aggregate, with no clinician identifiable.

What it cannot do

It has no authority over employment, compensation, contracting, scheduling, facility assignment or medical director designation, and may not direct management as to any of them. It may not receive or consider productivity, revenue or cost in connection with any review. The board keeps the power to revoke the delegation — and takes no case-level role while it stands.

The two departments

Primary Care and Psychiatry

Each owns the priorities and measures for its own discipline. Neither is chartered, and neither takes any part in peer review — which is what lets them stay unchartered.

Who sits on them, and where they connect

A chair for each — who also sits on the Quality Committee and on the Council

This is the join. The department chairs are the same people in all three rooms, which is how a discipline's priorities travel without any record travelling with them.

Practising clinicians of that discipline

The reason for a department layer with only two disciplines is participation: it brings the broader clinical community into the decisions instead of leaving them to a handful of senior roles.

They contribute to standards development and the education agenda, on material circulated to them for comment. That material is open — not privileged, and not treated as if it were.
Each department's chair recuses at the Council on any matter concerning a clinician in their own service line, without any further finding being needed.

What they never receive

The boundary CQC § 7.1
  • The Quality Committee's internal deliberative material — the protected half of its record.
  • Any observation or finding attributed to a named clinician. Not in aggregate with names attached, not informally, not at all.
  • Any part in a disposition — the departments are not in the room when a finding is closed out or referred.
  • Any part in peer review, in any form, at any stage.

Why the boundary has to be written down. Privilege breaks at the edge of an unchartered body. Because the departments touch only the open, circulated half of Quality's output, they need no charter of their own — but that boundary has to be written down, which is what § 7.1 does.

Still open

Two things are deliberately unsettled and will be decided before anything is adopted.

Audit Referral PolicyThe criteria by which an audit finding is allowed to reach peer review. Adopted by Quality, ratified by the Council, reviewed annually — and undrafted today. It is the largest remaining gap.
IndemnificationClinicians do not join peer review bodies without knowing the company stands behind them. Federal immunity is a presumption against damages, not protection from being sued. If the program cannot recruit members, none of the rest matters.
