# EQUiP Clinical Quality Measure — Corrective Insulin

Draft for discussion, 2026-09-11. Not adopted.

**This measure replaces the signature standards audit as the clinical quality component of the EQUiP bonus.** It is scored each quarter, is administered by management, and is conducted by the people who already carry out EQUiP review. It stands on its own and does not depend on the clinical governance structures now under consideration.

## Who reviews

**Advanced practice clinicians are reviewed by their supervising physician; physicians are reviewed by their regional CMO.** No new role and no additional staffing is required to run it.

## What is measured

**Whether the clinician has documented a review of how much corrective insulin the resident is actually receiving.** An order says only what is available to give. The amount administered is what indicates whether the regimen is working, and it is what should inform any change to a basal rate.

Corrective insulin in long-term care carries risks that differ from the inpatient setting. It is frequently not given at the time of a meal, which raises the risk of hypoglycemia, and it commits a resident to several injections a day where a simpler regimen may serve them better.

## Which residents count

Any resident on the clinician's panel with an active corrective insulin order, whether or not basal insulin is also ordered, where more than 30 days have passed since admission or a qualifying readmission, and the resident has been on that clinician's panel at least [30] days.

The 30 days is a population exclusion rather than a defense. A resident inside that window is not measured at all.

## What passes

A note within the cycle that states the corrective insulin the resident actually received over a stated period, together with either a reason for continuing it or a plan to change it with a point of reassessment.

Two examples, both passing:

- "Receiving approximately 4 units every other day over the past month. Continuing, because [reason]."
- "Receiving approximately 6 units with each meal. Increasing basal insulin, will monitor corrective insulin given over the next two weeks and reassess."

**The reviewer does not evaluate the reason.** The question is whether the clinician looked at what is being administered and said what they intend to do about it. The clinical decision itself remains theirs.

**Discontinuation passes by removing the resident from the measure.** Nothing here asks a clinician to continue corrective insulin.

## What does not pass

A note that addresses the order without reference to what is being given. Two reasons are specifically not sufficient:

- **Simplifying the regimen or minimizing injections.** A correction-only regimen means more injections than a single daily basal dose, not fewer, and carries greater hypoglycemia risk.
- **Resident or family refusal of scheduled insulin, stated without more.** Where refusal is the reason, the note should show that the alternative was explained and specifically declined.

## Scoring

Five eligible residents per clinician per cycle, taken in order from a list the reviewer does not choose. Where fewer than five are eligible, all of them are reviewed.

Each chart passes or does not. **A clinician meets the measure where no more than one reviewed chart does not pass.** Stating it as an allowance rather than a percentage holds every clinician to the same standard: a percentage would require a clinician with three eligible residents to pass all three, while a clinician with five could miss one.

The allowance also carries the standard forward without changing the rule. Reviewing five charts with one allowance sets the bar at eighty per cent; reviewing ten with the same allowance sets it at ninety. The sample size is the lever, and clinicians are told the same thing in either cycle.

**A clinician with no eligible residents meets the measure.** Few residents on long-standing corrective insulin is the result this measure exists to encourage. It also gives every clinician an outcome that maps to the bonus rather than an unresolved one.

Where the reviewer is unsure, the chart passes. Clinicians are not ranked against one another.

## Clarification

Each clinician sees which residents were sampled and what was missing, and may point to a note the reviewer did not find. The record as it stood at the close of the cycle is what counts.

## What this program does not do

**It produces a score and nothing else.** It makes no findings about any clinician, issues no notices, and produces no referrals. It is a compensation measure and carries no clinical authority over anyone.

## Where a clinical concern goes

**The measure asks whether the review happened. It does not evaluate the regimen.** A clinician who documents that a resident receives 6 to 10 units with each meal, records a reason, and continues unchanged quarter after quarter passes this measure every time. For a compensation instrument that is the right result, because a reviewer should not be asked to second-guess a colleague's clinical judgment with money attached.

It leaves the clinical question open. A reviewing physician may read that chart and conclude that the resident is on sliding scale insulin in all but name, on minimal basal, unchanged across several cycles, and that it warrants attention from someone other than the reviewer. Nothing in this program prevents that physician from acting on a clinical concern as they would on any other, and the program itself neither requires it nor records it.

**A concern raised this way today rests on one physician's judgment about a colleague, and there is no defined process behind it.** The proposed Clinical Quality Committee would give it three things it currently lacks: validation by a clinical body rather than a single reviewer's impression; the position to see whether the same pattern runs across a region or follows from an order set, which no single chart can show; and a referral that is the act of a committee rather than of an individual.

When that route exists, the referral is made in the physician's own name and rests on what they saw in the record. It does not reference the score, and the score does not travel with it. A chart may reach a physician's attention through coverage, a consultation, or a family's call; what follows rests on what the chart shows rather than on how it was reached.
