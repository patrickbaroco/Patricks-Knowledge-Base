Mechanical (shear) intravascular hemolysis caused by a prosthetic valve, degenerated bioprosthesis, or perivalvular leak — recognized by schistocytes, high LDH, low haptoglobin, and hemoglobinuria.

## Concept
Red cells passing through abnormal high-shear flow around a prosthetic or leaking valve are mechanically fragmented, producing **intravascular hemolysis** (see [[Intravascular Hemolysis]] and [[Hemolysis]]). Classic settings include **mechanical prosthetic valves**, **degenerated bioprostheses**, and especially **paravalvular/perivalvular regurgitant jets** where blood is forced through a narrow high-velocity channel ([[RLR 064 - Hematuria and Post-TAVR Perivalvular Leak|RLR 064]]).

## Pathophysiology
- **Shear fragmentation.** Turbulent, high-velocity flow (regurgitant jet through a paravalvular gap, or across a malfunctioning mechanical leaflet) shreds erythrocyte membranes, generating **schistocytes**.
- **A mechanical microangiopathy.** Unlike TTP/HUS, the driver is a device, not endothelial/ADAMTS13 pathology — but the smear and labs of intravascular hemolysis look similar.
- **Platelet consumption.** The same turbulent, high-shear environment also consumes **platelets**, so valve hemolysis can present with concurrent anemia *and* thrombocytopenia ([[RLR 064 - Hematuria and Post-TAVR Perivalvular Leak|RLR 064]]). This is why reading the anemia can unmask the platelet mechanism (see [[Thrombocytopenia]]).

## Laboratory Signature
Intravascular-hemolysis pattern ([[RLR 064 - Hematuria and Post-TAVR Perivalvular Leak|RLR 064]]):
- **Schistocytes** on the peripheral smear.
- **Elevated LDH** (often markedly).
- **Low/undetectable haptoglobin** (bound and cleared as free hemoglobin is released).
- **Elevated indirect bilirubin**, elevated reticulocytes.
- **Hemoglobinuria / hemoglobinemia** — free hemoglobin in plasma and urine; a positive urine dipstick for "blood" with **few or no RBCs** on microscopy (see [[Free Hemoglobin vs RBC Hematuria]]).
- **Negative direct antiglobulin test** (non-immune mechanism).

## Diagnosis and Management
- **Echocardiography** (transthoracic then transesophageal) to identify the responsible jet — a paravalvular leak, valve dehiscence, or prosthetic dysfunction.
- **Supportive care** — iron and folate repletion (ongoing urinary iron loss from hemoglobinuria), transfusion as needed, ESAs.
- **Definitive therapy** targets the mechanical lesion — percutaneous closure of a paravalvular leak or valve re-intervention when hemolysis is clinically significant.

## Reasoning Note
Valve hemolysis is the link that explains a co-timed anemia and thrombocytopenia after a valve procedure, and it can masquerade as bleeding when hemoglobinuria is mistaken for hematuria ([[RLR 064 - Hematuria and Post-TAVR Perivalvular Leak|RLR 064]]). Suspect a **[[TAVR Perivalvular Leak]]** when the timeline points to a recent valve.
